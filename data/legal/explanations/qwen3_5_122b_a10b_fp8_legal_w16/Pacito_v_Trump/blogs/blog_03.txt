Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## When Contracts Become Mandates: Judicial Intervention in Executive Termination of Cooperative Agreements

The Ninth Circuit's recent procedural maneuver in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a fascinating, if somewhat paradoxical, glimpse into the evolving boundaries of presidential power over immigration and the judiciary's role in policing administrative gaps. While the panel largely deferred to the President's broad statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions—a move that aligns closely with the Supreme Court's deferential stance in *Trump v. Hawaii*—the court drew a hard line at the government's attempt to dismantle the logistical infrastructure required for refugees already admitted to the United States. By staying its previous administrative stays and effectively ordering the State Department to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements with non-profit resettlement agencies, the Ninth Circuit signaled that executive discretion is not absolute when it collides with statutory duties owed to vulnerable populations currently within American borders.

The core tension in this order arises from a clash between two distinct legal regimes: the expansive grant of power to suspend "entry" found in § 1182(f), and the specific, conditional mandates for "resettlement" services codified in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 of the Refugee Act of 1980. The majority opinion acknowledges that the President has likely satisfied his burden under § 1182(f) by finding that continued refugee entry is detrimental to national interests. However, the court pivots sharply when addressing the government's subsequent actions: terminating all cooperative agreements with organizations tasked with providing reception and placement services for refugees who had already been admitted or processed before the suspension took effect. The panel reasoned that because Congress has appropriated funds and enacted statutes requiring specific services—such as employment training, English language acquisition, and interim cash assistance—the executive branch cannot simply opt out of these obligations by firing its vendors and claiming "uncertainty" about future contracting plans.

This distinction marks a significant development in administrative law jurisprudence regarding "statutory avoidance." The government argued, implicitly if not explicitly, that by terminating contracts rather than formally repealing regulations or refusing appropriations, it retained flexibility in managing foreign affairs budgets and operations. The Ninth Circuit rejected this strategy as legally insufficient. In doing so, they treated the network of cooperative agreements not merely as discretionary procurement tools, but as essential mechanisms for fulfilling a congressionally mandated duty. The court's directive forces the administration back into its pre-existing contractual relationships until it can demonstrate a viable path to delivering statutorily required services through new channels. This creates a novel constraint on executive power: even if an agency believes it lacks political will or operational capacity to fulfill a mandate, it cannot create a vacuum by abruptly severing ties with third-party providers without facing judicial intervention to restore service continuity.

The reasoning behind this outcome rests heavily on the textual analysis of § 1522, particularly subsections (d)(2)(B) regarding unaccompanied minors and (e)(7)(A) concerning medical assistance for recent arrivals. While Judge Lee's partial dissent correctly notes that much of § 1522 uses permissive language like "authorized," she overlooks what appears to be the majority's reading of specific subsections containing imperative verbs ("shall") coupled with clear congressional intent to ensure refugee welfare during transition periods. More importantly, Judge Lee misses the pragmatic reality highlighted by the majority: when an agency admits it is unsure whether it can replace its service providers, and refuses to reinstate existing ones, it leaves statutory beneficiaries without recourse. In such scenarios, courts may increasingly view contract termination not as a neutral budgetary adjustment, but as an actionable violation of substantive rights created by statute.

The downstream implications of this ruling extend far beyond refugee resettlement policies first instituted decades ago. If adopted more broadly across other circuits or affirmed on remand, *Pacito* suggests that agencies operating under complex regulatory schemes involving third-party contractors may find themselves locked into those arrangements whenever statutory duties are implicated. Agencies can no longer rely on simple contract expiration or unilateral termination as a shield against claims of non-performance if Congress has attached conditions or requirements to those services via appropriation acts or enabling legislation. This effectively transforms private sector partnerships from flexible instruments of policy execution into de facto extensions of federal obligation that courts can compel reinstatement upon failure to perform alternatives.

Ultimately, *Pacito v. Trump* serves as a reminder that while presidents enjoy wide latitude in determining who enters America, once individuals are admitted under existing laws, their right to certain supports becomes harder for executives to ignore—even through bureaucratic maneuvering designed to avoid direct confrontation with legislative mandates. As litigation continues toward a merits decision on appeal from district court rulings regarding Executive Order 14163, lower courts will likely scrutinize similar tactics where agencies attempt to bypass statutory duties through procurement strategies rather than formal rulemaking changes. Until then, non-profits serving marginalized communities may find themselves wielding stronger leverage than ever before against administrative inertia masquerading as fiscal prudence or policy recalibration.
