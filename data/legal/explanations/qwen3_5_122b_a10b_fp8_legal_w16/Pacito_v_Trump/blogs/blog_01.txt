Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Trump v. Hawaii Redux: The Ninth Circuit's Expansive Reading of Presidential Power Over Refugee Admissions

The Ninth Circuit's September 12, 2025, order in *Pacito v. Trump* marks a significant moment in the evolving jurisprudence of presidential immigration authority. While the court ultimately preserved a narrow lifeline for refugees already inside the United States regarding resettlement services, its handling of the broader challenge to President Trump's "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program" executive order suggests a profound expansion of deference under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). By staying the district court's injunction against the suspension of refugee admissions almost entirely, the panel signaled that the Supreme Court's landmark decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* may now serve as a nearly impenetrable shield for executive actions that shut down entire classes of immigration programs, regardless of the statutory context or the magnitude of the disruption caused.

At the heart of the majority's reasoning is a strict application of *Trump v. Hawaii*, specifically its holding that courts should not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The panel explicitly quoted Justice Roberts' assertion that § 1182(f) "exudes deference," noting that the statute requires only that the President find entry to be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." In *Pacito*, the court concluded that because President Trump issued a formal finding meeting this threshold, his determination was legally sufficient on its face. The panel refused to scrutinize whether a blanket suspension of all refugee admissions—a category distinct from individual visa denials based on security concerns—was rationally connected to national security or merely a political preference disguised as an executive order. This represents a high-water mark for judicial passivity: if a President can cite § 1182(f) to halt an entire humanitarian program based solely on an asserted finding of detriment, without needing to demonstrate specific threats posed by refugees as a class, then Congress's intent in passing the Refugee Act of 1980 risks being rendered moot by unilateral executive fiat.

This expansive reading creates a stark tension with traditional principles of statutory construction and separation of powers. The plaintiffs argued that suspending *all* refugee admissions violated specific mandates within the Refugee Act designed to ensure ongoing treatment for displaced persons. Yet, by categorizing § 1182(f) as having no textual limits beyond the President's subjective finding, the Ninth Circuit effectively elevated this single provision above conflicting statutory schemes. The court acknowledged that *Trump v. Hawaii* involved travel bans targeting specific countries with documented security issues; here, however, we see a total programmatic shutdown affecting vulnerable populations globally, many with no connection to terrorism or national security threats. By declining to distinguish between targeted restrictions and total program eliminations, the panel implies that once § 1182(f) is invoked, its breadth is absolute. This approach raises serious questions about whether lower courts are now barred from examining whether an executive order exceeds its statutory purpose when it conflicts with other clear congressional directives regarding immigration policy.

However, *Pacino* is not a complete victory for unchecked executive power; it contains a crucial doctrinal pivot regarding domestic resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. While granting relief on admissions, the majority distinguished between entering aliens and those already admitted but awaiting support services. Relying on mandatory language like "shall" found in § 1522(a), (d), and (e), coupled with recent Congressional appropriations totaling billions for migration assistance, the court held that while Congress gave the President wide latitude to stop people from coming in, it did not authorize him to abandon those already present or approved for entry who rely on federal funds for survival and integration. The court reasoned that terminating cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies left refugees without essential cash assistance, medical care, and placement services required by statute. This distinction preserves a vital check on executive overreach: even if the door can be slammed shut via § 1182(f), once someone crosses—or is legally deemed ready to cross—the government remains bound by its affirmative duties under separate statutes governing welfare and resettlement.

The partial dissent by Judge Lee highlights just how fragile this boundary might be in future litigation. Judge Lee argued persuasively that § 1522 functions as an authorization rather than a mandate, pointing out that key provisions use permissive language ("is authorized") and qualify obligations with phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations." If adopted by other circuits or reviewed by higher courts, this interpretation could dismantle even this modest protection for refugees currently in transit or newly arrived. The dissent underscores a growing ideological divide within immigration jurisprudence: one side views statutory grants as conditional permissions subject to budgetary discretion and executive prioritization, while others view them as binding commitments once Congress appropriates funds and sets specific goals like economic self-sufficiency for refugees.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as both a warning and a precedent for how courts navigate immigration emergencies in an era of aggressive presidential action. On one hand, it confirms that *Trump v. Hawaii*'s deferential standard is robust enough to survive challenges involving total program suspensions based on generalized assertions of harm. On the other hand, it demonstrates that courts still retain some capacity to enforce specific statutory commands when they involve direct aid to individuals rather than border control mechanics alone. As this case moves toward final judgment on appeal—and potentially toward certiorari—it will test whether these two lines of reasoning can coexist: can courts simultaneously grant presidents near-plenary power over who enters while strictly policing what happens after they arrive? The answer will define not just refugee policy for years to come, but also the balance of power between Article II authority and Article I legislative mandates in American immigration law forevermore
