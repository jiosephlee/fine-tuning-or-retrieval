Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Trump v. Hawaii Redux: The Ninth Circuit's Expansive Reading of Presidential Power Over Refugee Admissions

The Ninth Circuit's September 12, 2025, order in *Pacito v. Trump* marks a significant moment in the evolving jurisprudence of presidential immigration authority. While the court ultimately preserved a narrow lifeline for refugees already inside the United States regarding resettlement services, its handling of the broader challenge to President Trump's "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program" executive order suggests a profound expansion of deference under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). By staying the district court's injunction against the suspension of refugee admissions almost entirely, the panel signaled that the Supreme Court's landmark decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* may now serve as a nearly impenetrable shield for executive actions that shut down entire classes of immigration programs, regardless of the statutory context or the magnitude of the disruption caused.

At the heart of the majority's reasoning is a strict application of *Trump v. Hawaii*, specifically its holding that courts should not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The panel explicitly quoted Justice Roberts' assertion that § 1182(f) "exudes deference," noting that the statute requires only that the President find entry to be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." In *Pacito*, the court concluded that because President Trump issued a formal finding meeting this threshold, his determination was legally sufficient on its face. The panel refused to scrutinize whether a blanket suspension of all refugee admissions—a category distinct from individual visa denials based on security concerns—was rationally connected to national security or merely a political preference disguised as an executive order. This represents a high-water mark for judicial passivity: if a President can cite § 1182(f) to halt an entire humanitarian program based solely on an asserted finding of detriment, without needing to demonstrate specific threats posed by refugees as a class, then Congress's intent in passing the Refugee Act of 1980 risks being rendered moot by unilateral executive fiat.

This expansive reading creates a stark tension with traditional principles of statutory construction and separation of powers. The plaintiffs argued that suspending *all* refugee admissions violated specific mandates within the Refugee Act designed to ensure ongoing treatment for displaced persons. Yet, by categorizing § 1182(f) as having no textual limits beyond the President's subjective finding, the Ninth Circuit effectively elevated this single provision above conflicting statutory schemes. The court acknowledged that *Trump v. Hawaii* involved travel bans targeting specific countries with documented security issues; here, however, we see a total programmatic shutdown affecting vulnerable populations globally, many with no connection to terrorism or national security threats. By declining to distinguish between targeted restrictions and total program eliminations, the panel implies that once § 1182(f) is invoked, its breadth is absolute. This approach raises serious questions about whether lower courts are now barred from examining whether an executive order exceeds its statutory purpose when it conflicts with other clear congressional directives regarding immigration policy.

However, *Pacino* is not a complete victory for unchecked executive power; it contains a crucial doctrinal pivot regarding domestic resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. While granting relief on admissions, the majority distinguished between entering aliens and those already admitted but awaiting support services. Relying on mandatory language like "shall" found in § 1522(a), (d), and (e), coupled with recent Congressional appropriations totaling billions for migration assistance, the court held that while Congress gave the President wide latitude to stop people from coming in, it did not authorize him to abandon those already present or approved for entry who rely on federal funds for survival and integration. The court reasoned that terminating cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies left refugees without essential cash assistance, medical care, and placement services required by statute. This distinction preserves a vital check on executive overreach: even if the door can be slammed shut via § 1182(f), once someone crosses—or is legally deemed ready to cross—the government remains bound by its affirmative duties under separate statutes governing welfare and resettlement.

The partial dissent by Judge Lee highlights just how fragile this boundary might be in future litigation. Judge Lee argued persuasively that § 1522 functions as an authorization rather than a mandate, pointing out that key provisions use permissive language ("is authorized") and qualify obligations with phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations." If adopted by other circuits or reviewed by higher courts, this interpretation could dismantle even this modest protection for refugees currently in transit or newly arrived. The dissent underscores a growing ideological divide within immigration jurisprudence: one side views statutory grants as conditional permissions subject to budgetary discretion and executive prioritization, while others view them as binding commitments once Congress appropriates funds and sets specific goals like economic self-sufficiency for refugees.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as both a warning and a precedent for how courts navigate immigration emergencies in an era of aggressive presidential action. On one hand, it confirms that *Trump v. Hawaii*'s deferential standard is robust enough to survive challenges involving total program suspensions based on generalized assertions of harm. On the other hand, it demonstrates that courts still retain some capacity to enforce specific statutory commands when they involve direct aid to individuals rather than border control mechanics alone. As this case moves toward final judgment on appeal—and potentially toward certiorari—it will test whether these two lines of reasoning can coexist: can courts simultaneously grant presidents near-plenary power over who enters while strictly policing what happens after they arrive? The answer will define not just refugee policy for years to come, but also the balance of power between Article II authority and Article I legislative mandates in American immigration law forevermore



## Mandatory vs. Permissive: The Statutory Battle Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and Reception Services

The Ninth Circuit's September 12 order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a striking snapshot of the modern administrative state in crisis mode. While the panel unanimously agreed to stay the lower court's injunction blocking President Trump's suspension of refugee admissions—a move heavily anchored in the broad deference owed to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as interpreted in *Trump v. Hawaii*—the real legal fireworks lie in the court's partial reversal regarding domestic resettlement services. In a rare display of judicial intervention into executive budgetary discretion, the majority held that the government must reinstate cooperative agreements with NGOs to fulfill statutory mandates for "reception and placement" services. This bifurcated outcome turns entirely on a single, deceptively simple question of statutory construction: Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 merely authorize the government to help refugees, or does it command them to?

The majority's reasoning rests on a textualist reading that treats specific statutory commands as binding even amidst broader discretionary language. The panel focused on subsections of § 1522 that utilize mandatory phrasing, particularly the directive that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettling "shall... make available sufficient resources" for employment training and cash assistance, qualified only by the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations." Crucially, Congress had indeed appropriated billions for migration assistance in FY 2025, albeit with recent reductions via a rescission act. The court concluded that because funds existed, the executive branch could not simply terminate all existing cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies without violating its duty to provide these statutorily required services. By directing the reinstatement of these agreements, the Ninth Circuit effectively ruled that once Congress appropriates money and sets conditions for its use, the Executive cannot unilaterally decide to stop spending it on those specific purposes by firing its contractors.

Judge Lee's partial dissent provides a compelling counter-narrative rooted in structural separation-of-powers concerns and a strict reading of statutory titles and headers. She argues that § 1522 is fundamentally an authorization statute, not a mandate. Her analysis hinges on the section's title—"Authorization for programs"—and her observation that nearly every substantive provision within it uses permissive language ("is authorized," "may"). Judge Lee contends that the mandatory "shall" found in subsection (a) functions only as a conditional constraint: *if* the government chooses to fund these programs, *then* it must adhere to certain equity and efficiency standards (such as ensuring gender parity or prioritizing self-sufficiency). However, she argues this does not compel the government to actually run the programs at all. Under her reading, unless Congress explicitly strips away executive discretion with unambiguous mandatory language applicable across all subsections, courts should hesitate to convert budgetary authorizations into affirmative duties that force agencies to hire vendors they wish to fire.

This disagreement highlights a growing friction point in administrative law: how courts should parse statutes where Congress mixes permissive grants of authority with mandatory directives conditioned on funding availability. The majority's approach suggests that when Congress appropriates funds specifically tied to a statutory scheme containing "shall" language regarding service delivery, those conditions ripen into enforceable rights against arbitrary withdrawal by the Executive. Conversely, Judge Lee's view reflects a more traditional skepticism of judicial micromanagement of agency priorities, warning against transforming vague legislative intent into rigid operational mandates. If adopted broadly by other circuits, Judge Lee's logic could significantly insulate agencies from lawsuits challenging funding cuts or contract terminations, provided those actions can be framed as exercises of discretion rather than violations of explicit commands.

The downstream implications of *Pacito*'s split holding extend far beyond refugee resettlement. Many federal grant programs operate under similar hybrid frameworks where Congress appropriates funds but leaves execution largely to agency discretion through cooperative agreements or contracts. A precedent establishing that "shall... to the extent of available appropriations" creates an affirmative duty to maintain specific vendor relationships could embolden litigants in other sectors—from education grants to environmental remediation—to challenge sudden shifts in federal contracting policy whenever appropriations exist on paper but not in practice for specific recipients. It forces agencies into a difficult bind: either continue operating programs they politically oppose until Congress explicitly repeals them or amends their funding structure with clearer discretionary language, or face immediate injunctions restoring their previous operational status quo.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a reminder that even in areas where presidential power is at its zenith—such as border control and national security—the judiciary remains willing to intervene when statutory text appears clear enough to constrain executive action regarding domestic welfare obligations. While President Trump may successfully pause new arrivals under § 1182(f), his administration cannot easily dismantle the infrastructure supporting those already inside without navigating complex statutory hurdles created by decades-old legislation designed precisely for this moment: ensuring refugees are not just let in, but helped once they arrive. As this case moves toward a full merits opinion later this fall, watch closely whether other circuits adopt Judge Lee's flexible view of authorization statutes or follow Ninth Circuit precedent treating conditional appropriations as hard constraints on executive power.



## The Operational Void: Judicial Mandates to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements Amidst Executive Uncertainty

The Ninth Circuit's September 12 order in *Pacito v. Trump* presents a striking illustration of the friction between broad presidential immigration powers and the gritty realities of administrative execution. While the panel largely deferred to the Executive Branch's authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—leaning heavily on the "ample power" doctrine articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii*—it simultaneously carved out a narrow but profound exception regarding the administration of existing statutory duties. By staying the district court's injunction against the suspension of admissions while mandating the reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements for refugee resettlement services, the court has stepped into a logistical quagmire that raises difficult questions about judicial competence, separation of powers, and the limits of equitable relief when the government admits it lacks a plan.

The core tension in this order lies not in the legal standard applied to the suspension itself, but in how the court addressed the operational vacuum created by that suspension. The government successfully argued that § 1182(f) grants the President near-plenary authority to halt entries if deemed "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The panel agreed, finding that once the President makes that factual finding, judicial review is extremely limited. However, the court pivoted sharply when addressing 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs "reception and placement" services for refugees already admitted or awaiting admission. Here, unlike the discretionary nature of entry bans, § 1522 contains specific mandates regarding cash assistance, medical services, and legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors.

What makes *Pacito* particularly notable is not merely that the court found a statutory violation in cutting off these services, but *how* it chose to remedy it. The panel did not simply declare that funding must continue; it issued a specific directive ordering the State Department to reinstate its terminated cooperative agreements with private resettlement organizations. This remedy was driven by a stark admission from government counsel during oral arguments: despite telling lower courts weeks earlier that they were "actively preparing a request for proposals" for new agencies, officials at oral argument conceded they had no information on whether any progress had been made toward securing replacements. Faced with this uncertainty and a statutory obligation to care for vulnerable populations—including unaccompanied minors—the court concluded that leaving refugees without a provider was an unacceptable outcome and that returning to pre-existing contracts was the only viable path forward.

This approach invites scrutiny regarding the proper role of federal courts in managing administrative logistics. By ordering specific performance—effectively commanding an executive agency to re-sign contracts it had legally terminated—the Ninth Circuit has moved beyond traditional declaratory or prohibitory injunctions into direct management of executive operations. One might argue this encroaches on executive prerogative; however, as Judge Lee notes in his partial dissent, § 1522 is arguably an authorization rather than a mandate. If Judge Lee's reading were adopted—that Congress merely gave permission for these programs rather than requiring them—the entire foundation for this remedial order collapses. Yet, assuming § 1522 creates an affirmative duty (as implied by phrases like "shall... make available sufficient resources"), does a court have any other choice? If Congress appropriates funds for these services but leaves them unfunded because an agency refuses to hire vendors or sign contracts until new policies are drafted, who ensures compliance? The court seemingly decided that waiting for bureaucratic restructuring would result in irreparable harm to refugees who have nowhere else to turn.

The downstream implications of this order extend far beyond refugee resettlement. First, it establishes a precedent where courts may feel compelled to micromanage administrative continuity when an agency demonstrates incompetence or bad faith in executing statutory duties. If an agency can terminate essential service providers overnight without having replacement mechanisms ready, and faces immediate judicial intervention forcing them back into old arrangements, future administrations may be more cautious about abrupt policy shifts involving third-party contractors. Conversely, this could encourage agencies to litigate delays rather than seek quick resolutions if they believe courts are reluctant to intervene in complex procurement matters unless total chaos ensues—a threshold clearly met here given the vulnerability of unaccompanied minors and recent arrivals lacking housing or food security support networks.

Furthermore, this ruling highlights a growing disconnect between statutory mandates and fiscal reality within immigration law cycles. The opinion acknowledges Congressional appropriation changes via rescission acts yet insists on maintaining service levels regardless of budgetary constraints cited by defendants ("to the extent of available appropriations"). While courts generally avoid interfering with spending decisions absent clear statutory violations requiring expenditure from unappropriated funds (*Pacino*'s distinction being crucial here involves existing contractual obligations versus new spending), this case blurs those lines slightly by prioritizing human welfare over procedural flexibility favored by executive agencies during periods of political transition or policy overhaul.
