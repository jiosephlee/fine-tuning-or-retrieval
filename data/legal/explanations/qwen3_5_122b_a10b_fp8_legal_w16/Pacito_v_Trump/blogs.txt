Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## The Limits of Deference: How *Pacito* Carves Out Mandatory Spending from Presidential Immigration Power

The Ninth Circuit's September 12, 2025, stay order in *Pacito v. Trump* presents a fascinating study in judicial compartmentalization. At first glance, the panel's decision appears to grant the Biden-era administration—or rather, the returning Trump administration—a sweeping victory on national security grounds. By staying lower court injunctions that blocked the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), the court leaned heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, affirming that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) exudes "deference" to the President when he determines that alien entry is detrimental to U.S. interests. However, the true significance of *Pacito* lies not in what it allowed the executive branch to do, but in what it forbade: the total abandonment of statutory duties toward refugees already within American borders. In a single paragraph of discussion, the panel drew a sharp line between the plenary power to close the door and the mandatory obligation to care for those who have already walked through it.

The panel's reasoning regarding refugee admissions is straightforward and rooted firmly in existing precedent. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the majority held that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) sets a low bar for presidential action: a mere finding that entry would be "detrimental." Because President Trump issued an executive order making exactly that finding, the court concluded there was no textual limit exceeded by suspending new admissions. The panel explicitly declined to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the persuasiveness of the justification, echoing the Supreme Court's refusal to scrutinize national security rationales in immigration cases. Consequently, the district court's injunction blocking EO 14163 was stayed in its entirety regarding future admissions. This portion of the order reaffirms that when Congress delegates broad authority over border control and entry restrictions, courts are ill-equipped and unwilling to second-guess policy determinations about who gets let into the country.

Yet, where many might expect this deference to bleed over into all aspects of refugee policy, *Pacito* pulls back abruptly once it turns to resettlement services for refugees already admitted or processed for admission prior to January 20, 2025. Here, the court pivots from constitutional-style deference to strict statutory construction under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The Refugee Act of 1980 does more than authorize programs; according to the Ninth Circuit, it commands them. The panel highlighted specific language mandating that the Director of HHS "shall" provide employment training, cash assistance, and English language training "to refugees." Crucially, these statutes tie funding obligations to "available appropriations," acknowledging budgetary realities but refusing to accept complete non-performance as an option when funds exist or are being rescinded without replacing capacity.

The practical impact of this distinction is immediate and severe for how an administration can execute its immigration agenda. The government argued during oral argument that it had terminated all cooperative agreements with private resettlement agencies and was merely "preparing" new ones—a process that left a vacuum in care for thousands of refugees currently inside or transiting to the United States. The Ninth Circuit found this gap unacceptable under § 1522(d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which impose affirmative duties on HHS regarding unaccompanied minors and recent arrivals alike. By directing the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to fulfill these statutory mandates, the court effectively ruled that while the President can stop new people from coming in via proclamation, he cannot simply defund and dissolve his legal obligation to house and feed those who are already here because they were approved under previous rules or arrived before his order took effect.

This creates a potent doctrinal friction between two distinct bodies of immigration law: Entry vs. Status/Resettlement rights. Judge Lee's partial dissent captures this tension perfectly by characterizing § 1522 as purely permissive—an "authorization" rather than a mandate—and viewing terms like "shall" as conditional upon executive discretion regarding resource allocation ("to the extent of available appropriations"). While Judge Lee argues that Congress intended flexibility for political branches to scale programs based on shifting priorities, the majority interprets "authorization" titles alongside specific operative clauses containing mandatory verbs ("shall") as creating binding duties once Congress appropriates money for those purposes. This interpretation suggests that even under an expansive reading of presidential power over entry (§ 1182(f)), Congress retains its Article I power of appropriation as a check on executive conduct once funds are allocated for humanitarian protection (§ 1522).

The downstream implications of *Pacito* extend far beyond this specific executive order challenge. If upheld on appeal or adopted by other circuits, this reasoning limits how aggressively any administration can dismantle existing refugee infrastructure overnight without violating federal law. It establishes that while an Executive Order can freeze future intake indefinitely based on broad national security claims (subject only to rational basis review), it cannot unilaterally repeal statutory entitlements attached to past approvals or current residents without explicit congressional amendment or rescission of funding itself—which did not happen here; rather, funds remained appropriated despite attempts to withhold them administratively via contract terminations). ## 'Authorized' vs. 'Required': The Statutory Interpretation Battle Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522

The Ninth Circuit's September 12 order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a rare glimpse into the mechanics of a high-stakes immigration dispute before the court issues its final merits opinion. While the panel unanimously agreed to lift previous stays and broadly uphold President Trump's authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—leaning heavily on the Supreme Court's deferential precedent in *Trump v. Hawaii*—the real legal drama lies in the split over what happens after refugees arrive. In a partial concurrence and partial dissent, Judge Lee exposes a fundamental fracture in statutory interpretation that could reverberate far beyond refugee policy: Does the Refugee Act of 1980 merely give the executive branch permission to help newcomers, or does it legally compel the government to do so?

The majority's decision to partially vacate the stay hinges entirely on a specific reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the statute governing "programs for domestic resettlement." The panel concluded that while the President can freeze the front door, he cannot leave admitted refugees stranded without services once they cross it. The court pointed to specific subsections mandating that officials "shall" provide employment training, cash assistance, and medical services "to the extent of available appropriations." Crucially, the majority treated these "shall" commands as creating affirmative legal duties that survive even when Congress reduces funding levels, provided some appropriation exists. Because the government admitted during oral argument that it was unsure if it could fulfill these statutory mandates without reinstating its network of private resettlement agencies, the court ordered those cooperative agreements restored as an interim measure. This is a significant assertion of judicial power: it treats the Refugee Act not as a wish list for executive goodwill, but as a binding contract between Congress and vulnerable populations.

Judge Lee's dissent cuts against this grain with surgical precision, focusing on the linguistic architecture of § 1522 rather than its humanitarian outcomes. His argument rests on a classic textualist distinction between authorization and mandate. He notes that the section's title explicitly reads "Authorization for programs," and throughout subsections (b) through (e), Congress repeatedly states that agency directors are "authorized" to provide assistance. To Judge Lee, this pattern signals congressional intent to grant discretion, not impose obligation. He argues that even where subsection (a) uses mandatory language like "shall," it functions only as a conditional constraint—a rulebook for *how* to spend money if spending occurs—rather than a trigger forcing expenditure itself. Under his reading, phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations" act as an escape hatch for any perceived mandate; if funds are cut or repurposed by political will, the statutory duty evaporates because no money remains to satisfy the condition.

This disagreement highlights a persistent tension in administrative law regarding how courts parse titles versus operative text when determining legislative intent. The majority prioritized specific operative verbs ("shall") and substantive guarantees found deep within the code structure over the section heading's label ("Authorization"). They viewed these provisions as creating individual entitlements akin to social safety net statutes where eligibility triggers automatic benefits regardless of budgetary whims (within reason). Conversely, Judge Lee elevated the section title and recurring use of permissive verbs ("authorized") as controlling indicators of congressional design. His approach suggests that unless Congress speaks with unmistakable clarity in overriding terms—free from qualifying language about appropriations—the executive retains broad latitude to decide whether and how much aid to distribute.

The downstream implications of this interpretive battle extend well beyond refugee resettlement. If Judge Lee's view gains traction in future litigation, it could fundamentally alter how courts analyze thousands of federal welfare and aid programs across health care, education, and environmental grants that utilize similar "authorized... subject to appropriations" frameworks. A ruling favoring strict mandates forces agencies to maintain service levels even amidst shifting political priorities or tight budgets, potentially locking executives into rigid spending regimes they did not choose via regulation alone. However, adopting Lee's more permissive standard risks rendering statutory protections illusory; if an agency can simply cite lack of priority or redirected funds to avoid compliance with "shall" clauses conditioned on appropriations, many protective laws lose their teeth before they ever reach a courtroom.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a microcosm for current debates over administrative capacity and statutory fidelity in an era of polarized governance. The Ninth Circuit's interim order attempts a pragmatic compromise: allowing the President to control who enters while preventing him from abandoning those already inside due to bureaucratic inertia or political strategy enforced by funding freezes. Yet until an opinion resolves whether § 1522 is truly mandatory or merely permissive, lower courts face uncertainty in enforcing similar provisions nationwide. The coming merits decision will determine whether Congress spoke with enough force in 1980 to bind future administrations against cutting off lifelines for refugees—a question whose answer depends less on humanitarian sympathy than on whether judges believe Congress meant "authorize" when it wrote "authorization."



## When Contracts Become Mandates: Judicial Intervention in Executive Termination of Cooperative Agreements

The Ninth Circuit's recent procedural maneuver in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a fascinating, if somewhat paradoxical, glimpse into the evolving boundaries of presidential power over immigration and the judiciary's role in policing administrative gaps. While the panel largely deferred to the President's broad statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions—a move that aligns closely with the Supreme Court's deferential stance in *Trump v. Hawaii*—the court drew a hard line at the government's attempt to dismantle the logistical infrastructure required for refugees already admitted to the United States. By staying its previous administrative stays and effectively ordering the State Department to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements with non-profit resettlement agencies, the Ninth Circuit signaled that executive discretion is not absolute when it collides with statutory duties owed to vulnerable populations currently within American borders.

The core tension in this order arises from a clash between two distinct legal regimes: the expansive grant of power to suspend "entry" found in § 1182(f), and the specific, conditional mandates for "resettlement" services codified in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 of the Refugee Act of 1980. The majority opinion acknowledges that the President has likely satisfied his burden under § 1182(f) by finding that continued refugee entry is detrimental to national interests. However, the court pivots sharply when addressing the government's subsequent actions: terminating all cooperative agreements with organizations tasked with providing reception and placement services for refugees who had already been admitted or processed before the suspension took effect. The panel reasoned that because Congress has appropriated funds and enacted statutes requiring specific services—such as employment training, English language acquisition, and interim cash assistance—the executive branch cannot simply opt out of these obligations by firing its vendors and claiming "uncertainty" about future contracting plans.

This distinction marks a significant development in administrative law jurisprudence regarding "statutory avoidance." The government argued, implicitly if not explicitly, that by terminating contracts rather than formally repealing regulations or refusing appropriations, it retained flexibility in managing foreign affairs budgets and operations. The Ninth Circuit rejected this strategy as legally insufficient. In doing so, they treated the network of cooperative agreements not merely as discretionary procurement tools, but as essential mechanisms for fulfilling a congressionally mandated duty. The court's directive forces the administration back into its pre-existing contractual relationships until it can demonstrate a viable path to delivering statutorily required services through new channels. This creates a novel constraint on executive power: even if an agency believes it lacks political will or operational capacity to fulfill a mandate, it cannot create a vacuum by abruptly severing ties with third-party providers without facing judicial intervention to restore service continuity.

The reasoning behind this outcome rests heavily on the textual analysis of § 1522, particularly subsections (d)(2)(B) regarding unaccompanied minors and (e)(7)(A) concerning medical assistance for recent arrivals. While Judge Lee's partial dissent correctly notes that much of § 1522 uses permissive language like "authorized," she overlooks what appears to be the majority's reading of specific subsections containing imperative verbs ("shall") coupled with clear congressional intent to ensure refugee welfare during transition periods. More importantly, Judge Lee misses the pragmatic reality highlighted by the majority: when an agency admits it is unsure whether it can replace its service providers, and refuses to reinstate existing ones, it leaves statutory beneficiaries without recourse. In such scenarios, courts may increasingly view contract termination not as a neutral budgetary adjustment, but as an actionable violation of substantive rights created by statute.

The downstream implications of this ruling extend far beyond refugee resettlement policies first instituted decades ago. If adopted more broadly across other circuits or affirmed on remand, *Pacito* suggests that agencies operating under complex regulatory schemes involving third-party contractors may find themselves locked into those arrangements whenever statutory duties are implicated. Agencies can no longer rely on simple contract expiration or unilateral termination as a shield against claims of non-performance if Congress has attached conditions or requirements to those services via appropriation acts or enabling legislation. This effectively transforms private sector partnerships from flexible instruments of policy execution into de facto extensions of federal obligation that courts can compel reinstatement upon failure to perform alternatives.

Ultimately, *Pacito v. Trump* serves as a reminder that while presidents enjoy wide latitude in determining who enters America, once individuals are admitted under existing laws, their right to certain supports becomes harder for executives to ignore—even through bureaucratic maneuvering designed to avoid direct confrontation with legislative mandates. As litigation continues toward a merits decision on appeal from district court rulings regarding Executive Order 14163, lower courts will likely scrutinize similar tactics where agencies attempt to bypass statutory duties through procurement strategies rather than formal rulemaking changes. Until then, non-profits serving marginalized communities may find themselves wielding stronger leverage than ever before against administrative inertia masquerading as fiscal prudence or policy recalibration.
