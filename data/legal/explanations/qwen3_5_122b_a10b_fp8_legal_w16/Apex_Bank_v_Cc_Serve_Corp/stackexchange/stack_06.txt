Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Does an Intent-to-Use application grant priority rights superior to existing registrations during an opposition proceeding before actual use begins?
Question:
Apex filed an Intent-to-Use (ITU) application in August 2019 with a priority date tied to filing, while CC Serve had registered ASPIRE back in 1998 with a priority date of 1996. Since Apex hadn't launched their internet bank yet at the time of opposition, did they have any standing to argue their mark was valid against CC Serve's long-standing registration? How does ITU status interact with Section 2(d) oppositions when there is no actual commerce yet?
Answer:
**No.** An Intent-to-Use (ITU) application does **not** grant priority rights superior to an existing registration during a Section 2(d) opposition, nor does it allow the applicant to bypass the requirement of establishing a likelihood of confusion based on actual market conditions.

Under the Lanham Act and the precedent established in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL [Case Number], an ITU applicant possesses only a "constructive" priority date relative to future users who file later, but this right remains subordinate to any prior registered user who has already secured rights through use in commerce.

In *Apex*, the Federal Circuit explicitly affirmed that CC Serve's 1998 registration (with a 1996 priority date) held superior standing over Apex's 2019 ITU application. The court noted that Apex had not yet launched its internet bank or engaged in actual commerce at the time of the opposition. Despite Apex's claim that its filing date gave it leverage, the Board correctly applied Section 2(d) by analyzing whether Apex's proposed use would cause confusion with CC Serve's *existing* registered mark.

The core legal principle illustrated here is that an ITU application is merely a reservation of rights contingent upon eventual use; it does not create an immediate, superior property interest that can defeat a prior registrant. As the court stated, a Section 2(d) opposition requires determining if confusion is likely "because of concurrent use of the marks." While an ITU applicant can oppose another party's mark once they prove bona fide intent and subsequent use, they cannot successfully defend their own pending application against a senior registrant simply by virtue of their filing date.

Furthermore, the *Apex* opinion reinforces that without actual use, an applicant cannot alter the scope of protection afforded to a senior mark. The court vacated certain aspects of the Board's decision only because it failed to properly apply the *DuPont* factors regarding third-party usage (specifically Factor Six), not because Apex's ITU status granted it special immunity or superiority. The court affirmed that where services are "highly similar" (Factor Two)—as found between credit card services and general banking—the senior registrant retains the presumption of validity and priority unless overcome by specific evidence of weakness or lack of confusion, which Apex failed to provide initially due to its reliance on its filing date rather than market reality.

Therefore, until an ITU applicant files a Statement of Use and achieves registration based on actual commerce, their rights remain prospective and inferior to any pre-existing registration covering similar goods or services.
