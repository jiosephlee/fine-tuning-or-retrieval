Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What constitutes sufficient proof of 'commercial weakness' under DuPont Factor 6, and why did nine third-party uses fail to meet that threshold?
Question:
Apex submitted evidence showing nine other uses of 'Aspire' or Aspire-formative marks in credit card services. The Board dismissed this, saying nine wasn't enough to prove 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' use needed to weaken CC Serve's mark. They cited cases mentioning 14 or 26 examples as significant. Is there a hard numerical rule here? Or is it purely qualitative? If nine companies are using similar names in a crowded financial sector, shouldn't that logically make consumers more sophisticated at distinguishing sources? Why didn't nine suffice here?
Answer:
There is **no hard numerical rule** determining what constitutes "sufficient" proof of commercial weakness under the sixth *DuPont* factor. As the Federal Circuit clarified in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the sufficiency of third-party uses is a **qualitative inquiry** dependent on whether the evidence demonstrates that the market is sufficiently crowded to educate consumers to distinguish between similar marks.

In this case, the Board dismissed Apex's evidence of nine third-party uses of "Aspire" or "Aspire"-formative marks in credit card services because it deemed that number insufficient to show "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use. The Board relied on precedent where courts found significance in higher numbers—specifically citing *Jack Wolfskin*, which noted fourteen notable examples, and *Juice Generation*, which referred to twenty-six marks as a "considerable number." However, the Federal Circuit explicitly rejected the notion that these numbers created a rigid threshold. Instead, the Court emphasized that the core legal principle is whether the volume of similar marks indicates that consumers have become adept at distinguishing sources in a crowded field (*Juice Generation*; *Jack Wolfskin*).

The reason nine uses failed to meet the threshold in the Board's initial analysis was not merely because nine is lower than fourteen or twenty-six, but because those specific instances did not rise to the level of **"voluminous"** or **"ubiquitous"** use required to prove that CC Serve's mark had lost its distinctiveness. The Board reasoned that while CC Serve's mark might not possess exceptional commercial strength, Apex had failed to prove it was so weak as to warrant only a narrow scope of protection. Consequently, without evidence showing a saturation of similar marks that would force consumers to scrutinize differences closely, the presumption remained that an inherently distinctive mark like "ASPIRE" retains its normal scope of protection.

It is crucial to note, however, that while nine uses were deemed insufficient by the Board under its original (narrow) framework, the Federal Circuit ultimately **vacated** this finding due to a separate legal error: the Board improperly limited its search for third-party marks strictly to "credit card services," excluding broader banking and financing services despite having previously ruled those services were highly similar under Factor 2. Had those additional forty-two marks been included in the calculation as mandated by proper legal standards, the total count might have shifted from being "insufficient" to potentially demonstrating a crowded marketplace. Thus, while there is no magic number where nine automatically fails and twenty-six automatically succeeds, nine was insufficient here because it did not objectively demonstrate a market so saturated that consumer confusion was unlikely.
