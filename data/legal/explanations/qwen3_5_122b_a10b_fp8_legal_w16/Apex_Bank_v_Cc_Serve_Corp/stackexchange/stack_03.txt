Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Is there a requirement for consistency in defining 'similar goods/services' across different DuPont factors within the same proceeding?
Question:
This is where I'm most confused about the court's reasoning. The Board found in Factor 2 that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services were highly similar (even partially identical). Yet, when moving to Factor 6 (third-party marks), the Board suddenly narrowed its scope *only* to marks used strictly for 'credit card services,' excluding broader banking/financing marks. The Federal Circuit called this an error. Why is it legally required that if Factor 2 says 'these industries overlap,' Factor 6 must use that same broad definition of overlap? Can't one argue that while banking and credit cards are related generally (Factor 2), third-party competition is only fierce specifically within credit card administration (Factor 6)?
Answer:
Yes, there is a requirement for consistency in defining "similar goods" across different *DuPont* factors within the same proceeding. The Federal Circuit's decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* establishes that once a tribunal makes a factual finding regarding the relationship between the parties' services under one factor, it cannot arbitrarily narrow that definition when applying subsequent factors without a valid legal basis.

In this case, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) committed legal error by engaging in what amounts to "cherry-picking" the scope of similarity depending on which factor was being analyzed. Under **Factor 2** (similarity of goods/services), the Board explicitly found that Apex's "banking and financing services" and CC Serve's "credit card services" were **"highly similar"** and even **"partially legally identical."** The Board relied on dictionary definitions showing that banking encompasses issuing credit cards and cited third-party registrations covering both sectors to support the conclusion that these services could emanate from a single source.

However, when analyzing **Factor 6** (number and nature of similar marks in use), the Board abruptly shifted its analytical lens. Despite its earlier finding that banking and credit card services are closely related, the Board restricted the universe of relevant third-party marks to only those used strictly for "credit card services," excluding marks used for broader banking or financing services. The Board deemed these broader marks "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit rejected this approach as inconsistent. Writing for the panel, Judge Hughes noted that Factor 6 requires an evaluation of similar marks on **"similar goods,"** not necessarily *identical* goods (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*). Since the Board had already established as a matter of fact under Factor 2 that the parties' services are highly similar (and partially identical), there was no legal justification to apply a stricter definition of similarity under Factor 6. The court stated: *"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."*

To allow otherwise would create an illogical standard where two sets of goods can be considered sufficiently related to cause confusion between two specific parties (Factor 2) but insufficiently related to show that a crowded marketplace exists among third parties (Factor 6). Such inconsistency undermines the holistic nature of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, where all factors must rest upon a coherent set of factual premises regarding market reality. Consequently, because Factor 6 determines commercial strength—which directly impacts **Factor 1** (similarity of marks)—the Federal Circuit vacated both findings and remanded for reconsideration using a consistent definition of service similarity derived from Factor 2.
