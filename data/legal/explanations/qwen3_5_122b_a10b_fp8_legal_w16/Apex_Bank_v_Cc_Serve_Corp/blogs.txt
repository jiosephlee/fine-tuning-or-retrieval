Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## Inconsistency in Similarity Standards: Why the Federal Circuit Demands Uniformity Across DuPont Factors

In the intricate dance of trademark litigation, consistency is often the difference between a robust legal framework and a chaotic free-for-all. Yet, until recently, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) had developed a troubling tendency to shift the goalposts depending on which *DuPont* factor it was evaluating. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided September 25, 2025, the Federal Circuit put a stop to this compartmentalized approach. By vacating and remanding key aspects of the Board's likelihood-of-confusion analysis, the court established a critical procedural principle: once a tribunal determines that two sets of services are "similar," that definition must remain fixed throughout the multi-factor inquiry. You cannot declare banking and credit card services "highly similar" to justify blocking a registration, only to suddenly treat them as distinct worlds when counting third-party uses to gauge market saturation.

The dispute in *Apex Bank* arose from a classic conflict over the word "ASPIRE." CC Serve owned an early registration for "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services, while Apex Bank sought to register "ASPIRE BANK" for broader banking and financing services. The TTAB initially sided with CC Serve, sustaining an opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. The Board's logic seemed straightforward at first glance: it found that credit card services and general banking/financing services were so closely related—indeed, partially "legally identical"—that consumers would assume they came from the same source. Under the second *DuPont* factor (relatedness of goods/services), this finding heavily favored CC Serve.

However, cracks began to appear when the Board analyzed the sixth *DuPont* factor: the number and nature of similar marks on similar goods. This factor is crucial because if a marketplace is crowded with many users employing similar terms for similar services, it suggests that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing between them, thereby weakening any single claimant's rights. Here, Apex presented evidence of dozens of third-party marks using "Aspire" or variations thereof across the financial sector. The Board acknowledged these marks existed but dismissed most of them as irrelevant. It argued that because its earlier analysis focused on "credit card services," it should restrict its search for third-party marks strictly to that niche, excluding broader financial service providers. Finding only nine such marks within that self-imposed box was insufficient to prove weakness; therefore, CC Serve retained its broad scope of protection.

This is where Chief Judge Hughes drew a sharp line in the sand for the Federal Circuit's jurisprudence. The court identified a fundamental logical flaw in treating "similarity" as a fluid concept rather than a stable factual predicate. If the Board has already concluded under Factor Two that Apex's proposed banking services and CC Serve's credit card services are highly similar—so much so that they could emanate from a single source—it makes no sense to arbitrarily narrow that definition when applying Factor Six. As Judge Hughes wrote, "When analyzing the sixth DuPont factor... [the] Board restricted the universe of marks it considered... That was an error."

The court's reasoning highlights a deeper doctrinal necessity: coherence in administrative adjudication. The *DuPont* factors are not isolated silos; they are interlocking gears designed to paint a holistic picture of consumer perception. By narrowing the scope for Factor Six after establishing broad similarity for Factor Two, the Board effectively insulated CC Serve from evidence that might have diluted its mark's strength in exactly those markets where competition was fiercest. If consumers regularly encounter multiple entities offering financial products under names like "Aspire Finance," "Aspire Wealth," or simply "Aspire Banking," they are arguably better equipped to distinguish Apex's specific usage than if they only ever saw one player in that space. The Board's artificial limitation ignored this reality by ignoring half the market data available to it.

The implications of *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* extend far beyond this specific dispute over credit cards and checking accounts. For practitioners before both the TTAB and federal district courts handling trademark appeals, this decision serves as a warning against inconsistent definitional boundaries counsel can exploit—or tribunals can inadvertently create via oversight). Going forward, if an examiner or board finds goods or services to be related enough to trigger confusion concerns under one factor, they must maintain that relational scope when weighing other factors like trade channels, purchaser sophistication, or third-party use cases. A finding of similarity cannot be turned on or off like a dimmer switch depending on which side of the balance scale is being weighted at any given moment.

Furthermore, this ruling reinforces the Federal Circuit's role as a rigorous check on administrative agencies' reasoning processes without necessarily dictating outcomes based on policy preferences alone. The court did not rule that Apex definitely wins; indeed, it affirmed the Board's finding on Factor Two regarding service similarity and left open whether sufficient third-party use exists even under a corrected scope analysis upon remand what remains clear is that any future determination must rest on internally consistent premises regarding what constitutes "similar goods." 

Ultimately *Apex Bank* reminds us that trademark law relies heavily on how ordinary consumers perceive commercial realities—and those perceptions don't change just because an agency changes its analytical frame mid-stream by demanding uniformity across all applicable factors ensures decisions reflect actual marketplace dynamics rather than procedural artifacts ensuring fairness isn't sacrificed at altar convenience



## The Ripple Effect: How Third-Party Use Analysis Reshapes Commercial Strength and Mark Comparison

In the high-stakes arena of trademark oppositions, the Federal Circuit delivered a sharp reminder on September 25, 2025, that the *DuPont* factors are not isolated silos but an interconnected ecosystem where an error in one can topple the analysis of another. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) decision that had blocked Apex Bank's attempt to register "ASPIRE BANK." While the Board correctly identified that banking and credit card services are highly similar, its failure to look beyond a narrowly defined universe of third-party marks when assessing commercial strength created a fatal flaw in its likelihood-of-confusion calculus. For practitioners navigating Section 2(d) disputes, *Apex Bank* serves as a critical case study: the scope of your evidence regarding third-party use dictates not just the weight of a specific factor, but the very commercial impression of the mark itself.

The core dispute centered on whether Apex's proposed "ASPIRE BANK" marks would confuse consumers with CC Serve's existing "ASPIRE" registration for credit card services. The TTAB initially ruled against Apex, leaning heavily on two findings: first, that the parties' services were so similar they were effectively "legally identical," and second, that CC Serve's mark possessed sufficient distinctiveness to warrant broad protection because there was insufficient evidence of third-party use to dilute it. The Federal Circuit agreed with the Board on the similarity of services—a logical conclusion given that modern banking often involves issuing credit cards—but sharply diverged on how that similarity should inform the analysis of market saturation under Factor Six.

Factor Six asks whether there is a "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." If a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become adept at distinguishing subtle differences, narrowing the scope of protection for any single mark. Here lies where the Board stumbled. Although it had previously found that banking and credit card services were highly similar (in fact, overlapping), it artificially restricted its search for third-party "Aspire" marks to only those explicitly registered for "credit card services." Consequently, it ignored forty-two other third-party marks using "Aspire" or "Aspire-formative" terms across the broader financial sector. The Federal Circuit rejected this myopic approach as legally inconsistent. If services are sufficiently related to trigger confusion concerns under Factor Two, they must be treated as "similar goods" under Factor Six as well. By excluding relevant market data simply because it fell outside a rigid service description rather than reflecting actual market reality, the Board failed to gauge whether CC Serve's mark was truly strong or merely one voice in a crowded chorus.

This procedural misstep triggered what Judge Hughes termed a necessary vacuuming of Factor One—the comparison of the marks themselves in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, and commercial impression. The logic is elegant yet demanding: commercial strength is not an intrinsic property of a word; it is contextual. A common term like "Aspire" might command wide protection if it stands alone in a niche market with no competitors using variations of the name. However, if dozens of financial institutions use "Aspire," "MyAspire," or "Aspire Financial," the term becomes conceptually weak. Consumers learn to ignore the descriptive element and focus on other identifiers to distinguish sources. By forcing a remand on Factor Six before allowing Factor One to stand firm, the Federal Circuit underscored that you cannot accurately determine if two marks create similar commercial impressions without first understanding how much competition exists for those words in commerce. You cannot judge if two houses look alike without knowing how many houses exist on the street; similarly, you cannot judge if two trademarks are confusing without knowing how saturated the marketplace is with similar names.

The implications for future litigation are profound and practical. First, it reinforces that consistency in defining "relatedness" across all *DuPont* factors is non-negotiable once established by substantial evidence under one factor like service similarity. Second, it elevates the strategic importance of comprehensive discovery regarding third-party usage during opposition proceedings before administrative bodies like the TTAB or USPTO examiners during ex parte examination. Parties can no longer assume that limiting their search to exact service code matches will suffice; they must cast a wider net over functionally related industries to prove conceptual weakness or argue for narrow protection limits. Conversely, applicants facing opposition must be prepared to present robust evidence of market saturation across adjacent sectors to break down an opponent's claim of inherent distinctiveness.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* teaches us that trademark law remains deeply empirical despite its reliance on legal tests designed for abstraction. The Federal Circuit refused to let a technical definition override economic reality; if consumers perceive banking and credit card services as coming from related sources—as evidenced by shared branding practices—they must also be presumed capable of distinguishing among numerous players using similar aspirational language within that same sphere. As cases return from this remand cycle back to Washington D.C., expect practitioners to aggressively expand their evidentiary records on third-party use, knowing now that such data does more than satisfy a checklist item—it fundamentally reshapes how courts perceive the very identity of a brand in the minds of consumers.



## Legally Identical' Services vs. Competitive Reality: The Evolving Standard for Banking and Credit Card Confusion

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided late last month, the Federal Circuit delivered a ruling that serves as both a reinforcement of established trademark doctrine and a stark reminder of how rigid administrative frameworks can struggle to keep pace with a fluid financial landscape. At its core, the case asks a deceptively simple question: Are "banking and financing services" and "credit card services" distinct enough to coexist under similar marks, or are they so intertwined that they are effectively the same? The court answered definitively that they are, at least in part, "legally identical." Yet, while the Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) finding on this critical front, the decision simultaneously exposes cracks in the Board's broader analytical framework, particularly regarding how third-party usage defines the strength of a mark in an increasingly blurred market.

The dispute arose when Apex Bank sought to register the marks ASPIRE BANK for general banking services. Apex is a traditional brick-and-mortar institution that currently offers checking, savings, and loans but notably does not issue credit cards. Its competitor, CC Serve Corp., owns a registration for ASPIRE dating back to 1998, covering credit card services—a role CC Serve fills by partnering with banks to process accounts rather than issuing them directly. Despite these operational distinctions, the TTAB refused Apex's application under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, finding a likelihood of confusion. The Board leaned heavily on dictionary definitions to bridge the gap between "banking" and "credit card services," concluding that because banking involves extending credit and financing purchases—activities inherent to credit cards—the services were legally identical in part.

The Federal Circuit largely agreed with this assessment in its discussion of the second *DuPont* factor (similarity of goods/services). Judge Hughes wrote that substantial evidence supported the Board's conclusion that the services are highly similar. The court rejected Apex's argument that CC Serve merely acted as a servicer and not an issuer, noting that trademark law looks at the commercial reality of what consumers perceive rather than the internal corporate structure of who signs the checks. If dictionary definitions allow "banking" to encompass "extending credit," then for trademark purposes, those lines blur significantly. This affirmation sends a clear message to financial institutions: do not assume that being an issuer versus a servicer creates a safe harbor against confusion claims when your target markets overlap so fundamentally.

However, it is in the court's handling of the sixth *DuPont* factor—the strength of the senior mark—that *Apex Bank* reveals its most significant procedural nuance and potential impact on future litigation. The sixth factor examines whether many other entities use similar marks on similar goods; if they do, the senior mark is considered weaker and entitled to narrower protection. Here, Apex presented evidence of dozens of third-party uses of "Aspire" across various financial sectors. The Board dismissed most of these uses as irrelevant because they were not strictly limited to "credit card services," narrowing its universe of comparison despite having previously declared banking and credit card services "highly similar."

Judge Hughes correctly identified this inconsistency as legal error. He reasoned that if two categories of goods are sufficiently related to trigger confusion under Factor 2, they cannot suddenly become unrelated when assessing Factor 6 just to protect a senior registrant from competition logic. By restricting the relevant public solely to consumers looking for credit cards while ignoring those seeking broader banking solutions—despite finding those services legally identical—the Board applied an impermissibly double standard. Consequently, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board's analysis on Factors 6 and 1 (similarity of marks), forcing a remand where Apex can argue that "Aspire" is actually quite weak due to widespread use across the entire spectrum of financial services it now competes in.

This split disposition highlights a growing tension in intellectual property law within regulated industries like finance: static classifications versus dynamic market realities. For decades, courts have relied on fixed descriptions from registration databases or dictionaries to define service boundaries. But as seen here with Apex's pivot toward online banking without issuing cards itself, and CC Serve's role as a backend processor rather than an issuer, traditional distinctions are becoming obsolete consumers rarely care about corporate roles; they care about where their money goes and what tools they use to manage it. If "banking" includes credit cards by dictionary definition today, tomorrow it may include crypto-wallets or buy-now-pay-later apps under similar logic unless courts demand more granular proof of actual consumer confusion beyond semantic equivalence.

First, relying on technical distinctions between issuers and processors may fail if your proposed mark shares strong visual or phonetic ties with existing players operating adjacent functions; courts will look past corporate structures to find functional identity in consumer perception.
