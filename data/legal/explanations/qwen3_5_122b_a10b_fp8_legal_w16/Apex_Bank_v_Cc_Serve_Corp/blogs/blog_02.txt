Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## The Ripple Effect: How Third-Party Use Analysis Reshapes Commercial Strength and Mark Comparison

In the high-stakes arena of trademark oppositions, the Federal Circuit delivered a sharp reminder on September 25, 2025, that the *DuPont* factors are not isolated silos but an interconnected ecosystem where an error in one can topple the analysis of another. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) decision that had blocked Apex Bank's attempt to register "ASPIRE BANK." While the Board correctly identified that banking and credit card services are highly similar, its failure to look beyond a narrowly defined universe of third-party marks when assessing commercial strength created a fatal flaw in its likelihood-of-confusion calculus. For practitioners navigating Section 2(d) disputes, *Apex Bank* serves as a critical case study: the scope of your evidence regarding third-party use dictates not just the weight of a specific factor, but the very commercial impression of the mark itself.

The core dispute centered on whether Apex's proposed "ASPIRE BANK" marks would confuse consumers with CC Serve's existing "ASPIRE" registration for credit card services. The TTAB initially ruled against Apex, leaning heavily on two findings: first, that the parties' services were so similar they were effectively "legally identical," and second, that CC Serve's mark possessed sufficient distinctiveness to warrant broad protection because there was insufficient evidence of third-party use to dilute it. The Federal Circuit agreed with the Board on the similarity of services—a logical conclusion given that modern banking often involves issuing credit cards—but sharply diverged on how that similarity should inform the analysis of market saturation under Factor Six.

Factor Six asks whether there is a "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." If a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become adept at distinguishing subtle differences, narrowing the scope of protection for any single mark. Here lies where the Board stumbled. Although it had previously found that banking and credit card services were highly similar (in fact, overlapping), it artificially restricted its search for third-party "Aspire" marks to only those explicitly registered for "credit card services." Consequently, it ignored forty-two other third-party marks using "Aspire" or "Aspire-formative" terms across the broader financial sector. The Federal Circuit rejected this myopic approach as legally inconsistent. If services are sufficiently related to trigger confusion concerns under Factor Two, they must be treated as "similar goods" under Factor Six as well. By excluding relevant market data simply because it fell outside a rigid service description rather than reflecting actual market reality, the Board failed to gauge whether CC Serve's mark was truly strong or merely one voice in a crowded chorus.

This procedural misstep triggered what Judge Hughes termed a necessary vacuuming of Factor One—the comparison of the marks themselves in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, and commercial impression. The logic is elegant yet demanding: commercial strength is not an intrinsic property of a word; it is contextual. A common term like "Aspire" might command wide protection if it stands alone in a niche market with no competitors using variations of the name. However, if dozens of financial institutions use "Aspire," "MyAspire," or "Aspire Financial," the term becomes conceptually weak. Consumers learn to ignore the descriptive element and focus on other identifiers to distinguish sources. By forcing a remand on Factor Six before allowing Factor One to stand firm, the Federal Circuit underscored that you cannot accurately determine if two marks create similar commercial impressions without first understanding how much competition exists for those words in commerce. You cannot judge if two houses look alike without knowing how many houses exist on the street; similarly, you cannot judge if two trademarks are confusing without knowing how saturated the marketplace is with similar names.

The implications for future litigation are profound and practical. First, it reinforces that consistency in defining "relatedness" across all *DuPont* factors is non-negotiable once established by substantial evidence under one factor like service similarity. Second, it elevates the strategic importance of comprehensive discovery regarding third-party usage during opposition proceedings before administrative bodies like the TTAB or USPTO examiners during ex parte examination. Parties can no longer assume that limiting their search to exact service code matches will suffice; they must cast a wider net over functionally related industries to prove conceptual weakness or argue for narrow protection limits. Conversely, applicants facing opposition must be prepared to present robust evidence of market saturation across adjacent sectors to break down an opponent's claim of inherent distinctiveness.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* teaches us that trademark law remains deeply empirical despite its reliance on legal tests designed for abstraction. The Federal Circuit refused to let a technical definition override economic reality; if consumers perceive banking and credit card services as coming from related sources—as evidenced by shared branding practices—they must also be presumed capable of distinguishing among numerous players using similar aspirational language within that same sphere. As cases return from this remand cycle back to Washington D.C., expect practitioners to aggressively expand their evidentiary records on third-party use, knowing now that such data does more than satisfy a checklist item—it fundamentally reshapes how courts perceive the very identity of a brand in the minds of consumers.
