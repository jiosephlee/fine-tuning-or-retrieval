Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # Commercial Strength and Third-Party Use (Sixth DuPont Factor)

## Purpose of Sixth Factor: How Crowded Fields Weaken a Mark's Scope of Protection
In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit scrutinized the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's application of the sixth *DuPont* factor, which examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves a specific function within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis: it gauges the commercial strength of the senior user's mark. When a particular field is saturated with many similar marks, the law recognizes that a single mark cannot claim exclusive rights over common terms or designs because doing so would effectively monopolize language or symbols needed by competitors. Consequently, evidence of widespread third-party use demonstrates that a mark is "relatively weak," granting it only a "narrow scope of protection." Conversely, if few or no similar marks exist, the senior mark is considered strong and receives broader protection against infringement. In this case, the core dispute was not merely about counting third-party marks, but about correctly identifying which marks constituted "similar goods" for the purpose of this count.

## 'Consumer Education' Theory: Why Abundant Similar Marks Reduce Likelihood of Confusion
The theoretical underpinning of the sixth *DuPont* factor relies on what courts often describe as "consumer education." As noted by the Federal Circuit in *Juice Generation*, when consumers encounter a marketplace crowded with numerous entities using similar marks for related services, they become adept at distinguishing between them. If a consumer sees multiple companies offering financial services under names containing "Aspire," they learn to look for additional cues—such as logos, specific wording, or corporate affiliations—to identify the source accurately. This heightened ability to differentiate reduces the probability that a new entrant using a similar name will cause confusion. The court reasoned that if consumers are accustomed to navigating a sea of similar marks without being misled, they are less likely to assume that all such marks originate from a single source. Therefore, establishing significant third-party use suggests that the senior mark lacks inherent distinctiveness in that market context and should not be afforded an expansive zone of exclusivity.

## The Scope Error: Critique of Limiting Third-Party Analysis Strictly to 'Credit Card Services' After Finding Broader Service Similarity
The pivotal legal error committed by the Board in *Apex Bank* concerned its inconsistent definition of "similar goods" across different analytical steps. Under the second *DuPont* factor (relatedness of goods), the Board had explicitly found that Apex's proposed "banking and financing services" were highly similar to CC Serve's "credit card services," even noting they were partially "legally identical." However, when shifting to the sixth *DuPont* factor regarding third-party use, the Board arbitrarily narrowed its scope. It restricted its search for comparable third-party marks exclusively to those registered for "credit card services," dismissing 42 other third-party marks using variations of "Aspire" for broader banking and financing services as "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit held this approach to be legally flawed because it created an artificial disconnect between factual findings on service similarity and subsequent applications of those findings. Once the Board determines that two sets of services are highly similar—or even identical—it must maintain that same standard of similarity when evaluating third-party usage under other factors. By excluding marks used on services it had already deemed similar (banking/financing), the Board applied an impermissibly stringent standard to weaken Apex's argument while simultaneously accepting broad similarity for CC Serve's benefit. The appellate court emphasized that consistency is required; if credit card services and banking services are close enough to create confusion under factor two, then third-party uses in both categories must be counted together under factor six to accurately reflect market conditions.

## "Voluminous" vs. "Considerable": Evaluating Whether Nine Third-Party Uses Constitute Sufficient Weakness
Even assuming arguendo that limiting the universe to just credit card service providers was correct—a premise rejected by the court—the Board faced another evidentiary hurdle regarding the quantity required to prove weakness. The Board acknowledged only nine third-party uses of "Aspire"-formative marks within its self-imposed narrow category but concluded this number did not reach the threshold of being "considerable" or "ubiquitous." To support this conclusion, the Board cited precedents where courts found weakness only upon seeing significantly higher numbers, such as fourteen notable examples in *Jack Wolfskin* or twenty-six examples in *Juice Generation*.

However, this numerical comparison highlights why proper scope definition matters so profoundly. By artificially capping its dataset at nine items based on an erroneous exclusion of broader financial service marks, the Board failed to capture reality. Had it included all relevant third-party uses—including those 42 additional marks for general banking and financing—the total volume would have been substantially higher, potentially meeting or exceeding standards set in prior cases like *Juice Generation*. While nine instances might appear insufficient on their own compared to dozens in other industries, those numbers were derived from an incomplete picture caused by procedural error rather than actual market scarcity. Because this miscalculation stemmed from an incorrect legal standard regarding scope rather than simple factual disagreement over counts alone, it warranted vacatur and remand for a recalculation based on all relevant third-party evidence consistent with the finding that banking and credit card services are closely related.
