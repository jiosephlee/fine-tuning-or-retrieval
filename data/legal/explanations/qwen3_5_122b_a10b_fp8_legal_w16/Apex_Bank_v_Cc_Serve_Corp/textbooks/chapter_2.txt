Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 2: # Standard of Review and Legal Framework

## Appellate Standards: De Novo Review for Factual Findings
When a party appeals a decision from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the appellate court employs a bifurcated standard of review that distinguishes sharply between questions of law and questions of fact. As articulated in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reviews the Board's legal conclusions *de novo*. This means the appellate court gives no deference to the Board's interpretation of statutes or case law; instead, it determines the correct legal rule independently. Conversely, the Board's findings of fact are reviewed under the "substantial evidence" standard. Under this rigorous but deferential test, derived from *Consolidated Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*, a factual finding stands if there is "relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion."

In practice, this distinction dictates how an appellant must frame their arguments and how the court evaluates them. If an appellant challenges a legal error, such as the misapplication of a statute or an incorrect legal standard for similarity, the court will analyze that issue fresh without looking to see if the Board's conclusion was supported by evidence. However, if an appellant challenges a specific factual determination—such as whether two sets of services are sufficiently related—the court will not substitute its own judgment for that of the Board unless no reasonable person could have reached that conclusion based on the record. In *Apex Bank*, this framework meant that while Apex successfully argued that the Board applied an incorrect legal scope when defining "similar goods" for its third-party mark analysis (a legal error subject to *de novo* review), the Board's ultimate finding on service similarity was upheld because it was backed by substantial evidence in the record.

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: The Statutory Basis for Refusal
The substantive authority governing trademark opposition proceedings like *Apex Bank* stems from Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). This statute prohibits the registration of a mark if it so resembles a previously registered mark or one previously used by another person that confusion is likely to occur with respect to goods or services sold under both marks. The core inquiry is whether there is a "likelihood of confusion" arising from concurrent use. As noted in *Apex Bank*, citing *Application of E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, registration must be refused when confusion is probable because an applicant and a prior user are offering similar goods or services under confusingly similar marks.

It is crucial for students to understand that Section 2(d) creates a relative ground for refusal; it does not examine whether a mark is inherently registrable in isolation (such as being descriptive or generic) but rather focuses entirely on its relationship to existing rights in commerce. In *Apex Bank*, CC Serve relied on this provision to oppose Apex's application for "ASPIRE BANK," arguing that their earlier registration for "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services created a statutory barrier to Apex's registration for banking services. The Board's role under Section 2(d) is strictly limited to determining whether such confusion exists; if it does, registration must be denied regardless of good faith or actual market presence at the time of filing.

## The *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* Factors
To operationalize the statutory command against likelihood of confusion, courts utilize a comprehensive set of criteria known as the *DuPont* factors, established in *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). These thirteen factors serve as non-exclusive guides designed to evaluate all relevant circumstances surrounding potential consumer confusion. While there are thirteen distinct factors ranging from family resemblance among marks to conditions under which sales take place, they collectively aim to answer one central question: Would an ordinary purchaser exercising reasonable care be confused about the source or sponsorship of the goods?

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit explicitly treated likelihood of confusion as a question of law predicated on findings regarding these underlying facts. Specifically, each individual factor represents a discrete question of fact regarding aspects such as mark similarity (*factor 1*), relatedness of goods (*factor 2*), strength of fame (*factor 3*), and third-party usage (*factor 6*). The court emphasized that while likelihood itself is legal, its components are factual inquiries tested against substantial evidence on appeal. For instance, when analyzing Factor Two (relatedness of goods), the court looked at whether credit card services and banking services were legally identical or merely related enough that consumers might assume they came from one source—a factual determination rooted in dictionary definitions and industry practices found in the record. Similarly, Factor Six required an evidentiary showing regarding how many other entities use similar marks on similar goods to determine if CC Serve's mark deserved broad protection or narrow protection due to market saturation with similar terms like "Aspire."

## Relevance Requirement: Selective Consideration
A critical procedural nuance within this framework is that neither trial boards nor appellate courts are required to consider every single one of the thirteen *DuPont* factors in every case. As stated in *Apex Bank* and reiterated from precedent like *M2 Software*, Inc., courts need only consider those factors that are "relevant and on record." This relevance requirement acknowledges that certain factors may be neutral or entirely absent depending on the specific facts before them; forcing an analysis where no data exists would be futile and contrary to efficient judicial administration.

However, once a factor is deemed relevant based on the record evidence presented by either party, it must be analyzed correctly according to established law. In *Apex Bank*, this principle became pivotal during appellate review regarding Factor Six (third-party marks). Although Apex had introduced evidence showing numerous third-party uses of "Aspire" across various financial sectors—not just strict credit card services—the Board initially dismissed most as irrelevant because they fell outside its narrow definition of "credit card services." The Federal Circuit vacated this portion of decision-making precisely because it failed to apply consistent relevance standards: having already found under Factor Two that banking and credit card services were highly similar (even partially identical), restricting Factor Six solely to strict credit card users created an inconsistent scope of analysis for what constituted "similar goods." Thus, while courts have discretion to ignore irrelevant factors, they cannot arbitrarily exclude relevant evidence once they have established factual similarities between parties' offerings elsewhere in their analysis.
