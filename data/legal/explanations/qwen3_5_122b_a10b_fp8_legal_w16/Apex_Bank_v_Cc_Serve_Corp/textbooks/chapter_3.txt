Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 3: # Analysis of Relatedness of Goods (Second DuPont Factor)

In trademark opposition proceedings, the core inquiry often hinges on whether consumers are likely to believe that goods or services bearing similar marks originate from the same source. Under the *DuPont* framework, which governs likelihood-of-confusion analyses before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) and the Federal Circuit, the second factor addresses the "relatedness" of the parties' goods or services. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit provided a critical lesson on how courts interpret service descriptions, particularly when those descriptions involve complex financial instruments like credit cards versus traditional banking. The court affirmed the Board's conclusion that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex Bank's proposed "banking and financing services" were so closely related that they weighed heavily in favor of a finding of likelihood of confusion, rejecting Apex's argument that a lack of direct competition negated this relationship.

## Defining 'Credit Card Services': Interpreting Deleted Entries in the Trademark ID Manual

A unique procedural hurdle arose in this case regarding the specific language used to describe CC Serve's registered mark. CC Serve owned a registration for the mark ASPIRE covering "credit card services." However, during the pendency of Apex's application, the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) had removed the specific entry for "credit card services" from its Trademark Identification Manual (ID Manual). This deletion created ambiguity regarding exactly what services fell within the scope of CC Serve's prior rights. The Board faced a choice: dismiss the relevance of these services due to the manual's update or define their meaning based on historical usage and dictionary definitions.

The Board chose to look beyond the administrative deletion to determine the substantive meaning of the term at the time of registration and use. It reasoned that "credit card services" historically and functionally encompassed a broad ecosystem: issuing credit cards to finance purchases, counseling regarding debt, processing payments, authorizing transactions, monitoring accounts, and providing credit score access. By reconstructing this definition from evidence presented in the record rather than relying solely on current USPTO nomenclature, the Board established a concrete baseline for comparison against Apex's proposed marks. This approach underscores a vital principle for law students reading opinions: when statutory or administrative definitions shift or become ambiguous, tribunals often resort to functional definitions derived from market reality and dictionary meanings to ensure consistent application of trademark law.

## Legal Identity vs. Direct Competition: Why 'Banking/Financing' Overlaps with 'Credit Card Services'

Apex Bank argued that there was no likelihood of confusion because its intended services differed fundamentally from CC Serve's operations. Specifically, Apex noted that it was a retail bank offering checking accounts, mortgages, and loans but explicitly stated it did not currently offer credit cards. Furthermore, Apex highlighted that CC Serve acted merely as an administrator partnering with banks rather than acting as a bank itself. The crux of Apex's argument was that without direct competition—one party selling credit cards and the other selling traditional banking products—consumer confusion was unlikely.

The Federal Circuit firmly rejected this distinction between direct competition and legal identity in this context. Citing *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, the court reiterated that goods or services need not be identical; they need only be related in a manner where marketing circumstances could lead consumers to mistakenly believe they emanate from a single source. The Board had previously analyzed dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance," noting that these terms naturally encompass extending credit through credit card issuance. Consequently, even though Apex did not yet sell credit cards, its proposed category of "banking and financing services" legally overlapped with CC Serve's "credit card services." The court emphasized that in modern financial markets, these functions are intertwined; consumers reasonably expect banks to provide comprehensive financial solutions including credit facilities. Therefore, the absence of direct head-to-head competition at this exact moment did not sever the logical connection required for relatedness under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act.

## Third-Party Registrations as Evidence: Using Existing Registrations to Prove Common Sourcing Potential

Beyond dictionary definitions and functional overlap, another pillar supporting relatedness is evidence showing how other entities operate within these fields. In its analysis of *DuPont* factor two, the Board looked to third-party registrations as empirical proof that consumers perceive banking and credit card services as potentially originating from a single source. The record contained numerous instances where third-party entities held registrations covering both "credit card" services and "banking and financing" services simultaneously under single marks or related brands.

This evidence served a specific evidentiary purpose: it demonstrated industry norms regarding service bundling and consumer expectations. If multiple unrelated companies routinely offer both types of services under one brand name without causing immediate confusion among themselves (or if such combinations are standard practice), it reinforces the idea that an average consumer would assume a new entity offering both sets of services shares a common origin with an existing entity offering one set under a similar mark (*ASPIRE*). For students analyzing similar opinions, note that third-party registrations are not always admissible to prove genericness or weakness (which is factor six); however, under factor two (relatedness), they are powerful tools to illustrate that distinct categories like banking and lending are frequently marketed together by single sources, thereby increasing susceptibility to confusion when marks are similar.

## Substantial Evidence Standard: Affirming Findings Despite Appellate Challenges

Finally, understanding how appellate courts review these factual determinations is essential for grasping why Apex lost on this specific issue before moving forward on others on appeal. The Federal Circuit applies a deferential standard known as "substantial evidence" when reviewing factual findings made by administrative agencies like the Board. As defined by Supreme Court precedent cited in *M2 Software*, substantial evidence is simply such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion; it does not require overwhelming proof or unanimity among experts.
