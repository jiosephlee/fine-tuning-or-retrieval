Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties Overview: CC Serve Corp.'s Role vs. Apex Bank's Operations
To understand the dispute in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, one must first grasp the distinct yet overlapping commercial identities of the litigants. CC Serve Corporation operates as a specialized intermediary in the financial sector, offering credit card services rather than acting as a direct issuer of credit. Specifically, CC Serve partners with banks to launch "ASPIRE"-branded credit cards; while the partner bank issues the physical cards and holds the customer account relationships, CC Serve manages the servicing of these accounts, including transaction processing and customer support. This distinction is crucial because CC Serve does not provide general banking services like checking or savings accounts itself; its domain is strictly limited to credit card infrastructure and management.

In contrast, Apex Bank is a traditional retail bank chartered in Tennessee with eighteen physical branch locations. Its current operations include personal and business checking, savings accounts, home mortgages, and various loan products. Notably, Apex does not currently offer credit cards. However, the controversy arises from Apex's strategic pivot: it intends to launch an internet-based banking platform under new branding that incorporates the term "ASPIRE." While Apex remains a brick-and-mortar institution today, its future plans involve entering the digital banking space where it would compete indirectly with entities like CC Serve that facilitate credit products within the broader financial ecosystem. The tension lies in whether a bank offering general financing services can coexist with a service provider managing credit card programs without confusing consumers about their common origin.

## The Marks at Issue: Registered 'ASPIRE' vs. Proposed 'ASPIRE BANK'
The intellectual property rights at stake center on two specific marks with different legal statuses and scopes of protection. CC Serve owns Registration No. 2126948 for the standard character word mark "ASPIRE," registered in 1998 with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. This registration covers "credit card services" broadly interpreted by the Board to include issuing cards for purchase financing, debt counseling, payment processing, authorization, monitoring, account management, and access to credit scores. Because this mark has been in continuous use since at least 1996, it carries significant seniority and established goodwill in the marketplace for these specific services.

Opposing this senior right is Apex Bank's application for both word and design marks incorporating "ASPIRE BANK." Filed as intent-to-use applications in August 2019 with an effective filing date preceding its actual market entry, Apex sought registration for "[b]anking and financing services." Unlike CC Serve's existing registration which protects a generic aspirational verb used alone ("ASPIRE"), Apex's proposed marks combine that verb with the noun "BANK" to create a composite identifier ("ASPIRE BANK"). The legal conflict hinges on whether adding "BANK" sufficiently distinguishes Apex's proposed offerings from CC Serve's pre-existing "ASPIRE" mark to avoid consumer confusion regarding who provides or sponsors these financial products.

## Procedural History: From USPTO Examination to TTAB Opposition
The administrative journey began when Apex filed its intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). During prosecution, CC Serve exercised its right to submit a letter of protest asserting that Apex's proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own registered "ASPIRE" mark. Despite this objection from an interested third party, the USPTO examining attorney determined that no likelihood of confusion existed and approved Apex's marks for publication on December 17, 2019. Publication serves as a critical checkpoint in trademark law; once published in the Official Gazette of Trademarks, any party believing they would be damaged by registration has thirty days to file an opposition before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).

CC Serve acted swiftly upon publication by initiating an opposition proceeding in February 2020 before the TTAB. An opposition is essentially an adversarial trial within an agency setting where parties litigate whether a pending application should be refused registration based on statutory grounds such as likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1052(d)). Both parties presented evidence regarding their respective businesses, marketing channels, and consumer perceptions during this administrative hearing process. The Board served as the initial fact-finder and legal arbiter charged with weighing conflicting testimony and documentary evidence to determine if Congress intended to block Apex's registration due to potential market confusion with CC Serve's established brand.

## The Board's Decision: Sustaining Opposition Under Section 2(d)
After reviewing the record developed during discovery and trial-like proceedings before it had concluded that there was sufficient evidence of likely confusion between "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK." Applying Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act—which mandates refusal of registration if confusion is likely due to concurrent use—the Board sustained CC Serve's opposition against all counts raised by Apex regarding likelihood of confusion factors under *DuPont*. The Board analyzed thirteen factors derived from *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* focusing particularly on similarities between goods/services (Factor Two), commercial impressions created by marks themselves (Factor One), presence/absence third-party usage patterns affecting strength/scope protection available holder thereof (Factor Six). # Standard of Review and Legal Framework

## Appellate Standards: De Novo Review for Factual Findings
When a party appeals a decision from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the appellate court employs a bifurcated standard of review that distinguishes sharply between questions of law and questions of fact. As articulated in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reviews the Board's legal conclusions *de novo*. This means the appellate court gives no deference to the Board's interpretation of statutes or case law; instead, it determines the correct legal rule independently. Conversely, the Board's findings of fact are reviewed under the "substantial evidence" standard. Under this rigorous but deferential test, derived from *Consolidated Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*, a factual finding stands if there is "relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion."

In practice, this distinction dictates how an appellant must frame their arguments and how the court evaluates them. If an appellant challenges a legal error, such as the misapplication of a statute or an incorrect legal standard for similarity, the court will analyze that issue fresh without looking to see if the Board's conclusion was supported by evidence. However, if an appellant challenges a specific factual determination—such as whether two sets of services are sufficiently related—the court will not substitute its own judgment for that of the Board unless no reasonable person could have reached that conclusion based on the record. In *Apex Bank*, this framework meant that while Apex successfully argued that the Board applied an incorrect legal scope when defining "similar goods" for its third-party mark analysis (a legal error subject to *de novo* review), the Board's ultimate finding on service similarity was upheld because it was backed by substantial evidence in the record.

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: The Statutory Basis for Refusal
The substantive authority governing trademark opposition proceedings like *Apex Bank* stems from Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). This statute prohibits the registration of a mark if it so resembles a previously registered mark or one previously used by another person that confusion is likely to occur with respect to goods or services sold under both marks. The core inquiry is whether there is a "likelihood of confusion" arising from concurrent use. As noted in *Apex Bank*, citing *Application of E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, registration must be refused when confusion is probable because an applicant and a prior user are offering similar goods or services under confusingly similar marks.

It is crucial for students to understand that Section 2(d) creates a relative ground for refusal; it does not examine whether a mark is inherently registrable in isolation (such as being descriptive or generic) but rather focuses entirely on its relationship to existing rights in commerce. In *Apex Bank*, CC Serve relied on this provision to oppose Apex's application for "ASPIRE BANK," arguing that their earlier registration for "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services created a statutory barrier to Apex's registration for banking services. The Board's role under Section 2(d) is strictly limited to determining whether such confusion exists; if it does, registration must be denied regardless of good faith or actual market presence at the time of filing.

## The *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* Factors
To operationalize the statutory command against likelihood of confusion, courts utilize a comprehensive set of criteria known as the *DuPont* factors, established in *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). These thirteen factors serve as non-exclusive guides designed to evaluate all relevant circumstances surrounding potential consumer confusion. While there are thirteen distinct factors ranging from family resemblance among marks to conditions under which sales take place, they collectively aim to answer one central question: Would an ordinary purchaser exercising reasonable care be confused about the source or sponsorship of the goods?

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit explicitly treated likelihood of confusion as a question of law predicated on findings regarding these underlying facts. Specifically, each individual factor represents a discrete question of fact regarding aspects such as mark similarity (*factor 1*), relatedness of goods (*factor 2*), strength of fame (*factor 3*), and third-party usage (*factor 6*). The court emphasized that while likelihood itself is legal, its components are factual inquiries tested against substantial evidence on appeal. For instance, when analyzing Factor Two (relatedness of goods), the court looked at whether credit card services and banking services were legally identical or merely related enough that consumers might assume they came from one source—a factual determination rooted in dictionary definitions and industry practices found in the record. Similarly, Factor Six required an evidentiary showing regarding how many other entities use similar marks on similar goods to determine if CC Serve's mark deserved broad protection or narrow protection due to market saturation with similar terms like "Aspire."

## Relevance Requirement: Selective Consideration
A critical procedural nuance within this framework is that neither trial boards nor appellate courts are required to consider every single one of the thirteen *DuPont* factors in every case. As stated in *Apex Bank* and reiterated from precedent like *M2 Software*, Inc., courts need only consider those factors that are "relevant and on record." This relevance requirement acknowledges that certain factors may be neutral or entirely absent depending on the specific facts before them; forcing an analysis where no data exists would be futile and contrary to efficient judicial administration.

However, once a factor is deemed relevant based on the record evidence presented by either party, it must be analyzed correctly according to established law. In *Apex Bank*, this principle became pivotal during appellate review regarding Factor Six (third-party marks). Although Apex had introduced evidence showing numerous third-party uses of "Aspire" across various financial sectors—not just strict credit card services—the Board initially dismissed most as irrelevant because they fell outside its narrow definition of "credit card services." The Federal Circuit vacated this portion of decision-making precisely because it failed to apply consistent relevance standards: having already found under Factor Two that banking and credit card services were highly similar (even partially identical), restricting Factor Six solely to strict credit card users created an inconsistent scope of analysis for what constituted "similar goods." Thus, while courts have discretion to ignore irrelevant factors, they cannot arbitrarily exclude relevant evidence once they have established factual similarities between parties' offerings elsewhere in their analysis.

# Analysis of Relatedness of Goods (Second DuPont Factor)

In trademark opposition proceedings, the core inquiry often hinges on whether consumers are likely to believe that goods or services bearing similar marks originate from the same source. Under the *DuPont* framework, which governs likelihood-of-confusion analyses before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) and the Federal Circuit, the second factor addresses the "relatedness" of the parties' goods or services. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit provided a critical lesson on how courts interpret service descriptions, particularly when those descriptions involve complex financial instruments like credit cards versus traditional banking. The court affirmed the Board's conclusion that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex Bank's proposed "banking and financing services" were so closely related that they weighed heavily in favor of a finding of likelihood of confusion, rejecting Apex's argument that a lack of direct competition negated this relationship.

## Defining 'Credit Card Services': Interpreting Deleted Entries in the Trademark ID Manual

A unique procedural hurdle arose in this case regarding the specific language used to describe CC Serve's registered mark. CC Serve owned a registration for the mark ASPIRE covering "credit card services." However, during the pendency of Apex's application, the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) had removed the specific entry for "credit card services" from its Trademark Identification Manual (ID Manual). This deletion created ambiguity regarding exactly what services fell within the scope of CC Serve's prior rights. The Board faced a choice: dismiss the relevance of these services due to the manual's update or define their meaning based on historical usage and dictionary definitions.

The Board chose to look beyond the administrative deletion to determine the substantive meaning of the term at the time of registration and use. It reasoned that "credit card services" historically and functionally encompassed a broad ecosystem: issuing credit cards to finance purchases, counseling regarding debt, processing payments, authorizing transactions, monitoring accounts, and providing credit score access. By reconstructing this definition from evidence presented in the record rather than relying solely on current USPTO nomenclature, the Board established a concrete baseline for comparison against Apex's proposed marks. This approach underscores a vital principle for law students reading opinions: when statutory or administrative definitions shift or become ambiguous, tribunals often resort to functional definitions derived from market reality and dictionary meanings to ensure consistent application of trademark law.

## Legal Identity vs. Direct Competition: Why 'Banking/Financing' Overlaps with 'Credit Card Services'

Apex Bank argued that there was no likelihood of confusion because its intended services differed fundamentally from CC Serve's operations. Specifically, Apex noted that it was a retail bank offering checking accounts, mortgages, and loans but explicitly stated it did not currently offer credit cards. Furthermore, Apex highlighted that CC Serve acted merely as an administrator partnering with banks rather than acting as a bank itself. The crux of Apex's argument was that without direct competition—one party selling credit cards and the other selling traditional banking products—consumer confusion was unlikely.

The Federal Circuit firmly rejected this distinction between direct competition and legal identity in this context. Citing *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, the court reiterated that goods or services need not be identical; they need only be related in a manner where marketing circumstances could lead consumers to mistakenly believe they emanate from a single source. The Board had previously analyzed dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance," noting that these terms naturally encompass extending credit through credit card issuance. Consequently, even though Apex did not yet sell credit cards, its proposed category of "banking and financing services" legally overlapped with CC Serve's "credit card services." The court emphasized that in modern financial markets, these functions are intertwined; consumers reasonably expect banks to provide comprehensive financial solutions including credit facilities. Therefore, the absence of direct head-to-head competition at this exact moment did not sever the logical connection required for relatedness under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act.

## Third-Party Registrations as Evidence: Using Existing Registrations to Prove Common Sourcing Potential

Beyond dictionary definitions and functional overlap, another pillar supporting relatedness is evidence showing how other entities operate within these fields. In its analysis of *DuPont* factor two, the Board looked to third-party registrations as empirical proof that consumers perceive banking and credit card services as potentially originating from a single source. The record contained numerous instances where third-party entities held registrations covering both "credit card" services and "banking and financing" services simultaneously under single marks or related brands.

This evidence served a specific evidentiary purpose: it demonstrated industry norms regarding service bundling and consumer expectations. If multiple unrelated companies routinely offer both types of services under one brand name without causing immediate confusion among themselves (or if such combinations are standard practice), it reinforces the idea that an average consumer would assume a new entity offering both sets of services shares a common origin with an existing entity offering one set under a similar mark (*ASPIRE*). For students analyzing similar opinions, note that third-party registrations are not always admissible to prove genericness or weakness (which is factor six); however, under factor two (relatedness), they are powerful tools to illustrate that distinct categories like banking and lending are frequently marketed together by single sources, thereby increasing susceptibility to confusion when marks are similar.

## Substantial Evidence Standard: Affirming Findings Despite Appellate Challenges

Finally, understanding how appellate courts review these factual determinations is essential for grasping why Apex lost on this specific issue before moving forward on others on appeal. The Federal Circuit applies a deferential standard known as "substantial evidence" when reviewing factual findings made by administrative agencies like the Board. As defined by Supreme Court precedent cited in *M2 Software*, substantial evidence is simply such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion; it does not require overwhelming proof or unanimity among experts.

Apex challenged whether there was enough evidence to support finding their services highly similar given their non-overlapping product lines at launch timescale 2019-2020s? Yet , because substantial evidence existed —including dictionary definitions establishing legal overlap , historical usage patterns defining credit card administration , plus third party registrations showing combined offerings —the court felt bound to uphold . Even though appellate judges might have reached different conclusions weighing factors differently themselves , once reasonable minds can differ based on record evidence , reversal is improper . Thus , while later sections vacated aspects concerning mark strength (factor six ) due errors narrowing scope improperly here affirmance occurred precisely because Board meticulously considered all available data points supporting high degree similarity between financial sectors involved .

# Commercial Strength and Third-Party Use (Sixth DuPont Factor)

## Purpose of Sixth Factor: How Crowded Fields Weaken a Mark's Scope of Protection
In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit scrutinized the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's application of the sixth *DuPont* factor, which examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves a specific function within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis: it gauges the commercial strength of the senior user's mark. When a particular field is saturated with many similar marks, the law recognizes that a single mark cannot claim exclusive rights over common terms or designs because doing so would effectively monopolize language or symbols needed by competitors. Consequently, evidence of widespread third-party use demonstrates that a mark is "relatively weak," granting it only a "narrow scope of protection." Conversely, if few or no similar marks exist, the senior mark is considered strong and receives broader protection against infringement. In this case, the core dispute was not merely about counting third-party marks, but about correctly identifying which marks constituted "similar goods" for the purpose of this count.

## 'Consumer Education' Theory: Why Abundant Similar Marks Reduce Likelihood of Confusion
The theoretical underpinning of the sixth *DuPont* factor relies on what courts often describe as "consumer education." As noted by the Federal Circuit in *Juice Generation*, when consumers encounter a marketplace crowded with numerous entities using similar marks for related services, they become adept at distinguishing between them. If a consumer sees multiple companies offering financial services under names containing "Aspire," they learn to look for additional cues—such as logos, specific wording, or corporate affiliations—to identify the source accurately. This heightened ability to differentiate reduces the probability that a new entrant using a similar name will cause confusion. The court reasoned that if consumers are accustomed to navigating a sea of similar marks without being misled, they are less likely to assume that all such marks originate from a single source. Therefore, establishing significant third-party use suggests that the senior mark lacks inherent distinctiveness in that market context and should not be afforded an expansive zone of exclusivity.

## The Scope Error: Critique of Limiting Third-Party Analysis Strictly to 'Credit Card Services' After Finding Broader Service Similarity
The pivotal legal error committed by the Board in *Apex Bank* concerned its inconsistent definition of "similar goods" across different analytical steps. Under the second *DuPont* factor (relatedness of goods), the Board had explicitly found that Apex's proposed "banking and financing services" were highly similar to CC Serve's "credit card services," even noting they were partially "legally identical." However, when shifting to the sixth *DuPont* factor regarding third-party use, the Board arbitrarily narrowed its scope. It restricted its search for comparable third-party marks exclusively to those registered for "credit card services," dismissing 42 other third-party marks using variations of "Aspire" for broader banking and financing services as "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit held this approach to be legally flawed because it created an artificial disconnect between factual findings on service similarity and subsequent applications of those findings. Once the Board determines that two sets of services are highly similar—or even identical—it must maintain that same standard of similarity when evaluating third-party usage under other factors. By excluding marks used on services it had already deemed similar (banking/financing), the Board applied an impermissibly stringent standard to weaken Apex's argument while simultaneously accepting broad similarity for CC Serve's benefit. The appellate court emphasized that consistency is required; if credit card services and banking services are close enough to create confusion under factor two, then third-party uses in both categories must be counted together under factor six to accurately reflect market conditions.

## "Voluminous" vs. "Considerable": Evaluating Whether Nine Third-Party Uses Constitute Sufficient Weakness
Even assuming arguendo that limiting the universe to just credit card service providers was correct—a premise rejected by the court—the Board faced another evidentiary hurdle regarding the quantity required to prove weakness. The Board acknowledged only nine third-party uses of "Aspire"-formative marks within its self-imposed narrow category but concluded this number did not reach the threshold of being "considerable" or "ubiquitous." To support this conclusion, the Board cited precedents where courts found weakness only upon seeing significantly higher numbers, such as fourteen notable examples in *Jack Wolfskin* or twenty-six examples in *Juice Generation*.

However, this numerical comparison highlights why proper scope definition matters so profoundly. By artificially capping its dataset at nine items based on an erroneous exclusion of broader financial service marks, the Board failed to capture reality. Had it included all relevant third-party uses—including those 42 additional marks for general banking and financing—the total volume would have been substantially higher, potentially meeting or exceeding standards set in prior cases like *Juice Generation*. While nine instances might appear insufficient on their own compared to dozens in other industries, those numbers were derived from an incomplete picture caused by procedural error rather than actual market scarcity. Because this miscalculation stemmed from an incorrect legal standard regarding scope rather than simple factual disagreement over counts alone, it warranted vacatur and remand for a recalculation based on all relevant third-party evidence consistent with the finding that banking and credit card services are closely related.
