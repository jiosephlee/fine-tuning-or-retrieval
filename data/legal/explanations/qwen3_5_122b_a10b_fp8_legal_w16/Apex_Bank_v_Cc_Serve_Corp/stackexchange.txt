Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How does the Federal Circuit define 'substantial evidence' in the context of TTAB factual findings, and how does this differ from the 'de novo' review applied to legal conclusions?
Question:
In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court states it reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo but its findings of fact for substantial evidence. I understand 'de novo' means looking at the issue fresh, but I'm struggling with the 'substantial evidence' standard. Does this mean I just have to find *any* piece of evidence in the record that supports the Board, even if I think another interpretation is better? Or do I have to look at the whole record to see if a reasonable mind could accept it? How exactly does this standard limit my ability to overturn a factual finding like the similarity of services?
Answer:
**Substantial Evidence vs. De Novo Review in *Apex Bank***

To answer your specific questions: **No**, you cannot simply point to "any" piece of evidence to uphold a finding, nor can you substitute your own judgment for the Board's merely because you prefer an alternative interpretation. However, you **must** look at the record as a whole to determine if a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the conclusion.

Here is how the Federal Circuit defines these standards in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* and how they apply to your scenario regarding service similarity.

### 1. The Definition of "Substantial Evidence"
The court explicitly adopts the Supreme Court's standard from *Consolidated Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*: substantial evidence is **"such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion."** (*Opinion* at II).

This standard creates a specific boundary for appellate review:
*   **It is not "Any Evidence":** You cannot sustain a finding based on a stray remark or isolated document if the weight of the record contradicts it. The evidence must be "relevant" and "adequate."
*   **It is Not De Novo:** Even if you believe another interpretation of the facts is "better," you are bound by the Board's finding if substantial evidence exists to support it. The court does not re-weigh the evidence or make independent credibility determinations.
*   **The Whole Record Matters:** While the opinion doesn't use the phrase "whole record" in every sentence, the requirement that evidence be "adequate" implies a holistic view. If the Board ignored overwhelming contrary evidence while relying on weak support, it would fail this test. Conversely, if there is conflicting evidence but *some* solid ground remains for the Board's conclusion, that conclusion stands.

### 2. Application to Service Similarity (Factor Two)
In Part III(A) of *Apex Bank*, you see this standard in action regarding whether credit card services and banking/financing services are similar enough to cause confusion.

*   **The Board's Finding:** The Board found the services were "highly similar" and even "legally identical, in part," citing dictionary definitions showing both involve extending credit and third-party registrations covering both fields (*Opinion* at III.A).
*   **Appellant's Argument:** Apex argued their services were not directly competitive because CC Serve partners with banks rather than acting as one itself (*Opinion* at III.A).
*   **The Court's Application:** The Federal Circuit rejected Apex's argument not by re-evaluating who provides what service, but by asking: **"Does substantial evidence support [the Board's] finding?"** (*Opinion* at III.A).
    *   Because the record contained dictionary definitions linking banking and credit cards, plus third-party registrations covering both, a "reasonable mind" could accept that they are similar.
    *   Therefore, even though Apex had a plausible argument that they weren't direct competitors, the existence of supporting evidence meant the court could not overturn the factual finding on similarity alone.

### 3. Contrast with Legal Conclusions (De Novo Review)
You correctly noted that legal conclusions receive *de novo* review (Part II). This distinction becomes critical when looking at Part III(B) regarding Factor Six (third-party marks).

*   **Where it failed:** The Board made a factual finding that services were similar (under Factor Two), but then applied a legal rule under Factor Six that required considering only marks for *identical* credit card services, excluding broader banking marks (*Opinion* at III.B).
*   **The Error:** The court held this was an error of law ("legally flawed"). Under *de novo* review, the Federal Circuit decided independently that Factor Six requires considering marks on *similar*, not just identical, goods (*Olde Tyme Foods*).
*   **The Result:** Because this was a legal error in applying the scope of similarity across factors—not just a dispute over facts—the court vacated that portion of the decision without needing to check for "substantial evidence."

### Summary for Your Analysis
When reviewing TTAB decisions under *Apex Bank*:
1.  **For Facts (e.g., Are services similar?):** Ask yourself: Is there relevant proof in the record (like dictionaries or third-party regs) that a reasonable person could say proves this? If yes, you must affirm, even if you disagree with how much weight was given to other contradictory facts.
2.  **For Law (e.g., What does Factor Six require?):** Ignore whether there was evidence; ask instead: Did the Board apply the correct legal standard? If they narrowed their analysis incorrectly based on precedent (as they did with Factor Six), you reverse regardless of what facts were before them.

### Can a trademark registrant claim ownership over a generic term like 'ASPIRE' if they are merely an intermediary service provider rather than a direct issuer of financial products?
Question:
CC Serve owns the ASPIRE mark for credit card services, but they don't actually issue the cards; they partner with banks who do. Apex argued that since CC Serve isn't providing traditional banking services (like deposits or loans), their services aren't identical to Apex's planned 'banking and financing' services. However, the Board found them 'legally identical, in part.' If a company acts as an administrator or processor for credit cards rather than a lender, can they still assert rights against a new bank entering the market under a similar name? Doesn't this stretch the definition of 'services' too far?
Answer:
Yes, under the reasoning in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, a non-lender acting as an administrator or processor for credit cards can assert trademark rights against a new bank offering "banking and financing" services under a similar name. The Federal Circuit affirmed that these distinct operational roles do not preclude a finding of "likelihood of confusion" because the services themselves are sufficiently related, and in part, "legally identical."

**The Legal Standard: Relatedness Over Identity**
In trademark opposition proceedings under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the critical inquiry is not whether the parties provide *identical* services, but whether the services are **related** such that consumers might mistakenly believe they emanate from the same source. As the court reiterated citing *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, services need only be related in manner or marketed under circumstances that suggest a common origin.

Furthermore, when evaluating the strength of a mark (the sixth *DuPont* factor) and the similarity of marks (the first *DuPont* factor), courts must apply a consistent definition of "similar goods/services" across all factors once established.

**Application to Intermediary vs. Lender Roles**
The core of your question addresses whether CC Serve's status as an intermediary invalidates its claim against Apex Bank's direct lending services. The Federal Circuit rejected this distinction in Part III(A) of the opinion:

1.  **Definition of Services:** The Board interpreted "credit card services" broadly to include issuing cards for financing purchases, processing transactions, account management, and counseling on debt. Crucially, the Board noted that dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" inherently encompass extending credit via credit cards.
2.  **"Legally Identical":** Based on this functional overlap—where both entities facilitate consumer credit—the Board found the parties' services to be "**legally identical, in part.**"
3.  **Rejection of Operational Distinctions:** The court explicitly addressed Apex's argument that CC Serve is merely a partner/processor and not a direct lender (i.e., it doesn't hold deposits or make traditional loans). The court found this argument "**unavailing.**" Even though CC Serve partners with banks to issue cards while Apex intends to operate as a full-service bank, the specific service lines overlap significantly enough to satisfy the second *DuPont* factor (similarity of goods/services).

**Conclusion**
Therefore, operating as an administrator rather than a direct issuer does not strip a registrant of rights against a traditional bank if the specific services provided (in this case, credit card financing and management) functionally overlap with banking and financing offerings. The Federal Circuit held that substantial evidence supported the Board's finding that these services are highly similar, regardless of whether one entity is technically classified as a "lender" and the other as a "service provider."

However, note that while the court **affirmed** this finding regarding service similarity (Factor 2), it **vacated** and remanded the case regarding Factors 1 (commercial impression) and 6 (third-party use). The court ruled that because Factor 2 found the services were highly similar/legally identical, Factor 6 must consider third-party marks in *both* credit card and broader banking contexts—not just credit card-specific marks—to accurately gauge mark strength before making a final confusion determination on remand.

### Is there a requirement for consistency in defining 'similar goods/services' across different DuPont factors within the same proceeding?
Question:
This is where I'm most confused about the court's reasoning. The Board found in Factor 2 that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services were highly similar (even partially identical). Yet, when moving to Factor 6 (third-party marks), the Board suddenly narrowed its scope *only* to marks used strictly for 'credit card services,' excluding broader banking/financing marks. The Federal Circuit called this an error. Why is it legally required that if Factor 2 says 'these industries overlap,' Factor 6 must use that same broad definition of overlap? Can't one argue that while banking and credit cards are related generally (Factor 2), third-party competition is only fierce specifically within credit card administration (Factor 6)?
Answer:
Yes, there is a requirement for consistency in defining "similar goods" across different *DuPont* factors within the same proceeding. The Federal Circuit's decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* establishes that once a tribunal makes a factual finding regarding the relationship between the parties' services under one factor, it cannot arbitrarily narrow that definition when applying subsequent factors without a valid legal basis.

In this case, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) committed legal error by engaging in what amounts to "cherry-picking" the scope of similarity depending on which factor was being analyzed. Under **Factor 2** (similarity of goods/services), the Board explicitly found that Apex's "banking and financing services" and CC Serve's "credit card services" were **"highly similar"** and even **"partially legally identical."** The Board relied on dictionary definitions showing that banking encompasses issuing credit cards and cited third-party registrations covering both sectors to support the conclusion that these services could emanate from a single source.

However, when analyzing **Factor 6** (number and nature of similar marks in use), the Board abruptly shifted its analytical lens. Despite its earlier finding that banking and credit card services are closely related, the Board restricted the universe of relevant third-party marks to only those used strictly for "credit card services," excluding marks used for broader banking or financing services. The Board deemed these broader marks "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit rejected this approach as inconsistent. Writing for the panel, Judge Hughes noted that Factor 6 requires an evaluation of similar marks on **"similar goods,"** not necessarily *identical* goods (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*). Since the Board had already established as a matter of fact under Factor 2 that the parties' services are highly similar (and partially identical), there was no legal justification to apply a stricter definition of similarity under Factor 6. The court stated: *"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."*

To allow otherwise would create an illogical standard where two sets of goods can be considered sufficiently related to cause confusion between two specific parties (Factor 2) but insufficiently related to show that a crowded marketplace exists among third parties (Factor 6). Such inconsistency undermines the holistic nature of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, where all factors must rest upon a coherent set of factual premises regarding market reality. Consequently, because Factor 6 determines commercial strength—which directly impacts **Factor 1** (similarity of marks)—the Federal Circuit vacated both findings and remanded for reconsideration using a consistent definition of service similarity derived from Factor 2.

### What constitutes sufficient proof of 'commercial weakness' under DuPont Factor 6, and why did nine third-party uses fail to meet that threshold?
Question:
Apex submitted evidence showing nine other uses of 'Aspire' or Aspire-formative marks in credit card services. The Board dismissed this, saying nine wasn't enough to prove 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' use needed to weaken CC Serve's mark. They cited cases mentioning 14 or 26 examples as significant. Is there a hard numerical rule here? Or is it purely qualitative? If nine companies are using similar names in a crowded financial sector, shouldn't that logically make consumers more sophisticated at distinguishing sources? Why didn't nine suffice here?
Answer:
There is **no hard numerical rule** determining what constitutes "sufficient" proof of commercial weakness under the sixth *DuPont* factor. As the Federal Circuit clarified in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the sufficiency of third-party uses is a **qualitative inquiry** dependent on whether the evidence demonstrates that the market is sufficiently crowded to educate consumers to distinguish between similar marks.

In this case, the Board dismissed Apex's evidence of nine third-party uses of "Aspire" or "Aspire"-formative marks in credit card services because it deemed that number insufficient to show "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use. The Board relied on precedent where courts found significance in higher numbers—specifically citing *Jack Wolfskin*, which noted fourteen notable examples, and *Juice Generation*, which referred to twenty-six marks as a "considerable number." However, the Federal Circuit explicitly rejected the notion that these numbers created a rigid threshold. Instead, the Court emphasized that the core legal principle is whether the volume of similar marks indicates that consumers have become adept at distinguishing sources in a crowded field (*Juice Generation*; *Jack Wolfskin*).

The reason nine uses failed to meet the threshold in the Board's initial analysis was not merely because nine is lower than fourteen or twenty-six, but because those specific instances did not rise to the level of **"voluminous"** or **"ubiquitous"** use required to prove that CC Serve's mark had lost its distinctiveness. The Board reasoned that while CC Serve's mark might not possess exceptional commercial strength, Apex had failed to prove it was so weak as to warrant only a narrow scope of protection. Consequently, without evidence showing a saturation of similar marks that would force consumers to scrutinize differences closely, the presumption remained that an inherently distinctive mark like "ASPIRE" retains its normal scope of protection.

It is crucial to note, however, that while nine uses were deemed insufficient by the Board under its original (narrow) framework, the Federal Circuit ultimately **vacated** this finding due to a separate legal error: the Board improperly limited its search for third-party marks strictly to "credit card services," excluding broader banking and financing services despite having previously ruled those services were highly similar under Factor 2. Had those additional forty-two marks been included in the calculation as mandated by proper legal standards, the total count might have shifted from being "insufficient" to potentially demonstrating a crowded marketplace. Thus, while there is no magic number where nine automatically fails and twenty-six automatically succeeds, nine was insufficient here because it did not objectively demonstrate a market so saturated that consumer confusion was unlikely.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the analysis of DuPont Factor 1 (Similarity of Marks) based entirely on errors made in Factor 6?
Question:
The court affirmed Factor 2 but vacated Factors 1 and 6. My understanding is that Factor 1 looks at visual/sound/conceptual similarity ('ASPIRE BANK' vs. 'ASPIRE'). Usually, this seems like an independent inquiry based on how words look and sound. However, the court said they had to vacate Factor 1 because reconsidering Factor 6 might change the determination of 'commercial strength,' which affects commercial impression. Isn't commercial impression mostly about how consumers perceive two specific side-by-side marks regardless of how many other people use similar words elsewhere? How does third-party usage legally alter the inherent similarity between two specific marks?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's analysis of DuPont Factor 1 (similarity of marks) because the determination of "commercial impression"—which is central to Factor 1—is inextricably linked to the "commercial strength" of the senior mark, a metric derived from Factor 6 (third-party use). While it may seem intuitive that the visual or phonetic similarity between *ASPIRE BANK* and *ASPIRE* is a fixed, standalone observation, the court clarified that the **scope** of that similarity depends on how distinct and strong the senior mark is perceived to be in the marketplace.

Under established precedent cited in *Apex Bank*, evidence of third-party use (Factor 6) demonstrates that a field is crowded, thereby weakening a mark's commercial strength. When a mark is weak due to extensive third-party use, consumers are trained to look beyond minor similarities and focus on distinguishing features. Conversely, if a mark possesses significant commercial strength (is unique), even small similarities can lead to confusion. As the court noted, "When a field is crowded with similar marks... customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other."

In this case, the Board committed a legal error in Factor 6 by artificially narrowing its scope. Although the Board correctly found in Factor 2 that credit card services and general banking/financing services are "highly similar" (even "partially legally identical"), it improperly restricted its Factor 6 analysis *only* to third-party marks used specifically for "credit card services," excluding numerous other financial service marks using "Aspire." By doing so, the Board failed to accurately assess whether the *ASPIRE* mark was actually weak due to crowding across the broader relevant market.

Because Factor 6 determines commercial strength, and commercial strength dictates how much weight is given to similarities under Factor 1, an erroneous calculation in Factor 6 renders any conclusion in Factor 1 unreliable. If, upon remand, expanded consideration of third-party marks reveals that *ASPIRE* is commercially weak (because it is widely used across banking and finance), then the mere addition of "BANK" might be sufficient for consumers to distinguish *ASPIRE BANK* from *ASPIRE*. Thus, the court could not uphold the Factor 1 finding without first correcting the foundational error in Factor 6 regarding what constitutes "similar goods" for third-party comparisons.

### Does an Intent-to-Use application grant priority rights superior to existing registrations during an opposition proceeding before actual use begins?
Question:
Apex filed an Intent-to-Use (ITU) application in August 2019 with a priority date tied to filing, while CC Serve had registered ASPIRE back in 1998 with a priority date of 1996. Since Apex hadn't launched their internet bank yet at the time of opposition, did they have any standing to argue their mark was valid against CC Serve's long-standing registration? How does ITU status interact with Section 2(d) oppositions when there is no actual commerce yet?
Answer:
**No.** An Intent-to-Use (ITU) application does **not** grant priority rights superior to an existing registration during a Section 2(d) opposition, nor does it allow the applicant to bypass the requirement of establishing a likelihood of confusion based on actual market conditions.

Under the Lanham Act and the precedent established in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL [Case Number], an ITU applicant possesses only a "constructive" priority date relative to future users who file later, but this right remains subordinate to any prior registered user who has already secured rights through use in commerce.

In *Apex*, the Federal Circuit explicitly affirmed that CC Serve's 1998 registration (with a 1996 priority date) held superior standing over Apex's 2019 ITU application. The court noted that Apex had not yet launched its internet bank or engaged in actual commerce at the time of the opposition. Despite Apex's claim that its filing date gave it leverage, the Board correctly applied Section 2(d) by analyzing whether Apex's proposed use would cause confusion with CC Serve's *existing* registered mark.

The core legal principle illustrated here is that an ITU application is merely a reservation of rights contingent upon eventual use; it does not create an immediate, superior property interest that can defeat a prior registrant. As the court stated, a Section 2(d) opposition requires determining if confusion is likely "because of concurrent use of the marks." While an ITU applicant can oppose another party's mark once they prove bona fide intent and subsequent use, they cannot successfully defend their own pending application against a senior registrant simply by virtue of their filing date.

Furthermore, the *Apex* opinion reinforces that without actual use, an applicant cannot alter the scope of protection afforded to a senior mark. The court vacated certain aspects of the Board's decision only because it failed to properly apply the *DuPont* factors regarding third-party usage (specifically Factor Six), not because Apex's ITU status granted it special immunity or superiority. The court affirmed that where services are "highly similar" (Factor Two)—as found between credit card services and general banking—the senior registrant retains the presumption of validity and priority unless overcome by specific evidence of weakness or lack of confusion, which Apex failed to provide initially due to its reliance on its filing date rather than market reality.

Therefore, until an ITU applicant files a Statement of Use and achieves registration based on actual commerce, their rights remain prospective and inferior to any pre-existing registration covering similar goods or services.
