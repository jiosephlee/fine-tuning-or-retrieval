Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Procedural Posture and Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency

## Facts of the Case: The 'Pump and Dump' Social Media Securities Fraud Scheme

This chapter examines the appellate court's analysis regarding the sufficiency of an indictment in the Fifth Circuit. The underlying case involves defendants who engaged in a scheme known as "pump and dump" securities fraud. The government alleged that Edward Constantinescu and other defendants used social media followings to post misrepresentations about securities trading positions and potential prices, thereby artificially inflating share prices. The District Court then processed the indictment and dismissed it, concluding that the defendants sought to deprive followers of economic information rather than a traditional property interest. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed the dismissal, holding that the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud under the relevant securities statutes. Understanding this procedural posture is essential for interpreting the court's reasoning, as the sufficiency of an indictment is a threshold determination that precedes any analysis of the underlying fraudulent conduct.

## District Court Dismissal: Rationale regarding Lack of Traditional Property Interest

The district court granted the defendants' motion to dismiss, reasoning that the indictment failed to state an offense because it lacked an allegation of a traditional property interest. Under the theory relied upon by the district court, the fraud statutes protect property rights, and the government argued that protecting intellectual property or information is not covered. Specifically, the district court reasoned that merely depriving victims of valuable economic information—rather than actual assets or money—is insufficient to state a crime. This distinction was critical because it determined not just the merits of the case, but whether the indictment itself met the statutory requirements of criminal procedure. The appeal turned squarely on this legal question: did the government's allegations of inducing followers to purchase securities constitute a deprivation of property, or only the deprivation of information?

## Standard of Review: De Novo Sufficiency Analysis (Citing *United States v. Rafoi* and *Fontenot*)

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. Under this standard, the appellate court reviews the indictment from the beginning without regard to whether the evidence supports the facts alleged. As the court explained, "the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated." Id. at 60 F.4th at 993, quoting *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011). This instruction undercuts the burden placed on the court to question the truthfulness of the statements in the indictment. Instead, the court assumes a prima facie factual basis for the charges and examines whether the text of the indictment violates the requirements for sufficiency. This standard protects defendants by ensuring that they receive a fair notice of the charges against them, even though they need not have provided the evidence at the pretrial stage.

## Three-Part Indictment Sufficiency Requirement: Enumerating Elements, Informing Defendant, and Double Jeopardy Defense

Under the law, an indictment must satisfy three distinct requirements to be considered sufficient. First, the indictment must "enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense." Id. quoting *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Second, the indictment must "fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him." Id. Third, it must "provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." *Guzman-Ocampo*, 236 F.3d at 236 (quotation cleaned up). The Fifth Circuit has established that "to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense." In the context of this case, the indictment alleged the scheme to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Therefore, the analysis hinges on whether the text alleges those elements.

## The Primacy of Allegations: The General Rule for Judicial Review of Indictments

The general rule for judicial review, derived from the de novo standard, emphasizes that the court focuses on the allegations presented in the indictment, not the underlying evidence of the officers' conduct. Where the indictment is voidable, for example, of the underlying conduct. The *Rafoi* citation emphasizes that the court is "required to take the allegations of the indictment as true." This rule ensures that the defendant is protected by the promise of fair notice. In this case, the court concluded that the allegations in the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme and intent to defraud. The text of the indictment stated that defendants posted false and misleading information so other investors were induced to purchase the security. This text satisfied the requirement of a material misstatement intended to deceive and to cause harm. Because the indictment contained the required elements, the court treated the allegations as factual for the purpose of the sufficiency inquiry, reversing the dismissal and remanding for proceedings consistent with the opinion.

# Statutory Framework and Substantive Elements of Securities Fraud

This chapter outlines the legal framework underpinning the fraud charges found in *United States v. Constantinescu*, a Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals opinion addressing the sufficiency of an indictment. It focuses on the statutory basis of securities fraud charges and the substantive elements required to sustain such convictions. As read in this opinion, the court’s analysis relies heavily on the definition of what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" and an "'intent to defraud' under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. 

## 18 U.S.C. § 1348: Scheme to Deprive vs. Other Fraudulent Schemes

Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, a defendant may be convicted of securities fraud if they engage in a scheme to defraud any person in connection with a security, or a scheme to obtain money or property through false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. In *Constantinescu*, the government charged the defendants with conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and securities fraud under §§ 1348 and 2. The district court dismissed the indictment, ruling that it failed to allege a specific property interest was deprived. The government successfully argued that the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to deprive followers of money, a traditional property interest. The difference between the two theories is critical: one theory (right-to-control) would focus on depriving the victim of valuable economic information alone, while the other (fraudulent-inducement) focuses on tricking the victim into handing over actual money or property. The district court correctly interpreted the requirement for "a scheme to deprive," but incorrectly excluding the policy interest of informing victims of their potential loss. The court held that an indictment is sufficient if it alleges that defendants schemed to deprive victims of money or property, distinguishing this from the *Ciminelli* right-to-control ruling.

## Governing Legal Concepts: Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes (*United States v. Greenlaw*)

The Fifth Circuit interprets the securities fraud statute in conjunction with established mail and wire fraud concepts. As noted in *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023), the circuits have "borrowed key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes" and "given the terms similar treatment." Because there is scant caselaw construing securities fraud in this circuit specifically, the Court relies on mail and wire fraud precedents to define the statutory elements. This ensures that the terms "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" are interpreted consistently across fraud statutes. In *Constantinescu*, the Court applied these borrowed concepts to determine whether the language of the indictment met the threshold for sufficiency.

## Defining the 'Scheme to Defraud': Material Misrepresentation and Intent to Deceive

To sustain an indictment for securities fraud, the government must allege each material element of the charge. The "scheme to defraud" element requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value. In *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018), the Fifth Circuit defined this requirement: a "scheme to defraud" exists if a defendant used a material misstatement to deceive others in order to obtain a value, such as money, from the entity to be deceived. In *Constantinescu*, the Court referenced this standard to evaluate the indictment's allegations regarding the defendants' purchases of securities through misrepresentations. The Court determined that the indictment sufficiently alleged that defendants engaged in deception and obtained the victim's money or property, thereby meeting the "scheme to defraud" element.

## Defining the 'Intent to Defraud': Deception and Cause of Harm

The "intent to defraud" element is distinct from the scheme element. It requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." As established in *Evans*, a fraud statute is complete only when the defendant has an intent to deceive and cause harm. However, the defense in *Constantinescu* argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to harm victims, noting instead that the defendants intended to profit. The Court rejected this argument. It must be noted that while the indictment alleges an intent to profit, the Supreme Court has clarified in *Kousisis* that the statutes do not require the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss. Therefore, the distinction between seeking to profit and seeking to harm is legally irrelevant when the intent is to deceive to gain value.

## Judicial Precedent Coherence: *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) Overview regarding Fraud Terminology

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), provided the doctrinal clarity necessary to resolve the sufficiency of the indictment. *Kousisis* clarified the terminology regarding "fraudulent-inducement" versus other fraudulent theories. The Court held that a defendant violates fraud statutes when they use a "material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." This confirms that the "fraudulent-inducement theory" protects money and property and does not rely on the right-to-control theory rejection in *Ciminelli*. In *Constantinescu*, the Court integrated *Kousisis* to find that the indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped" securities to gain a profit was sufficient to allege an intent to defraud. The offense is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. Consequently, the indictment sufficiently alleged the necessary elements for the offense.

# Analysis: The 'Scheme to Defraud' and the Requirement of a Property Interest

## This chapter analyzes the first major issue appealing the indictment: the distinction between depriving a victim of information versus depriving them of property, and how *Ciminelli* was applied (or distinguished) in this context.

## The *Ciminelli* Rule and the Right-to-Control Theory: Fraud Without Property
To begin reading an indictment for securities fraud, a lawyer must understand the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Court addressed a claim that a construction company paid a lobbyist to obtain state-funded jobs solely to leverage the value of the lobbyist's contacts and economic information. The government relied on the "right-to-control theory," arguing that the defendant's property was the victim's "right to control [his] assets." The Supreme Court held that this theory could not form the basis for a fraud conviction because "[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." This ruling establishes a bright-line rule for criminal fraud: a defendant cannot be convicted of fraud simply for depriving another of potentially valuable economic information alone. When analyzing an indictment, one must look for a deprivation of *property*, not just of *knowledge*. If the government's case rests on the argument that an individual lost valuable information, they must prove that the transaction itself involved a transfer of a recognized property interest to satisfy the Constitution's Double Jeopardy Clause and federal fraud statutes.

## Distinguishing This Case from *Ciminelli*: Fraudulent-Inducement Theory vs. Right-to-Control Theory
The government's strategy in *Constantinescu* differs fundamentally from *Ciminelli* because it relies on the "fraudulent-inducement theory" instead of the "right-to-control theory." As clarified in the companion decision *Kousisis v. United States*, the fraudulent-inducement theory allows for a conviction when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." Unlike the *Ciminelli* right-to-control approach, which treats information as the commodity at stake, fraudulent inducement treats the information as a weapon to trick a victim into a transaction involving actual property. The Supreme Court explicitly held that this fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." This is a critical distinction for the reader: while *Ciminelli* rejected the idea that holding or withholding information alone can be fraud, *Kousisis* confirms that using false information to secure a transaction where money or property changes hands triggers liability. This distinction saves the *Constantinescu* indictment from being dismissed.

## Application to 'Pump and Dump' Facts: Forcing a Follow to Purchase Merchandise (Property)
In applying these legal theories to the specific facts of *Constantinescu*, the District Court incorrectly concluded that the prosecution could not traverse a清白 violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 because the defendants had not deprived individuals of property. However, the indictment alleges that defendants purchased a security first, then posted false information to make that security more attractive to other investors, inducing them to "part with their money or property" to acquire that security. The operation described is not purely informational; it is a direct mechanism for obtaining money. The indictment alleges that defendants induced their followers, through the false and misleading information, to purchase the security. Thus, the victims' property interest is the securities themselves, which they acquire by paying money. When an indictment alleges that a defendant used deception to induce a victim to enter into a financial transaction, the victim is no longer just deprived of information; the victim is parted with their money to secure the defendant's ill-gotten profit.

## Materiality of the Misrepresentation: Status of Economic Information vs. Securities
While the indictment alleges economic information drives the scheme, the key legal concern is the status of the item being obtained. The opinion notes that the Supreme Court rejected a theory where a victim loses the "right to control information" without suffering an economic loss. However, the indictment alleges transfers of "money or property" from the followers to the defendants. In this context, the securities are treated as commodities that change hands. The *Kousisis* decision further clarifies that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss of money must equal the defendant's gain. This means that from the prosecution's perspective, as long as the misrepresentation regarding the security's price (the economic information) caused the follower to part with money (the property interest), the requisite intent is satisfied. The "status" of the information does not negate the property interest of the securities being transferred.

## Conclusion of This Analysis: Why the Indictment Satisfies Ciminelli's Property Requirement
Ultimately, the Fifth Circuit concludes that the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, thereby reversing the district court's dismissal. The core reasoning is that the alleged "pump and dump" scheme involves the fraudulent inducement of a transaction. Because the scheme involves inducing investors to "part with their money or property" to purchase a security, the transaction is rooted in the deprivation of a property interest, not just the withholding of information. While the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* prohibited the "right-to-control theory" based on economic information, the fraudulent-inducement theory in *Kousisis* protects the transfer of actual money and property. Because *Constantinescu* fits within the narrower scope of fraudulent inducement—where deception secures a transfer of value rather than mere access to information—the indictment remains sufficient under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 1349.

# Analysis: 'Intent to Defraud' and the Absence of Victim Economic Loss

## The Argument: Does 'Intent to Defraud' Require Victim Economic Loss?

In the district court proceedings, the primary challenge mounted by the defendants was to the sufficiency of the indictment regarding the specific nature of the deception alleged. They argued that the indictment failed to state an offense because it did not allege that defendants schemed to deprive their followers of a protected property interest, but only that they intended to deprive them of potentially valuable economic information. Specifically, the indictment alleged that defendants utilized a “pump and dump” scheme to manipulate share prices, which allegedly induced investors to purchase securities they did not need but would generate profits for the sellers. The defendants contended that by this scheme, the victims suffered no deprivation of traditional property rights, and their loss was merely informational (the loss of information regarding trading positions). They submitted that the indictment alleged that defendants intended to take advantage of their followers to enrich themselves rather than injure their followers in a suffering financial sense. The district court, applying this narrow view regarding the "property interest" requirement under the securities fraud statute, dismissed the indictment, concluding that the allegations did not state a fraud offender who schemed to deprive victims of property. However, this dismissal rested on a broader assumption about the statutory language of the fraud statute versus the Supreme Court's recent guidance on the definition of fraud and the required economic mischief.

## Supreme Court Holding in *Kousisis*: Scheming to 'Obtain' vs. 'Harm' the Victim

The Fifth Circuit must first address whether recent Supreme Court precedent alters the standard of Fourth Amendment sufficiency for the specific intent to defraud element of the securities fraud statute. The core of the defense argument relied heavily on the *right-to-control* theory of fraud, which posits that fraud arises from the deprivation of valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. The Supreme Court addressed this issue in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), holding that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. In that case, the government sought to establish a fraud conviction by showing the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of the right to control assets, which the Court found was not a "traditional property interest" protected by federal fraud statutes. Consequently, *Ciminelli* rejected the *right-to-control* theory as a valid basis for a federal fraud conviction. In the *Constantinescu* opinion, the defendants attempt to analogize their situation to *Ciminelli*, arguing that their scheme only involved the deprivation of information rather than tangible assets. The response relies on the Supreme Court’s subsequent decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), which clarified that federal fraud statutes do not strictly require the victim to suffer an economic loss from the defendant’s scheme. In *Kousisis*, the Court held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." This distinction is critical because it shifts the legal focus from the victim's ultimate financial outcome to the defendant's conduct of obtaining property through an establishment of deception.

## The 'Fraudulent-Inducement Theory' Applied to the Pumps: Inducing Purchase Regardless of Zero Net Loss

To distinguish the *Constantinescu* case from *Ciminelli*, the *United States v. Constantinescu* opinion invokes the "fraudulent-inducement theory" rather than the "right-to-control theory." The fraudulent-inducement theory defines fraud as a scenario where a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." Under this theory, the fraud is consummated by the victim handing over the property, regardless of whether the fraudster provides consideration of equal value in return. This stands in contrast to the right-to-control theory, which the Supreme Court found rejected in *Ciminelli*. The *Constantinescu* opinion explicitly states that the fraudulent-inducement theory is "not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory" and that it protects money and property, not just information. In the context of the pump-and-dump scheme, this distinction is essential. The indictment alleges that defendants "induced" followers to purchase securities. If the followers purchase the stock based on the manipulation, they transfer their money (property) to the defendants in exchange for the stock. Even if the value of the security drops subsequently when the defendants sell their own positions (the dump), the initial transfer constitutes an "obtaining of money or property" under the fraudulent-inducement theory. Therefore, the indictment does not run afoul of *Ciminelli* because it does not rely on the deprivation of economic information alone, but on the actual transaction where the victim's money was induced to change hands.

## Application to Constantinescu: Inducing Followers to Buy Securities ≠ Inducing Followers to Lose Net Wealth

The *Constantinescu* opinion applies this legal rule specifically to the facts that led to the district court's dismissal. The district court dismissed the indictment because it argued that the alleged scheme did not allege that defendants harmed their followers, but rather just manipulated price. The opinion finds the district court's dismissal incorrect because the Supreme Court in *Kousisis* confirmed that a faction may be liable in fraud even when the victim receives "something of equal value in return." In the context of the securities fraud charge, the followers who buy the security receive market-value securities. The district court argued that this does not constitute economic harm, but the Fifth Circuit reasons that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement. The law does not require the victim's loss of money or property to supply the defendant's gain in a way that makes their net worth be more precise than the defendant's net worth. In this scenario, the well-adopted securities fraud charge hinges on the deception used to induce the purchase. The *Kousisis* analysis indicates that the victim's receipt of market-value securities does not obviate the fraud charges because the transaction itself involved the deprivation of money through deception. The defendants' scheme was to use their scheme to make out a trading position and extract profit, which satisfies the requirement to obtain property from the victim through the fraud.

## Why Profit Motive is Sufficient: Distinction Without a Difference in Fraud Statutes (*United States v. Baker* Cited)

A final point of contention for the defendants was the alleged intent to profit versus a general intent to defraud. The defendants argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to harm victims because the objective was clearly to make defendants' profits, not to cause a net financial loss to their followers. They suggested that a profit motive is distinguishable from a fraud motive without harm. The Supreme Court's reasoning in *Kousisis* rejected this assertion, holding that "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. The Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* notes that the distinction between profit and harm is often "without a difference" in the context of the federal fraud statutes. In *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019), the court explained that the fraud statutes do not require a scenario where the victim's loss of money or property supplied the defendant's gain. Instead, the statute protects the victim's property rights during the transaction. If the *Constantinescu* defendants allege that they "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security... so that other investors were induced to purchase the security," the indictment alleges they schemed to obtain money (followed the securities). That is a sufficient allegation of an intent to defraud under the *Kousisis* and *Baker* framework. Consequently, the motive to profit, rather than inflict economic harm, remains sufficient to meet the statutory intent to defraud requirement.

# Disposition of the Appeal and Implications

## The Sufficiency of the Indictment: Why the Appellate Court Rejected the Dismissal

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit focuses its analysis on whether the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu* met the constitutional threshold for sufficiency. Under the standard established by this court, an indictment must adequately set forth each element of the charged offense while shielding the defendant from double jeopardy. When the district court dismissed the indictment, it ruled that the government failed to state an offense by simply alleging that defendants spent their followers' time by depriving them of economic information. The appellate court clarifies that this dismissal was legally incorrect because courts must look at the totality of the allegations, determining if a scheme and intent to defraud were properly alleged. Section III of the opinion demonstrates that the government’s charge went beyond the mere deprivation of information to include specific actions that involve the protection of traditional property interests.

The court recognizes that a common error in prosecutorial theory involves capitalizing the distinction between the loss of value and the loss of control. While the Second Circuits often reject the deprivation of economic information as a standalone tort without a property interest foundation, the indictment here is not rooted in a claim of *right-to-control*. Instead, the indictment alleges that the defendants posted false and misleading information to induce followers to purchase securities. By facilitating a transaction where the followers traded money for securities, the defendants operated under a "fraudulent-inducement" framework. This framework is distinct from the rejected "right-to-control" theory, which suggests that property is merely an abstract right to decision-making. The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that because the indictment alleges the induction of a victim into a transaction requiring the handing over of money or property, the element of a protected "property interest" is satisfied even if the value was recently received.

## Procedural Outcome: Reversal of Dismissal and Ruling for the Indictment to Continue

Based on the court’s application of the *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli* precedents, the Fifth Circuit issues a formal decision to reverse the lower court’s dismissal. The order explicitly states that the district court's handling of the case is inconsistent with the current legal standards regarding federal fraud statutes. Consequently, the case is vacated and remanded for new proceedings consistent with the court's opinion. This remedial order ensures that the charge advances to the jury stage with the correct understanding that the "pump and dump" tactic does not require proof of actual economic loss to the victim to support a fraud conviction.

The procedural implication of this reversal is significant for state and federal co-prosecutors. Defendants are now required to defend themselves against the charges as they stand, potentially facing trial rather than the dismissal that would have otherwise shielded them. The remand is not limited to retrial but extends to ensuring that the prosecution can demonstrate the necessary nexus between the deception and the acquisition of money. For the status of the indictment, the Fifth Circuit maintains that the count itself, though requiring enrichment without direct loss, remains legally viable provided the evidence at trial can show that the forgetfulness of the information directly facilitated the transfer of property. The final disposition rests on the appellate determination that the defendant's intent to profit was sufficient to negate the need to plead specific mutilated loss elements in the indictment.

## The Broad Significance: Updating *Ciminelli* Precedent on Fraud Prototypes

A key pedagogical takeaway from this opinion is how the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* context interprets the earlier *Ciminelli* holding on the prohibition of plotting fraud against property rights. In *Ciminelli*, the Court established that fraud statutes require the deprivation of a traditional property interest; merely depriving a victim of valuable information, without that information being treated as property under a right-to-control theory, is insufficient. *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies that this does not render all "information-based fraud" convictions impossible. The opinion explains that *Kousisis* distinguishes between the two recognized property theories. The first, right-to-control, lacks a property interest in the mere data. The second, fraudulent-inducement, involves a victim who is tricked into a transaction where property must be handed over.

This opinion effectively bridges the gap left by *Ciminelli* and preserves the integrity of the fraud statutes by validating the fraudulent-inducement theory in context. The Fifth Circuit teaches that when a defendant deceives a victim to vent money into the hands of a target, *Ciminelli*'s ban on information-only fraud should not excuse a conviction. The holding underscores that while the Court decided in *Ciminelli* that the right-to-control theory cannot serve as the sole basis for a fraud conviction, that did not ban the fraudulent-inducement theory. The indictment in *Constantinescu* did not rely on a fine-tuned claim that the investors' right to control their assets was the fraud, but rather that they were induced to buy assets, thus losing their right to *not* buy those assets, which was a property interest. The Fifth Circuit interprets this as a "separate" mechanism that was not repackaged in the *Ciminelli* restriction.

## Impact on Social Media Fraud Prosecutions: Validating the 'Pump and Dump' Charge Violation

The disposition of this appeal has substantive implications for the prosecution of "pump and dump" fraud schemes increasingly detected via social media platforms. Indictments in this area often face a threshold dismissal defense based on the argument that the schemes were limited to information dissemination rather than property transfer. By rejecting the dismissal of the *Constantinescu* indictment, the Fifth Circuit validates the applicability of the mail and wire fraud statutes to "pump and dump" activities where the criminal activity centers on inducing downstream transactions through misrepresentation.

The court notes that the defendants' posting of false and misleading information was a direct procurement solicitation to purchase securities. The indictment did not stop at the allegation that the defendants wanted the followers to *know* something; it alleged that the defendants wanted the followers to *buy* something. This distinction is critical because it shifts the legal burden from proving an information-based intent to proving a transactional intent. Prosecutors can now proceed under the theory that the transaction itself provides the property interest needed for a fraud charge. Given the millions profited and the volume of transactions, the Fifth Circuit reinforces that these schemes fall squarely within the scope of securities fraud statutes where the object of the fraud is the acquisition of money or property, not the preservation of an abstract economic interest.

## Closing Brief on Legal Logic: How the Opinion Resolves Conflicting Property vs. Information Theories

The legal logic employed in *Constantinescu* resolves the conflict between the protection of traditional property rights and the rejection of economic information alone. The core tension lies in whether a "property interest" can include the knowledge held by a victim versus the actual economic value received or withheld. The Fifth Circuit concludes that the fraud statutes protect the interest in the exchange of property, not the interest in the symmetry of information. Therefore, the distortion in information that caused the exchange of money is sufficient to establish the property interest.

The final legal holding is that the object of the fraud is the "money" obtained by the defendant, not the information deprived by the defendant. The opinion concludes that the receipt of securities by the followers still counts as property. Even though the defendants sold and profited, the indictment alleged that the victim supplied that money, which satisfies the "scheme to obtain money or property" element under *Kousisis*. The court explicitly states that the defendant's intent to profit is sufficient to satisfy the intent to defraud, even when the victim suffers no net loss in comparison. This closes the loophole for defendants to argue that because they enriched themselves without causing a financial deficiency, there was no fraud. The opinion solidifies that obtaining property by deception is a crime, regardless of the victim's net position, aligning with the broad statutory language of U.S.C. § 1348.
