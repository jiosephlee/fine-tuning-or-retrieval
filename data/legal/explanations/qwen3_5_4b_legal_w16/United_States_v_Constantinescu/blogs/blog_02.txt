Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Profit Over Loss: How *Kousisis* Redefined Fraudulent Intent in Federal Indictments

## The *Ciminelli* Tax

The June 2025 Supreme Court decision in *Kousisis v. United States* effectively stripped prosecutors of one of their most common barriers to entry in fraud cases: the necessity of proving victim loss. *Kousisis* established that under the fraud statutes, a defendant violates the law when they schemed to “obtain” the victim’s money or property through deception, regardless of whether the victim ended up richer or poorer as a result. This autonomous shift in the defendant’s required intent has created a litigation landscape waiting in its wake, as illustrated by the Fifth Circuit’s October 2025 reversal in *United States v. Constantinescu*. In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit dealt with a prosecution that pivoted on social media activity flagged as a “pump and dump” scheme. The district court previously narrowly dismissed the indictment based on the franchise — a decision now overturned — which painted the federal fraud statutes as too narrow to capture criminal conduct that did not cause the victim money damage directly.

The district court focused on what is now known as the "right-to-control" theory of fraud. Under that theory, the fraudster deprives the victim of the "right to exercise discretion" over market value. *Constantinescu* employed this logic to strike down the charge, arguing that social media posts that alter market price amounted to a deprivation of information rather than money or property. Following *Ciminelli* (2025), the court reasoned that the "deprivation" of economic information alone is not a protected property interest. This was a significant win for defendants in class-action or whistleblower contexts, but the Fifth Circuit saw its end when the case was remanded to the panel. The court clarified that the plaintiffs’ documents did not allege a right-to-control theory; rather, they alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory. By alleging that defendants used false premises to induce followers to part with their own money for securities, the government satisfied the statutory requirement without needing to prove the investors had no money to begin with.

The result is a fundamental distinction between the two theories. The district court treated the loss of potential investment information as the equivalent of the general rule: you must take something of value in return for the fraud. However, the Fifth Circuit correctly identified that the right-to-control theory applies only when the victim's interest is the *information itself*, not a transactional exchange. The indictment in *Constantinescu* alleged that the defendants used false information to trick followers into purchasing securities. That transaction is a classic "fraudulent-inducement." Even if the follower didn't lose money because they bought a stock at fair market value, the intent was still to extract value via deception. The critical point here is the *Kousisis* precedent that the *intent* to profit must align with the *intent* to obtain something of value, creating a much tighter legal basis for conviction than previously thought.

## Inducing the Deed: Induced Fraud vs. Injured Assets

The verdant appeal for the prosecution in *Constantinescu* rests heavily on the rejection of the "clean profit" defense. The indictment alleged that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities by posting false information, but the defendants argued that they intended to profit from this scheme, not hurt their followers. They contended that without evidence of victim loss — or at least a decision to inject net negative value into their account — the "intent to defraud" element was missing. This hinges on the distinction between the act of poisoning the market and the outcome of the transaction. In the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, the mere result of a transaction does not negate the fraud, provided the transaction itself was based on a material misrepresentation.

This legal logic flips the traditional understanding of securities fraud, which often assumes a direct harm to the investor. In a pump-and-dump scenario, the follower buys a stock because they believe it will rise, and the public is misled. The attacker profits by selling the stock later at high prices, but the follower might still profit if the market rises. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that the *constancy* of the *intent to deflate* is the key violation of the federal schemes protection, not the *consequence* of the *transaction*. The law protects the act of obtaining value without sufficient capacity to trust the transaction. When the government alleges that the defendant used false pretenses to induce the victim to buy, the transaction was meant to transfer the victim's capital interest to the fraudster's benefit.

This also highlights the importance of the proper theory. If the prosecution proceeds under the right-to-control theory, the defect is that the information itself has no property right. But if the prosecution proceeds under the fraudulent-inducement theory, the property interest is the securities money itself. The Fifth Circuit rejected the defense's claim that the victims were not economically worse off, noting that the government does not need to allege that the victim lost money net. As long as the transaction was obtained by deception and resulted in a transfer of property, the intent requirement is met. This was a crucial breakdown of the defense argument that "profit motive" implies harm to the victim. The prosecution's approach is that the fraudster *obtains* the property, regardless of whether the victim *drains* the property.

## The *Kousisis* Shield Against Profit-Motive

The most significant doctrinal shift in *Constantinescu* revolves around the Supreme Court's *Kousisis* ruling regarding the economic loss element. The *Kousisis* court explicitly stated that a defendant may be liable for a fraud conviction when the victim receives an equal value in return. The *Kousisis* ruling validates the idea that the "profit motive" does not negate the "intent to defraud." In *Constantinescu*, the court relied on *Kousisis* to explain that the indictment alleged a scheme to take money, but did not require the indictment to allege a scheme to harm the victims bottom line. This provides the Fifth Circuit with a bright-line rule: prosecution of fraud need not establish net economic loss to the victim. The prosecution only needs to establish that the victim gave up property and the defendant obtained it, under false pretenses.

This is a massive step for federal prosecutors, particularly in SEC-enforcement friendly criminal cases. The "Clean Profit" defense must be met with careful scrutiny of the indictment and evidence. The prosecution can point to the *Kousisis* Supreme Court decision to dismantle the "intent to harm" defense. The Fifth Circuit's reasoning reinforces that if the fraudster intended to obtain value and deceived the recipient to do so, they were covered. Even if the defendant intended to profit, this is not separate from the intent to defraud; to profit is to take property. This makes the "intent to defraud" element easier to show in practice, meaning more "clean profit" cases can be avoided by the government in future indictments, provided the "inducement" theory holds. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion also signals that this will be the hot topic for the Supreme Court to review as well, but for now, this sets a standard of liability for "fraudulent-inducement" schemes in the Fifth Circuit without requiring a detailed accounting of every transaction's equity impact.

## What This Means for the Doctrinal Landscape

The practical implication of *Constantinescu* is that the distinction between the right-to-control theory and the fraudulent-inducement theory is now a matter of indictment drafting rather than immutable defense law. If the defense can credibly argue "we didn't take your property, we just gave you this information," the prosecution must attack the theory to show the property transfer was fraudulent-inducement. This shifts the burden toward proving the transactional element of the contract was fraudulent. For social media promoters, this means that an indictment alleging the explicit use of a performance scheme to induce purchase, without a right-to-control theory, is sufficient evidence. However, a defense that does not show intent to take property fails. The Fifth Circuit also signals that future indictments should avoid the right-to-control language entirely. Prosecutors must focus on the *inducement* of the transaction, not the *value* of the information. This removes the need for privacy in the case law and places the focus squarely on the transaction.

Ultimately, *Constantinescu* ensures that the 2025 *Kousisis* ruling does not simply become a theoretical decision, but a practical defense for federal indictments. The "profit vs. harm" debate ensures that the law is flexible, but the "intent to obtain" means it is not. The financial incentives to make these arguments into the next round of litigation will likely continue. This decision will likely see more cases filed where the defendant is sued for fraud with a focus on the "intent to deceive" rather than the "loss to the victim." The Fifth Circuit has created a pathway for this, where the indictment is sufficient on its face to prosecute defendants for profit-making schemes without disproving economic loss. This heralds a broader era of securities fraud prosecutions that rely on the "induced transaction" theory.
