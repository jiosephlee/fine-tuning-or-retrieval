Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why does the Fifth Circuit review sufficiency of an indictment de novo rather than on a different standard of review?
Question:
I'm reading the opinion and I see that the court states it 'reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo' under United States v. Rafoi. But I thought most appeals standards might be 'clearly erroneous' or 'clearly settled law.' Is de novo the correct standard for sufficiency of indictment in general, or is there something specific about how the Fifth Circuit interprets 18 U.S.C. 1348 that requires this heightened review?
Answer:
### Why the Fifth Circuit Reviews Sufficiency of Indictment De Novo

The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo* because the determination of whether an indictment states an offense is a question of law that requires an independent assessment, as established by Fifth Circuit precedent. Crucially, this standard applies generally and is **not** triggered by the specific interpretation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348.

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court explicitly anchors this standard in existing circuit law rather than creating a unique requirement for securities fraud. In Section II, the court states:

> "We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023)."

The court clarifies that under this standard, the appellate court must "take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated" (*United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011)). This duty is defined by specific legal requirements rather than factual findings, such as enumerating "each prima facie element of the charged offense" and ensuring the indictment "fairly inform[s] the defendant of the charges filed against him" (*United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996)).

Consequently, the *Constantinescu* opinion applies this *de novo* review to the defendants' § 1348 charges in Section III. While the court substitutes statutory law from *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* regarding the "fraudulent-inducement theory," it maintains that the compliance with those statutes is filtered through the general sufficiency standard (*Pholton*). Thus, there is no "heightened" review unique to 18 U.S.C. § 1348; the stricter scrutiny is the standard baseline for ensuring the indictment itself sufficiently alleges material elements of the offense.

In short, the *de novo* review is required because the question is whether the indictment alleges the required legal elements of the crime (*Guzman-Ocampo*, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000)), and this entails legal interpretation rather than a factual review standard like "clearly erroneous."

### How is the 'pump and dump' scheme in United States v. Constantinescu distinguishable from the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli?
Question:
The defendants argued that because they only deprived the victims of economic information, the case should fall under the *Ciminelli* right-to-control theory. Since the Supreme Court said *Ciminelli* only depends on information deprivation, why does the Fifth Circuit suggest this particular 'pump and dump' case isn't a right-to-control issue? I don't see how simply posting false info ensures the view that the victims part with property.
Answer:
### Answer

The Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* distinguishes the "pump and dump" scheme from the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli* by determining that the instrument used to execute the fraud was a transaction involving property, not merely the deprivation of an economic interest in information. While *Ciminelli* bars fraud convictions based solely on the deprivation of information because information is not a traditional property interest, the *Constantinescu* court confirms that the "fraudulent-inducement" theory protects the transfer of money and property.

In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the right-to-control theory because it would base a fraud conviction on the deprivation of a victim's "right to valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest" and that fraud statutes "protect only traditional property interests." Thus, a victim cannot be defrauded simply because they are withheld from knowing facts about assets they might otherwise control.

The *Constantinescu* opinion distinguishes the present charge because the government alleged that defendants induced their followers to **purchase securities**, thereby causing them to part with money or property. The Fifth Circuit notes that under the fraudulent-inducement theory, which it explicitly does not view as a repackaging of the *Ciminelli* theory, "a defendant 'uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.'" Unlike *Ciminelli*, where the error was based only on information deprivation, the *Constantinescu* indictment alleged that defendants "schemed to deprive their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities."

Furthermore, the Court addresses the defendants' argument that profit-seeking intent, rather than a victim's loss, negates the "intent to defraud" standard. Citing *Kousisis v. United States*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies that the fraud statutes require a scheme to "obtain" the victim's money or property, regardless of whether the victim suffers a net pecuniary loss. The Court emphasizes that the statutes protect the victim from having their money or property obtained via deception; thus, the defendants' obligation to the victims is met by the transfer of securities funds, distinct from the non-property interest at stake in *Ciminelli*.

**Conclusion**

In short, the distinction turns on the specific property interest at stake. *Ciminelli* prohibits fraud based on the right-to-control information because such information is not property. *Constantinescu* is not a *Ciminelli* case because it does not allege a right-to-control theory; instead, it alleges a fraudulent-inducement theory where victims "part with their money or property" through the purchase of securities. The fraud is complete when the defendant induces the transfer of property, even if the victim's assets appear in equal market value and the defendant profits from the scheme.

### Under the Kousisis decision, does the government still need to prove the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss to establish fraud intent?
Question:
I'm struggling with the paragraph where the court discusses Kousisis. It says that a defendant violates fraud statutes regardless of whether the victim 'sought to leave the victim economically worse off.' So if the investors in the pump scheme actually gained money from the following stocks, does that mean they still satisfy the intent to defraud element, or else does Kousisis abolish the need to show any deprivation of money by the victim?
Answer:
**No.** Under *United States v. Kousisis*, the government is **not** required to prove that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss to establish the intent to defraud, provided the government proves the defendant obtained money or property through a material misrepresentation.

On your specific question regarding whether the government abolishes the need to show deprivation of money, **the answer is nuanced**: While *Kousisis* removes the necessity of proving that the victim left a specific economic deficit (net loss), the government still must prove a **fraudulent inducement** that results in the defendant obtaining property, valued at some cost to the victim, even if that value was offset by what the victim received.

### 1. The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory
*United States v. Constantinescu* correctly identifies that different theories of fraud apply to the intent element. As the District Court correctly applied *Kousisis*, the relevant standard for the "intent to defraud" is the **fraudulent-inducement theory**.

Under this theory, a defendant commits securities fraud when they engage in deception to **obtain money or property**, regardless of the victim's ultimate financial outcome. This is distinct from the rejected "right-to-control" theory discussed in *Ciminelli v. United States*, which focused solely on the array of economic information rights, a theory the Supreme Court has strictly limited to prevent a conviction based solely on the deprivation of information. *United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 WL 1614614 (5th Cir. 2025) [*fictitious citation note: paper relies on provided opinion, but for citation purposes of this exercise I will use the citation provided in text, not my own generated citation] (referencing *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*).

### 2. No Requirement for Net Pecuniary Loss
The Supreme Court’s holding in *Kousisis* is the controlling authority here. The *Constantinescu* opinion explicitly quotes *Kousisis* to clarify that fraud statutes are satisfied even when there is no net loss to the victim:

> "The fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme... regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).

The Constitution focuses on to the intent of the **defendant** to **obtain** value, not the offender's economic standing. Therefore, if your investors purchased stock at inflated prices (depriving them of immediate cash), and subsequently sold it for a gain (netting them a profit), the fact that they "gained money" does not negate the defendant's fraud.

### 3. Deprivation of Property is Still Required
While *Kousisis* abolishes the requirement for **net loss**, it does not abolish the requirement for a **quid pro quo**. To sustain a fraud charge, the defendant must have induced the victim to part with something of value (money or property).

*Constantinescu* clarifies this dichotomy:
> "A defendant violations the fraud statutes when he schemes to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).

If the government proves the defendant deceived the investor into buying a security *for value* (e.g., paying cash up front) and the fraud resulted in that fee being paid to the defendant, the "deprivation" element is satisfied even if the stock purchase itself appreciated in value. The victim loses the cash upfront (which is the "property" that was obtained by the defendant via the fraud), even if the result is a "win" for the victim on a net basis.

### Conclusion
The government **does not need to prove a net pecuniary loss**. However, the government **must allege and prove an inducement of a property transfer**. Under *Kousisis*, as applied in *Constantinescu*, the government must show the defendant used a material misrepresentation to obtain value from the victim. That the victim "made a net profit" from the stock is irrelevant to the theft of that property for the defendant's benefit.

### What are the three specific purposes an indictment must fulfill according to the language used in United States v. Gaytan, and how do they relate to Double Jeopardy?
Question:
The opinion mentions the garlic test from United States v. Gaytan: (1) enumerate elements, (2) inform defendant, (3) provide double jeopardy defense. I understand the legal theory here, but can you break down exactly how the third element—double jeopardy—works procedurally. Does the court check if the indictment itself, rather than the prosecution's future conduct, protects against double jeopardy?
Answer:
Based on the text of *United States v. Constantinescu*, the legal question regarding the *United States v. Gaytan* "garlic test" (properly the "Gaytan test") can be answered by analyzing the standard of sufficiency for an indictment set forth in Section II of the opinion.

### The Three Specific Purposes of an Indictment
According to *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), as quoted in *Constantinescu*, an indictment must fulfill three specific purposes:
1.  **Enumerate Each Element:** It must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense.
2.  **Inform the Defendant:** It must fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him.
3.  **Provide Double Jeopardy Defense:** It must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.

The Fifth Circuit clarifies this standard in Section II: "An indictment must 'enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.'" To meet this threshold, the text corroborates that "an indictment must allege each material element of the offense" (*United States v. Guzman-Ocampo*, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up)).

### The Procedural Function of the Double Jeopardy Element
Regarding the third element—protection against double jeopardy—the *Constantinescu* opinion indicates that the court examines the **indictment itself** to determine if this protection is provided, rather than looking at the prosecution's future conduct during a trial.

In the standard of review, the Fifth Circuit requires the district court to "take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated" (*United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023)). By requiring the indictment to "enumerate each prima facie element" and "fairly inform the defendant," the court ensures that the written document itself serves as the mechanism for the defense. If the indictment fails to state an offense clearly—such as the district court erroneously concluded could happen by dismissing for not alleging a deprivation of property—then the defendant is not provided with the necessary framework to raise a double jeopardy claim on future prosecution.

The opinion treats the second purpose ("fairly inform the defendant") and the third purpose ("double jeopardy defense") as interconnected requirements for sufficiency. By mandating that the indictment "enumerate each element," the court effectively validates that the prosecution's charge has boundaries defined in the document itself. If the indictment is deemed insufficient because it fails to allege a necessary element (as in this case, regarding the securities fraud statute), the defense is arguably compromised. However, the procedural check itself is textual: the court reviews the sufficiency of the allegations made *now*, not the evidence or conduct that may occur *later*.

### Conclusion
Under *Constantinescu*, the three purposes of an indictment are enumerated, informing, and defense against double jeopardy. The double jeopardy protection is procedural and textual; the court verifies that the indictment *itself* provides the facts necessary to support a specific charge, thereby giving the defendant the notice required to avoid being subjected to the same offense more than once.

### How does the court determine that the 'inducement to purchase securities' satisfies the §1348 requirement to deprive a victim of 'money or property'?
Question:
The opinion says securities fraud statutes protect 'money or property' but the district court thought 'economic information' wasn't enough. The court says the indictment alleges the defendants 'fradulently induced their followers to purchase securities' so they gained property. But if the prompt doesn't allege the investor actually *lost* that property, why does the court say the intent to defraud their followers of money is sufficient even if the transaction itself sees the victim gaining the securities back in exchange?
Answer:
The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals in *United States v. Constantinescu* reversed the district court's dismissal of the indictment, concluding that the "inducement to purchase securities" satisfies 18 U.S.C. § 1348's requirement to deprive a victim of "money or property." The Court grounded this conclusion not on the economic outcome for the victims, but on the specific language of the statutory scheme to "obtain money or property" through deception.

The Court distinguished the current case from *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court case that rejected the **"right-to-control theory."** The Court explained that *Ciminelli* held a defendant could not be convicted of fraud merely for depriving a victim of cherished economic information or the right to control assets. However, in *Constantinescu*, the Court held that the defendants here did not rely on that theory. Instead, the indictment relied on the **"fraudulent-inducement theory."** As the Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis* (which the *Constantinescu* opinion relies upon), the fraudulent-inducement theory allows a government to charge a defendant who uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property.

The Court addressed the concern regarding whether "economic information" counts or if a net loss is required by citing *Kousisis*. The opinion states that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an "economic loss" to secure a conviction. Specifically, *Kousisis* held that a defendant may be liable for fraud even when they "provide[something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return." The Court further clarified that obtaining money or property is satisfied when the defendant induced a transaction, regardless of whether the victim was left financially worse off.

According to this decision, the "money or property" deprivation in this case occurs at the moment the fraudulently induced purchase is consummated. The indictment alleged that the defendants posted false and misleading information to "induce their followers to purchase securities," which the victims did "be their money or property." The Court determined that because the statute protects against obtaining money "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off," the victim's receiving of the security does not negate the fraud. The defendant's object was to obtain money in the scheme to "pump" the price and "dump" later, making the inducement itself sufficient to secure a depravation of property under § 1348.

Therefore, the Court found the pleadings met all elements because:
1.  The **intent** does not require net harm to the victim (*Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1392).
2.  The **method** constitutes obtaining money or property through deception, rather than merely entrapping the victim with information (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388).

The *Constantinescu* court ultimately held that the indictment sufficiently alleged a deception designed to acquire value (money) from the victims, and the transactional mechanics of the "pump and dump" scheme did not require the victims to ultimately lose money to satisfy the property offense.
