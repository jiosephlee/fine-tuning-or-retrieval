Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 1: # Procedural Posture and Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency

## Facts of the Case: The 'Pump and Dump' Social Media Securities Fraud Scheme

This chapter examines the appellate court's analysis regarding the sufficiency of an indictment in the Fifth Circuit. The underlying case involves defendants who engaged in a scheme known as "pump and dump" securities fraud. The government alleged that Edward Constantinescu and other defendants used social media followings to post misrepresentations about securities trading positions and potential prices, thereby artificially inflating share prices. The District Court then processed the indictment and dismissed it, concluding that the defendants sought to deprive followers of economic information rather than a traditional property interest. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed the dismissal, holding that the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud under the relevant securities statutes. Understanding this procedural posture is essential for interpreting the court's reasoning, as the sufficiency of an indictment is a threshold determination that precedes any analysis of the underlying fraudulent conduct.

## District Court Dismissal: Rationale regarding Lack of Traditional Property Interest

The district court granted the defendants' motion to dismiss, reasoning that the indictment failed to state an offense because it lacked an allegation of a traditional property interest. Under the theory relied upon by the district court, the fraud statutes protect property rights, and the government argued that protecting intellectual property or information is not covered. Specifically, the district court reasoned that merely depriving victims of valuable economic information—rather than actual assets or money—is insufficient to state a crime. This distinction was critical because it determined not just the merits of the case, but whether the indictment itself met the statutory requirements of criminal procedure. The appeal turned squarely on this legal question: did the government's allegations of inducing followers to purchase securities constitute a deprivation of property, or only the deprivation of information?

## Standard of Review: De Novo Sufficiency Analysis (Citing *United States v. Rafoi* and *Fontenot*)

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. Under this standard, the appellate court reviews the indictment from the beginning without regard to whether the evidence supports the facts alleged. As the court explained, "the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated." Id. at 60 F.4th at 993, quoting *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011). This instruction undercuts the burden placed on the court to question the truthfulness of the statements in the indictment. Instead, the court assumes a prima facie factual basis for the charges and examines whether the text of the indictment violates the requirements for sufficiency. This standard protects defendants by ensuring that they receive a fair notice of the charges against them, even though they need not have provided the evidence at the pretrial stage.

## Three-Part Indictment Sufficiency Requirement: Enumerating Elements, Informing Defendant, and Double Jeopardy Defense

Under the law, an indictment must satisfy three distinct requirements to be considered sufficient. First, the indictment must "enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense." Id. quoting *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Second, the indictment must "fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him." Id. Third, it must "provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." *Guzman-Ocampo*, 236 F.3d at 236 (quotation cleaned up). The Fifth Circuit has established that "to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense." In the context of this case, the indictment alleged the scheme to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Therefore, the analysis hinges on whether the text alleges those elements.

## The Primacy of Allegations: The General Rule for Judicial Review of Indictments

The general rule for judicial review, derived from the de novo standard, emphasizes that the court focuses on the allegations presented in the indictment, not the underlying evidence of the officers' conduct. This rule ensures that the defendant is protected by the promise of fair notice. In this case, the court concluded that the allegations in the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme and intent to defraud. The text of the indictment stated that defendants posted false and misleading information so other investors were induced to purchase the security. This text satisfied the requirement of a material misstatement intended to deceive and to cause harm. Because the indictment contained the required elements, the court treated the allegations as factual for the purpose of the sufficiency inquiry, reversing the dismissal and remanding for proceedings consistent with the opinion.
