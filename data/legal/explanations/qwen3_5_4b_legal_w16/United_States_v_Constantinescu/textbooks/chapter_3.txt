Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 3: # Analysis: The 'Scheme to Defraud' and the Requirement of a Property Interest

## This chapter analyzes the first major issue appealing the indictment: the distinction between depriving a victim of information versus depriving them of property, and how *Ciminelli* was applied (or distinguished) in this context.

## The *Ciminelli* Rule and the Right-to-Control Theory: Fraud Without Property
To begin reading an indictment for securities fraud, a lawyer must understand the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Court addressed a claim that a construction company paid a lobbyist to obtain state-funded jobs solely to leverage the value of the lobbyist's contacts and economic information. The government relied on the "right-to-control theory," arguing that the defendant's property was the victim's "right to control [his] assets." The Supreme Court held that this theory could not form the basis for a fraud conviction because "[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." This ruling establishes a bright-line rule for criminal fraud: a defendant cannot be convicted of fraud simply for depriving another of potentially valuable economic information alone. When analyzing an indictment, one must look for a deprivation of *property*, not just of *knowledge*. If the government's case rests on the argument that an individual lost valuable information, they must prove that the transaction itself involved a transfer of a recognized property interest to satisfy the Constitution's Double Jeopardy Clause and federal fraud statutes.

## Distinguishing This Case from *Ciminelli*: Fraudulent-Inducement Theory vs. Right-to-Control Theory
The government's strategy in *Constantinescu* differs fundamentally from *Ciminelli* because it relies on the "fraudulent-inducement theory" instead of the "right-to-control theory." As clarified in the companion decision *Kousisis v. United States*, the fraudulent-inducement theory allows for a conviction when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." Unlike the *Ciminelli* right-to-control approach, which treats information as the commodity at stake, fraudulent inducement treats the information as a weapon to trick a victim into a transaction involving actual property. The Supreme Court explicitly held that this fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." This is a critical distinction for the reader: while *Ciminelli* rejected the idea that holding or withholding information alone can be fraud, *Kousisis* confirms that using false information to secure a transaction where money or property changes hands triggers liability. This distinction saves the *Constantinescu* indictment from being dismissed.

## Application to 'Pump and Dump' Facts: Forcing a Follow to Purchase Merchandise (Property)
In applying these legal theories to the specific facts of *Constantinescu*, the District Court incorrectly concluded that the prosecution could not traverse a清白 violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 because the defendants had not deprived individuals of property. However, the indictment alleges that defendants purchased a security first, then posted false information to make that security more attractive to other investors, inducing them to "part with their money or property" to acquire that security. The operation described is not purely informational; it is a direct mechanism for obtaining money. The indictment alleges that defendants induced their followers, through the false and misleading information, to purchase the security. Thus, the victims' property interest is the securities themselves, which they acquire by paying money. When an indictment alleges that a defendant used deception to induce a victim to enter into a financial transaction, the victim is no longer just deprived of information; the victim is parted with their money to secure the defendant's ill-gotten profit.

## Materiality of the Misrepresentation: Status of Economic Information vs. Securities
While the indictment alleges economic information drives the scheme, the key legal concern is the status of the item being obtained. The opinion notes that the Supreme Court rejected a theory where a victim loses the "right to control information" without suffering an economic loss. However, the indictment alleges transfers of "money or property" from the followers to the defendants. In this context, the securities are treated as commodities that change hands. The *Kousisis* decision further clarifies that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss of money must equal the defendant's gain. This means that from the prosecution's perspective, as long as the misrepresentation regarding the security's price (the economic information) caused the follower to part with money (the property interest), the requisite intent is satisfied. The "status" of the information does not negate the property interest of the securities being transferred.

## Conclusion of This Analysis: Why the Indictment Satisfies Ciminelli's Property Requirement
Ultimately, the Fifth Circuit concludes that the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, thereby reversing the district court's dismissal. The core reasoning is that the alleged "pump and dump" scheme involves the fraudulent inducement of a transaction. Because the scheme involves inducing investors to "part with their money or property" to purchase a security, the transaction is rooted in the deprivation of a property interest, not just the withholding of information. While the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* prohibited the "right-to-control theory" based on economic information, the fraudulent-inducement theory in *Kousisis* protects the transfer of actual money and property. Because *Constantinescu* fits within the narrower scope of fraudulent inducement—where deception secures a transfer of value rather than mere access to information—the indictment remains sufficient under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 1349.
