Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Statutory Framework and Substantive Elements of Securities Fraud

This chapter outlines the legal framework underpinning the fraud charges found in *United States v. Constantinescu*, a Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals opinion addressing the sufficiency of an indictment. It focuses on the statutory basis of securities fraud charges and the substantive elements required to sustain such convictions. As read in this opinion, the court’s analysis relies heavily on the definition of what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" and an "'intent to defraud' under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. 

## 18 U.S.C. § 1348: Scheme to Deprive vs. Other Fraudulent Schemes

Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, a defendant may be convicted of securities fraud if they engage in a scheme to defraud any person in connection with a security, or a scheme to obtain money or property through false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. In *Constantinescu*, the government charged the defendants with conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and securities fraud under §§ 1348 and 2. The district court dismissed the indictment, ruling that it failed to allege a specific property interest was deprived. The government successfully argued that the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to deprive followers of money, a traditional property interest. The difference between the two theories is critical: one theory (right-to-control) would focus on depriving the victim of valuable economic information alone, while the other (fraudulent-inducement) focuses on tricking the victim into handing over actual money or property. The district court correctly interpreted the requirement for "a scheme to deprive," but incorrectly excluding the policy interest of informing victims of their potential loss. The court held that an indictment is sufficient if it alleges that defendants schemed to deprive victims of money or property, distinguishing this from the *Ciminelli* right-to-control ruling.

## Governing Legal Concepts: Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes (*United States v. Greenlaw*)

The Fifth Circuit interprets the securities fraud statute in conjunction with established mail and wire fraud concepts. As noted in *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023), the circuits have "borrowed key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes" and "given the terms similar treatment." Because there is scant caselaw construing securities fraud in this circuit specifically, the Court relies on mail and wire fraud precedents to define the statutory elements. This ensures that the terms "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" are interpreted consistently across fraud statutes. In *Constantinescu*, the Court applied these borrowed concepts to determine whether the language of the indictment met the threshold for sufficiency.

## Defining the 'Scheme to Defraud': Material Misrepresentation and Intent to Deceive

To sustain an indictment for securities fraud, the government must allege each material element of the charge. The "scheme to defraud" element requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value. In *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018), the Fifth Circuit defined this requirement: a "scheme to defraud" exists if a defendant used a material misstatement to deceive others in order to obtain a value, such as money, from the entity to be deceived. In *Constantinescu*, the Court referenced this standard to evaluate the indictment's allegations regarding the defendants' purchases of securities through misrepresentations. The Court determined that the indictment sufficiently alleged that defendants engaged in deception and obtained the victim's money or property, thereby meeting the "scheme to defraud" element.

## Defining the 'Intent to Defraud': Deception and Cause of Harm

The "intent to defraud" element is distinct from the scheme element. It requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." As established in *Evans*, a fraud statute is complete only when the defendant has an intent to deceive and cause harm. However, the defense in *Constantinescu* argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to harm victims, noting instead that the defendants intended to profit. The Court rejected this argument. It must be noted that while the indictment alleges an intent to profit, the Supreme Court has clarified in *Kousisis* that the statutes do not require the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss. Therefore, the distinction between seeking to profit and seeking to harm is legally irrelevant when the intent is to deceive to gain value.

## Judicial Precedent Coherence: *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) Overview regarding Fraud Terminology

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), provided the doctrinal clarity necessary to resolve the sufficiency of the indictment. *Kousisis* clarified the terminology regarding "fraudulent-inducement" versus other fraudulent theories. The Court held that a defendant violates fraud statutes when they use a "material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." This confirms that the "fraudulent-inducement theory" protects money and property and does not rely on the right-to-control theory rejection in *Ciminelli*. In *Constantinescu*, the Court integrated *Kousisis* to find that the indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped" securities to gain a profit was sufficient to allege an intent to defraud. The offense is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. Consequently, the indictment sufficiently alleged the necessary elements for the offense.
