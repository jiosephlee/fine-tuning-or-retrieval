Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # Disposition of the Appeal and Implications

## The Sufficiency of the Indictment: Why the Appellate Court Rejected the Dismissal

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit focuses its analysis on whether the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu* met the constitutional threshold for sufficiency. Under the standard established by this court, an indictment must adequately set forth each element of the charged offense while shielding the defendant from double jeopardy. When the district court dismissed the indictment, it ruled that the government failed to state an offense by simply alleging that defendants spent their followers' time by depriving them of economic information. The appellate court clarifies that this dismissal was legally incorrect because courts must look at the totality of the allegations, determining if a scheme and intent to defraud were properly alleged. Section III of the opinion demonstrates that the government’s charge went beyond the mere deprivation of information to include specific actions that involve the protection of traditional property interests.

The court recognizes that a common error in prosecutorial theory involves capitalizing the distinction between the loss of value and the loss of control. While the Second Circuits often reject the deprivation of economic information as a standalone tort without a property interest foundation, the indictment here is not rooted in a claim of *right-to-control*. Instead, the indictment alleges that the defendants posted false and misleading information to induce followers to purchase securities. By facilitating a transaction where the followers traded money for securities, the defendants operated under a "fraudulent-inducement" framework. This framework is distinct from the rejected "right-to-control" theory, which suggests that property is merely an abstract right to decision-making. The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that because the indictment alleges the induction of a victim into a transaction requiring the handing over of money or property, the element of a protected "property interest" is satisfied even if the value was recently received.

## Procedural Outcome: Reversal of Dismissal and Ruling for the Indictment to Continue

Based on the court’s application of the *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli* precedents, the Fifth Circuit issues a formal decision to reverse the lower court’s dismissal. The order explicitly states that the district court's handling of the case is inconsistent with the current legal standards regarding federal fraud statutes. Consequently, the case is vacated and remanded for new proceedings consistent with the court's opinion. This remedial order ensures that the charge advances to the jury stage with the correct understanding that the "pump and dump" tactic does not require proof of actual economic loss to the victim to support a fraud conviction.

The procedural implication of this reversal is significant for state and federal co-prosecutors. Defendants are now required to defend themselves against the charges as they stand, potentially facing trial rather than the dismissal that would have otherwise shielded them. The remand is not limited to retrial but extends to ensuring that the prosecution can demonstrate the necessary nexus between the deception and the acquisition of money. For the status of the indictment, the Fifth Circuit maintains that the count itself, though requiring enrichment without direct loss, remains legally viable provided the evidence at trial can show that the forgetfulness of the information directly facilitated the transfer of property. The final disposition rests on the appellate determination that the defendant's intent to profit was sufficient to negate the need to plead specific mutilated loss elements in the indictment.

## The Broad Significance: Updating *Ciminelli* Precedent on Fraud Prototypes

A key pedagogical takeaway from this opinion is how the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* context interprets the earlier *Ciminelli* holding on the prohibition of plotting fraud against property rights. In *Ciminelli*, the Court established that fraud statutes require the deprivation of a traditional property interest; merely depriving a victim of valuable information, without that information being treated as property under a right-to-control theory, is insufficient. *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies that this does not render all "information-based fraud" convictions impossible. The opinion explains that *Kousisis* distinguishes between the two recognized property theories. The first, right-to-control, lacks a property interest in the mere data. The second, fraudulent-inducement, involves a victim who is tricked into a transaction where property must be handed over.

This opinion effectively bridges the gap left by *Ciminelli* and preserves the integrity of the fraud statutes by validating the fraudulent-inducement theory in context. The Fifth Circuit teaches that when a defendant deceives a victim to vent money into the hands of a target, *Ciminelli*'s ban on information-only fraud should not excuse a conviction. The holding underscores that while the Court decided in *Ciminelli* that the right-to-control theory cannot serve as the sole basis for a fraud conviction, that did not ban the fraudulent-inducement theory. The indictment in *Constantinescu* did not rely on a fine-tuned claim that the investors' right to control their assets was the fraud, but rather that they were induced to buy assets, thus losing their right to *not* buy those assets, which was a property interest. The Fifth Circuit interprets this as a "separate" mechanism that was not repackaged in the *Ciminelli* restriction.

## Impact on Social Media Fraud Prosecutions: Validating the 'Pump and Dump' Charge Violation

The disposition of this appeal has substantive implications for the prosecution of "pump and dump" fraud schemes increasingly detected via social media platforms. Indictments in this area often face a threshold dismissal defense based on the argument that the schemes were limited to information dissemination rather than property transfer. By rejecting the dismissal of the *Constantinescu* indictment, the Fifth Circuit validates the applicability of the mail and wire fraud statutes to "pump and dump" activities where the criminal activity centers on inducing downstream transactions through misrepresentation.

The court notes that the defendants' posting of false and misleading information was a direct procurement solicitation to purchase securities. The indictment did not stop at the allegation that the defendants wanted the followers to *know* something; it alleged that the defendants wanted the followers to *buy* something. This distinction is critical because it shifts the legal burden from proving an information-based intent to proving a transactional intent. Prosecutors can now proceed under the theory that the transaction itself provides the property interest needed for a fraud charge. Given the millions profited and the volume of transactions, the Fifth Circuit reinforces that these schemes fall squarely within the scope of securities fraud statutes where the object of the fraud is the acquisition of money or property, not the preservation of an abstract economic interest.

## Closing Brief on Legal Logic: How the Opinion Resolves Conflicting Property vs. Information Theories

The legal logic employed in *Constantinescu* resolves the conflict between the protection of traditional property rights and the rejection of economic information alone. The core tension lies in whether a "property interest" can include the knowledge held by a victim versus the actual economic value received or withheld. The Fifth Circuit concludes that the fraud statutes protect the interest in the exchange of property, not the interest in the symmetry of information. Therefore, the distortion in information that caused the exchange of money is sufficient to establish the property interest.

The final legal holding is that the object of the fraud is the "money" obtained by the defendant, not the information deprived by the defendant. The opinion concludes that the receipt of securities by the followers still counts as property. Even though the defendants sold and profited, the indictment alleged that the victim supplied that money, which satisfies the "scheme to obtain money or property" element under *Kousisis*. The court explicitly states that the defendant's intent to profit is sufficient to satisfy the intent to defraud, even when the victim suffers no net loss in comparison. This closes the loophole for defendants to argue that because they enriched themselves without causing a financial deficiency, there was no fraud. The opinion solidifies that obtaining property by deception is a crime, regardless of the victim's net position, aligning with the broad statutory language of U.S.C. § 1348.
