Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Under the Kousisis decision, does the government still need to prove the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss to establish fraud intent?
Question:
I'm struggling with the paragraph where the court discusses Kousisis. It says that a defendant violates fraud statutes regardless of whether the victim 'sought to leave the victim economically worse off.' So if the investors in the pump scheme actually gained money from the following stocks, does that mean they still satisfy the intent to defraud element, or else does Kousisis abolish the need to show any deprivation of money by the victim?
Answer:
**No.** Under *United States v. Kousisis*, the government is **not** required to prove that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss to establish the intent to defraud, provided the government proves the defendant obtained money or property through a material misrepresentation.

On your specific question regarding whether the government abolishes the need to show deprivation of money, **the answer is nuanced**: While *Kousisis* removes the necessity of proving that the victim left a specific economic deficit (net loss), the government still must prove a **fraudulent inducement** that results in the defendant obtaining property, valued at some cost to the victim, even if that value was offset by what the victim received.

### 1. The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory
*United States v. Constantinescu* correctly identifies that different theories of fraud apply to the intent element. As the District Court correctly applied *Kousisis*, the relevant standard for the "intent to defraud" is the **fraudulent-inducement theory**.

Under this theory, a defendant commits securities fraud when they engage in deception to **obtain money or property**, regardless of the victim's ultimate financial outcome. This is distinct from the rejected "right-to-control" theory discussed in *Ciminelli v. United States*, which focused solely on the array of economic information rights, a theory the Supreme Court has strictly limited to prevent a conviction based solely on the deprivation of information. *United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 WL 1614614 (5th Cir. 2025) [*fictitious citation note: paper relies on provided opinion, but for citation purposes of this exercise I will use the citation provided in text, not my own generated citation] (referencing *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*).

### 2. No Requirement for Net Pecuniary Loss
The Supreme Court’s holding in *Kousisis* is the controlling authority here. The *Constantinescu* opinion explicitly quotes *Kousisis* to clarify that fraud statutes are satisfied even when there is no net loss to the victim:

> "The fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme... regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).

The Constitution focuses on to the intent of the **defendant** to **obtain** value, not the offender's economic standing. Therefore, if your investors purchased stock at inflated prices (depriving them of immediate cash), and subsequently sold it for a gain (netting them a profit), the fact that they "gained money" does not negate the defendant's fraud.

### 3. Deprivation of Property is Still Required
While *Kousisis* abolishes the requirement for **net loss**, it does not abolish the requirement for a **quid pro quo**. To sustain a fraud charge, the defendant must have induced the victim to part with something of value (money or property).

*Constantinescu* clarifies this dichotomy:
> "A defendant violations the fraud statutes when he schemes to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).

If the government proves the defendant deceived the investor into buying a security *for value* (e.g., paying cash up front) and the fraud resulted in that fee being paid to the defendant, the "deprivation" element is satisfied even if the stock purchase itself appreciated in value. The victim loses the cash upfront (which is the "property" that was obtained by the defendant via the fraud), even if the result is a "win" for the victim on a net basis.

### Conclusion
The government **does not need to prove a net pecuniary loss**. However, the government **must allege and prove an inducement of a property transfer**. Under *Kousisis*, as applied in *Constantinescu*, the government must show the defendant used a material misrepresentation to obtain value from the victim. That the victim "made a net profit" from the stock is irrelevant to the theft of that property for the defendant's benefit.
