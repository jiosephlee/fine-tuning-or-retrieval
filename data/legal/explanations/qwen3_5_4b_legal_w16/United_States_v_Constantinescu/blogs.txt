Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# The Information Predicament: Why *Ciminelli*’s Property Rule Didn’t Defeat *Constantinescu*

The intersection of digital media and securities fraud has recently become a flashpoint in federal criminal law, particularly in light of the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*. While those rulings were intended to tighten the pleading requirements for fraud charges to protect the Rule against convictions based solely on the deprivation of information, prosecutors and courts have long grappled with how to structure "pump and dump" schemes on social media without running afoul of *Ciminelli*’s strict property interest requirements. The Fifth Circuit’s resolution in *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies a crucial distinction: prosecutors do not need to prove an information-based injury where the *Ciminelli* right-to-control theory faces scrutiny, as long as the complaint specifically alleges a transaction involving the actual deprivation of property under a fraudulent-inducement theory.

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, defendants with large social media followings were indicted for conspiring to pump and dump securities via false and misleading posts. The district court found that the indictment failed to state an offense because the government alleged only that the defendants wanted to deprive followers of valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. Relying on the Fifth Circuit’s precedent that the fraud statutes protect only property, the district judge’d dismissed the indictment on the grounds that the information deprivation alone did not suffice.

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reversed. Judge Kurt Engelhardt’s opinion demonstrates that while the Supreme Court’s right-to-control theory remains sound, it does not bar all fraud prosecutions involving valuable economic information. Instead, the key is the mechanism of the deception. The indictment charged that the defendants induced investors to purchase securities based on misrepresentations. Under this framework, the money changed hands. By depositing funds and signing securities, the victims parted with their property. The Fifth Circuit correctly noted that this fits the "fraudulent-inducement theory" rather than the "right-to-control theory."

The *Ciminelli* Supreme Court majority determined that a fraud conviction couldn't hinge on the desire to control or delimit the right to make discretionary economic decisions solely by holding information. But the Court emphasized that fraudulent-inducement—tricking a victim into handing over money or property—is perfectly permissible. The Indictment here alleged that defendants posted false information to induce purchasing which resulted in the transfer of assets from the followers to the defendants. The appeal acknowledged that the *Ciminelli* same-day ruling was rejected because the municipal right to control information isn't property, the new holdings on inducement require no loss of money.

Furthermore, the *Kousisis* Supreme Court 2025 decision bolsters this position as the Fifth Circuit held that a fraud charge is sufficient even if the victim ends up no worse off than before. The indictment did not claim that the investors lost money, only that they paid money they shouldn't have. This distinction is vital because it avoids the "mirror image requirement" that often plagues financial fraud litigations. The Fifth Circuit found that the Supreme Court’s holding—that a defendant may be liable even when the fraudster provides an item of equivalent value in return—applied here, because the victims received securities and the defendants received money. The prosecutors did not need to prove the investors were dumber than the defendants.

**Doctrinal Implications for SEC and DOJ**
This ruling likely offers a beacon of hope for prosecutors targeting modern digital markets. When it was announced that "information only" frauds couldn't be prosecuted under the *Ciminelli* right-to-control bar, many grappled with how to target social media investors manipulating crypto or penny stocks. *Constantinescu* suggests that the crucial element is the transaction, not just the information. A successful case does not rest on the theory that the victim gained less knowledge than they initially thought. Rather, the case must focus on the victim's initial transfer of assets. This ensures that the statute's protection against "information-only" loss remains intact, but it preserves the ability to target those who incur a transaction cost.

This decision effectively cements the "fraudulent-inducement theory" as the dominant framework for social media stock manipulation cases. It aligns with the broader trend in Supreme Court decisions limiting fraud claims to those where property rights have been violated. However, the decision also clarifies that the lack of "harm"—the investor's financial loss—was never the entire bar. The Fifth Circuit implicitly noted that the harm lies in the transaction itself, not the net outcome.

**The Bottom Line**
*Constantinescu* serves as a reminder to prosecutors that they must tailor their arguments to the specific theory the victim holds. If the government cannot prove the "right-to-control" theory, they should pivot to the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. This distinction protects the defendant's property rights while giving the state a valid narrative regarding the transaction. For investors, the decision reinforces that information alone isn't the enemy; property transfer is. For the courts, it sets a precedent that the government has ample opportunity to prove that the securities were transferred under the deception, regardless of whether the fraudster or victim ends up with a financial loss.



# Profit Over Loss: How *Kousisis* Redefined Fraudulent Intent in Federal Indictments

## The *Ciminelli* Tax

The June 2025 Supreme Court decision in *Kousisis v. United States* effectively stripped prosecutors of one of their most common barriers to entry in fraud cases: the necessity of proving victim loss. *Kousisis* established that under the fraud statutes, a defendant violates the law when they schemed to “obtain” the victim’s money or property through deception, regardless of whether the victim ended up richer or poorer as a result. This autonomous shift in the defendant’s required intent has created a litigation landscape waiting in its wake, as illustrated by the Fifth Circuit’s October 2025 reversal in *United States v. Constantinescu*. In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit dealt with a prosecution that pivoted on social media activity flagged as a “pump and dump” scheme. The district court previously narrowly dismissed the indictment based on the franchise — a decision now overturned — which painted the federal fraud statutes as too narrow to capture criminal conduct that did not cause the victim money damage directly.

The district court focused on what is now known as the "right-to-control" theory of fraud. Under that theory, the fraudster deprives the victim of the "right to exercise discretion" over market value. *Constantinescu* employed this logic to strike down the charge, arguing that social media posts that alter market price amounted to a deprivation of information rather than money or property. Following *Ciminelli* (2025), the court reasoned that the "deprivation" of economic information alone is not a protected property interest. This was a significant win for defendants in class-action or whistleblower contexts, but the Fifth Circuit saw its end when the case was remanded to the panel. The court clarified that the plaintiffs’ documents did not allege a right-to-control theory; rather, they alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory. By alleging that defendants used false premises to induce followers to part with their own money for securities, the government satisfied the statutory requirement without needing to prove the investors had no money to begin with.

The result is a fundamental distinction between the two theories. The district court treated the loss of potential investment information as the equivalent of the general rule: you must take something of value in return for the fraud. However, the Fifth Circuit correctly identified that the right-to-control theory applies only when the victim's interest is the *information itself*, not a transactional exchange. The indictment in *Constantinescu* alleged that the defendants used false information to trick followers into purchasing securities. That transaction is a classic "fraudulent-inducement." Even if the follower didn't lose money because they bought a stock at fair market value, the intent was still to extract value via deception. The critical point here is the *Kousisis* precedent that the *intent* to profit must align with the *intent* to obtain something of value, creating a much tighter legal basis for conviction than previously thought.

## Inducing the Deed: Induced Fraud vs. Injured Assets

The verdant appeal for the prosecution in *Constantinescu* rests heavily on the rejection of the "clean profit" defense. The indictment alleged that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities by posting false information, but the defendants argued that they intended to profit from this scheme, not hurt their followers. They contended that without evidence of victim loss — or at least a decision to inject net negative value into their account — the "intent to defraud" element was missing. This hinges on the distinction between the act of poisoning the market and the outcome of the transaction. In the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, the mere result of a transaction does not negate the fraud, provided the transaction itself was based on a material misrepresentation.

This legal logic flips the traditional understanding of securities fraud, which often assumes a direct harm to the investor. In a pump-and-dump scenario, the follower buys a stock because they believe it will rise, and the public is misled. The attacker profits by selling the stock later at high prices, but the follower might still profit if the market rises. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that the *constancy* of the *intent to deflate* is the key violation of the federal schemes protection, not the *consequence* of the *transaction*. The law protects the act of obtaining value without sufficient capacity to trust the transaction. When the government alleges that the defendant used false pretenses to induce the victim to buy, the transaction was meant to transfer the victim's capital interest to the fraudster's benefit.

This also highlights the importance of the proper theory. If the prosecution proceeds under the right-to-control theory, the defect is that the information itself has no property right. But if the prosecution proceeds under the fraudulent-inducement theory, the property interest is the securities money itself. The Fifth Circuit rejected the defense's claim that the victims were not economically worse off, noting that the government does not need to allege that the victim lost money net. As long as the transaction was obtained by deception and resulted in a transfer of property, the intent requirement is met. This was a crucial breakdown of the defense argument that "profit motive" implies harm to the victim. The prosecution's approach is that the fraudster *obtains* the property, regardless of whether the victim *drains* the property.

## The *Kousisis* Shield Against Profit-Motive

The most significant doctrinal shift in *Constantinescu* revolves around the Supreme Court's *Kousisis* ruling regarding the economic loss element. The *Kousisis* court explicitly stated that a defendant may be liable for a fraud conviction when the victim receives an equal value in return. The *Kousisis* ruling validates the idea that the "profit motive" does not negate the "intent to defraud." In *Constantinescu*, the court relied on *Kousisis* to explain that the indictment alleged a scheme to take money, but did not require the indictment to allege a scheme to harm the victims bottom line. This provides the Fifth Circuit with a bright-line rule: prosecution of fraud need not establish net economic loss to the victim. The prosecution only needs to establish that the victim gave up property and the defendant obtained it, under false pretenses.

This is a massive step for federal prosecutors, particularly in SEC-enforcement friendly criminal cases. The "Clean Profit" defense must be met with careful scrutiny of the indictment and evidence. The prosecution can point to the *Kousisis* Supreme Court decision to dismantle the "intent to harm" defense. The Fifth Circuit's reasoning reinforces that if the fraudster intended to obtain value and deceived the recipient to do so, they were covered. Even if the defendant intended to profit, this is not separate from the intent to defraud; to profit is to take property. This makes the "intent to defraud" element easier to show in practice, meaning more "clean profit" cases can be avoided by the government in future indictments, provided the "inducement" theory holds. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion also signals that this will be the hot topic for the Supreme Court to review as well, but for now, this sets a standard of liability for "fraudulent-inducement" schemes in the Fifth Circuit without requiring a detailed accounting of every transaction's equity impact.

## What This Means for the Doctrinal Landscape

The practical implication of *Constantinescu* is that the distinction between the right-to-control theory and the fraudulent-inducement theory is now a matter of indictment drafting rather than immutable defense law. If the defense can credibly argue "we didn't take your property, we just gave you this information," the prosecution must attack the theory to show the property transfer was fraudulent-inducement. This shifts the burden toward proving the transactional element of the contract was fraudulent. For social media promoters, this means that an indictment alleging the explicit use of a performance scheme to induce purchase, without a right-to-control theory, is sufficient evidence. However, a defense that does not show intent to take property fails. The Fifth Circuit also signals that future indictments should avoid the right-to-control language entirely. Prosecutors must focus on the *inducement* of the transaction, not the *value* of the information. This removes the need for privacy in the case law and places the focus squarely on the transaction.

Ultimately, *Constantinescu* ensures that the 2025 *Kousisis* ruling does not simply become a theoretical decision, but a practical defense for federal indictments. The "profit vs. harm" debate ensures that the law is flexible, but the "intent to obtain" means it is not. The financial incentives to make these arguments into the next round of litigation will likely continue. This decision will likely see more cases filed where the defendant is sued for fraud with a focus on the "intent to deceive" rather than the "loss to the victim." The Fifth Circuit has created a pathway for this, where the indictment is sufficient on its face to prosecute defendants for profit-making schemes without disproving economic loss. This heralds a broader era of securities fraud prosecutions that rely on the "induced transaction" theory.



# Influencers in Flight: *Constantinescu*'s Impact on Digital Pump-and-Dump Prosecutions

The intersection of social media influence and securities regulation has become a frequent flashpoint in American criminal law, yet *United States v. Constantinescu* brings nuanced precision to the equation. The Fifth Circuit's decision to reverse the dismissal of an indictment in this case moves beyond simple application of prior precedent to solidify the legal framework for prosecuting "pump and dump" schemes in the influencer economy. For the District Court, the case seemed to turn on a technicality involving property interests and information rights. For the Fifth Circuit, though, it was a question of statutory construction regarding what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" in the modern digital age. Emerging today (October 2025), the appellate ruling seals the door on the dismissal argument, affirming that capitalizing on social media manipulation to inflate stock prices before dumping them remains a viable and prosecutable offense.

## Distinguishing Information Rights from Property Rights

The central doctrinal hurdle in *Constantinescu* was the government's ability to allege intent without proving immediate economic loss to a victim. The District Court had dismissed the indictment on the theory that the defendants had merely deprived their audience of "valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit explicitly rejected the *Ciminelli* right-to-control theory as the basis for conviction in this instance, noting that *Ciminelli* prohibited convictions based solely on information deprivation. That is well-established. But the court found a clear distinction between preventing the *transference* of property and manipulating the *incentive structure* of that transfer. The indictment alleged a specific scheme to induce followers to purchase securities—that is, to move money from the follower's pocket to the defendant's account. *Kousisis* confirms that fraudulent诱导 (fraudulent inducement) obligates a defendant to secure some value for themselves without guaranteeing the loss to the other party.

This distinction is critical because it carves out a protected behavior space where the protection of information disappears when the fraud results in the actual procurement of assets through deception. The Fifth Circuit framed it as a fraudulent-inducement theory versus the right-to-control theory. Because the indictment alleges the defendants tricked victims into contracts that required handing over money, the deprivation of an informational right becomes secondary to the taking of property rights. This ensures that the statute does not become overly broad as a mechanism for punishing the withholding of information, but does not bypass the core requirement of economic exchange. Prosecutors must prove, or allege, that the misrepresentation was the means of inducing the exchange of value.

## The "Mirror Image" Requirement Dead

Perhaps the most consequential provision in this opinion is the rejection of any requirement that the victim's loss must mirror the defendant's gain. The District Court's initial dismissal rested heavily on the notion that because the defendants profited, there must be a direct loss to back it up. *Kousisis* had already raised the bar on this frontier, and the Fifth Circuit reinforces it. The court made clear that a scheme is complete once the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses. It does not matter if the victim ends up with market-value securities that are equal in effective value to what the defendant sought to extract, particularly in the context of a "pump and dump" where the price is artificially inflated.

From a doctrinal standpoint, this means the definition of harm is decoupled from the remedial outcome of clean pockets. This is a significant victory for the government's ability to address scams that operate with sophisticated accounting against the backdrop of algorithmic amplification. If a fraudster could claim immunity by simply proving they didn't cause a "net pecuniary loss," it would render the statute useless against high-yield manipulation schemes where profit is extracted from the public's capital, not just their knowledge. Proving the intent to obtain money is sufficient to support conviction, and the Fifth Circuit has codified that finding in a circuit that has historically struggled with securities fraud statutes.

## Implications for the Algorithmic Fraudster

The practical impact of this ruling is profound for the prosecution of social media influencers. By affirming that the sufficiency of the indictment stands despite the district court's dismissal, the Fifth Circuit signals that digital influencers are not a unique legal stratum where traditional fraud statutes don't apply simply because the victim received a "fair" share in the transaction. This sets the stage for a tightening of scrutiny in cases where trading signals are monetized through platform engagement. The algorithmic nature of the scheme does not absolve the fraudster of the need to deceive to initiate the transfer. This brings clarity to the prosecutorial landscape, stripping away the defense that the victim "didn't lose money, so the defendant didn't need to pay."

Looking ahead, the Fifth Circuit's reliance on *Rafoi* for the sufficiency standard suggests that districts will be cautious about early dismissals where conspiracies are complex and blend modern media with traditional financial fraud. The case also highlights that proving a specific property interest (money or securities) is the key syntactic element that changes the nature of the case from regulatory to indictable. Ultimately, *Constantinescu* ensures that the standards for the prosecution of retail investors remain robust and grounded in the principle that fraud is about the taking of value, not just the leaking of secrets.
