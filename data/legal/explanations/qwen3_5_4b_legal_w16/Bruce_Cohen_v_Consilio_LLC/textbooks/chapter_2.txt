Chapter 2: ### II. Statutory Analysis of State Law Penalties (MPWA and MWTA)

The central task in this chapter is to understand how the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit analyzed the Plaintiff's claims under Minnesota statute to determine eligibility for civil penalties. When a district court grants summary judgment, the Court of Appeals reviews the decision de novo, treating the lower court's ruling as pure error on law or fact with no deference to the lower court's view. This approach ensures that the appellate court clarifies the statutory intent of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and other relevant state laws.

## Statutory construction of MPWA § 181.101 and the Commissioner requirement for penalties

The Court began its statutory analysis by examining the text of section 181.101 of the MPWA. When interpreting a statute like the MPWA, the foundational canon of construction is that the language must be read literally and without presumption against the drafter. The Court argued that readers must look at the legislative text to embody the intent of the legislature. Section 181.101 is a plain provision that outlines a specific administrative process. It explicitly states that penalties are only obtained if the Commissioner first "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." At the beginning of the provision, the text refers to "the Commissioner" engaging with the employer rather than the aggrieved employee directly. The Court applied the principle from *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* that when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect. Therefore, without an explicit plea for penalty rights from the employee in the statute itself, the text does not support a private penalty claim through the payee.

## Distinction between Commissioner rights and employee rights regarding successive demands

To clarify the statutory limits on who can seek penalties, the Court looked to the entire act rather than the specific provision under claim. The distinction lies in whether the legislature extended an "employee demand" right to the Commissioner or the employee to pursue. Section 181.101 places the burden of demand and penalty collection on the Commissioner. There is a clear difference established elsewhere in the act which allows for different remedies depending on the context of the discharge or demand. For example, the Court cited evidence regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act, where the mandate changes depending on the relationship between the parties and the stage of demand. Some other sections of the act, however, allow for immediate penalties upon demand. The Court reasoned that the legislature had the power to grant employees the right to sue for penalties if it wanted to. Instead of granting that power in section 181.101, it limited the remedy to the Commissioner. This distinction creates an administrative precedent where the employee must first rely on the Commissioner to advance their claim for penalties, significantly altering how wage claims are litigated to ensure consistency in wage recovery methods.

## The conclusion that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under the MPWA

Because the Court found that the language of section 181.101 does not grant employees the right to recover average daily wage penalties independently, the lower court's dismissal of the claim was correct. The Court stated that it was against the interests of the legislature to allow the employee to seek penalties under this specific section because such penalties are payable to the Commissioner, not the employee. The statute provides that if the Commissioner collects funds from an employer, the Commissioner must return the money to the employee, indicating that the employee's right to funds is secondary to the Commissioner's enforcement power. This holding reinforces the summary judgment standard applied to the district court's decision. Consequently, the appellate court held that no dispute existed over the statutory eligibility for penalties in this MPWA claim. The Court affirmed that the employee in this situation could not bypass the statutory requirement of the Commissioner's demand process before seeking statutory penalties.

## Procedural waiver of the MWTA claim due to lack of meaningful argument

Turning to the Minnesota Wage Theft Act, the Court noted that the issue regarding statutory penalties had not been briefed properly. The standard for this phase of appellate review requires that the被上诉人's brief or pleadings be submitted for notice of the issue before the trial. Cohen argued that the district court should have addressed the MWTA claim, but he failed to provide a statutory argument allowing for civil penalties under this law. The Court applied the principle from *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, noted in the opinion that if the defendant’s brief does not support an assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue. Therefore, the court concluded that the court would not disturb the district court’s order dismissing this claim. The procedural posture required that because there was no meaningful argument in support of reversal and Cohen only pointed to no statutory authority, the court was obligated to treat it as waived and not reassume jurisdiction on that claim.

## Impact of legislative inaction or action on interpreting potential penalty statutes

In interpreting these statutes, the Court utilized the principle that the legislature can choose to give rights to the Commissioner rather than the aggrieved employee itself. This interpretation included reference to the legislative choice regarding the consequences of the statute. If the legislature wanted to give employees the right to penalties, it would have done so explicitly. The Court cited *Lagasse v. Horton*, explaining that the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so. For example, sections 181.13(a) and 181.14 allow certain employees to recover unpaid wages or commissions as well as a penalty upon discharge. This explicit statutory language appears coincidentally to provide penalties to the employee. The absence of this specific language in section 181.101 confirms that the decision to allow the Commissioner to seek penalties was a consistent and distinct choice by the legislature. This principle of legislative interpretation helps ensure restrictions like the MPWA penalties are applied to the specific text suggested by the legislature.

## Affirmance of MPWA Disposition

Following the analysis above, the Court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MPWA claim, specifically the denial of penalties to Cohen. The district court had correctly concluded that the statute mandates the Commissioner's involvement for penalties. The decision highlights the importance of statutory construction when the language of a statute is plain. It also reinforces the policy reason that employees must be compensated through the Commissioner's enforcement mechanism to maintain consistency in wage theft penalties across the state. The employee is entitled to the wages themselves, but the specific statutory remedy for penalties is reserved for the Commissioner under this act. This conclusion supports the need to rely on the Commissioner’s administrative powers when enforcing statutory wage payment laws within the state.

## Conclusion

The statutory analysis of MPWA and MWTA reveals the strict compliance requirements imposed by the Minnesota legislature on penalty claims. The Court distinguished between the agency and the employee, limiting the employee's ability to seek penalties for specific wage violations. The procedural waivers further emphasize the importance of aligning statutory arguments with legislative authority. These decisions ensure the proper enforcement of the MPWA and MWTA regarding wage recovery penalties, requiring the employee to seek wages without a penalty claim in this context. The Court vacated the district court's order regarding MFLSA, but in MPWA, the MPWA analysis stands.
