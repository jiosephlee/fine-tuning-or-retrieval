Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The "Commissioner-Only" Rule: Why Private Employees Can't Sue for MPWA Penalties

In the fast-evolving landscape of wage theft litigation, the boundary between private rights and public enforcement remains a critical legal frontier. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* reinked into this boundary by narrowly interpreting Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). While Cohen secured some relief through stipulated payments, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of claims for statutory average daily wage penalties, establishing a "commissioner-only" reading of the statute. This ruling is particularly consequential for class action plaintiffs in wage cases under state statutes mirroring federal law, as it signals that mere allegations of unpaid overtime may not suffice for punitive damages if the legislature did not explicitly authorize private plaintiffs to enforce them.

The crux of the MPWA decision rests on statutory construction. Minnesota’s MPWA generally allows for government enforcement or private suits seeking damages, but private statutes often include specific mechanisms for penalty recovery. In section § 181.101(a) of the MPWA, the court found no explicit authority for private parties to demand penalties from the employer directly. Cohen argued the plain language authorized penalties, but the court noted that the section required a demand be served by the Commissioner first. If payment is not made by the Commissioner upon that demand, the Commissioner may charge and collect wages and a penalty, with any collected funds then paid to the employee. This structure suggests that the private right to claim the penalty itself is limited to the Commissioner as the initial enforcer.

Further support for the "commissioner-only" construction came from the legislature’s own legislative history. The court highlighted that Minnesota Statute § 181.13, which applies to discharged employees, explicitly states the employee "may charge and collect a penalty." Similarly, § 181.14 provides a parallel right for employees who quit or resign. The court reasoned that because the legislature included specific language empowering employees to pursue penalties in those distinct contexts while excluding it here in § 181.101, it was clear this section was not intended to extend to private penalties. As the court noted, "the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so."

Beyond the MPWA holding, the decision contained a significant vacatur regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim, specifically concerning statutory penalties payable to the Commissioner. The Eighth Circuit found it lacked continuing jurisdiction to rule on these specific penalties. While Cohen received direct compensation for overtime and liquidated damages, the potential for penalties to serve a deterrent function was challenged on grounds of mootness. The court cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services* to emphasize that private plaintiffs generally seek penalties because they "redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury." However, if the defendant pays the plaintiff’s private damages and there is no uncertainty the wrongful conduct will recur, the case may become moot. The court vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine if the violative policy was ongoing or no longer in place.

Analytically, the *Consilio* opinion underscores a hesitation by federal appellate courts to render definitive rulings on public enforcement claims where the private plaintiff has been made whole for their direct economic injury. There is a broader tension in employment law between the need for robust statutory civil penalties to deter violations and the principled limitation of standing when the specific harm has already been remedied. The *Consilio* court were cautious in holding the MFLSA penalties potentially moot, effectively returning the decision to the trial court to evaluate if the public policy interest in stopping the violation outweighed the monetary compensation Cohen had already received.

Doctrinally, the *Consilio* ruling should strengthen the argument for requiring clear language in employee wage statutes before allowing private plaintiffs to seek punitive penalties exceeding actual backwages. Class action plaintiffs will need to heed this distinction when drafting claims against large document review companies that utilize overtime policies to cover compression work. If the specific subsection of a state statute mirrors the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, which provides for liquidated damages of double the wrongdoer, plaintiffs may still face scrutiny on standing issues if the employer modifies the policy after payment.

Overall, the Eighth Circuit’s *Consilio* decision serves as a reminder that wage law is not monolithic. While the *Consilio* ruling does not strip Class Actions of all remedies, it highlights the necessity of statutory interpretation where government and private interests diverge. Law prosulators, employees, and counsel must navigate this distinct line between what the statute authorizes the employee directly versus what the statute authorizes only the government. If other circuits encounter similar state statutes, the *Consilio* precedent may be particularly attractive in constraining the scope of private penalties-in-place analysis, especially in cases where the employer has already attempted to pay out the underlying claim.



### Mootness vs. Standing: Does Paying Overtime Wages Kill an MFLSA Penalty Claim?

## The Intersection of Settlement and Statutory Penalties
Labor law often revolves around a binary question: did the employer fail to pay the law's minimums? When the answer is yes, the prevailing remedy in federal class actions is backpay and interest. But a growing crop of state statutes like the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) opens the door to statutory penalties and liquidated damages designed to deter widespread violations. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* highlights a critical tension in how courts handle these "deterrence" claims. At the heart of the dispute lies a stark question: once an employer settles its baseline wage claims by paying the employees in question, does the case evaporate entirely, or can the legal system still pursue revenue-generating penalties that the employer will remit to the state? Cohen's analysis and the Eighth Circuit's instructions suggest that the latter is permissible, provided the court first confirms it still has jurisdiction.

## The State Statutory Limitation
The Eighth Circuit first squeezed the statute by limiting who can demand specific statutory penalties under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Cohen argues that as a private aggrieved party, he could simply elect to seek the employer's average daily wage penalty if the employer was found negligent. The court rejected this reading of the MPWA, noting that subsection 181.101(a) explicitly limits penalty collection to the state commissioner if the commissioner serves a demand and the payment is not made within ten days. The court reasoned that while the employee can sue for the wages themselves, the legislature did not authorize the private employee to seek the penalty directly. Instead, the statute reserves that administrative function for the Commissioner. This is a significant doctrinal distinction that clarifies state labor policy: in this specific statutory construct, the remedy is privatized (wages) from the administrative enforcement of penalties. This means that for Minnesota employers facing lawsuits under MPWA, an out-of-court settlement regarding the wage claim alone does not automatically trigger an administrative penalty, because only the Commissioner can legally enforce that specific statutory relief.

## The Federal MFLSA and the Jurisdictional Mantel
The more intriguing legal dynamics emerge under the MFLSA claim. Cohen stipulated that he was paid all overtime basis and liquidated damages he was entitled to under the federal act, yet he pursued a separate claim for statutory penalties. While the court initially stated that penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the commissioner rather than the employee, the real hurdle is jurisdiction. The Eighth Circuit cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services* to emphasize that the "requisite personal interest" must exist throughout the litigation. The irony here is that once the employer pays the employee, the injury usually ceases to exist in the eyes of the court. The Eighth Circuit recognized that Cohen might face a mootness issue if Consilio permanently stopped violating the policy. However, the "voluntary cessation" doctrine from *Friends of the Earth* provides a caveat: if the defendant stops the practice merely to avoid the lawsuit, it does not deprive the court of power to determine the legality of the practice. The court vacated its judgment on Cohen's MFLSA claim because it was not clear whether the employer had actually discontinued its voluntary overtime policy. Consequently, the court remanded for the lower court to determine if the employer's conduct was truly ceased or merely paused to resolve the litigation. This creates a procedural flywheel where lower court judges must ensure the employer control over future compliance is not solely a tactic for avoiding discovery.

## The Concurrent Thinking on Standing
Looking closely at the concurrences and special opinions, one can see that the question of standing for penalties is not yet seamlessly answered in federal law. In his special concurrence, Judge K. noted that Cohen might not even have standing to seek statutory penalties under *Friends of the Earth* standards. This aligns with the idea that penalties are an inseparable part of the legal relationship rather than a common law remedy. If the court cannot uphold the penalty on a due process or standing basis, the penalty claim rests on the voluntary cessation rule alone. By vacating the MFLSA claim without deciding the penalty issue first, the Eighth Circuit strikes a balance that prioritizes jurisdictional safety over hasty adjudication. In practice, this means the Seventh, Ninth, and other federal circuits will likely scrutinize whether labor experiments or warrants for further litigation are present before moving forward with these claims.

## Implications for Class Actions and Future Litigation
Ultimately, this case offers guidance on the procedural roadmap for wage-and-hour class actions. Class counsel will need to assess how to structure their requests for statutory penalties. In a case where the employer has already agreed to pay all overtime, simply remitting statutory penalties for future violations doesn't offer the class a windfall for past wages but could still seek to deter future conduct. However, the Eighth Circuit’s decision to remand imposes a gatekeeper function on the lower court. They must determine if the employer has permanently ceased past violations. If the answer is "no," then the court can keep the case alive to determine the lawfulness of the behavior. This suggests that a textbook "settlement" does not automatically moot a liability claim. Instead, it creates a new terrain where the focus shifts from just indemnification to potential permanent changes in the employer's compliance. The court’s ruling ensures that the lower court doesn't miss a clear legal violation simply because the employer paid the immediate costs.



# The Waiver Trap: How Courts Dismiss Claims That Were Stipulated Away

Wage theft cases often follow a predictable trajectory. An employee sues for unpaid overtime and potential liquidated damages. The employer, often trailing on exact calculations or facing proof burdens, agrees to pay the disputed sums. Parties sign a stipulation that the payment is made and the controversy is settled. But as the August 2025 Eighth Circuit decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* demonstrates, a "clean" settlement agreement can work in reverse, inadvertently stripping the court of necessary relief if the stipulation clears away the precise arguments needed to reach the statute. The Eighth Circuit’s ruling vacated the MFLSA claim due to potential mootness from consistent payment, but it also provided a stark warning on how courts handle claims that were swept aside via stipulation or late-stage procedural choices. In *Cohen*, the plaintiff secured partial relief, but the path forward was not a straight victory. The court emphasized that procedural posture matters as much as substantive law, and that the willingness of parties to "resolve" a lawsuit without fully exhausting claims can determine whether the remaining relief ever reaches the court.

## The Dangers of Stipulations

The primary lesson from *Cohen* concerns the intersection of stipulations and waiver. In this case, Cohen and Consilio reached a critical agreement: the employer would pay the alleged overtime and liquidated damages, and the employee stipulated that "no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved." Based on this settlement logic, the Eighth Circuit nonetheless dismissed Cohen's remaining statutory penalties, particularly under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and regarding injunctive relief. The court reasoned that for the MWTA claim, Cohen stipulated that he received all necessary pay and "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." Because his brief contained no meaningful argument to the contrary, the court held that Cohen waived the issue. Similarly, regarding the request for an injunctive order to rescind Consilio's policy, the court noted that while Cohen had sought specific forms of relief in his complaint, these changed in his summary judgment briefing without an amended complaint. The court ruled that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs," meaning the injunctive relief issue was not properly before the appellate court. This creates a procedural trap where an employer and employee may inadvertently agree to dismiss all claims necessary to enforce the underlying labor protections.

## Mootness and Jurisdictional Boundaries

While the MWTA and injunctive relief claims were denied due to waiver and procedural posture, the MFLSA penalty claim received a different treatment that highlights the fragility of jurisdiction when stipulations are involved. The court addressed Cohen's standing and the potential mootness of his claim regarding statutory penalties. Cohen stipulated that the employer paid all wages, which shifted the discussion from whether he was owed *now* to whether the policy could recur. The Eighth Circuit held that if Consilio’s policy remains in place, the claim against penalties may remain viable. However, "a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice" only if the practice continues to change. But if it is "absolutely clear that its wrongful conduct will not recur," the claim may become moot. Because it was unknown whether Consilio’s policy remained or was rescinded, the court vacated the lower court's grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded the specific question of jurisdiction. This underscores that while a stipulation regarding payment can moot a claim for penalties, it does not automatically moot all claims, requiring the district court to verify the reality of the policy change before dismissing.

## Lessons for the Bar

The downline effects of these holdings suggest that legal counsel must be more precise when framing the final handshake agreement that requires the other side to disintegrate claims. In the wage theft arena, parties often settle by filtering the obligations into a lump sum, but they should avoid inadvertently waiving penalties, injunctive relief, or compliance orders that constitute a secondary right to the paid wages. Attorneys for both sides must resist the urge to let the stipulation define the litigation's end if a partial or empty victory remains. Furthermore, courts are increasingly unwilling to improvise remedies based on partial filings or late-stage arguments, as seen in the injunctive relief rejection. For parties seeking to resolve wage disputes efficiently, this means they must be explicit in their briefing and amendments. Being fully coherent in their position before a stipulation is signed helps ensure that the final judgment reflects the full legal posture, rather than a truncated version. In *Cohen*, the court respected the parties' agreement to resolve the payment dispute, but it acknowledged that the machinery of the law requires proper raising of claims before dismissal can stand.
