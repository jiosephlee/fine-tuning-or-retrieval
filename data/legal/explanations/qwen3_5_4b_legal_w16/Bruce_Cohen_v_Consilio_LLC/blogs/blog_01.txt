Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The "Commissioner-Only" Rule: Why Private Employees Can't Sue for MPWA Penalties

In the fast-evolving landscape of wage theft litigation, the boundary between private rights and public enforcement remains a critical legal frontier. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* reinked into this boundary by narrowly interpreting Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). While Cohen secured some relief through stipulated payments, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of claims for statutory average daily wage penalties, establishing a "commissioner-only" reading of the statute. This ruling is particularly consequential for class action plaintiffs in wage cases under state statutes mirroring federal law, as it signals that mere allegations of unpaid overtime may not suffice for punitive damages if the legislature did not explicitly authorize private plaintiffs to enforce them.

The crux of the MPWA decision rests on statutory construction. Minnesota’s MPWA generally allows for government enforcement or private suits seeking damages, but private statutes often include specific mechanisms for penalty recovery. In section § 181.101(a) of the MPWA, the court found no explicit authority for private parties to demand penalties from the employer directly. Cohen argued the plain language authorized penalties, but the court noted that the section required a demand be served by the Commissioner first. If payment is not made by the Commissioner upon that demand, the Commissioner may charge and collect wages and a penalty, with any collected funds then paid to the employee. This structure suggests that the private right to claim the penalty itself is limited to the Commissioner as the initial enforcer.

Further support for the "commissioner-only" construction came from the legislature’s own legislative history. The court highlighted that Minnesota Statute § 181.13, which applies to discharged employees, explicitly states the employee "may charge and collect a penalty." Similarly, § 181.14 provides a parallel right for employees who quit or resign. The court reasoned that because the legislature included specific language empowering employees to pursue penalties in those distinct contexts while excluding it here in § 181.101, it was clear this section was not intended to extend to private penalties. As the court noted, "the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so."

Beyond the MPWA holding, the decision contained a significant vacatur regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim, specifically concerning statutory penalties payable to the Commissioner. The Eighth Circuit found it lacked continuing jurisdiction to rule on these specific penalties. While Cohen received direct compensation for overtime and liquidated damages, the potential for penalties to serve a deterrent function was challenged on grounds of mootness. The court cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services* to emphasize that private plaintiffs generally seek penalties because they "redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury." However, if the defendant pays the plaintiff’s private damages and there is no uncertainty the wrongful conduct will recur, the case may become moot. The court vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine if the violative policy was ongoing or no longer in place.

Analytically, the *Consilio* opinion underscores a hesitation by federal appellate courts to render definitive rulings on public enforcement claims where the private plaintiff has been made whole for their direct economic injury. There is a broader tension in employment law between the need for robust statutory civil penalties to deter violations and the principled limitation of standing when the specific harm has already been remedied. The *Consilio* court were cautious in holding the MFLSA penalties potentially moot, effectively returning the decision to the trial court to evaluate if the public policy interest in stopping the violation outweighed the monetary compensation Cohen had already received.

Doctrinally, the *Consilio* ruling should strengthen the argument for requiring clear language in employee wage statutes before allowing private plaintiffs to seek punitive penalties exceeding actual backwages. Class action plaintiffs will need to heed this distinction when drafting claims against large document review companies that utilize overtime policies to cover compression work. If the specific subsection of a state statute mirrors the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, which provides for liquidated damages of double the wrongdoer, plaintiffs may still face scrutiny on standing issues if the employer modifies the policy after payment.

Overall, the Eighth Circuit’s *Consilio* decision serves as a reminder that wage law is not monolithic. While the *Consilio* ruling does not strip Class Actions of all remedies, it highlights the necessity of statutory interpretation where government and private interests diverge. Law prosulators, employees, and counsel must navigate this distinct line between what the statute authorizes the employee directly versus what the statute authorizes only the government. If other circuits encounter similar state statutes, the *Consilio* precedent may be particularly attractive in constraining the scope of private penalties-in-place analysis, especially in cases where the employer has already attempted to pay out the underlying claim.
