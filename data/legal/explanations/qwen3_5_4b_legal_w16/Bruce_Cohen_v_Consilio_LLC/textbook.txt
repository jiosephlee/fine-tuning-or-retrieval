Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# 1. Procedural Background, Claims, and Initial Litigation History

## Basic Employment Facts and the Establishment of a Putative Class
To understand the litigation context, it is essential to establish the employment relationship at issue. Bruce Cohen began working for Consilio, LLC, a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota, in 2018. He initially worked as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. The central factual dispute arose in July 2019 when Consilio emailed employees, including Cohen, informing them that a new premium overtime policy would be instituted in August 2019. This new policy eliminated overtime premium pay, stipulating that permissible employees like Cohen would now be paid at their base pay rate for all hours worked. Cohen alleged that this unilateral policy change caused an unjust enrichment of the employer at the expense of the employee. In August 2020, Cohen filed a lawsuit on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. The court identifies the nature of the dispute not as isolated damages regarding individual termination, but as a systemic class-wide action regarding compensation practices. Cohen alleged that the employer's actions violated specific state statutes governing wage payment and penalties.

## The Specific Statutory Claims Under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101, § 177.27, § 181.032
In their complaint, Cohen sought redress under a triad of Minnesota statutes, each providing a different remedy mechanism. First, he alleged the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1. Second, he brought a claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8. Third, he asserted a claim under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. These statutes are designed to enforce various wage requirements, typically by offering purposes such as mandatory overtime pay, liquidated damages, and statutory penalties. While the specific remedies available under each statute differ, the classification of the dispute under these codes determines the scope of the potential relief and the burden of proof. The plaintiff sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief to ensure future compliance with these state laws.

## Summary Judgment Motions and the Stipulation Regarding Payments
A critical procedural element of this case is the settlement before trial regarding the monetary relief sought. Following the initial filing, Consilio paid Cohen and other employees $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded pursuant to the MFLSA claim. Specifically, Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. Notably, the parties entered into a stipulation agreeing that Cohen was paid for all overtime wages he alleges are owed. Furthermore, they agreed that Cohen does not dispute the liquidated damages entitlement under the MFLSA. The controversy narrowed significantly to two remaining categories: penalties under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA. Cohen argued these statutes entitled him to significant additional penalties, approximately $220,000. Consilio argued the statutes did not permit these penalties. Consequently, the parties stipulated that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants' summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Cohen and Consilio acknowledged that if the court granted summary judgment in full, the claims and the case would be fully resolved.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment
Based on the stipulation regarding the expedience of concluding the case, the District Court granted Consilio's motion for summary judgment on all three claims. The court ruled in favor of Consilio, finding no genuine issues of material fact that would allow Cohen to proceed to trial. The District Court's decision effectively terminated the employment dispute as the plaintiff had received the agreed-upon wages and liquidated damages. Cohen did not appeal the judgment regarding the payment of his underlying wages but sought to challenge the sufficiency of the ruling regarding the statutory penalties. By granting summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, the District Court resolved the employment dispute at the pleading threshold, preventing the parties from engaging in discovery regarding the validity of the policy change further than necessary. This disposition set the stage for the appellate review, as the plaintiff sought to overturn the summary judgment decision regarding the specific statutory penalties.

## The Standard of Review for Summary Judgment
Although the Eighth Circuit affirmed the District Court's broad ruling, the legal framework for reviewing such decisions is strict. When the Eighth Circuit reviews a grant of summary judgment, it conducts a de novo review. This means the appellate court does not defer to the Lower Court's factual determinations; rather, it reviews the legal conclusions de novo. This standard treats the District Court's decision as an error if there were no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. To support this review, the appellate court views the evidence and the inferences drawn from the evidence in a light most favorable to the nonmoving party. This ensures that the summary judgment standard protects the plaintiff who has not yet had to prove their case through trial, ensuring they are treated as entitled to the full relief sought unless the defendant demonstrates they are clearly entitled to judgment without factual disputes.

### II. Statutory Analysis of State Law Penalties (MPWA and MWTA)

The central task in this chapter is to understand how the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit analyzed the Plaintiff's claims under Minnesota statute to determine eligibility for civil penalties. When a district court grants summary judgment, the Court of Appeals reviews the decision de novo, treating the lower court's ruling as pure error on law or fact with no deference to the lower court's view. This approach ensures that the appellate court clarifies the statutory intent of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and other relevant state laws.

## Statutory construction of MPWA § 181.101 and the Commissioner requirement for penalties

The Court began its statutory analysis by examining the text of section 181.101 of the MPWA. When interpreting a statute like the MPWA, the foundational canon of construction is that the language must be read literally and without presumption against the drafter. The Court argued that readers must look at the legislative text to embody the intent of the legislature. Section 181.101 is a plain provision that outlines a specific administrative process. It explicitly states that penalties are only obtained if the Commissioner first "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." At the beginning of the provision, the text refers to "the Commissioner" engaging with the employer rather than the aggrieved employee directly. The Court applied the principle from *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* that when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect. Therefore, without an explicit plea for penalty rights from the employee in the statute itself, the text does not support a private penalty claim through the payee.

## Distinction between Commissioner rights and employee rights regarding successive demands

To clarify the statutory limits on who can seek penalties, the Court looked to the entire act rather than the specific provision under claim. The distinction lies in whether the legislature extended an "employee demand" right to the Commissioner or the employee to pursue. Section 181.101 places the burden of demand and penalty collection on the Commissioner. There is a clear difference established elsewhere in the act which allows for different remedies depending on the context of the discharge or demand. For example, the Court cited evidence regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act, where the mandate changes depending on the relationship between the parties and the stage of demand. Some other sections of the act, however, allow for immediate penalties upon demand. The Court reasoned that the legislature had the power to grant employees the right to sue for penalties if it wanted to. Instead of granting that power in section 181.101, it limited the remedy to the Commissioner. This distinction creates an administrative precedent where the employee must first rely on the Commissioner to advance their claim for penalties, significantly altering how wage claims are litigated to ensure consistency in wage recovery methods.

## The conclusion that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under the MPWA

Because the Court found that the language of section 181.101 does not grant employees the right to recover average daily wage penalties independently, the lower court's dismissal of the claim was correct. The Court stated that it was against the interests of the legislature to allow the employee to seek penalties under this specific section because such penalties are payable to the Commissioner, not the employee. The statute provides that if the Commissioner collects funds from an employer, the Commissioner must return the money to the employee, indicating that the employee's right to funds is secondary to the Commissioner's enforcement power. This holding reinforces the summary judgment standard applied to the district court's decision. Consequently, the appellate court held that no dispute existed over the statutory eligibility for penalties in this MPWA claim. The Court affirmed that the employee in this situation could not bypass the statutory requirement of the Commissioner's demand process before seeking statutory penalties.

## Procedural waiver of the MWTA claim due to lack of meaningful argument

Turning to the Minnesota Wage Theft Act, the Court noted that the issue regarding statutory penalties had not been briefed properly. The standard for this phase of appellate review requires that the被上诉人's brief or pleadings be submitted for notice of the issue before the trial. Cohen argued that the district court should have addressed the MWTA claim, but he failed to provide a statutory argument allowing for civil penalties under this law. The Court applied the principle from *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, noted in the opinion that if the defendant’s brief does not support an assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue. Therefore, the court concluded that the court would not disturb the district court’s order dismissing this claim. The procedural posture required that because there was no meaningful argument in support of reversal and Cohen only pointed to no statutory authority, the court was obligated to treat it as waived and not reassume jurisdiction on that claim.

## Impact of legislative inaction or action on interpreting potential penalty statutes

In interpreting these statutes, the Court utilized the principle that the legislature can choose to give rights to the Commissioner rather than the aggrieved employee itself. This interpretation included reference to the legislative choice regarding the consequences of the statute. If the legislature wanted to give employees the right to penalties, it would have done so explicitly. The Court cited *Lagasse v. Horton*, explaining that the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so. For example, sections 181.13(a) and 181.14 allow certain employees to recover unpaid wages or commissions as well as a penalty upon discharge. This explicit statutory language appears coincidentally to provide penalties to the employee. The absence of this specific language in section 181.101 confirms that the decision to allow the Commissioner to seek penalties was a consistent and distinct choice by the legislature. This principle of legislative interpretation helps ensure restrictions like the MPWA penalties are applied to the specific text suggested by the legislature.

## Affirmance of MPWA Disposition

Following the analysis above, the Court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MPWA claim, specifically the denial of penalties to Cohen. The district court had correctly concluded that the statute mandates the Commissioner's involvement for penalties. The decision highlights the importance of statutory construction when the language of a statute is plain. It also reinforces the policy reason that employees must be compensated through the Commissioner's enforcement mechanism to maintain consistency in wage theft penalties across the state. The employee is entitled to the wages themselves, but the specific statutory remedy for penalties is reserved for the Commissioner under this act. This conclusion supports the need to rely on the Commissioner’s administrative powers when enforcing statutory wage payment laws within the state.

## Conclusion

The statutory analysis of MPWA and MWTA reveals the strict compliance requirements imposed by the Minnesota legislature on penalty claims. The Court distinguished between the agency and the employee, limiting the employee's ability to seek penalties for specific wage violations. The procedural waivers further emphasize the importance of aligning statutory arguments with legislative authority. These decisions ensure the proper enforcement of the MPWA and MWTA regarding wage recovery penalties, requiring the employee to seek wages without a penalty claim in this context. The Court vacated the district court's order regarding MFLSA, but in MPWA, the MPWA analysis stands.

# III. Federal Law, Standing, and Mootness in MFLSA Penalty Claims

## The federal requirement that penalties often benefit the government rather than individual plaintiffs

The core of the dispute regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim turns on the nature of the relief sought. While the defendant, Consilio, paid Cohen for all overtime wages and liquidated damages, Cohen contested the inclusion of statutory penalties, arguing he was entitled to approximately $72,080 in total penalties under various statutes. However, Cohen and Consilio agreed that any statutory penalties imposed would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally. This distinction between compensatory damages and civil penalties is material to the court's jurisdictional analysis because civil penalties serve a public, rather than private, purpose. As this Court noted in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), civil penalties affording redress to citizen plaintiffs often function to discourage ongoing unlawful conduct and deter future violations. Consequently, the presence of a plaintiff who bears no personal benefit from a penalty payment creates a significant question of whether that plaintiff has met the threshold requirements to pursue the claim before a federal court.

## Procedural standing and continued personal interest during litigation

To proceed on the merits, a plaintiff must generally demonstrate standing for the claim he seeks to press and for the specific relief sought. *Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) establishes that a plaintiff must demonstrate a requisite personal interest that exists at the commencement of the litigation and continues throughout its existence. COHEN stipulated that Consilio had compensated him for all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA. However, the issue remains whether he has a continuing personal stake in a penalty claim that benefits the state rather than him directly. As *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) explained, standing must persist to prevent courts from acting beyond their constitutional authority. In this case, Cohen argued that federal courts possess jurisdiction over civil penalties, even when paid to a government entity, because they redress future threats of injury. Yet, the district court's earlier analysis did not address whether Cohen maintained a sufficient personal interest in the penalties once the investment of his wages and liquidated damages was resolved. This jurisdictional uncertainty is why the parties could not agree on specific damages, focusing instead on the applicability of the federal standing doctrine to the penalty specifically.

## The doctrine of mootness: whether the threatened injury was resolved or voluntary cessation

The Court identified a serious concern regarding its jurisdiction to decide this issue because Cohen's claim for penalties could become moot if the underlying wrongful behavior ceased to exist. According to the standard established in *Friends of the Earth*, volition should not be expected to deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of a practice, a principle derived from prior case law such as *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982). However, voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not automatically preserve jurisdiction against a challenge, particularly if the defendant demonstrates an absence of the ability to violate any statutory requirement in the future. In *Friends*, the Court noted that a plaintiff must demonstrate that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur. If Consilio is willing to comply with overtime laws moving forward, this is a mark of cancellation of the argument of liability. The Court must determine if the practice of withholding加班 wages has occurred and whether it has been terminated by the district court's ruling. If the policy no longer exists, the plaintiff must show that the defendant will not return to the practice, which is a high burden of proof.

## Analysis of whether the alleged policy remains in place as of the hearing

The factual reciprocity of the case hinges on whether Consilio's 2019 overtime policy remains in effect as of the hearing of the summary judgment motion. While the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed, they could not agree on the status of the new overtime policy itself. If Consilio's policy remains in place, Cohen's claim for civil penalties may still be viable under the MFLSA because the illegal conduct is ongoing. However, if Consilio's policy is no longer in place and it is "absolutely clear" that its wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may in fact be moot. The distinction between an administrative demand for payment and an injunction of a future decline is difficult. The Court noted that federal courts are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest." The ambiguity regarding the policy's status is not a matter of old law or new obligations, but a question of continued conduct which requires further factual findings to determine if a legal right to relief is intact or if the controversy has dissipated entirely.

## The Court's decision to vacate jurisdiction and remand to determine mootness conclusively

Based on the foregoing, the court determined that the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim was the appropriate decision to vacate and remand for further proceedings. The Court reasoned that it could not resolve the jurisdiction question on summary judgment, particularly regarding the MLLSA penalties, without a clear answer regarding the status of the policy and the magisterial role of the nation. The decision to vacate applies specifically to Cohen’s claim for summary judgment under the MFLSA, leaving the MMPA claim affirmed according to the statutory text which ties the penalty to the Commissioner's involvement. The court does not rule on the merits of the penalty claim itself, as it would require the lower court to determine if the requisite standing and mootness requirements are satisfied. Consequently, the Court remands for the district court to determine its jurisdiction. The Court confirms the dispositions for the MPWA and MWTA claims but recognizes that the MFLSA penalty claim requires a ruling on mootness first. Therefore, the case is remanded solely to assess jurisdiction regarding the penalty portion.

# IV. Procedural Deficiencies, Injunctive Relief, and Final Disposition

## Procedural Failures: Waiver of Claims and Injunctive Relief

This section of the opinion highlights critical procedural markers that define the boundaries of a court's jurisdiction to decide claims. First, you must recognize how district courts respond to procedural posture when suits proceed on summary judgment. When Cohen moved for injunctive relief, he initially requested specific forms of relief, specifically setting out a 2019 policy rescission in his complaint. However, during the summary judgment phase, he withdrew this request and instead asked the court to consider the issue in his briefing. The Eighth Circuit noted that the district court could not consider this late assertion of relief. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), new claims are properly raised through amended complaints or docketed via a motion to amend. Opposition briefs are substitutes for these procedural mechanisms and are notgrounds for the court to raise or decide new requests for relief for the first time in the record. This outcome demonstrates that procedural compliance is essential; legal theories cannot be smuggled in after the opportunity initially provided to shape the case has passed. Furthermore, the court observed that Cohen waived his claim for the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) penalties because the statute reserved the right for the employee to "prosecute a claim for wages." Since Cohen offered no legal authority to support penalty relief under the MWTA in his summary judgment briefing, the Eighth Circuit decided not to address the issue. The court explicitly stated that it declined to disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim because the relevant argument had not appeared in the record.

In procedural analysis, consider how the timing and nature of an argument affect its viability. Since Cohen stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him regarding the wage theft claims, but failed to articulate how the MWTA violations would translate to private penalty relief, he effectively conceded that no legal basis existed. When students encounter a summary judgment briefing where one party asserts a remedy without statutory support or factual foundation, they should note that the appellate court will not create law to fill that gap. The Eighth Circuit reiterated that it did not address the issue for reasons of waiver, emphasizing that if a party's brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, the issue is considered waived. This procedural rule prevents the appellate court from drifting further than the issues presented by the parties.

## Final Disposition, Remand Instructions, and Jurisdictional Barriers

The core of the court's decision rests on the court's power to determine the legality of a defendant's conduct in light of the effects of their voluntary cessation. Because Cohen had already stipulated that Consilio agreed to pay all wages and liquidated damages demanded under the MFLSA, but the parties could not agree on whether the violative overtime policy remained in place, the Eighth Circuit determined it was premature to decide the merits of the statutory penalties. The opinion underscored a vital concept in standing and mootness: a plaintiff must demonstrate a continuing personal interest that must exist throughout the litigation's existence. While the district court's grant of summary judgment on penalties was vacated, the remand instructions demanded a determination of jurisdiction. If Consilio's policy remains, the claim is viable; if the policy no longer stands and wrongful conduct is unlikely to recur, the claim may be moot. The court remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction and issue penalties only after this threshold question of mootness is resolved. Students must understand that a voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice, but retention of jurisdiction is not a license to retain case over one or both of the parties plainly lacking a continuing interest.

Regarding the final operation of the ruling, the court rehearsed the disposition as a composite of affirmative decisions and vacatur. The Eighth Circuit vacated the district court's ruling on Cohen's MFLSA claim for penalties and remanded for further proceedings. Conversely, it affirmed the summary judgment against Cohen on the MPWA and MWTA claims. In the MPWA analysis, the court found that the statute authorizes penalties only through the Commissioner, reserving this right for the state agent. Similarly, on the injunctive relief claim, the court noted the parties agreed that injunctive relief had already been sought through the withdrawal of the policy, but the district court had not considered it. The final disposition was therefore: affirm on MPWA/MWTA, vacate on MFLSA penalties, and remand.

## Concurring Opinion on Fees and Penalties

Finally, it is important to note the specific dissenting opinions that highlight alternative legal theories submitted by the majority or concurring judges. Judge Kobi KOBES filed a concurrence in which she questioned the majority's reliance on the Americans with Disabilities Act or similar standing precedents to validate Cohen's statutory penalties claim under the MFLSA. She suggested that he may lack standing to seek statutory penalties under *Friends of the Earth*, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While she agreed with the vacatur of the judgment regarding the MFLSA claim and the remand order, she noted that if the statutes were not violated, Cohen still lacks standing to seek penalties. Furthermore, Judge Kobes vacated the district court's ruling on Cohen's claim for attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. She noted that on remand, the district court should determine the mootness question first. This concurrence serves as a valuable supplement to understanding that while the majority viewed the mootness issue as the primary hurdle, the concurrence emphasized the underlying jurisdictional standing issues independent of mootness, suggesting a dual test for the court's jurisdiction.

## Examining the Disposition and Remand

When studying this chapter, focus on the interplay between the statute's terms and the procedural posture of the litigation. The MPWA analysis showed that the Massachusetts statute implies a penalty mechanism that is exclusive to the Commissioner, whereas the MFLSA analysis allowed for a private remedy but was subject to mootness. When analyzing the remand, note that the district court was given back the case to decide jurisdiction rather than requiring them to resolve the substantive penalty amount immediately. This ensures the district court first separates a pending case from a moot one. The final disposition was thus: vacate the district court's order regarding Cohen's claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings; otherwise affirm (KU).

**General Rule:** As a student, remember that procedural posture constraints, such as the requirement for Rule 15(a) amendments or the waiver of issues not briefed, can cause even the most meritorious legal arguments to result in partial vacatur rather than a definitive affirmance on the merits.
