Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Why did the Fifth Circuit decline to certify statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court?
Question:
The opinion states that the appellate court is 'chary about certifying questions of law' and declines the request because the term's lack of definition by a state supreme court is 'not sufficient.' However, plaintiffs argued this is important to the state's insurance industry. Under what specific circumstances does fraud on the court principles or judicial economy override the Florida rule of certifying for state statutory construction questions when a federal court of appeals is reviewing the validity of a lower state court's summary judgment?
Answer:
The Fifth Circuit declined to certify the statutory interpretation questions in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* by adhering to a standard of caution regarding requests to define state law. While the question references a "Florida rule," *Williams* explicitly relies on **Fifth Circuit precedent** (specifically *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass'n*, 106 F.3d 1245 (5th Cir. 1997)) rather than Florida jurisprudence.

Based on the text provided, the court applied the following standard and reasoning for the decline:

**1. The General Standard: "Chary" Approach**
The Fifth Circuit stated it is "chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so." The availability of certification is described as an "important resource," and the court pledges not to "risk its continued availability by going to that well too often." This reflects the judicial economy interest in the limited use of the certification process.

**2. Plaintiffs' Argument and Rejection**
The plaintiffs argued the interpretation was "important to the state's insurance industry." However, the court held that "That a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification.'" The mere fact that a statutory term is ambiguous and the issue carries economic weight for an industry did not constitute a "compelling reason."

**3. Judicial Economy vs. Overrides**
The opinion does not recognize "fraud on the court" or "judicial economy" principles as circumstances that would *override* the refusal to certify to *grant* a request. Instead, these concepts support the refusal. The court treated certification as a resource constraint ("such an important resource"), using it to explain why the district court’s interpretation of the statute should stand. Consequently, without a defined statutory term that requires clarification to resolve a conflict between state and federal authority, or an identified "compelling reason" beyond general industry importance, the Fifth Circuit will decline.

In summary, the Fifth Circuit affirmed the dismissal and refused certification because the statutory ambiguity (lack of "receipt" or signature definitions) did not meet the high threshold set by *Jefferson* to justify the precious use of the certification process.
