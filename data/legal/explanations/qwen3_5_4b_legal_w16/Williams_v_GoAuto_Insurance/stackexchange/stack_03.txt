Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How does the Fifth Circuit reconcile Black's Law Dictionary's definition of 'receipt' (physical possession) with automated e-mail cancellation?
Question:
The court quotes Black's Law Dictionary defining 'receipt' as 'the act of receiving something... specifically by taking physical possession.' However, it subsequently concludes that an e-mailed notice automatically meeting a computer prompt satisfies the statute. What is the legal theory for when an automated web service interaction is legally deemed 'receipt' without a human intermediary, and how does this override the statutory text's strict literalism?
Answer:
The Fifth Circuit resolves the tension between *Black's* definition of "receipt" and the automated e-mail procedure through **functional statutory interpretation based on modern technological capacity**.

The court acknowledges that *Black's Law Dictionary* defines receiving as "taking physical possession," **La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)** and that **"receive" as "to take... possession of."** However, the court limits the application of this strict literalism to the "era in which this modern statute is written" and how technology has changed the means of compliance.

### 1. Technological Equivalence
The court posits that because the statute is a **"modern statute set in current times,"** the "means" of receiving a notice need not mirror the physical acts of the past. As the opinion states:

> "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto’s method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

By focusing on the result rather than the modality, the court treats the computer system's "listening" mechanism as the functional equivalent of a physical hand taking the document. When the APAC computer system "calls" the GoAuto web service and "hears its name," the computer system "performs the activity," thereby effecting a "receipt" on behalf of the company.

### 2. Strict Adherence vs. Proxies
While Louisiana law requires "strict adherence" to statutory procedures to fix the "exact time and date of cancellation," the court determined that the "receipt" requirement is satisfied once the insurer treats the notice as having been received, regardless of whether a human stamp affirms it. The court notes:

> "Accepting these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

The court reconciles this with the dictionary definition by distinguishing between the *definition* of receipt and the *method* of fulfillment. A computer acting as the mechanism for "taking possession" in the early morning hours suffices, as long as it happens prior to the actual cancellation. This avoids the argument that GoAuto's procedures violated the "private carrier" statute or the "receipt" statute because the "receipt" was twice or three times more efficient than human processing.

### 3. Purpose-Based Interpretation
Ultimately, the legal theory relies on the statutory purpose of preventing misrepresentation and ensuring a minimum time to cure default. The court emphasizes that:

> "Strict adherence to the statutes... serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured."

The automatic e-mail system fixes the time of receipt precisely (corresponding with the "morning hours"). The court held that **strict adherence requires compliance with the statutory standard but does not require the addition of procedural formalities not required by the text.** Therefore, the absence of a human signature or physical stamp in the electronic "receipt" process does not impair the statutory goal of the notice, provided the company obtains the notice and responds to it as required.

### Conclusion
The Fifth Circuit overrides the dictionary's strict literalism of "physical possession" by adopting a **technological neutrality doctrine**: the "modern statute" allows for modern instruments (computers, web services) to satisfy the functional legal requirement of "receipt" (taking possession/delivery), effectively splitting the concept of "possession" between the dictionary's physical definition and the statutory trust in modern business automation.

*Affirmed.*
