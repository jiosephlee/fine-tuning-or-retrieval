Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Case Overview and Statutory Framework
This chapter provides the background context and statutory framework necessary to understand the core legal issues in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*. The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial agency tasked with ensuring a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." In this case, the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) initiated suit to compel OSC to investigate whether the Department of Justice (DOJ) arbitrarily denied a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the district court initially dismissed AFL's claims, the appellate court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing to pursue the matter, vacating the judgment and remanding for dismissal. This chapter explains the design of the OSC and the specific civil service statutes that determine OSC's authority to investigate, highlighting the tension between OSC's independent investigative powers and judicial discretion.

## The Role and Design of the Office of Special Counsel
To understand the scope of the dispute, one must first grasp the statutory mandate and design of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC). Established under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC functions as an investigative and prosecutorial agency dedicated to seeking "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." As cited in the opinion, under this framework, OSC is empowered to investigate "prohibited personnel practices" by federal civil servants and, if warranted, to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board. However, this agency's scope is broad, reaching beyond typical personnel fraud. The opinion notes that OSC also possesses authority to investigate certain other forms of misconduct by government employees. For instance, Section 1216 empowers OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding of information by government employees. Understanding this "roadmap" is critical because the dispute centers on whether OSC can exercise this broad investigatory authority without triggering stricter judicial prerequisites laid out in the FOIA enforcement provisions.

## Prohibited Personnel Investigations under 5 U.S.C. § 1214
The primary mechanism for OSC enforcement when a "prohibited personnel practice" (PPP) is found lies in 5 U.S.C. § 1214. Under this statute, OSC investigates PPPs and may seek corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board. Crucially, § 1214 sets forth specific procedural requirements governing these corrective actions. For example, Section 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D) explicitly provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of the investigation. AFL, in its litigation posture, relied heavily on these procedural protections. AFL argued that OSC's refusal to investigate violated its procedural right to receive information required by § 1214. However, the appellate court must analyze whether these statutory notices constitute a "concrete, particularized" injury in fact. If the right itself is procedural and "in vacuo" (in the abstract) without a tangible loss of information or interest, standing is unlikely to attach. Thus, § 1214 is not merely an enforcement tool; it is central to determining whether the plaintiff suffered a sufficient injury before a court can review OSC's discretion.

## OSC's Investigation Powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)
Distinct from the PPP enforcement provision, Section 1216(a)(3) provides OSC with a separate investigation trigger. The opinion states that this section directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—referring to the FOIA. In the case before the court, AFL requested DOJ records related to judicial recusals. DOJ claimed it denied the request because it does not search employee emails without consent. AFL sued DOJ under FOIA, which DOJ produced records for. Simultaneously, AFL asked OSC to investigate this alleged DOJ policy of not searching employee emails. AFL invoked § 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation." OSC, however, disclaimed authority because the three predicates for triggering an investigation under § 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. AFL argued that § 1216 mandated an investigation even if those FOIA-predicates were not satisfied. While the district court disagreed, noting that OSC's decision was within its discretion, the appellate court must view § 1216 within the full statutory context to determine if it creates a binding obligation on OSC to override the discretion implied by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

## FOIA Agreements and the 552(e) Enforcement Clause
In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court specifically examines 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to determine if OSC is required to investigate without the triggers specified in the FOIA enforcement provision. The "enforcement clause" cited in the statute states that OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted" only if (1) a court orders production of records, (2) fees are awarded, and (3) the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding." AFL's legal theory was that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under § 1216. AFL contended that § 1216(a)(3) created a sufficient mandamus obligation to investigate the alleged FOIA policy even when the specific § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) mandates were not met. However, the opinion clarifies that the court does not need to resolve whether § 1216 creates such an independent obligation because AFL fails to establish standing regardless. The distinction between the procedural triggers available under 552 and 1216 is the underlying basis for AFL's claim that OSC violated its procedural right, but the court recognizes that the absence of any protected entitlement prevents the standing necessary for judicial review.

# Procedural Posture and Litigation History

## AFL's Request for DOJ Records and OSC Inquiry

In the initial phase of this litigation, the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel federal agencies to investigate a potential violation of the Freedom of Information Act. AFL maintained that the Department of Justice (DOJ) possessed certain emails that should have been disclosed in response to a FOIA request regarding judicial recusals. After the district court process, AFL sought to clarify the procedural path for seeking independent enforcement of such federal policies. Specifically, AFL requested the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails for FOIA compliance without consent. This request was grounded in the arbitrary or capricious withholding of information claims found under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). The distinction between the initial FOIA production by DOJ and the independent OSC investigation request is central to understanding why AFL must sue the OSC to enforce its internal procedural obligations under § 1216.

## OSC's Determination to Decline Investigation

The OSC did not agree to investigate AFL’s claim. In its initial response to the inquiry, OSC relied on section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of the FOIA to disclaim authority, arguing that section 1216(a)(3) did not impose an investigative mandate beyond the specific predicates required under the standard FOIA enforcement provision. AFL argued otherwise, asserting that § 1216(a)(3) mandated an investigation regardless of whether the stricter FOIA enforcement predicates were met. The district court agreed with the FONIA framing, noting that OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a legal error regarding the definition of OSC's authority. However, because § 1216(c) generally commits these decisions to the agency's discretion, the district court declined to order an investigation for lack of authority due to the discretion held. Consequently, OSC was instructed to review the matter again.

## District Court Dismissal of Claims on the Merits

The district court initially ruled in part for AFL but ultimately dismissed the case for failure to state a claim. Following the remand to OSC for further consideration, OSC again declined to investigate, leading to a second dismissal of the remaining claims by the district court. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment, and the district court affirmed the dismissal. At this stage, the prosecution's right to the full relief sought was not granted because the OSC declined the investigation a second time. Importantly, the district court's dismissal was treated as a failure to state a claim rather than a grant for lack of jurisdiction, even though the appellate court would later vacate for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This sequence of dismissal and remand is critical because it highlights the agency's exercise of discretion and the judicial court's deferential initial handling of the issue, which was later overridden by the appellate court's jurisdictional threshold analysis.

## Circuit Court Review Guidelines and Remand

On appeal, the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit conducted a de novo review. De novo review requires the appellate court to examine the issue independently, without being bound by the trial court's factual findings. In this case, the review focused on whether the District Court had subject matter jurisdiction prior to reaching the merits of the case. Since the appellate court found that AFL lacked Article III standing, it did not reach the substantive law regarding the OSC's enforcement discretion. The appellate court vacated the district court's judgment and remanded the case with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This disposition confirms that while the OSC’s request for the investigation was a valid claim, it was procedurally barred by the plaintiff's inability to demonstrate Article III standing, making the full merits of the OSC's claim moot.

# The Article III Standing Framework

## The Constitution's Limits on Federal Jurisdiction

Article III of the Constitution defines the scope of the federal courts' power, restricting it to the resolution of specific "Cases" and "Controversies." This restriction creates the doctrine of standing, which functions as a jurisdictional filter before a court can address the merits of a claim. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit Court emphasizes that Article III limits the judicial power, and this doctrine follows from the case-or-controversy requirement inherent to the Constitution. To have standing, a plaintiff must plausibly allege facts that satisfy the tripartite test established in *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*. While the district court here proceeded to the merits of AFL's claims regarding the Office of Special Counsel, the appellate court determined that the Article III requirements were not met. The Court notes that standing issues often arise where neither party initially addresses them, but addressed standing prevents the judicial power from being violated unnecessarily. In *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, the Supreme Court reinforced that courts must assess whether a plaintiff has a concrete stake in the disposition before addressing the substantive rules.

The appellate court in *Jamieson Greer* interprets Article III standing not merely as a question of fairness, but as a fundamental injury requirement for the exercise of federal power. Plaintiffs cannot rely on generalized grievances or abstract offenses to invoke federal jurisdiction. In this case, the district court analyzed the case for failure to state a claim on the merits, but the appellate court reviewed the complaint under de novo standing standard. The court notes that at the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of the standing requirement. Because AFL failed to establish a jurisdictional interest in the case, the district court's instruction to the OSC was moot; the case must be dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction regardless of whether a district court had properly adjudicated the underlying statutory issue.

## Injury in Fact: Concrete, Actual, and Particularized

The first component of the *Lujan* test is injury in fact. This is defined by Supreme Court precedent as "an invasion of a legally protected interest that is 'concrete and particularized' and 'actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.' *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). Plaintiffs must show a tangible loss or harm resulting from the challenged defendant conduct. In *Jamieson Greer*, the plaintiff American First Legal Foundation (AFL) claimed a procedural injury derived from the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) refusal to investigate a DOJ FOIA violation. The court applies the principle that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer Article III standing.

While statutes may grant procedural rights, these rights alone do not confer standing unless tied to a concrete injury. In *Jamieson Greer*, the Court distinguishes between substantive and procedural injury in the context of花瓣eros. Citing *Center for Law & Educ. v. Dep't of Educ.*, the court notes that, even in cases involving alleged procedural injury, courts relax the strict requirements of imminence and redressability, but they do not eliminate the requirement of an "injury in fact" or causation. AFL made two claims regarding injury: first, a denial of access to DOJ records, and second, a denial of information required under § 1216. The court found that AFL had no entitlement to the information from OSC in the first place, as the district court held § 1216(c) committed the decision-making to OSC's discretion. Because AFL could not show a concrete interest to be impaired by OSC's discretion in § 1216, the procedural right was in vacuo. The court concludes that AFL satisfied neither the requirements nor even the relaxed standard for redressability.

## Causal Link: Traceability to Opposing Party

The second component of the *Lujan* test is traceability. A plaintiff must demonstrate that the "injury in fact" is fairly traceable to the actions of the defendant. In *Jamieson Greer*, AFL argued that OSC's failure to investigate DOJ under § 1216 caused AFL to be denied records DOJ initially requested under FOIA. The Court finds this causal chain too attenuated to support standing. The alleged chain of events requires speculation about choices by government agencies not before the courts: if OSC had investigated, it might have pursued the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which might have sanctioned DOJ employees, which might have led DOJ to produce records. The Court cites *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* to clarify that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." By relying on the unclear discretionary choices of MSPB and DOJ and the lack of certainty regarding OSC's own potential enforcement discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*, AFL failed to show that a favorable judgment "would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the claimed informational injury.

The court further notes that redressability is generally tied to how a judgment binds the parties before the court. For the causal link to exist, the proposed remedy must directly affect the defendant whose conduct caused the injury. In AFL's case, an order against OSC to investigate DOJ would not, in and of itself, compel the DOJ to release records. IPL's argument was that OSC could not choose to investigate a violation of FOIA policy without an OSC mandate. However, the court determines that the causal link between OSC's investigation and DOJ's record production is speculative, and thus the injury cannot be attributed to OSC in a way that satisfies Article III requirements for redressability.

## Redressability: The Likelihood of Judicial Relief

The third component of the *Lujan* test is redressability. A plaintiff must demonstrate that a favorable judicial decision would redress the alleged injury. A redressable injury is one that is likely to be cured by the relief sought. In *Jamieson Greer*, the second injury theory relied on an informational interest in the fruits of OSC's investigation itself. The court notes that a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a statute entitled him to receive. However, the court found that § 1216(a)(3) creates no such entitlement. AFL acknowledged that OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary, citing *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.* Consequently, absent an alleged entitlement to receive information, a judicial order mandating an investigation would not redress the alleged injury.

The Court also emphasizes the limitation on the effect of a judicial order. Citing *Haaland v. Brackeen*, the court explains that redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale "might persuade actors who are not before the court." Even if an OSC investigation were ordered, the court cannot assume that OSC would exercise discretion to produce new information. The court finds that because AFL cannot satisfy even the more relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, it lacks redressability regardless of OSC's potential statutory duties. Thus, an order mandating an investigation under section 1216 would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL's second alleged injury. The court vacates the district court's judgment and remands with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

This chapter details the specific legal analysis where the court applies the standing test to the OSC investigation claims, concluding why the plaintiff lacks a protected legal interest.

# The Failure of Causation Regarding Informational Injury
In evaluating whether a plaintiff satisfies Article III standing, one of the crucial elements is the causal connection between the defendant's action and the plaintiff's injury. The District Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit noted that to have standing, a plaintiff must show that their alleged injury was "caused by the defendant." In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation (AFL), argued that it sought information regarding judicial recusals via the Department of Justice's (DOJ) Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. AFL contended that DOJ was withholding records based on a policy of not searching employee emails without employee consent. When the District Court ordered AFL to sue under FOIA and DOJ produced records, AFL still sought an investigation into the alleged FOIA policy from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC). AFL claimed OSC's refusal to investigate deprived it of information about DOJ's conduct.

The appellate court identified a fatal flaw in this causal chain in assessing Article III standing. The court reasoned that any informational injury stemming from DOJ's refusal to disclose agency records is traceable directly to the DOJ, not OSC. For a favorable judicial decision to redress AFL's alleged injury, OSC would need to "promptly initiate a proceeding," which would involve the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) potentially sanctioning DOJ employees for arbitrary withholding. The court noted that AFL's standing theory relied on a series of speculative choices made by independent actors like the MSPB and DOJ itself. Relying on *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), the appellate court held that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." As stated in the opinion, the court found that AFL did not demonstrate a causal link between OSC's inaction and the specific withholding of records. Consequently, because the injury could not be proven to be caused by OSC, AFL could not satisfy the redressability component of standing based on its interest in the information corporate withheld from DOJ.

# The Absence of a Statutory Entitlement to Information
Even where a plaintiff claims injury from the denial of a procedural right, Article III standing requires more than a theoretical grievance; the plaintiff must suffer a "concrete injury." *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992). In this context, the court applied the less strict standards for procedural rights but maintained that the injury must still be "concrete and particularized." *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009). Specifically, the appellate court noted that Article III standing is not met simply by challenging a statutory right to information. The court cited *Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau*, reaffirming that a "deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer standing.

Regarding AFL's second standing theory, the court focused on whether OSC's refusal to investigate created an "informational injury." The rule for informational standing requires that the plaintiff be denied information "that a statute entitled him to receive." AFL argued that OSC was required under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings and that doing so would implicitly require OSC to provide notices under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, similar to those for prohibited personnel practices. However, the court correctly observed that OSC acknowledged that its "decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements" in a section 1216 investigation remains "discretionary." Since AFL did not allege a statutory entitlement to receive notice or information in the first place—it was asserting an injury from the *absence* of an investigation that produced no information—AFL lacked a concrete injury. As the court reasoned, "To the contrary, [AFL] acknowledges cases holding that OSC's 'decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements' in a section 1216 investigation is 'discretionary.'" Without a specific entitlement to the information itself, the injury was not concrete enough to sustain Article III standing.

# Redressability: The Limits of Judicial Power Against Agencies
Standing doctrine requires that the plaintiff must show their injury is "redressed by a favorable decision." The court held that judicial orders must bind the parties before the court and not just persuade independent actors. In this case, the redressability issue centers on the enforcement power afforded to the judicial branch versus the agency. The court highlighted that even if OSC might be legally obligated to investigate, this did not solve the problem for AFL. If an order compelled OSC to investigate, it would likely require OSC to exercise enforcement discretion to obtain a finding or sanction, which ultimately affects the government agencies.

The court relied heavily on *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), which established that courts should not enter judgments compelling administrative agencies to exercise their discretion or to take enforcement action. This principle is vital in *Jamieson Greer* because OSC operates with inherent authority over administrative investigations. The court reasoned that AFL's theory relied on "speculation about [OSC's] choices," which courts generally may not compel. As stated in the opinion, "AFL overlooks the principle that redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" Because a judgment cannot force an agency to perform a discretionary act in a way that directly redresses the plaintiff's specific claim, the standing claim fails. Without redressability, the district court lacked the authority to order the investigation.

# Disposition: Vacatur and Instructions for Dismissal
Having analyzed the failure of causation, the lack of proprietary entitlement, and the general limits of agency redressability, the appellate court determined that AFL had not met the threshold requirements for standing. Since lack of standing precludes federal courts from hearing the case, the court determined that the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction over the claims. The judgment of the lower court, which had partially remanded the case and then dismissed it, was found to be without constitutional authority to proceed.

To resolve this, the appellate court vacated the district court’s judgment. The conclusion is: "Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court's judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction." Consequently, the case was returned to the lower court to dismiss the action without further consideration of the merits of OSC's enforcement powers or FOIA policies.
