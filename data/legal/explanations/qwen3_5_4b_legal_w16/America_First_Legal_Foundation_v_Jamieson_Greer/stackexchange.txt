Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why does a statutory procedural right (5 U.S.C. § 1216) generally fail to satisfy Article III standing without a concrete injury?
Question:
In 'Frazier v. MSPB' and 'Allen v. Wright,' the D.C. Circuit emphasizes that standing requires an 'invasion of a legally protected interest' that is 'concrete and particularized.' In this case, AFL argued they had a 'procedural right' under the Civil Service Reform Act to an OSC investigation. Despite the D.C. Circuit's recognition in cases like 'Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep't of Educ.' that standing standards may be relaxed for procedural injuries, the court here rejected the relaxation. Why was 'OSA's' failure to investigate considered 'procedural right in vacuo' instead of a violation of a right that protects a threatened concrete interest?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on the unanimous opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL __ (D.C. Cir. 10/3/2025), a statutory procedural right generally fails to satisfy Article III standing without a concrete injury because the constitutional bar on justiciable "Cases or Controversies" is absolute regarding injury-in-fact and causation, even when a statute supervises procedural duties. The court of appeals clarifies that a plaintiff must show an "invasion of a legally protected interest" that is "concrete and particularized." Merely alleging a violation of a procedural right—such as the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate—is insufficient if that procedural right exists without protecting an underlying tangible interest.

The court identifies this principle by stating that a "deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient to confer Article III standing" (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); *Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024)). While the D.C. Circuit acknowledges a modified standard for procedural cases (*Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005)), even those relaxed standards require a plaintiff to prove that the statutory violation caused "injury" to a "concrete, particularized interest."

In this dispute, AFL failed to meet this threshold for two reasons derived from the text of the opinion:

*   **Causation and Redressability Fail for Informational Standing:** AFL argued that OSC's refusal to investigate under § 1216 prevented them from obtaining requested FOIA records from the Department of Justice (DOJ). The court found this chain of causation to be too speculative. The alleged informational injury was "plainly traceable to DOJ," not OSC. Consequently, a favorable judgment against OSC would not likely redress the injury because the remedy would depend on "unfettered choices made by independent actors... like the MSPB and DOJ" (*FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024)).
*   **Injury in Fact Fail due to Lack of Entitlement:** Even regarding complaints about the informational fruits of the investigation itself (notices under § 1214), AFL lacked the necessary concrete injury. The court held that § 1216 creates a duty to investigate, but does not create an entitlement to information. Because the decision to follow § 1214 notice requirements in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary," AFL cannot claim a concrete injury without an existing entitlement to the specific information (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6.D.C. Cir. 2011)).

Therefore, the statutory procedural right under § 1216 alone does not suffice to satisfy the injury requirement of Article III, as it does not, in this instance, protect against a violation of a concrete interest.

### How does redressability fail when enforcement involves third-party federal agencies like the MSPB and DOJ?
Question:
The opinion describes a redressability chain where OSC would investigate → OSC pursues corrective action (1214/1215) → MSPB sanctions → DOJ produces records. The court cites 'FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.' to claim 'causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors.' Does this mean a plaintiff's redressability is inherently more difficult to prove if the potential remedy requires action by a separate administrative board or agency (like the MSPB) that is not a defendant in the suit?
Answer:
Yes, as a matter of Article III standing doctrine, proving redressability is indeed significantly more difficult when the alleged judicial remedy relies on subsequent actions by independent administrative bodies or agencies that are not parties to the suit. The Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* does not simply find a procedural hurdle; it highlights that standing requires a concrete, direct causal link that is not sufficiently attenuated by the "unfettered choices" of independent actors.

To understand why redressability fails in this context, one must examine how the D.C. Circuit reconciles the statutory enforcement chain with the constitutional requirement of redressability.

### The Standard of Redressability
First, under Article III standing, a plaintiff must allege an injury "likely to be redressed by a favorable decision." This means there must be a high probability that a court order compelling relief against the named defendant will actually cure the plaintiff's injury. The Supreme Court has clarified that the scope of redressability is determined by the legal effect of a court judgment. Crucially, a judgment binds only the parties before the court. It cannot bind third parties who are not before the bench.

*Haaland v. Brackeen* (cited at 599 U.S. 293–94 in the opinion) establishes this principle: "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'"

### The "Independent Actor" Problem
The specific challenge in this case arose because AFL’s proposed remedy relied on a hypothetical chain of events involving agencies they could not control. The causal chain AFL described was:

1.  OSC investigates (court order).
2.  OSC pursues corrective/disciplinary action (OSC discretion).
3.  MSPB sanctions DOJ employees (MSPB discretion).
4.  DOJ produces records (production depends on MSPB sanctions).

The D.C. Circuit rejected this chain on two specific grounds regarding independent actors and speculation:

**1. Unfettered Choices by Independent Actors**
The court relied on *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. v. United States*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), to note that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." In this case, that meant the MSPB and the DOJ. Because the court cannot guarantee how the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) will exercise its authority or how the DOJ will respond to potential sanctions, a judgment against OSC arguably would not "very likely [spur the next steps] necessary to redress" information injury.

**2. Discretionary Enforcement by Named Defendant**
The plaintiff also faced issues regarding the named defendant, OSC. While OSC searched for a statutory duty to investigate, the plaintiff acknowledged OSC's discretion in how it exercises enforcement power. The opinion notes that the chain "requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion," which "the courts generally may not compel," citing *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Even if OSC were to have a duty to investigate under § 1216, the injury (missing records from DOJ) is "attenuated" because it depends on outcomes beyond OSC’s direct control.

### Conclusion
In short, *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* confirms that while redressability is "relaxed" for procedural rights cases under the "injury in fact" prong (*Center for Law & Educ. v. Dept of Educ.*), it remains strictly limited by the "unfettered" nature of administrative enforcement. If the plaintiff's right to redress requires the plaintiff to assume an administrative board or agency will act in a specific way upon the defendant’s order, the injury is speculative and redressability fails. The court must focus on whether the judgment binds the defendant (OSC) to a specific change in conduct, not whether it indirectly influences the conduct of third parties like the MSPB.

Therefore, reliance on third-party federal agencies like the MSPB and DOJ makes redressability inherently harder to prove because the judgment cannot create the necessary causal continuity through actors who are legally and functionally distinct from the defendant.

### Why does an information claim lack standing when the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a statutory entitlement to the information?
Question:
The court distinguishes between 'injury in fact' from an informational perspective and the requirement that the plaintiff be granted information 'that a statute entitled him to receive.' AFL argued they were interested in the 'fruits of the OSC investigation.' However, the court noted OSC could exercise discretion over whether to provide § 1214 notices. Since AFL acknowledges OSC has 'discretion' in § 1216 investigations, why did the court conclude that even § 1216(a)(3)'s mandate to investigate does not create an entitlement to the specific disclosures needed to establish Article III standing?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Under the "injury in fact" standard, a plaintiff seeking information must show a concrete, legally protected interest. As the court explains, this means the plaintiff must demonstrate that the statute **entitles them to receive the specific information they claim to have been denied**. Because the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) retained discretion under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to determine whether to follow notice requirements from 5 U.S.C. § 1214, the mandatory investigation provision in § 1216 does not create a statutory entitlement to the disclosures necessary to establish standing.

**Detailed Analysis**

To answer why an information claim lacks standing without a statutory entitlement, one must look to the court’s definition of "concrete injury" for information claims and how it applies to the OSC’s statutory authority.

**1. The Statutory Entitlement Standard**
When plaintiffs claim injury based on the denial of information, Article III standing is not established unless they prove they were entitled to that information under the relevant statute. The court borrows this standard from *Asarco v. Feld Ent., Inc.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011), holding that:

> “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a statute entitled him to receive.”

Consequently, the court must first examine whether the underlying statute imposes a non-discretionary duty to provide the specific information the plaintiff requests.

**2. Investigation vs. Disclosure Rights**
AFL argued that 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) required the OSC to investigate, and that this investigation should yield the same disclosures as a § 1214 investigation. The court rejected this because the statutes are distinct:
*   **§ 1216 Mandatory Investigation:** This section mandates that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation" of FOIA violations.
*   **§ 1214 Optional Notices:** This section governs notifications to the party *if* OSC seeks corrective action.

As the court notes regarding AFL's second standing theory, while § 1216 mandates the investigation, it does not automatically mandate specific disclosures regarding the results. AFL acknowledged in their own submission cases holding that OSC's decision is "discretionary” regarding whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements within a § 1216 investigation (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Coun., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)*).

**3. Lack of Entitlement Renders No Injury**
Because the statute does not confer a right to receive § 1214 notices in a § 1216 context, AFL cannot claim a concrete injury. The court concluded:

> “AFL does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations… Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory.”

Furthermore, because the OSC has discretion not to follow those notice requirements, a judicial order would not "reasonably likely redress[ the alleged injury."

**Conclusion**
The court distinguishes between an informational harm resulting from a refusal to disclose and a harm resulting from a breach of a statutory right to disclosure. Since OSC’s discretion prevents a finding that AFL "statutorily entitled" them to the information, AFL fails the "injury in fact" requirement, and their case lacks Article III standing regardless of whether § 1216 mandates the investigation itself.

### Why did the appellate court vacate for standing rather than decide the district court's specific interpretation of 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)?
Question:
At the district court level, there was a ruling finding OSC's decision legal error because § 1216(a)(3) 'mandated' such an investigation regardless of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates. However, the D.C. Circuit declared OSC had discretion under § 1216(c) and vacated the judgment solely for lack of standing. Why did the Court choose to forego deciding the substantive statutory interpretation question, leaving the OSC's actual authority to investigate undiscovered in the face of a successful standing dismissal?
Answer:
**Why Standing Comes Before Merits: A Ruling on *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer***

The appellate court in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* decided to vacate the judgment for lack of standing rather than ruling on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) because Article III standing is a mandatory jurisdictional prerequisite to determining the merits of a case. Because the appellate court found the plaintiff failed to demonstrate a "concrete interest" and "redressability" sufficient to satisfy standing requirements, it was legally required to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The court effectively stated that in the absence of standing, "we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive."

**The Doctrine of Article III Standing**
Article III of the Constitution restricts the federal judicial power to resolving "Cases" and "Controversies." Accordingly, a plaintiff must establish an "invasion of a legally protected interest" that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent." The opinions in this case cite *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t* and *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife* to reinforce that standing is necessary before a court can reach the merits of the underlying claim.

In this instance, the plaintiff claimed a procedural injury arising from OSC’s alleged refusal to investigate. However, the court noted: "The deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient to confer Article III standing." To establish standing for a procedural violation, the plaintiff must show that the violation resulted in injury to a concrete interest. The D.C. Circuit observed that AFL satisfied neither the injury-in-fact nor causation requirements even under the "relaxed standing requirements that apply to cases involving alleged procedural injury."

**Absence of Redressability and Concrete Injury**
The court’s decision to avoid ruling on the statutory interpretation of § 1216(a)(3)—which the district court had held to "mandated" OSC's action—centered on the plaintiff's failure to prove that a dispute regarding OSC's liability was justiciablly actionable. The plaintiff failed on two primary theories:

1.  **FOIA Records (Causation):** The plaintiff argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented them from receiving records from DOJ. The appellate court found this chain of causation too speculative: "...causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Because any injury from the records was traceable to DOJ, and the connection to OSC was theoretical, the plaintiff could not show that a favorable judgment would redress the injury.
2.  **OSC Information Notice (Entitlement):** The plaintiff argued that an investigation would require OSC to provide notices under § 1214. However, the court noted that section 1216 does not create an entitlement to this information. As the opinion states: "Even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information." Furthermore, since OSC has no duty to engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto § 1216 investigations, there was no concrete interest affected.

**Conclusion**
As the opinion concludes regarding the disposition: "We vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction." The appellate court could not interpret the statutory mandate of § 1216(a)(3) because the plaintiff had not proven a concrete, participatory stake in the dispute. Without legal standing, the judicial question of whether OSC was legally required to investigate never matured into a valid action. Therefore, the court dismissed jurisdictionally rather than construing the statute.
