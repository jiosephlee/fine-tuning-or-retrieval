Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The 'Independent Actor' Dilemma: How Judicial Orders Fail to Cure Administrative Discretion

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that even if a statute commands an agency to act, a plaintiff may still lack the standing to enforce that command in court. While the D.C. Circuit generally allows for review of arbitrary administrative failures, the bar on Article III standing remains a formidable filter for cases involving third-party sovereign actors. In a case revolving around the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) power to investigate Freedom of Information Act violations, the Fourth Circuit saw Judge Katsas's treatise opinion vacate a decision on the merits because the plaintiff could not show that a judicial order would actually redress its alleged injury.

The underlying dispute involves OSC, an agency empowered to investigate "prohibited personnel practices," including the "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," which refers to FOIA violations. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is described as having a duty to conduct an investigation if such withholding occurs. In this instance, AFL alleged the Department of Justice (DOJ) had an arbitrary policy of not searching employee emails for FOIA requests without employee consent. Even though the district court found OSC had likely erred in law, the D.C. Circuit noted that the court has no power to issue judicial relief that mandates action by independent actors outside the investigation's reach. This highlights how the law distinguishes between agency discretion regarding disciplinary investigation and the inability of courts to compel compliance by agencies like DOJ in these contexts.

The standing analysis hinged on causation, a doctrine that the D.C. Circuit cited recently in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* as a barrier to intervention in agency-firm relationships. The court reasoned that a plaintiff cannot simply speculate that an OSC investigation would lead to corrective action by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and, subsequently, to DOJ record production. If OSC finds a violation, it may recommend corrective action under § 1214; the MSPB may then sanction the employee; the DOJ may subsequently decide to produce records. However, these choices are none of the government actors before the court. As the opinion notes, causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the "unfettered choices made by independent actors." This logic effectively severs the line between what an agency might legally do and what a court can order it to do when the outcome depends on the unfettered will of a third party with discretion.

Another dimension to this decision is the court's treatment of prospective obligations and entitlements. AFL argued that it suffered an informational injury because the OSC investigation failed to provide procedural notices required under § 1214. However, the court rejected the claim that OSC's duties under § 1216(a)(3) automatically triggered § 1214 notice requirements. The D.C. Circuit cited *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.* to establish that OSC retains discretion over whether to follow § 1214 procedural standards during an investigation for a prohibited personnel practice. Without a statutory entitlement to receive those specific notices or information from the OSC itself, the assertion remained non-entitled and therefore not concrete. This means that the court found no redressability even if the statutory mandate exists. A judicial order would not compel OSC to provide the notices it has deemed discretionary, rendering the claimed injury hypothetical.

Doctrinally, this case reinforces the principle that standing remains the prerequisite to judicial intervention in the broad field of administrative law. While Congress may explicitly create an investigative mechanism like OSC under § 1216, it cannot create the jurisdictional standing that allows a court to overrule agency discretion on issues that don't involve a direct claim right. This mimics the Supreme Court's stance in *Heckler v. Cheney*, which held that courts may not compel OSC to initiate enforcement proceedings. By emphasizing the boundaries of judicial power, the D.C. Circuit ensures that even when the OSC is mandated to investigate, judicial coercion remains limited to the direct relationship between the parties before the court.

Ultimately, the "Independent Actor" Dilemma suggests that statutory mandates for enforcement mechanisms do not bypass the strictures of Article III. A court's order must bind the defendant, yet if the defendant is part of a larger chain of independent judgment, the rest of the chain is not bound. In this case, the court's refusal to extend standing protects the judiciary from attempting to dictate the future decisions of the MSPB and DOJ through the language of a procedural right violation. The result is that even if OSC's statutory mandate is clear, the administrative landscape remains largely immune from judicial coercion if the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a concrete, actionable entitlement to the information sought.
