Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Statutory Duty vs. Judicial Discretion: Does the OSC Investigation Mandate Override FOIA?

## The Tension Between Merits and Standing

The decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* underscores a persistent friction in federal administrative law: when does a plaintiff have a right to compel a specialized regulator to do its job? The D.C. Circuit, in a 2-1 opinion authored by Circuit Judge Katsas, vacated and remanded the case to dismiss the plaintiff’s claims for lack of Article III standing. While the standing dismissal is procedural, the underlying statutory battle is substantive. The America First Legal Foundation relied on the office of special counsel's (OSC's) broad mandate to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, pushing it into a confrontation with a statutory framework designed for judicial rather than executive enforcement.

The reason this case matters lies in the potential use of OSC as a leverage point for FOIA plaintiffs. The FOIA itself grants OSC authority to investigate specific types of misconduct only when a court has already ordered production and identified arbitrary withholding. However, the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) retained a section of the Civil Service Reform Act (5 U.S.C. § 1216) that, on its face, appears to grant OSC's power to investigate arbitrary withholding without a prior judicial predicate. AFL sought to utilize this broader gap to bypass the need for immediate judicial orders on its part, arguing that OSC had a duty to investigate the DOJ's refusal to share emails upon FOIA request. By dismissing the case on standing grounds alone, the D.C. Court signaled that it will not become the arbiter of OSC's policy discretion, even when the statutory mandate seems clear.

## The Standing Obstacle

Under Article III jurisprudence, a plaintiff must show that an invasion of a legally protected interest is concrete and particularized, that it was actually caused by the defendant action, and that a favorable court decision would likely redress that injury. In *America First Legal Foundation*, the court identified a double-folding of causal and redressability problems that stripped the plaintiff of standing even under the relaxed procedural doctrines.

The first problem was attributing the informational injury. AFL argued that the failure to investigate ultimately meant DOJ would never produce the records. The court correctly pointed out that any "informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." The causal chain required speculation about whether an OSC investigation would force the Merit Systems Protection Board to discipline DOJ employees and compel DOJ to produce records, or whether DOJ would do so anyway. Since courts generally cannot speculate on the unfettered choices of independent actors, the remedy mandate became irrelevant to the actual injury.

The second problem concerned the procedural injury. AFL claimed a concrete interest in the information OSC would produce during an investigation, specifically citing Section 1214 notice requirements. However, OSC's decision on whether to follow those procedural requirements was discretionary. When the plaintiff conceded they didn't have an established entitlement to Section 1214 notices in a Section 1216 context they conceded neither element of standing was met. The court emphasized that attacking the investigation is insufficient; the plaintiff must show that the statute actually created an entitlement to receive the process itself. Without that, the injury is abstract, making the lack of standing functionally immunity rather than a matter of interpretation.

## Doctrinal Tensions and Enforcement

The *America First Legal Foundation* opinion leaves open the doctrinal question of whether § 1216 is truly a separate statutory duty or if it is subordinate to the FOIA-specific § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers. The opinion did not resolve whether § 1216 allows OSC to investigate without a prior FOIA court order. The lower court found OSC committed an error of law by refusing to investigate, but then granted discretion on whether to do so. This suggests a split view in the D.C. Judiciary, where the judicial branch can see but not engage with.

This creates a potential downstream rift in administrative law. Future plaintiffs might attempt to raise the merits question without the standing hurdle, or argue that the standing dismissal is insufficient. The D.C. Circuit's refusal to cover the investigation question means that the OSC's statutory power is pragmatic rather than theoretical. If a court cannot compel the investigation, the statutory duty is existential. This could lead to more aggressive litigation by plaintiffs who believe the OSC should simply be mandated to act on the merits, ignoring that many administrative agency actions are subject to the enforcement discretion standard. The D.C. Court's distinction between the merits (where § 1216 might apply) and the standing (which always bars the suit) creates a structural check on the OSC's reach. If the OSC refuses to investigate and the court validates the refusal on an independent basis, the remedies for statutory violations will remain limited to judicial enforcement, which is not available to OSC in many administrative contexts without its own agreement.

Ultimately, the case is about the limits of statutory interpretation in an era where agencies often claim broad mandates. The court maintains that Article III boundaries remain intact, ensuring that the judiciary does not become a tool for planning future administrative adjudications. The opinion suggests that for OSC to be effectively utilized as an investigative check, plaintiffs must still meet the high bar of standing, reinforcing the core principle that administrative obligations cannot be forced into suits where the harm is not sufficiently particularized.
