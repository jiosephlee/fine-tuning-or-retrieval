Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Beyond 'Redressability': The 'Concrete Injury' Bar for Institutional Transparencies

## Standing as a Barrier to Administrative Enforcement Strategies

The intersection of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the administrative enforcement powers of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) has long offered public interest organizations a unique vehicle to challenge agency noncompliance. However, the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit has recently solidified a high bar for success in such undertakings. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the panel authored by Judge Katsas ruled that a public interest NGO lacks Article III standing to compel OSC investigations intended to force the Department of Justice (DOJ) to disclose records. While the court noted that OSC is empowered under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings, it dismissed the suit based on a rigorous application of standing doctrine, specifically targeting the "concrete injury" and causal link requirements. This decision marks a significant tightening of the rules for NGOs attempting to use administrative mandates as a proxy for information disclosure, reaffirming that statutory enforcement powers do not automatically equate to litigation rights for third-party plaintiffs.

## The Rejection of 'Procedural Rights in Vacuo'

A foundational theme in the linguage of modern administrative litigation stems from the D.C. Circuit's reliance on *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and recent cases like *Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau*. In *Summers*, the Supreme Court established that a plaintiff suffering a "procedural injury in vacuo"—that is, a deprived procedural right without some concrete interest affected—is insufficient for standing. In *America First Legal Foundation*, the plaintiffs attempted to rely on their procedural right to have OSC investigate alleged policy violations in DOJ. The Court, however, reasoned that without a concrete entitlement to the fruits of that specific investigation or statutory notice, no actual injury is incurred by the refusal to proceed. The plaintiffs argued that they deserved the same information as notifying parties under Section 1214 by engaging OSC under Section 1216, but the Court was dismissive of this expansion. While Section 1214 creates a right to notice for "prohibited personnel practices," Section 1216 does not require OSC to engraft those specific notice procedures onto its investigations of FOIA violations, nor does it create a statutory entitlement to the specific information flowing from the investigation.

## The Causation and Redressability Problem

Perhaps the most limiting doctrinal hurdle in the opinion is the Court's analysis of causation and redressability. The American First Legal Foundation argued that OSC's failure to investigate meant DOJ would never disclose the internal records released under the broader FOIA request. The Court dismantled this link instantly, noting that the plaintiff's informational injury was traceable to the DOJ, the primary respondent for the FOIA request, not the OSC. The opinion draws a clear causal chain the Court deemed too speculative for standing purposes: OSC investigation -> OSC corrective action -> Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) sanction -> DOJ production of records. The D.C. Circuit explicitly stated that Article III standing cannot rely on speculation about the choices of independent actors, specifically here including the MSPB and DOJ. The opinion leans heavily on the principle that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties in front of the court, rather than on the likelihood of "persuasive" impacts on the government's future discretionary decisions. This effectively carves out the central, yet highly contested, power of OSC from the hands of Third-party plaintiffs, preventing them from suing for a remedy that is essentially a contingent internal government process shielded by consideration of discretion.

## Doctrinal Implications for FOIA and Administrative Litigation

The lasting effect of *America First Legal Foundation* on administrative law and standing doctrine is the erosion of the "instrumental" use of administrative agencies in FOIA enforcement. In previous instances, plaintiffs have been allowed to argue that the absence of agency enforcement obscures the public record. This decision suggests that unless a plaintiff has a personal concrete interest in the outcome of the administrative proceeding or a clear statutory entitlement to the specific information, the Administrative branch remains largely invulnerable to suit when it declines to enforce its own powers on behalf of a whistleblower or agency. This does not necessarily negate legitimate FOIA claims, as the substantive discoveries in the *America First* case were not disputed themselves, but it severely limits the strategy where NGOs use the OSC as a super-FOIA office. For practitioners, the path forward remains on the grounds of a specific agency's deliberate withholding of records through FOIA rather than trying to pierce that withholding via an administrative investigation.



# The 'Independent Actor' Dilemma: How Judicial Orders Fail to Cure Administrative Discretion

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that even if a statute commands an agency to act, a plaintiff may still lack the standing to enforce that command in court. While the D.C. Circuit generally allows for review of arbitrary administrative failures, the bar on Article III standing remains a formidable filter for cases involving third-party sovereign actors. In a case revolving around the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) power to investigate Freedom of Information Act violations, the Fourth Circuit saw Judge Katsas's treatise opinion vacate a decision on the merits because the plaintiff could not show that a judicial order would actually redress its alleged injury.

The underlying dispute involves OSC, an agency empowered to investigate "prohibited personnel practices," including the "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," which refers to FOIA violations. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is described as having a duty to conduct an investigation if such withholding occurs. In this instance, AFL alleged the Department of Justice (DOJ) had an arbitrary policy of not searching employee emails for FOIA requests without employee consent. Even though the district court found OSC had likely erred in law, the D.C. Circuit noted that the court has no power to issue judicial relief that mandates action by independent actors outside the investigation's reach. This highlights how the law distinguishes between agency discretion regarding disciplinary investigation and the inability of courts to compel compliance by agencies like DOJ in these contexts.

The standing analysis hinged on causation, a doctrine that the D.C. Circuit cited recently in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* as a barrier to intervention in agency-firm relationships. The court reasoned that a plaintiff cannot simply speculate that an OSC investigation would lead to corrective action by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and, subsequently, to DOJ record production. If OSC finds a violation, it may recommend corrective action under § 1214; the MSPB may then sanction the employee; the DOJ may subsequently decide to produce records. However, these choices are none of the government actors before the court. As the opinion notes, causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the "unfettered choices made by independent actors." This logic effectively severs the line between what an agency might legally do and what a court can order it to do when the outcome depends on the unfettered will of a third party with discretion.

Another dimension to this decision is the court's treatment of prospective obligations and entitlements. AFL argued that it suffered an informational injury because the OSC investigation failed to provide procedural notices required under § 1214. However, the court rejected the claim that OSC's duties under § 1216(a)(3) automatically triggered § 1214 notice requirements. The D.C. Circuit cited *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.* to establish that OSC retains discretion over whether to follow § 1214 procedural standards during an investigation for a prohibited personnel practice. Without a statutory entitlement to receive those specific notices or information from the OSC itself, the assertion remained non-entitled and therefore not concrete. This means that the court found no redressability even if the statutory mandate exists. A judicial order would not compel OSC to provide the notices it has deemed discretionary, rendering the claimed injury hypothetical.

Doctrinally, this case reinforces the principle that standing remains the prerequisite to judicial intervention in the broad field of administrative law. While Congress may explicitly create an investigative mechanism like OSC under § 1216, it cannot create the jurisdictional standing that allows a court to overrule agency discretion on issues that don't involve a direct claim right. This mimics the Supreme Court's stance in *Heckler v. Cheney*, which held that courts may not compel OSC to initiate enforcement proceedings. By emphasizing the boundaries of judicial power, the D.C. Circuit ensures that even when the OSC is mandated to investigate, judicial coercion remains limited to the direct relationship between the parties before the court.

Ultimately, the "Independent Actor" Dilemma suggests that statutory mandates for enforcement mechanisms do not bypass the strictures of Article III. A court's order must bind the defendant, yet if the defendant is part of a larger chain of independent judgment, the rest of the chain is not bound. In this case, the court's refusal to extend standing protects the judiciary from attempting to dictate the future decisions of the MSPB and DOJ through the language of a procedural right violation. The result is that even if OSC's statutory mandate is clear, the administrative landscape remains largely immune from judicial coercion if the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a concrete, actionable entitlement to the information sought.



# Statutory Duty vs. Judicial Discretion: Does the OSC Investigation Mandate Override FOIA?

## The Tension Between Merits and Standing

The decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* underscores a persistent friction in federal administrative law: when does a plaintiff have a right to compel a specialized regulator to do its job? The D.C. Circuit, in a 2-1 opinion authored by Circuit Judge Katsas, vacated and remanded the case to dismiss the plaintiff’s claims for lack of Article III standing. While the standing dismissal is procedural, the underlying statutory battle is substantive. The America First Legal Foundation relied on the office of special counsel's (OSC's) broad mandate to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, pushing it into a confrontation with a statutory framework designed for judicial rather than executive enforcement.

The reason this case matters lies in the potential use of OSC as a leverage point for FOIA plaintiffs. The FOIA itself grants OSC authority to investigate specific types of misconduct only when a court has already ordered production and identified arbitrary withholding. However, the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) retained a section of the Civil Service Reform Act (5 U.S.C. § 1216) that, on its face, appears to grant OSC's power to investigate arbitrary withholding without a prior judicial predicate. AFL sought to utilize this broader gap to bypass the need for immediate judicial orders on its part, arguing that OSC had a duty to investigate the DOJ's refusal to share emails upon FOIA request. By dismissing the case on standing grounds alone, the D.C. Court signaled that it will not become the arbiter of OSC's policy discretion, even when the statutory mandate seems clear.

## The Standing Obstacle

Under Article III jurisprudence, a plaintiff must show that an invasion of a legally protected interest is concrete and particularized, that it was actually caused by the defendant action, and that a favorable court decision would likely redress that injury. In *America First Legal Foundation*, the court identified a double-folding of causal and redressability problems that stripped the plaintiff of standing even under the relaxed procedural doctrines.

The first problem was attributing the informational injury. AFL argued that the failure to investigate ultimately meant DOJ would never produce the records. The court correctly pointed out that any "informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." The causal chain required speculation about whether an OSC investigation would force the Merit Systems Protection Board to discipline DOJ employees and compel DOJ to produce records, or whether DOJ would do so anyway. Since courts generally cannot speculate on the unfettered choices of independent actors, the remedy mandate became irrelevant to the actual injury.

The second problem concerned the procedural injury. AFL claimed a concrete interest in the information OSC would produce during an investigation, specifically citing Section 1214 notice requirements. However, OSC's decision on whether to follow those procedural requirements was discretionary. When the plaintiff conceded they didn't have an established entitlement to Section 1214 notices in a Section 1216 context they conceded neither element of standing was met. The court emphasized that attacking the investigation is insufficient; the plaintiff must show that the statute actually created an entitlement to receive the process itself. Without that, the injury is abstract, making the lack of standing functionally immunity rather than a matter of interpretation.

## Doctrinal Tensions and Enforcement

The *America First Legal Foundation* opinion leaves open the doctrinal question of whether § 1216 is truly a separate statutory duty or if it is subordinate to the FOIA-specific § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers. The opinion did not resolve whether § 1216 allows OSC to investigate without a prior FOIA court order. The lower court found OSC committed an error of law by refusing to investigate, but then granted discretion on whether to do so. This suggests a split view in the D.C. Judiciary, where the judicial branch can see but not engage with.

This creates a potential downstream rift in administrative law. Future plaintiffs might attempt to raise the merits question without the standing hurdle, or argue that the standing dismissal is insufficient. The D.C. Circuit's refusal to cover the investigation question means that the OSC's statutory power is pragmatic rather than theoretical. If a court cannot compel the investigation, the statutory duty is existential. This could lead to more aggressive litigation by plaintiffs who believe the OSC should simply be mandated to act on the merits, ignoring that many administrative agency actions are subject to the enforcement discretion standard. The D.C. Court's distinction between the merits (where § 1216 might apply) and the standing (which always bars the suit) creates a structural check on the OSC's reach. If the OSC refuses to investigate and the court validates the refusal on an independent basis, the remedies for statutory violations will remain limited to judicial enforcement, which is not available to OSC in many administrative contexts without its own agreement.

Ultimately, the case is about the limits of statutory interpretation in an era where agencies often claim broad mandates. The court maintains that Article III boundaries remain intact, ensuring that the judiciary does not become a tool for planning future administrative adjudications. The opinion suggests that for OSC to be effectively utilized as an investigative check, plaintiffs must still meet the high bar of standing, reinforcing the core principle that administrative obligations cannot be forced into suits where the harm is not sufficiently particularized.
