Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and the Standard for Staying Injunctions

## Procedural Background and Expedited Briefing Order

To understand the procedural posture of the Ninth Circuit Panel Order in *Pacito v. Trump*, it is essential to view the case not merely as a dispute over immigration policy, but as a series of temporary judicial interventions designed to manage the potential halt of refugee admissions pending a final decision on the merits. By the time of this order, issued on September 12, 2025, the district court in Western District of Washington had already issued multiple preliminary injunctions in the months prior to the case transferring to the appellate level. On February 25, 2025, the district court orally issued an injunction prohibiting the enforcement of Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the USRAP. This was followed by written orders on February 28 and March 24, which expanded the scope of the injunction to include the suspension of USRAP funding and the termination of cooperative agreements. The government filed notices of appeal on both dates, prompting the Ninth Circuit to issue partial stays to prevent the nationwide suspension from becoming immediately absolute.

The court in this decision works on an expedited basis for urgent constitutional challenges involving immigration policy. Oral arguments were held on September 3, 2025, and the panel issued this interim Order on September 12, 2025. This timeline demonstrates the urgency of the case regarding the immediate real-world effect of the injunctions. While the court typically allows a full trial to conclude, the nature of an injunction concerns the government and the public interest that "permanent validity" must be evaluated through a temporary mechanism. This order is technically an Order of Stay Pending Appeal, meaning it is not the court's final opinion on the merits of the case. Instead, it serves as a procedural vehicle to ensure that the district court's orders do not remain effective without a final appellate ruling. The government and plaintiffs were required to continue briefing until this point, though they had been notified that oral argument was already held for the merits.

## The Fourth Factor Test: *Nken v. Holder* Standards

The central legal standard governing a judge's decision to stay an order pending appeal is established in the Supreme Court case of *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). In this opinion, the Ninth Circuit explicitly anchored its decision to vacate and issue a new stay within the framework set forth by *Nken*. The standard requires the court to consider four specific factors when deciding whether to temporarily halt a judicial order before a final appeal decision is rendered: (1) whether the applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether irreparable injury will occur in the absence of a stay; (3) whether issuance of a stay will substantially injure other parties involved in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. While the *Nken* Court acknowledged multiple factors, it identified the first two as the most critical for initial relief.

In this case, the panel order relies heavily on the first two factors to determine the scope of the stay. The text notes that the government is likely to succeed on the merits regarding the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. The court cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to support the conclusion that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is an expansive grant of authority to the President. It states that the language is "clear, and the Executive Order does not exceed any textual limit." Because the court is confident in the President's authority under the Immigration and Nationality Act, the "showing of success on the merits" favors the government. The panel is reluctant to subject the President's justifications to a "searching inquiry" at the interim stage. This reliance on the *Nken* test allows the appellate court to exercise its control over the district court's injunctions based on what appears likely to happen on the case's final merits.

## Vacating Administrative Stays: The Court's Action

A critical component of the Panel Order is the court's active management of procedural interventions through the mechanism of vacating prior stays. Prior to this order, the court had issued various administrative stays, starting with an administrative stay on July 18, 2025. There were also previous stays issued on March 25, as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The text refers to "the panel’s administrative stay" as a specific term of art, describing the procedural status of a ruling that affects the proceedings but is not the final judgment. "Vacating" in this context means formally canceling a previous stay, thereby making the current Order the governing force for the remaining duration of the stay.

This action was taken as the parties filed further motions, including the government's emergency motion of July 17. The court required the district court’s orders to be stayed "by judicial action." By vacating the earlier administrative stays, the court ensured that the procedures were aligned and did not create conflict between a three-date stay of the injunction and the subsequent attempts to enforce the stay. This vacatur mechanism is common when appeals evolve into complex disputes with overlapping jurisdiction. By vacating the previous administrative stay, the court clarified its authority on the procedural issue of how to enforce the final stay pending the merits opinion.

## Scope of the Final Stay vs. District Court Orders

The ultimate disposition of *Pacito v. Trump* regarding relief is defined by the relationship between the appellate court's stay order and the district court orders. The Panel Order states, "The district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 preliminary injunctions are stayed in their entirety," with the notable exception being the specific "modalities" mentioned in the opinion. This means the temporary injunction that prohibited the suspension of the refugee program in the district court remains in a state of suspension.

While the text references a direction to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the core procedural lesson here is in "waiting" for the opinion on the merits. The court is staying the District Court orders because the final ruling on the merits of the § 1182(f) authority is reserved for a subsequent opinion. The order establishes a procedural buffer: it prevents the district court's injunction from standing in finality while the permanent court opinion is drafted. Essentially, the district court's orders are cast aside and re-evaluated against the backdrop of the Ninth Circuit's final opinion on the immigration statutes' constitutionality and statutory application. The remaining factors, such as domestic interest as determined under statutory interpretation, generally favor the United States as well.

# Chapter 2: Branch Discretion and the Suspension of Refugee Admissions

When reading a panel order such as this one from *Pacito v. Trump*, students must recognize that even in cases described as "on an expedited basis," the court often uses intermediate orders to clarify the procedural standards of stay or invoke administrative precedents before issuing a full merits opinion. In this particular section of the opinion, the Ninth Circuit analyzes the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions. To understand the court’s reasoning, a student must identify how the majority affirms the standard set forth in *Trump v. Hawaii* to determine that the government’s assertion of power is presumptively valid.

## Factual Summary: Executive Orders 14163 and 14169

The background section of the opinion establishes the legislative and executive actions that form the basis of the plaintiffs’ claim. The facts note that on January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” This order suspended the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) pending further findings that entry would not be detrimental to the United States. Concurrently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended funding for USRAP pursuant to Executive Order No. 14169. The plaintiffs, whom the court characterizes as refugees outside the United States or those approved for resettlement, challenged the termination of these programs. They alleged in February 2025 that the executive orders violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act. While the district court granted a preliminary injunction against the enforcement of these orders, this Ninth Circuit order serves as a stay pending full resolution, where the court distinguishes between the suspension of entry and the implementation of services for admitted refugees.

## Statutory Authority: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

To determine whether the President has the authority to suspend refugee entry, the court looks to the specific statutory authority granted by Congress. The opinion relies heavily on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which provides that the President may suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens if he finds that such entry would be detrimental to the interest of the United States. For the student, note how the opinion quotes the statute directly to anchor the President’s power: "he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The court interprets this provision not as a mandate requiring the President to suspend, but as a grant of power. This statutory language gives the Executive Branch the lead authority to define what constitutes "detrimental to the interests of the United States." In reading the opinion, students should observe that the court focuses on the text of § 1182(f) to establish the baseline from which the President’s discretion is measured.

## Deference Standard Reaffirmed: *Trump v. Hawaii* Principles

Because the plaintiffs challenge the President’s refusal to admit refugees, the court must apply a standard of judicial review appropriate for executive power. In this section, the majority reaffirms the deference standard established in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). The opinion states that the authority under § 1182(f) "exudes deference" and vests the President with "ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated." The court explicitly notes that it must not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications," citing *Hawaii* at 585 U.S. at 684. This is a critical procedural takeaway for law students: under this specific deference framework, the reviewing court does not act as a substantive policy arbiter. As the court explains, "The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The court treats the President’s determination of detriment as conclusive for the purposes of this stay order.

## Application: Finding of Unreversed Deference and Valid Entry Suspension

The final subtopic requires the student to understand how the court applies the deference standard to the specific lawful claims. In the Discussion section, the court finds that the government is "likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions." Consequently, the court holds that the remaining factors of the stay-of-action inquiry, including those touching on foreign interests and the broad discretion conferred on the President, favor the United States. The opinion concludes that the Executive Order does "not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority" to suspend the entry of a class of aliens. The application here demonstrates that when a challenge rests solely on the statutory proof of "detrimental to the interest of the United States," the Court of Appeals defers to the executive claim. This application results in the stay of the district court’s preliminary injunction regarding the suspension of USRAP funding and repairs the prior administrative stay, leaving the first order on the merits regarding entry suspended pending further review.

# Chapter 3: Resettlement Services and the Statutory Duty of the Executive Branch

## Chapter Description

This chapter examines the corresponding ruling on the funding and rescheduling of refugee resettlement services. It contrasts the standard for emergency stays with the court's analysis of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and the statutory text's implications for funding obligations versus discretionary authority.

# APA Claim and Statutory Basis: 8 U.S.C. § 1522

The plaintiffs in *Pacito v. Trump* filed suits in the Western District of Washington alleging that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee admissions program and the Department of State’s defunding of services violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq., and the Refugee Act of 1980. While the district court granted preliminary injunctions to restrain the suspension of refugee admissions (Executive Order 14163) based on *Trump v. Hawaii* deference credentials, the Ninth Circuit panel focused its interim stay order on the distinct statutory requirements for domestic resettlement services. Specifically, the court addressed 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs the domestic resettlement of refugees and the provision of reception and placement services.

The Administrative Procedure Act provides the procedural framework for challenging agency actions, including the decision to terminate cooperative agreements or suspend funding without statutory justification. In *Pacito*, the panel noted that the government argued the termination of agreements did not violate the APA because they were acting under the Executive Order and within budgetary constraints. However, the panel found the government’s variance between the statutory text and its actions significant. The key issue was not whether the administration could choose to issue an Executive Order, but whether the unification of those orders violated statutory mandates regarding specific resource obligations for refugees. The GOP must have had a reason to understand the statute as authorization rather than mandate to justify the termination without funding.

## The Obligation to Provide Reception and Placement Services

The Governing Body held that the Government is directed to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 for refugees admitted to the United States before the suspension. This directive reflects the court’s determination that the government’s uncertainty about its ability to provide these services compelled an inquiry into the statutory obligations rather than a deferral to discretionary judgment. The majority explained that the previous orders from the lower court were stayed, but specifically instructed the government to reinstate the agreements.

The court relied on the text of § 1522 to craft this instruction, noting that the statute houses provisions for employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and interagency coordination. The opinion highlighted that these services are often referred to as "reception and placement" services and must be achieved as quickly as possible. The court reasoned that the termination of cooperative agreements by the State Department created an immediate gap in the statutory mandate. By directing the reinstatment of agreements, the court signaled that the government had a duty to fulfill the statutory requirements for newly admitted refugees, even during the period where executive discretion regarding admissions is suspended.

The majority’s reasoning hinged on the functional necessity of maintaining the status quo of reception services to protect the rights of those already inside the country. Where the government terminated agreements for processing support abroad or domestic resettlement services, the court found that this action essentially halted the statutory process of resettlement. The stay order functioned as a temporary enforcement of the statutory duty, holding that the government could invoke budgetary concerns later, but could not simply terminate the ability to serve refugees pending the conclusion of the appeal. This provided the legal signal that the refugee protection mechanisms had protection from individual discretion regarding the government's internal funding pipeline.

## Statutory Interpretation: "Authorization" vs. "Mandate"

The legal debate regarding § 1522 centers on whether the statutory language imposes a mandatory duty on the executive branch or merely authorizes discretionary programs. The Majority's opinion interprets § 1522 (Title "Authorization for programs...") as imposing a substantive obligation to provide services when admitted. Conversely, Circuit Judge Lee, in a concurrence, argues that the statutory text uses permissive language indicative of authorization rather than a mandate. The majority deferred to the statutory text which states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance under specific subparts, implying that while funding is mandated by appropriation, the services themselves are obligatory if funding exists.

Judge Lee’s dissent contends that the word "authorization" appears prominently in the statute’s title and throughout the provisions. For example, § 1522(b)-(e) states the Director "is authorized" to make cash assistance available. Judge Lee interprets "shall" in earlier provisions only as imposing conditions on the exercise of discretion, not creating a duty to exercise that discretion. Specifically, § 1522(a) imposes "conditions and consideration," but the dissent argues these take effect "In providing assistance under this section." Therefore, the power to provide assistance remains at the agency's discretion. Judge Lee’s dissent emphasizes that the Executive Branch possesses broad discretion with appropriations flowing, and the lack of a firm mandate means the court lacks a basis to order the agency to reinstate agreements without statutory requirement. This case illustrates the tension between the judiciary's ability to enforce statutory obligations and the executive's authority over administrative budgets and policy directions.

## Disparity Against Government: Abiding by the 2025 Appropriations

The panel repeatedly referenced the Federal Appropriations Act of FY 2025 to address the government's claim of budgetary constraints or war. The text notes Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, but this sum was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The court used this context to determine that the reduction in appropriations was not total even if grants ceased. The Majority reasoned that while available funding might fluctuate, the statutory obligation to provide services to admitted refugees persists regardless of the specific dollar amount, as long as funding is available.

This reasoning relies on the statutory phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" found in § 1522. The court noted that the government had acknowledged the funding constraints but admitted to uncertainty regarding the ability to secure new agreements for a new agency. By citing the appropriations landscape, the panel highlighted that the government could not cite lack of funds as a blanket exemption from the statutory duty to reinstate agreements. The discrepancy between the initial funding levels and the rescissed amounts meant the government’s actions to terminate agreements independently of the statutory shift were inconsistent with the requirement to provide services "to the extent of available appropriations." The court thereby recognized that while funding levels matter, they do not suspend the broader statutory mandate to maintain the legal framework for resettlement.

# Chapter 4: Dissenting Opinion and the Role of Judicial Discretion in Funding

## Judge Lee's Core Argument Against Judicial Mandate

Circuit Judge Lee's concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part opinion provides a critical counterpoint to the majority's stay order, focusing specifically on the boundaries between judicial authority and executive budgetary discretion. While the majority utilized the administrative stays to halt the District Court’s preliminary injunctions regarding refugee admissions suspension, Lee argues that the judiciary should have stayed the entire injunction without ordering the reinstatement of specific cooperative agreements. Interestingly, Lee concedes that the President possesses broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, aligning with the majority’s suspension of the primary policy challenge. However, he dissents sharply on the issue of domestic service funding. He posits that the District Court's orders, which required the Department of State to reinstate cooperative agreements, effectively commanded the Executive Branch to expend funds and execute administrative duties. In the eyes of Judge Lee, this crosses the line from judicial oversight into judicial expenditure, forcing the government to act contrary to its assessment of available resources. His core argument is that the courts lack the competence to mandate funding levels or service continuity that Congress may have intentionally left to executive choice, particularly in the context of immigration where agencies balance foreign policy and logistical realities.

## Textual Analysis: "Authorization" as Permissive, Not Compulsory

Central to Judge Lee’s dissent is a rigorous textual analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the statute governing refugee assistance. He objections to the majority's interpretation of this statute relies heavily on legislative drafting choices that suggest permissiveness. The dissent begins by pointing to the section title, which reads "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." In statutory construction law, the title of a section often informs its substantive scope, and "authorization" implies permission rather than obligation. Furthermore, Lee emphasizes the statutory language throughout subsections (b) through (e), which repeatedly uses the word "authorized" regarding specific services such as employment training, English language courses, and cash assistance. For example, the statute notes that the Director "is authorized" to make resources available for employment training. Lee argues that the use of "authorized" distinctively signals Congressional deference to the Executive Branch regarding funding availability. He cites the "conditions and consideration" subsection (a) to highlight that even the mandatory language ("shall") is qualified by "to the extent of available appropriations." Therefore, the requirement to provide services is conditional on the existence of appropriations, which the Executive Branch controls, rather than a flat mandate. This textualist approach suggests that the statute grants the Executive discretion to forgo services if funding stops, rather than creating a judicially enforceable duty to provide them regardless of agency readiness.

## Interim Conclusion: Prompt Remedies vs. Substantive Mandates

In his final section of the dissent, Judge Lee addresses the procedural posture and the appropriate remedy for a stay order. While the majority issued a stay pending final resolution and directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements, Lee advocates for a broader stay that does not compel specific administrative actions. This distinction highlights a fundamental tension in public law litigation: the difference between preventing an unlawful action (remedy) and mandating a legislative intent (substantive mandate). Lee argues that while the courts must address violations of clear rights, they cannot rewrite executive policy through spurious funding orders. His dissent suggests that the judiciary should focus solely on halting the suspension of the refugee program if the President found sufficient grounds, rather than intervening in the logistics of resettlement. He warns that creating judicial mandates for funding violates the separation of powers principle, particularly when Congress has elected to allow the Executive to manage the "Executive Branch's discretion over funding." For a law student, this signifies that not all statutory prohibitions can be overcome by litigation; if the statute explicitly grants discretion, the court generally cannot force the executive to utilize that discretion unless the agency is acting *ultra vires* (beyond its authority) in a way that defies the text.

## Significance for Future Immigration Litigation and Funding Claims

Judge Lee’s approach offers significant lessons for future immigration and policy litigation, specifically regarding statutory interpretation of funding statutes. In cases where Congress authorizes programs rather than mandating them, litigants who argue for the existence of a funding mandate must overcome the presumption of permissive language. This case illustrates the importance of parsing the verb used in the statute—whether the agency is "required" or "authorized"—to determine the scope of judicial intervention. Furthermore, this dissent highlights the risk associated with injunctive relief in areas where courts possess limited expertise over economic and administrative policy. It suggests that when the remedy sought is an order to perform new administrative duties or spend money, courts must be cautious not to encroach upon the Executive Branch's traditional margin of discretion. For future litigators, this implies that challenges to funding actions should focus on the *process* of funding and *appropriations* history rather than demanding specific service continuity unless Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity. Additionally, it underscores that detention or service continuation orders are less likely to be upheld when the underlying funding is not Congressional money, but rather executive resource decisions, meaning the bar for injunctive relief in these specific funding contexts is higher.
