Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Deference to Entry, Not Service: How *Pacito* Distinguishes Presidential Power in Immigration Admissions from Mandatory Resettlement Duties

The recent general trend of tightening refugee protections in the judiciary has placed immense pressure on executive branch discretion, particularly regarding the boundaries of emergency measures versus statutory obligations. The Ninth Circuit’s panel order in *Pacito v. Trump* helps clarify this boundary during a moment of heightened policy tension. While the panel paused the injunctions litigated by the plaintiffs, the underlying merits analysis suggests a critical doctrine: the President possesses broad, deferential authority to control the threshold of who enters the United States, but the federal government retains a distinct, statutory duty to provide resettlement services to those who have already been admitted. This distinction matters profoundly for public trust and the long-term stability of the refugee resettlement system, establishing a precedent where exclusionary entry power does not necessarily shield the administration from accountability regarding care and accommodation of people already at risk of harm.

## Presumptive Validity of Admission Suspension

In the first major legal fork, the Ninth Circuit analyzed the suspension of entry under Executive Order 14163. The court applied the deferential standard established in *Trump v. Hawaii*, finding the government likely to prevail on the claim that the President has authority to determine refugee entry. The court reasoned that the statutory authorization under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is broad, requiring only a finding of "detriment" to the United States, which the Executive has met. Consequently, the panel determined it could not engage in a searching inquiry into the President's justifications for the suspension.

This alignment with *Trump v. Hawaii* signals that courts are willing to treat the enforcement of the 1182(f) categorical ban with significant deference. This is a substantive ruling on the merits that bears a limit on judicial intervention regarding the *admission* component of the policy. By not granting a final ruling on the merits in this stage of the stay, the court enforces the principle that the Executive's decision to suspend the refugee “channel” is broadly acceptable unless clearly contrary to the statutory framework. This creates a permissive window for the administration to implement new entry policies or maintain existing restrictions pending further legislative action or judicial review on the substantive record created by 1182(f).

## Resettlement Services as a Statutory Mandate

The second prong of the decision created a notable separation from the admission issue. While the President’s power over entry was treated as largely plenary, the panel held that the government’s failure to secure cooperative agreements for receiving and placing refugees violated the requirements of the Refugee Act of 1980, specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Under this section of law, Congress sets specific conditions and obligations that the Office of Refugee Resettlement must meet should it utilize available appropriations to administer services to admitted refugees.

The court distinguished the administrative deflection of the government’s argument from its own likely suspension on presidential authority. The text indicates that the government was not likely to prevail on the challenge that the *APA* was satisfied regarding these services. The panel reasoned that although the program of resettlement services relies on appropriations—conferred limits on "to the extent of available appropriations"—the statutory language imposes "conditions and considerations" on any use of such funds. Even if interpreted broadly, the court found that the executive branch could not simply terminate a network of resettlement contracts without seeking to provide equivalent services within the confines of the law. This ordering of reinstatement suggests that the government's discretion under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is constrained by the statutory duty to provide specific reception services for refugees already in transit or admitted.

## Doctrinal Implications and Future Accountability

The *Pacito* order establishes a nuanced framework for administrative discretion within the immigration apparatus. The most significant outcome is the separation of powers regarding the entry point from the status point of a refugee. The court validates the President's ability to halt entry without certain judicial scrutiny but simultaneously reinforces that once a refugee admits to the United States and is part of the resettlement pipeline, the Executive Branch cannot simply unilaterally sever the funding streams or support networks established by Congress.

This creates a potential downstream effect where the administrative justification of the "funding shift" in relocation cannot simply override the statutory obligations of services provided in compliance with the 8th Section of the Refugee Act. It establishes that a statutory framework for reception and placement is a separate entity from the foreign policy authority to regulate entry. This may signal to future courts and the Executive Branch that while admission can be paused, the humanitarian infrastructure required to receive those individuals is not subject to the same margin of appreciation as the power to turn away immigrants at the border.

The dissent suggested that the language in the Refugee Act was merely permissive authorization rather than a mandatory mandate, arguing the government was free to choose whether to provide services. However, the majority’s opinion leans heavily into the statutory "shall" language, prioritizing congressional intent on service delivery over executive discretion on funding implementation. This interpretation suggests that the executive must provide for the basic reception requirements of refugees, emphasizing that even in times of fiscal constraint or administrative uncertainty, the statutory protections for refugees already admitted remain intact. The decision effectively partitions the arena of refugee policy into two tracks: entry, which remains largely an instrument of executive power, and resettlement, which is increasingly treated as a protected statutory obligation.



# Statutory Mandates or Administrative Permits? A Breakdown of the *Pacito v. Trump* Debate over 8 U.S.C. § 1522

## The Interplay Between Executive Discretion and Spending Authority

The Ninth Circuit’s recent panel order in *Pacito v. Trump* marks a significant new pivot in how lower courts are parsing the tension between presidential discretion in immigration enforcement and statutory obligations regarding refugee services. While the case is currently being decided on an expedited basis and sits pending full merits opinions, the interim order serves as a striking signal of the court’s willingness to intervene in administrative contracting when the executive branch claims a statutory duty to remain active. The decision effectively splits the case into two tracks: a concession regarding the President’s sweeping power to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and a firm directive on the operational requirements of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 regarding reception and placement services. This bifurcation highlights the ongoing struggle in administrative law between the administration’s right to pause programs during periods of uncertainty versus the legislative entitlement of new arrivals to be absorbed under statutory frameworks.

## The Majority’s Textual Breakdown

The core of the controversy lies in how the Ninth Circuit interprets Congress’s language under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, specifically the mandate for reception and placement services. While the District Court ordered the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations, the government had argued that it was merely suspending funds and not taking over the waivers. The Ninth Circuit majority, writing the panel order, chose to reinstate the agreements in order to provide services to refugees already admitted. In the reasoning, the court looked to section 1522(a) titled "Conditions and considerations." The court notes that this section utilizes the word "shall" in conjunction with "to the extent of available appropriations" and mandats specific training and assistance.

The court appears to treat the "shall" language in the conditions not merely as guidance but as an obligation tied to statutory appropriations. By finding that the government "is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA," the panel implies that Congress imposed a duty to fund and administer these services. This reading is crucial because it relies on the assumption that funded programs require active administration and contracting to discharge that funding, and a government that cannot fulfill its contracting duties will fail its statutory duty to the refugee already admitted.

## A Critical View on Administrative Mandatory Spending

The majority's textual reading of "shall" as mandatory creates a significant doctrinal wrinkle for executive agencies. Traditionally, in the context of spending, courts have been cautious about mandating administrative compliance unless the statute uses unequivocal language. However, the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation here treats the legislative appropriation of funds for refugee services not just as an authorizing grant but as a structural commitment. The court references the existence of appropriated funds—specifically noting the FY 2025 appropriation of $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance—and suggests that because Congress has provided the specific appropriation for these activities, the executive branch cannot simply pause the programs that the money was intended to support.

This signals a potential shift where administrative law may require a different degree of scrutiny on "authorization clauses" that historically permitted the executive to operate at a strategic whim. The majority seems to prioritize the practical reality of refugee resettlement over the procedural discretion typically afforded to the executive branch in the absence of court orders. By maintaining injunctions against the termination of agreements, the court ensures that refugees admitted prior to the suspension of the program have legal access to resettlement services that were agreed to by the State Department and Congress’s appropriation of funds. The implication is that if the executive branches chooses to use the funds it has been provided, it cannot simply contract out to a state agency or cancel the agreements without providing a clear path for the intended recipients.

## Divergence in the Sanctuary Note

The dissenting opinion by Judge Lee offers a sharp counterpoint to this reasoning. Judge Lee, who agreed with the majority's deference to the President in the admission context, fundamentally disagreed with the interpretation of section 1522 as a mandatory obligation. Lee argues that the title "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement" itself indicates permissive language rather than a mandate. citing the phrase "Director is authorized" throughout the statute, Lee contends that section 1522 is a menu of options available to the executive branch when appropriations flow.

Lee points out that the "shall" found in section 1522(a) is qualified by "in providing assistance under this section," effectively making it a condition on funding if the executive chooses to use it, not a directive requiring the executive to utilize the funding. This view creates a tension between the literal play of the text (who is mandated by the use of "shall") and the legislative drafters' intent to provide tools to the executive with discretion. If Lee's view prevails, courts must reconsider whether the "shall" in the conditions section truly imposes a binding duty on the government to maintain contracts or merely outlines conditions the government must follow if it pauses to manage its operations.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The impact of this case extends to how future administrative orders will be enforced in immigration contexts. If the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of the "shall" mandates is adopted as precedent, it could embolden administrative agencies to assert near-mandatory compliance when government appropriations target service provision. This could limit the ability of executive branches to utilize statutory "authorizations" as broad discretion to shift or pause services, particularly those involving humanitarian obligations like refugee resettlement.

However, the court’s reservation to issue a full opinion suggests that the Supreme Court may weigh in on the statutory interpretation of § 1522. The tension between the statutory "shall" and the "authorization" nature of the program is a classic political question that will likely tour its way into larger immigration policy debates. For the government, this case sets a high bar for arguing feasibility and administrative reduction when funds exist but contracts must be maintained. For the plaintiffs, the court gives them a procedural victory in enforcing the statutory duty of agency services.

Ultimately, *Pacito v. Trump* remains a reminder of the complex balance between executive flexibility and congressional intent in immigration law. By demanding the executive branch to reinstate agreements, the Ninth Circuit is prioritizing the immediate welfare of the admitted refugee population over the administrative efficiency of the State Department's contracting process. If the Supreme Court adopts the minority view on the understanding of § 1522, the executive branch could gain significant latitude to manage its funding and service obligations.



# Staying the Hand of Discretion: How the *Pacito* Stay Order Employs *Nken v. Holder* Standards in a Humanitarian Crisis

The Ninth Circuit has issued an unprecedented stay order in *Pacito v. Trump*, effectively pausing the lower court's preliminary injunction on the suspension of the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) while the government simultaneously contends with statutory mandates to provide resettlement services. This order, issued on September 12, 2025, represents a critical procedural pivot point in the fight over executive authority in immigration law. By utilizing the factors established in *Nken v. Holder*, the Ninth Circuit has balanced the deference owed to the President's broad immigration powers against the potential for irreparable harm to specific Refugee Act programs. As the court notes in its "Discussion" section, the lower court’s orders to enforce the suspension were stayed in full, saving the plaintiffs and refugee organizations from immediate statutory enforcement disruption pending a final ruling on the underlying merits.

The central tension in this case stems from the automatic deference granted to executive actions such as an executive order suspending refugee admissions, authorized under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). In applying the *Nken* factors, the court explicitly determined that the government is "likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163." By quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel affirmed that the statutory language vests the President with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." However, the court was compelled to apply *Nken*'s strict standard against a backdrop of humanitarian urgency. First, the court recognized that barring the defendant from enacting the EO was a political question where Congress left the President's discretion vast. Second, the court acknowledged the government's interest in the suspension but weighed it against the district court's injunction on the funding side of the equation.

## Procedural Mechanics of the Stay Order

In a move distinct from typical stays, the Ninth Circuit vacated its own administrative stays from July and March, 2025, and issued a unified stay pending final resolution of the appeal. The district court's orders from February and March 2025, which prohibited enforcement or implementation of the suspension clauses, were stayed in their entirety. This ensures that the administrative machinery of the State Department must reinstate cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations immediately. The court utilized this interim stay to prevent an immediate dead end for refugee organizations that had already spent millions on processing overseas and reform domestic services.

However, the stay was not absolute in its effect on service delivery. The text directed that the government be "reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522." This directive acknowledges that while the President has the authority to stop the *entry* of aliens (EO 14163), Congress has mandated specific *support* for those already admitted through the USRAP. The stay effectively creates a "circuit" around the district court's command while the final merits opinion on the funding obligations and statutory interpretations of the Refugee Act follows in due course.

## The Merits of Discretion vs. Mandate

The Ninth Circuit's reasoning on the merits within the order highlights a fundamental split between the authority to admit refugees and the authority to fund them after admission. While the President enjoys "vast discretion and deference," the court notes that the government is "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA." This challenges the government's position on providing human support to refugees. The court intervened to ensure the State Department provides services vital to refugees already in the country—specifically cash assistance, employment training, and English language instruction—grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

An anonymous dissent by Judge Lee raised the question of whether the "shall" language in the statute functions as a mandatory disclosure or merely an authorization. The majority opinion, in contrast, leans toward a substantive mandate where the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must provide public or private refugee services to the extent of available appropriations. The practical effect of this reasoning is significant: even if the government can temporarily suspend new admissions under the Executive Order, it cannot legally suspend the post-admission support systems required by Congress. This distinction preserves a safety net for refugees already processed while leaving the open question of where lines are drawn for future admission limits.

## Future of Executive Authority and Precedent

The acquiescence and subsequent stay in *Pacito* signal how the judiciary will handle similarly humanitarian demands from the executive branch in 2025 and beyond. By expanding the deference standard for the President's statutory authority under the Administration Act while simultaneously compelling the government to adhere to specific funding mandates, the court sets a precedent where executive power in admissions is limited by post-entry statutory obligations. This ensures that "stay" orders are not merely procedural pauses but can be substantive orders that compel agency compliance with congressional intent.

Ultimately, the stay order stabilizes the refugee population within the United States while leaving the political battle over future refugee quotas to the political branches rather than the courts. Whether *Nken* standards will continue to prioritize the public interest in executive discretion or the irreparable harm to specific humanitarian programs will depend on future decisions. However, *Pacito* establishes a clear boundary: the President cannot make the *admission* decision via EO without triggering the statutory reality of funding post-admission care, and the judiciary will ensure that administrative stay orders do not inadvertently suspend the government's core legal duties to citizens already within its borders.
