Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the practical significance if Congress rescended $800 million in funding via the Rescissions Act of 2025, would the order to reinstate agreements remain enforceable regarding funding availability?
Question:
The text notes that FY 2025 appropriation was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. If the order directs that the government 'must provide' these services 'to the extent of available appropriations,' what happens if there is no money left after the rescission? Does the court implicitly treat the appropriation as available regardless of the rescission (implying future availability), or does 'reinstate agreements' fail if the financial obligation falls below zero? Is the District Court's procedural link to appropriations severed by the Rescissions Act?
Answer:
### The Enforcement Necessity of "Available Appropriations" Post-Rescission

Based on the provided opinion in *Pacito v. Trump*, the reinstatement order remains legally enforceable despite the Rescissions Act of 2025, but its practical scope regarding funding is bounded by the statutory language "to the extent of available appropriations."

#### 1. Judicial Determination of "Available Appropriations"
The Ninth Circuit explicitly acknowledges the Rescissions Act of 2025 within its discussion. The Court notes that FY 2025 appropriations were originally set at $3.178 billion (Pub. L. No. 119-4) and reduced by $800 million (Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6)). The Court does not treat the District Court’s order to reinstate as voided by this reduction; rather, the order enforces the statutory mandate of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which is qualified by the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations."

Because the District Court’s order to reinstate agreements is grounded in the statutory requirement for "available appropriations" (8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A)), the rescission does not sever the procedural link between the court and the appropriation power. Instead, the rescission becomes a factual limitation on the *amount* of services the government is legally required to fund under the reinstated agreements.

#### 2. Enforcement Without Implicit Future Assumptions
The Sixth Circuit Order does not imply that the appropriation will automatically replenish to cover the $800 million rescission. The Court instructs the government to reinstate agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522" while simultaneously noting the specific funding reduction. 

Therefore, if the available appropriations are zero or nominal after the rescission, the government’s obligation under the reinstated order is proportionally limited. The District Court does not assume future funding availability; it enforces the specific statutory authority. As stated in the opinion, the government must provide services "to the extent of available appropriations." If the rescission reduces funding below the levels necessary for full reinstatement, the government’s legal obligation is limited by that *actual* availability, not by the District Court’s hypothetical expectations regarding money flow.

#### 3. Procedural Limits on the Executive Branch
While the Reignited Agreement Order remains enforceable, the *Pacito* opinion reinforces that this enforceability is subject to the limitations Congress set via the Rescissions Act. In the *Dissenting Opinion* (Judge Lee), the concern is raised that "Section 1522 authorizes the executive branch to craft programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow." This dissent highlights the caveat that statutory funding mandates are not absolute without the flow of money.

The Majority correctly navigates this by ordering reinstatement without mandating specific dollar amounts beyond the statutory framework. The "procedural link" is preserved because the Court utilizes the funding context to guide the execution of the statute, not to bypass it. The order remains valid on the surface but requires the Executive Branch to prioritize services or negotiate funding cuts within the statutory "available appropriation" rubric.

### Conclusion
The order to reinstate agreements is **not severed** by the Rescissions Act. While the $800 million reduction limits the financial capacity for full service delivery, the injunction survives post-rescission. The government is obligated to reinstate, but only to the extent that appropriations allow. The order remains enforceable, but the practical effect is a capped funding obligation rather than an unconditional mandate to perform all services.
