Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Ex Parte Anecdotes in the FOIA Record: When Secret Declarations Dictate Public Release

The article on Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation is a story about what disappears when cameras turn off. Usually, when a FOIA requester like Farahi sues over an FBI file, the legal battle rages over the substance of withheld records: Is this actual evidence? Is this privileged? Is this national security? But in *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit知道了 that the procedural architecture of FOIA could shape the outcome as effectively as the content itself becomes more clear. The case serves as a reminder that FOIA exemptions are not just substantive protections for sensitive information but also procedural vehicles that allow the executive to shield specific investigative processes from the public eye. By affirming the district court's reliance on secret declarations to determine whether documents were "reasonably segregable," the Court reinforced the idea that a government agency can use closed-door processes to define the boundaries of the public's right to see material in their possession—provided those processes are technically consistent with existing law.

## The Procedural Intertwinement
The D.C. Circuit reviewed a situation where the FBI withheld approximately 10,750 pages submitted by foreign government and national security sensors. The court's analysis confirms that the government bears the burden to show that the requested records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and that their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." As the opinion notes, this Court and its predecessors have had the power to apply Exemption 7(A) to "pending or reasonably anticipated" investigations. However, the decision stands out because of the specific procedural steps taken to ensure the court could rule without ever seeing the final version of the FBI documents.

The agreement of the court relies on an ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of the FBI's Record and Information Dissemination Section. The district court relied on this sealed declaration to determine if the withheld material could be separated from the releasable information. In Section V, the opinion explicitly states that "nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration." The court explains that while ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs, that problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." This procedural move underscores the tension in FOIA litigation where the judiciary's need for a clear legal basis to dismiss a record conflict directly relies on documents the seeking public正是因为 the government refuses to release them.

## Waiver and Category Inconsistencies
Perhaps most importantly for future proceduralists, the court addressed the exact issue of why the FBI could hide the details of the segregability analysis without prejudicing Farahi's rights under FOIA. The appellant argued that the government failed to adequately justify the categories of documents used to explain withholding decisions, a requirement in FOIA cases to allow a court to determine whether exemptions are properly applied to each category. The D.C. Circuit noted, however, that the appellant "provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad" and "forfeited his arguments on this score."

This procedural forfeiture is significant because it suggests that the burden of proving overbreadth falls heavily on the plaintiff to point to specific defects in the agency's categorization before a sealed document can suffice as the deciding factor. This creates a distinct procedural advantage for the agency. It means if an agency can show, even in ex parte contours, that individual words are likely not relevant, the courts can find a meaningful lack of segregability. This is consistent with precedents like *Mead Data Cent. Inc. v. U.S. Dep't. of Air Force*, which hold that if nonexempt portions are "interspersed" within exempt material line by line, they are not reasonably segregable. The court here applied this standard but was enabled to do so without the files themselves leaking.

## A Necessary Tension
Ultimately, *Foad Farahi* reaffirms the court's understanding of the "inextricably intertwined" concept in Exemption 7(A). The scientific legal community has grown to accept that, in cases involving counterterrorism investigations, the courts must sometimes defer to agency opinions on whether investigations remain ongoing. Farahi himself, having navigated his own security clearance, did not challenge the FBI's assertions that the investigation remained active as of March 2023. When a plaintiff does not object to the acceptance of ex parte declarations during the summary judgment or segregability stages, the court treats the reliance on those sealed documents as statutory compliance.

This result raises a difficult question for transparency scholars. A system that permits the complete withholding of a record because a sealed declaration overrides the need for segreability suggests that the courts are willing to accept short of a specific categorical error when the agency's internal processes are sealed from the public eye. However, the court's opinion notes that the agency "seemingly" met its initial burden, and that the ex parte documents served to confirm that this pendency had not changed during the delay. The decision is not an endorsement of secrecy as an end, but rather as a means to protect a process that appears to be ongoing without public threat to enforcement. It suggests that for agencies like the FBI, the process of disclosure often means the process of judgment must be insulated from the very information being sought to ensure the security of the investigation.

## Conclusion
The *Farahi* decision reconfirms that FOIA is not just a request for paperwork, but a negotiation over confidence between the government and the judiciary. As long as courts are permitted to rely on sealed ex parte declarations to make final determinations on the public record, they must continue to analyze whether that reliability is the exception or the norm in future FOIA requests. If the agency can prove the documents are inseparable or that the "pending" issue remains ongoing without public inquiry, the plaintiffs' ability to challenge the process is significantly narrowed.
