Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Defining the 'Pending': How the D.C. Circuit Clarifies FOIA Exemption 7(A) for Ongoing Investigations

## The 'Pending' Standard for Official Integrity

The tension between public access to government information and the legitimate need for investigation confidentiality is central to the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The recent decision in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit reinforces the agency's ability to withhold vast swathes of records when national security and law enforcement proceedings are threatened by disclosure. In a landmark ruling that affirmed the lower court’s decision, the D.C. Circuit held that the FBI properly invoked Exemption 7(A) to withhold a comprehensive file on its agency relations with the Iranian national named as the appellant. The key legal takeaway for legal practitioners and scholars observing the FOIA regime is the treatment of "proceedings." Specifically, the court clarified that ongoing criminal investigations, even those that have not yet culminated in a formal prosecution or arrest, count as "proceedings" pending or reasonably anticipated under Exemption 7(A). This interpretation significantly narrows the window for transparency in contexts where law enforcement architectures are still interpreting evidence.

The opinion clarifies longstanding precedent by reaffirming that "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes." This is not merely boilerplate; it impacts the timeline of how long records may be shielded from the public eye. The immediate issue was whether passage of time since the 2002 visa overstay and the 2019 summary judgment initial motion invalidated the FBI's claim that proceedings were pending. The court found that the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard does not require a future arrest to be imminent. Instead, as long as the investigation is active and gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case, disclosure could be reasonably expected to interfere with the proceedings. The court focused on the FBI's confirmation that "the relevant proceedings remain ongoing" as of March 2023, twelve years after Farahi originally filed for asylum. This holds that the "vague nature" of an assertion of investigation existence, whether asserted at the time or updated later, is acceptable provided it is contextualized with other evidence.

If the mere existence of an active investigation is sufficient, this underscores the D.C. Circuit's broad deference to the FBI in counterterrorism matters. The court explicitly noted that "because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference" and "Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security." Consequently, the inquiry did not hinge on whether an indictment or probable cause was the specific proof required, but rather on the ongoing status of the fact-finding mission. This helps agencies maintain the investigative momentum without the pressure of adjudication over public disclosure.

## Burden of Proof and Ex Parte Declarations

A notable trajectory in this opinion revolves around the burden of proof and the admissibility of ex parte declarations to support that burden. In FOIA litigation, agencies must generally justify exemptions *de novo* on the record. The *CREW* case set the bar for "pending or reasonably anticipated" proceedings, requiring the government to show a concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding. Here, the court applied these holdings but navigated the procedural hurdle of the government relying on ex parte declarations to confirm the timeline. The government moved for summary judgment, submitting public declarations initially, followed by updated declarations under seal. The district court required an update on whether proceedings were still pending by the time of judgment.

The D.C. Circuit upheld the district court's reliance on the ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of Record/Information Dissemination. The court noted that while Farahi argued the declaration appeared "surprisingly terse," the context counseled against considering it in isolation. The agency submitted the declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while the district court was considering the renewal of the motion. The court credited the FBI’s background context, including public allegations in immigration proceedings that Farahi was a "recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood" and linked to "known Al Qaeda members." This combination of public declarations and sealed updates satisfied the burden. This suggests that ex parte evidence is not inherently categorically barred, provided it is used for specific, narrow confirmations rather than broad policy announcements, and provided the public record is sufficient to confirm the general landscape of the investigation.

This also touches the issue of segregation and the "reasonable segregability" standard. The court affirmed the lower court's decision that no meaningful non-exempt material was reasonably segregable. Even in a 10,000-page file, the court acknowledged that "if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,' that material is not likely to be 'reasonably segregable.'" This conservative application of the standard effectively creates a higher threshold for releasing sensitive narrative. It means agencies can withhold entire document categories if the content is inextricably intertwined with exempt information.

## Doctrinal Implications for Investigative Access

The decision has significant implications for how later FOIA requests handled regarding ongoing investigations must be structured and adjudicated. Lawyers representing whistleblowers, journalists, or public interest groups may find the threshold for obtaining "interlocutory" assistance from what was revealed in these files effectively raised. By confirming that ongoing investigations are reliably treated as "pending" based on the government's attestation, the court limits the incremental basis for the court to be pivotal.

There is also a critical lesson for agencies in terms of the burden of application. While the D.C. Circuit has consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive when national security concerns are implicated under Exemption 7(A), the agency must still establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of its law enforcement duties. The court noted the agency carried this burden here through the detailed ex parte and public declarations.

Furthermore, the decision implicitly broadens the scope of frozen disclosure in national security intelligence contexts. Given the ongoing terrorism cases mentioned in Farahi's background, the ruling suggests that agencies willing to maintain a secure environment will find that ongoing work is sufficient reason to withhold more than just finalized arrest records. However, the continued relevance of the court's finding that the FBI must still disburse records to contain exempt portions (noting the ex parte declaration had to confirm the investigation was ongoing) means that some degree of record management is still required. This is a narrow "safe harbor" for the government that prioritizes the integrity of an investigation over immediate public access to the investigative file. Ultimately, the ruling solidifies the role of the D.C. Circuit in protecting sensitive law enforcement work from FOIA requests that would otherwise expose law enforcement tactics to the public.

## Conclusion

*Foad Farahi* serves as a sober reminder of the stringent protections afforded to ongoing federal investigations under the FOIA. While transparency remains a constitutional and statutory value, the court's rigorous adherence to Exemption 7(A) in this counterterrorism context demonstrates that the public interest in knowing what is happening to a suspect in a national security investigation is carefully weighed against the factual integrity of that investigation.
