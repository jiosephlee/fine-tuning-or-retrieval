# Defining the 'Pending': How the D.C. Circuit Clarifies FOIA Exemption 7(A) for Ongoing Investigations

## The 'Pending' Standard for Official Integrity

The tension between public access to government information and the legitimate need for investigation confidentiality is central to the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The recent decision in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit reinforces the agency's ability to withhold vast swathes of records when national security and law enforcement proceedings are threatened by disclosure. In a landmark ruling that affirmed the lower court’s decision, the D.C. Circuit held that the FBI properly invoked Exemption 7(A) to withhold a comprehensive file on its agency relations with the Iranian national named as the appellant. The key legal takeaway for legal practitioners and scholars observing the FOIA regime is the treatment of "proceedings." Specifically, the court clarified that ongoing criminal investigations, even those that have not yet culminated in a formal prosecution or arrest, count as "proceedings" pending or reasonably anticipated under Exemption 7(A). This interpretation significantly narrows the window for transparency in contexts where law enforcement architectures are still interpreting evidence.

The opinion clarifies longstanding precedent by reaffirming that "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes." This is not merely boilerplate; it impacts the timeline of how long records may be shielded from the public eye. The immediate issue was whether passage of time since the 2002 visa overstay and the 2019 summary judgment initial motion invalidated the FBI's claim that proceedings were pending. The court found that the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard does not require a future arrest to be imminent. Instead, as long as the investigation is active and gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case, disclosure could be reasonably expected to interfere with the proceedings. The court focused on the FBI's confirmation that "the relevant proceedings remain ongoing" as of March 2023, twelve years after Farahi originally filed for asylum. This holds that the "vague nature" of an assertion of investigation existence, whether asserted at the time or updated later, is acceptable provided it is contextualized with other evidence.

If the mere existence of an active investigation is sufficient, this underscores the D.C. Circuit's broad deference to the FBI in counterterrorism matters. The court explicitly noted that "because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference" and "Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security." Consequently, the inquiry did not hinge on whether an indictment or probable cause was the specific proof required, but rather on the ongoing status of the fact-finding mission. This helps agencies maintain the investigative momentum without the pressure of adjudication over public disclosure.

## Burden of Proof and Ex Parte Declarations

A notable trajectory in this opinion revolves around the burden of proof and the admissibility of ex parte declarations to support that burden. In FOIA litigation, agencies must generally justify exemptions *de novo* on the record. The *CREW* case set the bar for "pending or reasonably anticipated" proceedings, requiring the government to show a concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding. Here, the court applied these holdings but navigated the procedural hurdle of the government relying on ex parte declarations to confirm the timeline. The government moved for summary judgment, submitting public declarations initially, followed by updated declarations under seal. The district court required an update on whether proceedings were still pending by the time of judgment.

The D.C. Circuit upheld the district court's reliance on the ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of Record/Information Dissemination. The court noted that while Farahi argued the declaration appeared "surprisingly terse," the context counseled against considering it in isolation. The agency submitted the declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while the district court was considering the renewal of the motion. The court credited the FBI’s background context, including public allegations in immigration proceedings that Farahi was a "recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood" and linked to "known Al Qaeda members." This combination of public declarations and sealed updates satisfied the burden. This suggests that ex parte evidence is not inherently categorically barred, provided it is used for specific, narrow confirmations rather than broad policy announcements, and provided the public record is sufficient to confirm the general landscape of the investigation.

This also touches the issue of segregation and the "reasonable segregability" standard. The court affirmed the lower court's decision that no meaningful non-exempt material was reasonably segregable. Even in a 10,000-page file, the court acknowledged that "if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,' that material is not likely to be 'reasonably segregable.'" This conservative application of the standard effectively creates a higher threshold for releasing sensitive narrative. It means agencies can withhold entire document categories if the content is inextricably intertwined with exempt information.

## Doctrinal Implications for Investigative Access

The decision has significant implications for how later FOIA requests handled regarding ongoing investigations must be structured and adjudicated. Lawyers representing whistleblowers, journalists, or public interest groups may find the threshold for obtaining "interlocutory" assistance from what was revealed in these files effectively raised. By confirming that ongoing investigations are reliably treated as "pending" based on the government's attestation, the court limits the incremental basis for the court to be pivotal.

There is also a critical lesson for agencies in terms of the burden of application. While the D.C. Circuit has consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive when national security concerns are implicated under Exemption 7(A), the agency must still establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of its law enforcement duties. The court noted the agency carried this burden here through the detailed ex parte and public declarations.

Furthermore, the decision implicitly broadens the scope of frozen disclosure in national security intelligence contexts. Given the ongoing terrorism cases mentioned in Farahi's background, the ruling suggests that agencies willing to maintain a secure environment will find that ongoing work is sufficient reason to withhold more than just finalized arrest records. However, the continued relevance of the court's finding that the FBI must still disburse records to contain exempt portions (noting the ex parte declaration had to confirm the investigation was ongoing) means that some degree of record management is still required. This is a narrow "safe harbor" for the government that prioritizes the integrity of an investigation over immediate public access to the investigative file. Ultimately, the ruling solidifies the role of the D.C. Circuit in protecting sensitive law enforcement work from FOIA requests that would otherwise expose law enforcement tactics to the public.

## Conclusion

*Foad Farahi* serves as a sober reminder of the stringent protections afforded to ongoing federal investigations under the FOIA. While transparency remains a constitutional and statutory value, the court's rigorous adherence to Exemption 7(A) in this counterterrorism context demonstrates that the public interest in knowing what is happening to a suspect in a national security investigation is carefully weighed against the factual integrity of that investigation.



# Ex Parte Anecdotes in the FOIA Record: When Secret Declarations Dictate Public Release

The article on Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation is a story about what disappears when cameras turn off. Usually, when a FOIA requester like Farahi sues over an FBI file, the legal battle rages over the substance of withheld records: Is this actual evidence? Is this privileged? Is this national security? But in *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit知道了 that the procedural architecture of FOIA could shape the outcome as effectively as the content itself becomes more clear. The case serves as a reminder that FOIA exemptions are not just substantive protections for sensitive information but also procedural vehicles that allow the executive to shield specific investigative processes from the public eye. By affirming the district court's reliance on secret declarations to determine whether documents were "reasonably segregable," the Court reinforced the idea that a government agency can use closed-door processes to define the boundaries of the public's right to see material in their possession—provided those processes are technically consistent with existing law.

## The Procedural Intertwinement
The D.C. Circuit reviewed a situation where the FBI withheld approximately 10,750 pages submitted by foreign government and national security sensors. The court's analysis confirms that the government bears the burden to show that the requested records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and that their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." As the opinion notes, this Court and its predecessors have had the power to apply Exemption 7(A) to "pending or reasonably anticipated" investigations. However, the decision stands out because of the specific procedural steps taken to ensure the court could rule without ever seeing the final version of the FBI documents.

The agreement of the court relies on an ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of the FBI's Record and Information Dissemination Section. The district court relied on this sealed declaration to determine if the withheld material could be separated from the releasable information. In Section V, the opinion explicitly states that "nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration." The court explains that while ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs, that problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." This procedural move underscores the tension in FOIA litigation where the judiciary's need for a clear legal basis to dismiss a record conflict directly relies on documents the seeking public正是因为 the government refuses to release them.

## Waiver and Category Inconsistencies
Perhaps most importantly for future proceduralists, the court addressed the exact issue of why the FBI could hide the details of the segregability analysis without prejudicing Farahi's rights under FOIA. The appellant argued that the government failed to adequately justify the categories of documents used to explain withholding decisions, a requirement in FOIA cases to allow a court to determine whether exemptions are properly applied to each category. The D.C. Circuit noted, however, that the appellant "provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad" and "forfeited his arguments on this score."

This procedural forfeiture is significant because it suggests that the burden of proving overbreadth falls heavily on the plaintiff to point to specific defects in the agency's categorization before a sealed document can suffice as the deciding factor. This creates a distinct procedural advantage for the agency. It means if an agency can show, even in ex parte contours, that individual words are likely not relevant, the courts can find a meaningful lack of segregability. This is consistent with precedents like *Mead Data Cent. Inc. v. U.S. Dep't. of Air Force*, which hold that if nonexempt portions are "interspersed" within exempt material line by line, they are not reasonably segregable. The court here applied this standard but was enabled to do so without the files themselves leaking.

## A Necessary Tension
Ultimately, *Foad Farahi* reaffirms the court's understanding of the "inextricably intertwined" concept in Exemption 7(A). The scientific legal community has grown to accept that, in cases involving counterterrorism investigations, the courts must sometimes defer to agency opinions on whether investigations remain ongoing. Farahi himself, having navigated his own security clearance, did not challenge the FBI's assertions that the investigation remained active as of March 2023. When a plaintiff does not object to the acceptance of ex parte declarations during the summary judgment or segregability stages, the court treats the reliance on those sealed documents as statutory compliance.

This result raises a difficult question for transparency scholars. A system that permits the complete withholding of a record because a sealed declaration overrides the need for segreability suggests that the courts are willing to accept short of a specific categorical error when the agency's internal processes are sealed from the public eye. However, the court's opinion notes that the agency "seemingly" met its initial burden, and that the ex parte documents served to confirm that this pendency had not changed during the delay. The decision is not an endorsement of secrecy as an end, but rather as a means to protect a process that appears to be ongoing without public threat to enforcement. It suggests that for agencies like the FBI, the process of disclosure often means the process of judgment must be insulated from the very information being sought to ensure the security of the investigation.

## Conclusion
The *Farahi* decision reconfirms that FOIA is not just a request for paperwork, but a negotiation over confidence between the government and the judiciary. As long as courts are permitted to rely on sealed ex parte declarations to make final determinations on the public record, they must continue to analyze whether that reliability is the exception or the norm in future FOIA requests. If the agency can prove the documents are inseparable or that the "pending" issue remains ongoing without public inquiry, the plaintiffs' ability to challenge the process is significantly narrowed.



### The Duty to Reveal: When 'Stale Declarations' Lose Deference in FOIA Cases

The Freedom of Information Act is designed to empower the public to monitor the exercise of official power, serving as a critical counterbalance to government opacity. However, its most potent shield, Exemption 7(A), which protects law enforcement records from disclosure to prevent interference with investigations, stands as a significant hurdle in this balance. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed the lower court’s dismissal of a citizen’s FOIA request against a massive repository of intelligence and file data. As this decision underscores, while agencies are entitled to deference, silence and obsolescence are not sufficient defenses. By reaffirming the necessity of updated justifications and strict standards for withholdings, the court reminds agencies that the statutory duty to maintain the integrity of their disclosure records survives over years of inaction.

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national with documented ties to foreign extremist organizations and individuals designated as enemies of the state, filed a request for his FBI file. The FBI located over 10,000 pages of records and initially asserted that the information was compiled for law enforcement purposes and that its release would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The district court initially granted the government’s motion for summary judgment but granted the government an *ex parte* motion to supplement its declarations for the first time, rejecting the government’s initial assessment of segregability and confirming pending proceedings were not yet stale. The D.C. Circuit reviewed the case de novo, applying a standard of deference to the executive’s determination that the requested files were legitimate law enforcement records. The court ultimately affirmed that withholding the files was constitutional and legally appropriate under Exemption 7(A).

Crucially to this discussion, the appellate court validated the district court’s procedural requirement to update stale declarations before the agency could maintain its exemption. The lower court reasoned that the significant passage of time since the first summary judgment order necessitated a fresh assessment of whether the enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. In its opinion, the D.C. Circuit agreed with this path, noting only as significant that the FBI’s files were linked to investigations into violations of national security and international terrorism. While the agency was entitled to deference in verifying that these matching investigations remained "ongoing," the court acknowledged that courts may rely only on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated. By affirming the district court’s ruling that the government could rely on updates to the Hardy and Bender declarations, the D.C. Circuit reinforced that a static defense of secrecy is no longer viable in FOIA litigation.

The decision’s holding rests on two primary pillars: the pendency of enforcement proceedings and the nature of the segregability of exempt materials. Under existing precedent, the government must show that the withholding of records is necessary to protect ongoing investigations; the court agreed that the FBI’s assertion that the investigation into Farahi’s connections to terror groups continued at the time of the request was sufficient. This standard is not problematic per se, but it is particularly deferential in the second juncture: segregability. When specific records are withheld to protect enforcement interest, agencies must explain why the document cannot be split into secret and publicly available components. In this instance, the government’s updated submissions convinced the court that the requested information was "inextricably intertwined" with the exemption-triggering elements, rendering it unsegregateable.

The broader doctrinal implications of this ruling are significant for future FOIA litigation. First, it establishes that the passage of time is an active variable, not a passive backdrop. An agency’s initial justification for secrecy can become legally vulnerable if it fails to address how the current landscape of investigations overlaps with long-stalled files. If an agency relies on an old declaration to hide current records, a district court may still strike that down if the "innovation" of the argument is no longer sufficient. Second, it highlights the extreme high bar set for segregating national security records. If the nonexempt material is interspersed "line-by-line" throughout the document, Congress and the courts have clearly discouraged the release of most of that material in aggregate. This means that under *Faruqi*, agencies could face a world where they must compile "clean" versions of files that essentially strip out all specific advice while maintaining the redaction of "suspect" information.

However, the decision is not without its limits. While the court emphasized the need for current justification, it ultimately deferred to the FBI’s detailed declarations regarding the national security context. The opinion did not expose the specific details of the ex parte submission, meaning the precise line between "legitimate law enforcement activity" and "state secrets" remains heavily guarded. The ruling is essentially a procedural reinforcement; it tells the FBI to keep the record current and does not instruct the district court to filter *less* data from the files themselves.

Ultimately, the D.C. Circuit’s affirmation ensures that the public’s right to know has limits in the face of security-concerning law enforcement records. By requiring agencies to update their severities and privatizing the inspecting process via ex parte declarations, the court preserved the government’s claim to secrecy while acknowledging that its ongoing justification must not rely on the armor of time. For FOIA litigants, this serves as a potential principle of reality: the government must be more accurate in its records, and the agency cannot simply wait a few years to assert that their secrecy remains valid. The result is a system that demands a higher standard of candor from the FBI, but one that still permits the withholding of massive amounts of sensitive information under the guise of "interference."
