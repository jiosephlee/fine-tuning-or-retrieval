Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What statutory standard does the D.C. Circuit apply to distinguish between 'randomly interspersed' and 'inextricably intertwined' documents under the Mead test?
Question:
The court discusses segregability in Section V, citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. and noting that a 'small amount... interspersed line-by-line' is not likely to be reasonably segregable. How does the court determine when information is not 'randomly interspersed' versus when the exempt content creates an 'inextricable' mix? In FOIA cases, why is the 'focus' on information rather than just words, and does that distinguish the lines between releasable and withheld data?
Answer:
### Answer

**Short Answer**

Under the D.C. Circuit’s analysis in *Farahi*, the statutory standard for distinguishing between releasable "randomly interspersed" lines and withheld "inextricably intertwined" material is found in **5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)** and the segregability requirement under **5 U.S.C. § 552(b)**, applied through the framework established in **Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Air Force**.

The court determines that exempt information must be withheld in its entirety only when exempt and non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined," rather than when they are merely mixed. However, the court explicitly adopts from *Mead* a standard that treats **document mixing of a "line-by-line" nature as presumptively not segregable.** The "focus" on information rather than individual words prevents plaintiffs from releasing partial data that would not constitute meaningful information on its own.

### Detailed Explanation

**The Segregability Standard and *Mead Data Cent.* Application**
The D.C. Circuit reviews segregability under FOIA Exemption 7(A) to ensure agencies take "reasonable steps" to segregate nonexempt information. In Section V, the court cites *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977), establishing that withholding a record is permissible only when nonexempt portions are "reasonably segregable." If they are not, the agency may withhold the entire record.

Specifically, the court applies *Mead* to determine if material is "inextricably intertwined." The court notes that if nonexempt information is "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions," disclosure is barred. However, in determining whether segregability exists, the court relies on the government’s burden to demonstrate conclusively that non-exempt information cannot be separated without destroying the exempt nature of the material.

**Distinguishing "Randomly Interspersed" vs. "Inextricably Intertwined"**
The *Farahi* opinion clarifies *how* this distinction applies in practice, noting that the physical mechanics of the document do not always dictate release. Section V states:
> "But if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,' that material is not likely to be 'reasonably segregable.'" *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261.

This indicates that while the text signifies a distinction, the standard line-by-line interspersion is treated as **non-segregable**. This means the mere presence of words mixed within a singular document pattern is not enough to satisfy FOIA disclosure; if the relationship between the exempt and non-exempt content is so dense (interspersed line-by-line) that separation would be unreasonable, the court will find the information is not segregable. Conversely, a mix is only releasable if the exempt content and non-exempt content are distinct enough to be separated, or if the aggregator can articulate how the exempt content does not taint the unreleased portion.

**The Focus on Information vs. Words**
The *Farahi* opinion addresses why plaintiffs should not be able to demand the release of "words" without the surrounding "information." The court relies on the language from *Mead* defining the scope of what is discoverable. Section V states:
> "So, while Farahi is correct that individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI’s interests, that is neither here nor there. The focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55.

This establishes that a redacted release is limited by substantive utility. The "focus on information" means that plaintiffs may challenge withholding only if they can identify meaningful information separated from the exempt material. Terrestrial or isolated words that do not convey substantial information are excluded from the analysis of releasable data. If a word has no independent informational import, its inclusion or exclusion does not change the freeing or seizing of "information." Therefore, the decision to release depends on whether the *content* can be segregated, not just whether the string of characters can be typed.

**Conclusion**
According to *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit applies the *Mead* standard where the court must accept the government’s declarations regarding whether exempt material is inextricably intertwined. The court does not validate the segregation of random line-by-line interspersion unless the government demonstrates meaningful separability that preserves information integrity. This aligns with the goal of relief being based on meaningful information.
