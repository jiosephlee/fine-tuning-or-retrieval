Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Why does the D.C. Circuit accept a 'conclusory' declaration that an investigation remains 'ongoing' despite the passage of significant time since the initial request?
Question:
The plaintiff argued that years have passed since the initial filing and that the FBI's assertion that investigations are 'ongoing' was too vague under the precedents. However, the D.C. Circuit found the declaration sufficient because of the context and prior declarations. Can you explain why 'vague' assertions regarding 'ongoing investigations' do not automatically expire an agency's ability to hide information under Exemption 7(A) months or years later? What factors 'counseled against' looking at the declaration in isolation?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Under *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit rejected the argument that an agency's declaration must regularly update its "ongoing" investigation status because the later declaration was not considered in isolation. Instead, the court viewed the September 2023 declaration as a procedural update to an earlier, more detailed public declaration that had already established a concrete pendency of enforcement proceedings. Because the underlying investigation involved serious counterterrorism activity and the later declaration merely confirmed continuity rather than creating new factual grounds, the "vague" nature of the update did not defeat the FOIA Exemption 7(A) protection against the agency having to prove the investigation still exists.

### Detailed Legal Explanation

**1. The Applicable Standard: Exemption 7(A) and Deferential Review**
Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings," provided those proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." (*CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014)). To secure this exception, an agency must show a nexus between the investigation and its law enforcement duties. In FOIA cases involving law enforcement, particularly national security, the court grants "a fair measure of deference" to the agency's determination. (*Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998)).

**2. Why Time Is Not Automatic Expiration (The "Context" Doctrine)**
The plaintiff in *Farahi* argued that the government's assertion that investigations were "ongoing" became implausible years after the initial filing, and that vague assertions do not suffice under *CREW*. However, the D.C. Circuit held that the declaration did not automatically "expir[e]" due to the passage of time. The court provided three specific reasons why the declaration was sufficient:

*   **The Declaration Was an Update, Not a Standalone Assertion:** The court reasoned that the public declaration the Facebook entry relied on (from Joseph Bender) was submitted not to establish the pendency of proceedings from scratch, but "to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending" (*Farahi*, D.C. Cir. at 364). The court explicitly stated that the "Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document."
*   **Preceding Detailed Declarations Founded Pendency:** The public declaration submitted initially (by David Hardy) established the existence of the investigations with specific detail regarding national security violations and potential pending proceedings. The subsequent declaration did not attempt to "establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch." (*Farahi*, D.C. Cir. at 365).
*   **Breadth of Underlying Investigation:** The court looked broadly at the FBI's underlying investigation to validate the "ongoing" status. Given Farahi's connection to "foreign terrorist organizations," "Al Qaeda members," and "convicted terrorists," the breadth of the investigation makes it plausible that a prospective criminal proceeding continued as of the later filing. (*Farahi*, D.C. Cir. at 366).

**3. Segregation is Secondary**
The court also noted that the agency has met its burden to show that non-exempt information is not reasonably "segregable." Because the federal government specializes in law enforcement, its determination on the scope of the withheld categories is entitled to deference. If the nonexempt portions of the records are "interspersed line-by-line" or otherwise meaningless outside the context of the still-pending investigation, they cannot be safely segregated even if the "ongoing" issue had been better explained. (*Meat Data Cent.*, Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977)).

**Conclusion**
The D.C. Circuit’s acceptance of the declaration rests on the principle that the agency’s burden does not require a constant update when the relevant law enforcement context has not materially changed. Where an agency’s initial detailed declaration establishes a high level of pendency of enforcement proceedings, a subsequent declaration confirming that the investigation remains "ongoing" is sufficient provided the underlying investigation remains sufficiently "concrete" to justify continued law enforcement withholding. The court effectively reasons that a declaration of "ongoing" status, while potentially vague in isolation, is legally standard and sufficient when supported by a history of specific factual findings regarding the nature of the investigation.
