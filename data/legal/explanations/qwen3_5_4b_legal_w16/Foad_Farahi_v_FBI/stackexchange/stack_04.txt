Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Is a FOIA plaintiff required to oppose an agency's use of ex parte declarations, or does that create an automatic basis for respecting the agency's findings?
Question:
Regarding Section V, the court notes that Farahi forfeited his challenge to the district court's reliance on ex parte declarations because he did not oppose the agency's motion to file them under seal. I understand ex parte filings raise concerns under FOIA. Does a plaintiff's failure to object during the lawsuit effectively waive their ability to argue that the ex parte submission itself was the only basis for withholding agreement? Why does the court rely on affidavits given a separation regarding the standard?
Answer:
### Answer

Based on the holdings in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), yes, a FOIA plaintiff’s failure to object to an agency's motion to submit ex parte declarations under seal constitutes a waiver of the right to challenge the district court’s reliance on those specific documents. It effectively prevents the plaintiff from arguing that the district court should not have accepted the declarations as the sole basis for upholding an exemption.

**1. Procedural Waiver of Reliance on Ex Parte Declarations**
The central ruling on this issue is found in Section V of the opinion. While acknowledging that ex parte declarations generally "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," the appellate court held that Farahi forfeited his ability to challenge the process used by the district court. Specifically, the D.C. Circuit stated:

> "Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."
> (See *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep't of Veterans Affs.*, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C. Cir. 2023)).

The opinion explains that this limitation is inherent in FOIA litigation involving national security or enforcement materials. Because the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the secret material is through an ex parte declaration, allowing a plaintiff to challenge the declaration's admissibility without objecting would undermine the procedural mechanism necessary for adjudication.

**2. Substantive Burden vs. Reliance on Evidence**
While the failure to object waives procedural challenges to the *reliance* on the sealed documents, it does not automatically prevent the appellate court from conducting a substantive review of whether Exemption 7(A) applies on the record. The court's analysis of whether an exemption applies remains governed by the independent standards of law such as:

*   **Duty for Law Enforcement:** The agency must show the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." *Id.*
*   **Interference with Proceedings:** The agency must show release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated." *Id.*
*   **Deference:** Agencies specializing in enforcement receive deference on these determinations, provided the record shows a reasonable nexus. *Campbell v. DOJ*; *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ*.

Therefore, while the plaintiff lost the argument that the *method* of withholding (via ex parte declaration) was improper, the substantive burden of proof regarding whether the withholding was justified remained valid.

**3. Why Affidavits Were Sufficient (Section V)**
The court relied on the ex parte affidavits because they were the primary means for the district court to form findings on segregability without violating the secrecy of the records. The opinion notes that ex parte submissions allow courts to find that exempt material is "inextricably intertwined" with non-exempt information without publishing the rivalrous analysis on the public record. Since Farahi did not oppose the filing of these declarations initially, he may not attack the sufficiency of the agency's "meaningful information" to support the segregability determination later.

**Summary**
In *Farahi*, the failure to file a timely objection to the ex parte submission triggered a procedural forfeiture. Consequently, the court could not entertain arguments challenging the district court's reliance on those declarations. However, this was a procedural bar, not a substantive judgment of the agency's authority to withhold; the court independently deferred to the agency’s findings on Exemption 7(A) and aggregated the public claim evidence to determine if the withholding was proper.
