Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the specific 'nexus' burden the government must meet to establish records were compiled for law enforcement purposes under Campbell?
Question:
In Section IV.A, the court quotes Campbell v. DOJ regarding the 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties.' In practice, how specific must the agency's declaration be regarding this nexus to satisfy Exemption 7(A)? Is it enough to state a person had 'connections' to violence, or does the agency need to detail how the investigation specifically fulfills a federal law enforcement duty?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Under *Campbell v. DOJ* as applied in *Farah v. FBI*, the agency must establish a **rational nexus** between the investigation and a specific **federal law enforcement duty**. It is not sufficient for the agency to merely claim that a person had "connections." The declaration must specifically explain that the investigation was conducted to fulfill a federal enforcement obligation and connect the individual or incident to a possible violation of federal law or security risk.

***

**Legal Analysis**

Your question addresses the specificity requirement for Exemption 7(A), which protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." In *Farah*, the D.C. Circuit clarifies that while the agency enjoys deference, it bears the burden of proof.

### 1. The Specificity of the "Nexus" Burden
In Section IV.A of the opinion, the court explicitly defines the standard derived from *Campbell*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998). The opinion states that the government's declaration must establish:

> "a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,' as well as a 'connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'" *Farah*, 2025 WL 5243, at *8 (quoting *Campbell*, 164 F.3d at 32); see also *Farah* at *9.

This standard serves as the answer to your specific query: **You cannot satisfy Exemption 7(A) with a generic assertion of "connections."** The agency must detail how the specific investigation fulfills a statutory or regulatory law enforcement obligation (e.g., preventing national security attacks or detecting terrorism) and why the individual's involvement poses a risk that links to federal law.

In *Farah*, the Court found the government satisfied this burden because the declaration linked the withheld records to investigations "of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject's association with individuals directly involved in such violations." *Id.* at *10. The court required the government to show that the investigation was not merely administrative but was directly tied to an ongoing or anticipated enforcement action.

### 2. Public vs. Ex Parte Declarations
While the *Campbell* nexus requires specificity, the burden of explaining this varies based on the type of declaration submitted.
*   **Public Declarations:** The government must provide a "reasonable specificity" in its public declarations to justify secrecy. In *Farah*, this meant the initial public declaration from David Hardy had to explain that the records were "compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations... pending law enforcement proceedings." *Farah* at *10.
*   **Supplemental/Ex Parte Declarations:** In cases where the government's burden involves potentially revealing details about ongoing threats to national security, the opinion allows for more detailed justifications to be submitted ex parte (outside of public record). In *Farah*, the Agency used an ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender and an update on the Hardy declaration to confirm that the investigation "remained ongoing" without disclosing the specific content of the ex parte justification.

### 3. Deference and Burden Sharing
Although the government submits declarations, the question remains whether the nexus exists. The *Campbell* principle established in *Farah* is that the agency bears the burden of proving the exemption applies. However, the court notes that the agency is entitled to "defence" in its determination. In *Farah*, the agency met this burden by "confirm[ing] that investigations remained ongoing" and detailing the specific nature of the alleged ties to foreign terrorist organizations. *Farah* at *11.

***

**Conclusion**

To be precise with the standard established in *Farah*: **A generic statement that a person "had connections" is likely insufficient.** The agency must detail the **specific investigation purpose** (e.g., counter-terrorism, national security) and articulate how the records were compiled to **fulfill a federal law enforcement duty** (e.g., investigating alliances, potential violations of federal law). The declaration must make it clear that the investigation targets an individual's ties to a recognized enforcement duty, as the court did not view the FBI's declaration sufficient without that explicit "nexus" of enforcement duty against a "connection to a security risk."
