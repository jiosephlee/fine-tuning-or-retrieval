Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Factual Background and Procedural History

## Parties: Foad Farahi (Appellant) vs. Federal Bureau of Investigation (Respondent)

In this appeal before the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, the conflict centers on an individual citizen against a federal executive agency. The appellant is Foad Farahi, an individual residing in the United States since 1993. Farahi initiated this appeal against the Federal Bureau of Investigation, hereafter referred to as the "FBI," which served as the respondent agency. As a party, Farahi filed the original suit after the FBI's failure to comply with a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request in its entirety. The FBI acted as the agency responsible for defense, moving for summary judgment before the case reached this level of appellate review. Understanding the identities of the litigants is foundational to grasping the procedural posture; as an individual, Farahi holds a citizen's right of access to federally held records, while the FBI acts as a law enforcement agency with specific statutory duties and exemptions.

## Subject Matter: FOIA Request for FBI File Regarding Farahi's Immigration and Asylum Proceedings

The core subject matter of this dispute stems from a regulatory and privacy conflict under the Freedom of Information Act, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552. The case involves a FOIA request seeking access to approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs comprising the FBI's file on Farahi. Farahi, having filed requests, fought for the FOIA rights to access records held by a federal agency. The dispute was not a simple request for a record; however, it was a dispute over whether the record was exempt from public disclosure. The FBI correctly identified Exemption 7(A) as the relevant statutory provision for withholding this file. In FOIA litigation, the agency containing the records must determine whether they fall under the exemptions; here, the FOIA framework created the obligation for the FBI to review its file against statutory exemptions, revealing the grounds for withholding information. The subject matter dictates the burden of proof, which falls on the agency to demonstrate that the exemption applies.

## Factual Context: Farahi's Involvement in Immigration Removal and Terrorism-Related Investigations

To understand the agency's decision, one must look at the underlying factual context that formed the FBI file. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security moved Farahi into removal proceedings due to a visa overstay. During these proceedings, Farahi applied for asylum and protection under the Convention Against Torture. The Department presented documents proving, through FBI testimony, that Farahi had connections to high-level terrorists. This background information establishes the non-public nature of the file. Farahi has further requested release of records regarding "investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism." The fact that the FBI is a law enforcement agency investigating terrorism custody means that the documents are "compiled for law enforcement purposes," a prerequisite for Exemption 7(A). This context explains why the District Court emphasized the agency's difficulty releasing information that might jeopardize ongoing investigations.

## Procedural Timeline: FBI's Initial Response, Miss on Statutory Deadline and District Court's Initial Order Without Prejudice

Procedurally, the case arrived at the D.C. Circuit after a series of steps in the District Court. The FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to the request, prompting Farahi to file suit. While litigation resolved by summary judgment, the firms did not respond immediately to the request. Over several years, the agency reviewed records, disclosed some, and withheld most. When the FBI submitted a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supporting declarations were submitted by David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section. A crucial point in the case history is that the Administrative Law Judge or the district judge determined that the records were pending or reasonably anticipated because Farahi was in immigration proceedings and the FBI had a note that pending investigations continued. However, the District Court in reality did not agree. The District Court ruled that the agencies had to update their assessment regarding the pendency of enforcement proceedings because of significant passage of time. The court denied summary judgment without prejudice in October 2020, noting it needed more information to address segregability.

## Procedural Timeline: FBI's Submission of Updated Declarations and Renewal of Motion for Summary Judgment

Following the district court's decision without prejudice, the procedural posture shifted to the agency's opportunity to re-evidence its claims. The FBI resubmitted declarations under seal from Joseph Bender, who was the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. This supplemental declaration addressed the district court's concerns regarding whether the pending proceedings were valid. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration elaborated further on the investigation and segregability issues. The district court granted the renewed motion, crediting the FBI's confirmation regarding the ongoing nature of the relevant proceedings. However, the Court of Appeals considers the impact of this renewal on the standard of review later in the opinion.

## Procedural History: Farahi's Litigation on Appeal to the D.C. Circuit

This current stage involves the review of the lower court's order. In FOIA cases, appellate courts review a grant of summary judgment de novo, a standard that allows the appellate court to make its own conclusion regarding the facts and law without deference to the district court. The government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies, which the FBI must prove. The FBI's declaration is entitled to deference because it involves law enforcement expertise. The case reached the assertion to the Tribunal to review the agency's interpretation of the law. The case is noteworthy for the specific deference given to the agency in Exemption 7(A), particularly when national security concerns are implicated. The district court's decision in this chapter is predicated on the idea that Exemption 7(A) protects the withheld records. For clarity on the basis of the appeal, Farahi argued the passage of time casts doubt on the pendency of enforcement proceedings.
