Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Chapter 1: Factual Background and Procedural History

## Parties: Foad Farahi (Appellant) vs. Federal Bureau of Investigation (Respondent)

In this appeal before the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, the conflict centers on an individual citizen against a federal executive agency. The appellant is Foad Farahi, an individual residing in the United States since 1993. Farahi initiated this appeal against the Federal Bureau of Investigation, hereafter referred to as the "FBI," which served as the respondent agency. As a party, Farahi filed the original suit after the FBI's failure to comply with a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request in its entirety. The FBI acted as the agency responsible for defense, moving for summary judgment before the case reached this level of appellate review. Understanding the identities of the litigants is foundational to grasping the procedural posture; as an individual, Farahi holds a citizen's right of access to federally held records, while the FBI acts as a law enforcement agency with specific statutory duties and exemptions.

## Subject Matter: FOIA Request for FBI File Regarding Farahi's Immigration and Asylum Proceedings

The core subject matter of this dispute stems from a regulatory and privacy conflict under the Freedom of Information Act, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552. The case involves a FOIA request seeking access to approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs comprising the FBI's file on Farahi. Farahi, having filed requests, fought for the FOIA rights to access records held by a federal agency. The dispute was not a simple request for a record; however, it was a dispute over whether the record was exempt from public disclosure. The FBI correctly identified Exemption 7(A) as the relevant statutory provision for withholding this file. In FOIA litigation, the agency containing the records must determine whether they fall under the exemptions; here, the FOIA framework created the obligation for the FBI to review its file against statutory exemptions, revealing the grounds for withholding information. The subject matter dictates the burden of proof, which falls on the agency to demonstrate that the exemption applies.

## Factual Context: Farahi's Involvement in Immigration Removal and Terrorism-Related Investigations

To understand the agency's decision, one must look at the underlying factual context that formed the FBI file. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security moved Farahi into removal proceedings due to a visa overstay. During these proceedings, Farahi applied for asylum and protection under the Convention Against Torture. The Department presented documents proving, through FBI testimony, that Farahi had connections to high-level terrorists. This background information establishes the non-public nature of the file. Farahi has further requested release of records regarding "investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism." The fact that the FBI is a law enforcement agency investigating terrorism custody means that the documents are "compiled for law enforcement purposes," a prerequisite for Exemption 7(A). This context explains why the District Court emphasized the agency's difficulty releasing information that might jeopardize ongoing investigations.

## Procedural Timeline: FBI's Initial Response, Miss on Statutory Deadline and District Court's Initial Order Without Prejudice

Procedurally, the case arrived at the D.C. Circuit after a series of steps in the District Court. The FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to the request, prompting Farahi to file suit. While litigation resolved by summary judgment, the firms did not respond immediately to the request. Over several years, the agency reviewed records, disclosed some, and withheld most. When the FBI submitted a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supporting declarations were submitted by David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section. A crucial point in the case history is that the Administrative Law Judge or the district judge determined that the records were pending or reasonably anticipated because Farahi was in immigration proceedings and the FBI had a note that pending investigations continued. However, the District Court in reality did not agree. The District Court ruled that the agencies had to update their assessment regarding the pendency of enforcement proceedings because of significant passage of time. The court denied summary judgment without prejudice in October 2020, noting it needed more information to address segregability.

## Procedural Timeline: FBI's Submission of Updated Declarations and Renewal of Motion for Summary Judgment

Following the district court's decision without prejudice, the procedural posture shifted to the agency's opportunity to re-evidence its claims. The FBI resubmitted declarations under seal from Joseph Bender, who was the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. This supplemental declaration addressed the district court's concerns regarding whether the pending proceedings were valid. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration elaborated further on the investigation and segregability issues. The district court granted the renewed motion, crediting the FBI's confirmation regarding the ongoing nature of the relevant proceedings. However, the Court of Appeals considers the impact of this renewal on the standard of review later in the opinion.

## Procedural History: Farahi's Litigation on Appeal to the D.C. Circuit

This current stage involves the review of the lower court's order. In FOIA cases, appellate courts review a grant of summary judgment de novo, a standard that allows the appellate court to make its own conclusion regarding the facts and law without deference to the district court. The government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies, which the FBI must prove. The FBI's declaration is entitled to deference because it involves law enforcement expertise. The case reached the assertion to the Tribunal to review the agency's interpretation of the law. The case is noteworthy for the specific deference given to the agency in Exemption 7(A), particularly when national security concerns are implicated. The district court's decision in this chapter is predicated on the idea that Exemption 7(A) protects the withheld records. For clarity on the basis of the appeal, Farahi argued the passage of time casts doubt on the pendency of enforcement proceedings.

### Chapter 2: Governing Legal Standards and Statutory Framework

## Statutory Text of Exemption 7(A)

The core legal standard for most federal agency withholdings under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) stems from 28 U.S.C. § 552(b), specifically Exemption 7(A). Section 552(b)(7)(A) protects federal agency records that are "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." This two-part test requires distinct compliance. First, the agency must establish that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, meaning they relate to the investigation of a violation of federal laws including internal agency rules, discipline, and criminal investigations. Second, disclosure of these records must pose a risk of interference. As established by the D.C. Circuit in the *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ* ("CREW") analysis, the "enforcement proceedings" referenced in the exemption must be either pending or reasonably anticipated.

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the court applied this statutory text directly to the Bureau's withheld records regarding Mr. Farahi's immigration cases. The government conceded that the files were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism. Whether the disclosure would interfere with the investigation was the pivot point for the exemption's application. The statute does not require a concrete disclosure event to occur but rather a reasonable expectation based on the nature of the investigation.

## Procedural Requirements for Withholding

Beyond the statutory text, agencies face specific procedural obligations when invoking Exemption 7(A). The FOIA requires that agencies demonstrate on the record why the withheld material fits the exemption. To meet this burden, agencies may utilize ex parte declarations, provided they satisfy specific evidentiary requirements regarding specificity. In *Foad Farahi*, the United States Information and Dissemination Section (RIDS) submitted declarations from Joseph Bender and David Hardy. These included both public and ex parte (non-public) statements. The appellate court clarified that while ex parte declarations carry inherent restrictions, they are permissible when necessary to demonstrate why specific material cannot be disclosed without compromising law enforcement interests.

Crucially, the content of the declaration must be specific enough to support the exemption. As emphasized in the circuit decision, agencies do not need to disclose the exact "proceedings" if they demonstrate the investigation is ongoing. The FARAHII court noted that throughout the litigation, the Bureau met this procedural hurdle. In this specific instance, the government had to provide ample detail to show that the investigation remained active because, after a significant passage of time since the initial request, the agency needed to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still reasonably anticipated. The court accepted the declaration that the investigation was ongoing because the underlying facts regarding Farahi's associations with foreign terrorist organizations were still legally relevant.

## Mandatory Segregation Standard

The FOIA does not allow agencies to withhold records in their entirety if they contain both releasable and exempt information. Instead, agencies must take "reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information." Under 28 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and 28 U.S.C. § 552(b), any reasonably segregable portion of a record must be provided after deletion of the exempt portions. The standard for segregation requires a court to determine whether the non-exempt information can be severed without harming the agency's ability to protect the information.

In *Foad Farahi*, the district court required the FBI to show why information across the 10,750 pages was, inextricably intertwined with exempt portions. The agency had argued that the information was not reasonably segregable because the contents of the documents were intrusive into the specific private life of the individual. The appellate court noted that if non-exempt information is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is likely not reasonably segregable. However, the evaluation in *Farahi* hinged on the agency's ability to justify why further segmentation would compromise the nature of the investigation. The Bureau asserted that the FBI's decision to withhold was based on the risk to ongoing investigations. If a document contains both testimonial statements relevant to the defense of the case (exempt) and statistical data on the case (releasable), the court must assess whether the disclosure of the data would ruin the integrity of the investigation. In this appeal, the court affirmed that the government met this burden, as the agency sufficiently demonstrated the inextricable link.

## Standard of Review

When a plaintiff appeals a grant of summary judgment, the appellate court reviews the decision de novo. This means the appellate court weighs the competing evidence itself rather than deferring to the district court's factual findings. The burden rests on the government to show that the FOIA exemption applies. Section 552(a)(4)(B) of the FOIA places this burden squarely on the agency. The court must determine whether the government has established by clear and convincing evidence that the eight statutory exemptions apply to the documents.

In *Foad Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit reviewed the district court's decision whether the agency's withholding of the FBI files was justified. The appellate court noted that the government had the burden to demonstrate the necessity of withholding the records. Because summary judgment eliminates the factual dispute, the court looks at the entire record to see if the government met its burden under § 552(b)(7)(A). The appellate court found that the lower court's summary judgment denial or grant was subject to this de novo review. In reviewing the evidence, the appellate court also looked at whether the government satisfied the requirement of specific reasonable specificity when explaining the exemption. This standard ensures that plaintiffs are not forced to litigate voluminous volumes of data without clear government safeguards.

## Deference to Law Enforcement and National Security

FBI documents and law enforcement claims receive a higher level of deference than ordinary government agencies in the FOIA context. The D.C. Circuit has consistently stated that because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke Exemption 7(A) is entitled to a fair measure of deference. This deferral is particularly strong when national security concerns are implicated, especially regarding counterterrorism investigations.

The court in *Foad Farahi* relied on precedent such as *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*. In this analysis, the deference doctrine acts as a shield against the government's burden. The appellate court reasoned that the FBI's specialized judgment regarding national security and ongoing investigations is distinct. This deference is not blind waiver but a contextual recognition of agency expertise. In *Farahi*, the court noted that the government's declaration regarding pending investigations of ties to foreign terrorist organizations warranted significant respect regarding the need for secrecy in those records. This deference applies specifically to Exemption 7(A) claims involving documents related to counterterrorism investigations, reinforcing the government's rationale for withholding sensitive documents that could interfere with enforcement proceedings.

## Inextricably Intertwined Material Standard

The government may withhold records in their entirety "when nonexempt portions 'are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.'" This standard covers instances where the agency cannot legally segregate the content without destroying the protective utility of the withheld information. Under this standard, the court may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated. In *Foad Farahi*, the district court granted summary judgment based on this principle.

The government's assessment that the FBI withheld information based on the Binder declaration, which confirmed that investigations remained ongoing, supported the inextricably intertwined claim. The court noted that if a small amount of non-exempt material were randomly interspersed, it would not be likely to be reasonably segregable in the context of the investigation's integrity. In this specific instance, the court affirmed that the FBI had justified the withholding of the entire file because the specific exceptions applied to the category of the records. The court concluded that the government's refusal was justified because the very meaning of the document included the need to protect the ongoing investigation. When the material is intertwined, the government need not disclose words isolated from the investigation context. The court placed weight on the government's specific justification regarding the pending nature of the investigation as a way to establish the intertwined nature of the records.

# Chapter 3: Analysis and Application of Legal Standards to Facts

## Law Enforcement Purpose Analysis: Sufficiency of declarations establishing nexus to terrorism investigations

Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), Exemption 7(A) protects information that was compiled for law enforcement purposes and if disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. A critical threshold inquiry in any Exemption 7(A) claim is whether the records at issue meet the statutory requirement of being "compiled for law enforcement purposes." To validate this claim, the government bears the burden of establishing a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties, as well as a connection between an individual and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit applied this standard by assigning the government substantial deference regarding its classification of records, while still requiring a substantive justification. The FBI, as a law enforcement agency, specializes in this area and thus its determination is entitled to deference, though not absolute.

The Court evaluated the sufficiency of the FBI's declarations against this standard. The public declaration by David Hardy asserted that the withheld records were compiled in furtherance of investigations of national security violations and law enforcement proceedings against Farahi, who was the subject of the investigation. The Court found that this statement satisfied the requirement of establishing a nexus, particularly given the factual record of Farahi’s immigration proceedings. Farahi had previously conceded in the district court that the FBI investigated him for associations with foreign terrorist organizations, known Al Qaeda members, and other convicted terrorists. Because the underlying investigation focused on predicates that clearly implicated federal law violations—such as terrorism charges—there was no doubt the FBI met its burden of showing a rational nexus to its law enforcement duties. Thus, the application of the requirement hinged not entirely on the abstract statutory definition but on the court's examination of the specific factual context provided by the government’s declarations.

## Assessment of Pending/Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings: Validity of agency statements regarding ongoing investigations despite time lapse

In conjunction with the law enforcement purpose requirement, Exemption 7(A) requires a showing that the disclosure of the records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." While the government may rely on ex parte declarations, the courts have historically held that vague assertions regarding the ongoing status of investigations may not suffice if the passage of time creates a reasonable doubt about the antecedent. In *Farahi*, the plaintiff argued that the significant passage of time, spanning from Farahi’s 2002 removal to the 2020s, cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending. The D.C. Circuit addressed this by examining whether the agency’s declarations remained responsive to the standard, or whether they constituted a micro-management of time-sensitive enforcement details without sufficient specific justification.

The Court clarified that ongoing criminal investigations count as proceedings for FOIA purposes, even if they have not resulted in an arrest warrant or trial. The government may establish the existence of these proceedings by attestation. The district court had initially ordered the FBI to update its assessment after a summary judgment motion because of the lapse in time. On remand, the FBI provided updated declarations from Joseph Bender. The Court reasoned that the original public declaration from Hardy established the pendency of the enforcement proceedings at the time of the initial filing. The updated ex parte declaration was viewed as confirming that "nothing had changed" while the initial motion for summary judgment was pending. The Court emphasized that the Bender declaration was not a standalone document meant to prove the investigation commenced anew, but rather a confirmation of continuity. Given the factual allegations in Farahi’s immigration proceedings—which detailed specific ties to terrorist organizations and other convicted terrorists—this context rendered the ex parte declaration of "ongoing" investigation plausible, satisfying the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard without violating the principle that agencies must articulate the specificity of their concern.

## Segregation Analysis: Court's finding that documents were not reasonably segregable due to interspersed exempt material

The Freedom of Information Act commands agencies to segregate and release nonexempt information whenever reasonably possible. This principle is fundamental to the act, as it seeks to release as much information as possible consistent with national interests. However, when the exempt and non-exempt information is inextricably intertwined, the agency need not split documents into separate files; rather, the agency can withhold the entire document if the non-exempt portions are not "reasonably segregable." In *Foad Farahi*, the Court applied this standard to the FBI's vast collection of documents, concluding that if the agency failed to provide a detailed justification for why documents could not be split, the court should scrutinize whether the exempt and non-exempt material could be divided without compromising the investigation.

The district court found that no meaningful non-exempt information was reasonably segregable from the requested records, leading the court to grant summary judgment in favor of the government on this ground. The Court supported this finding by relying on the principle that "if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document, that material is not likely to be reasonably segregable." This reflects a practical limitation of segregability analysis in the electronic era, where documents are often transcribed or stored in ways that make physical or digital separation impossible without destroying the context. In *Foad Farahi*, the FBI had provided a detailed justification via ex parte submission for why the exempt material could not be separated. Since Plaintiff Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of these declarations, the appellate court found no merit in Farahi’s objection, affirming the district court's reliance on ex parte information to reach the rational conclusion that no meaningful information could be released without harming the enforcement proceedings.

## Procedural Justification: Reliance on ex parte declarations and district court's handling thereof

FOIA disputes often involve security concerns that prevent the public disclosure of the rationale behind agency decisions, leading courts to permit agencies to rely on ex parte declarations. This procedural posture arises where agencies seek to distinguish government-held documents from those that can be released to the public. In *Foad Farahi*, the district court relied heavily on ex parte declarations from the FBI to find that segregation was impossible and that enforcement proceedings were ongoing. While ex parte declarations can impose hazards on FOIA plaintiffs, the D.C. Circuit has recognized that this "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

The Court analyzed the procedural posture noting that Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations in the motion for summary judgment. Consequently, the appellate court reasoned that Farahi could not effectively challenge the district court's reliance on them because he had not raised the specific objections in time. The agency, in this case, did not have to put its specific analysis of the segregation issue onto the public record if it was determined that doing so would impair the investigation. The Court affirmed that the district court’s procedural reliance on these closed-door submissions was permissible and sufficient, as long as the agency could demonstrate the existence of the enforcement proceeding without disclosing the statuses that would bar access to the rest of the file.

## Rebuttal of Plaintiff's Contentions: Response to arguments summarizing time and overly specific categorization by the agency

Plaintiffs sometimes raise procedural barriers in FOIA requests, including the failure to identify the categories of documents withheld or the use of vague timing exemptions. In *Foad Farahi*, the government had withheld documents across specific categories, and Farahi argued that the government had not adequately defined these categories to facilitate review, but also that the time lapse invalidated the pending proceedings claim. The Court determined that Farahi’s contentions regarding the categorization of the withheld documents were forfeited because he failed to provide specific explanations for why the categories were overbroad. The precedent of *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI* establishes that in FOIA cases, the government has the burden to explain the exemptions applied, but the parties must navigate the procedural requirements correctly to avoid forfeit issues.

Regarding the time argument, the Court provided a robust rebuttal noting that the passage of time alone does not immunize the exemption from FOIA requests. The Court highlighted that the government’s earlier public declarations had established the existence of the enforcement proceedings, making the subsequent updates simply confirmations of the same. The Court also noted that Farahi’s own past statements admitting to being connected to Al Qaeda members or sometimes convicted terrorists already substantiated the existence of a security risk and a potential investigation. By confronting the factual context established in Farahi’s immigration case, the D.C. Circuit decided that the government had met its burden, and the plaintiff’s reliance on the passage of time was insufficient to overcome the declaration of ongoing law enforcement proceedings. Thus, the Court affirmed the district court’s decision to release only the withholding of documents.

# Chapter 4: Disposition and Legal Significance

## Dispositive Holding: Affirmation of the district court's grant of summary judgment

The ultimate holding of the opinion in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* is an affirmation of the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the government. In reaching this disposition, the Court of Appeals exercised de novo review of the district court’s decision. As the opinion states, in FOIA cases, as in all appeals, the appellate court reviews the ruling on summary judgment de novo. This standard of review requires the court to independently assess whether the moving party (the government) has met its heavy burden of showing that the applicable statutory exemption applies.

In this instance, the appeal flows from the district court’s denial of the FBI’s first motion for summary judgment without prejudice. Initially, the district court had ordered the FBI to update its declarations because of significant time had passed since the initial filing. Upon receiving updated declarations from agency officials, including Joseph Bender and David Hardy, the district court granted the motion. The appellate court agreed, confirming that nothing in the record retracted the region’s conclusion that Exemption 7(A) justified the withholding. The Court focused on whether the government met the statutory burden to prove two elements: that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

The appellate court’s disposition rests largely on whether the government satisfied these elements given the specific declarations submitted. The opinion notes that the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies. It is important for students to recognize that summary judgment is a threshold where the government must still provide evidence; as here, the declarations were crucial. The court affirmed because it found that the government had effectively demonstrated through both public and ex parte declarations that the contested files were integral to active investigations. This disposition signals to students that when an agency invokes a FOIA exemption, the burden of production often shifts immediately at the appellate level to the appellant (the agency), and the court does not entertain vague submissions.

## Key Distinctions: Differentiation of case from vague assertions of ongoing investigation status in prior circuit precedent

A critical logical distinction in this opinion is the court’s treatment of "ongoing investigation" assertions in light of prior circuit precedent, specifically *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice* (*CREW*). Farahi argued that the Bender declaration, which simply stated the investigations "remain ongoing" was reminiscent of a "vague nature" assertion rejected by the D.C. Circuit in *CREW*. *CREW* held that a court could not rely on a vague notice that an investigation is ongoing to support the invocation of Exemption 7(A).

However, the Farahi court distinguished this case by analyzing the declarations in context. While the public Hardy declaration explained the nature of the investigation and the ex parte declaration supplied detail, the Bender declaration was viewed as a supplemental confirmation rather than a standalone justification for the withholding started in the first round. The court noted that the FBI had already "seemingly" met its burden to show pending enforcement proceedings during the initial summary judgment motion. Therefore, the Bender declaration was read as an update confirming that nothing had materially changed while the summary judgment motion was pending.

Furthermore, the opinion points to the breadth of the underlying investigation as the substantive driver for the *CREW* distinction. Farahi himself conceded in the district court that he was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood and connected to known Al Qaeda members and enemy combatants. Given the FBI’s investigation of these specific facts, the "ongoing" assertion in the declaration was supported by a concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding. Thus, for students analyzing the opinion, the distinction is not that the declaration became less vague, but that the broader evidentiary record validated the "update," turning it from a mere statement of continuation into a confirmed part of a larger evidentiary framework.

## Limitations on Plaintiff's Arguments: Forfeiture of unapprehended arguments regarding agency categories

This opinion serves as a procedural lesson on the necessity of raising specific arguments carefully. In Section III.C, Farahi attacks the government’s categorization of documents, arguing that categories were not distinct enough to allow a court to determine if specific claimed exemptions are properly applied to each category. The court cites *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Washington* (*CREW*) and *Khine v. DHS* to hold that when an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine the proper application of exemptions.

However, the opinion explicitly states that Farahi forfeited his arguments on this score. The prompt for this forfeiture section notes that Farahi provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad. This is a critical lesson for law students regarding how to read and argue civil litigation. An appellate court often will not consider issues not distinctly raised in the brief or omitted from the record as valid.

This case demonstrates that the procedural posture of the appeal matters immensely. Here, the plaintiff did not adequately argue that the category definitions were unworkable on the public record because the categories themselves were never explicitly articulated in a way that could be challenged by their very wording. The court relies on *Khine* to confirm that after thorough review of the brief, the court may not entertain this "forfeited argument" without more. This teaches students that to successfully challenge an agency's exegetical decision, plaintiffs must articulate the specific defect in the agency's categorization scheme once and clearly, as procedural naivety can lead to the dismissal of otherwise meritorious claims.

## Educational Takeaways: Doctrinal clarity on Exemption 7(A) burden and national security deference

*Foad Farahi* reinforces the core doctrinal requirements for students of administrative and FOIA law, particularly regarding Exemption 7(A). The opinion clarifies that the government must satisfy two statutory requirements to withhold records: the records must be compiled for law enforcement purposes, and their disclosure must reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The court reiterates that for law enforcement purposes, as noted in *Campbell v. DOJ*, this is an area where the FBI’s determination is entitled to deference, particularly when national security concerns are implicated.

The opinion also provides significant clarity on the concept of segregability and when that mechanism applies. In Section V, students learn from the *Foad* decision that segregability is not automatic. If nonexempt portions are not reasonably segregable—for example, when information is interspersed line-by-line with exempt material in the record—they will not be released. This aligns with *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. Dept of Air Force*, noting that while individual words might be releasable, if the record itself is inextricably intertwined, the document remains exempt. This creates a practical understanding of the agency's burden in segregating data versus the record's complexity.

Finally, the opinion synthesizes the deference principle regarding national security from *Centers for Nat'l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ* (*CNSS*). It teaches that in Exemption 7(A) cases involving records related to counterterrorism, the executive’s politica judgment receives considerable weight. The holding in this case affirms that if the government demonstrates a specific nexus between an investigation and the individual involved, the burden is met even if the factual proof must be updated via declaration. For students, this section cements the dual role of de novo review on the law and deference on the facts, balancing the public’s right to know with the solemn need for law enforcement operations.
