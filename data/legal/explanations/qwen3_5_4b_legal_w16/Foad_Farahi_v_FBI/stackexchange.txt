Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Why does the D.C. Circuit grant deference to the agency on the law enforcement purpose requirement yet review the decision de novo?
Question:
In this opinion, the court states that while it reviews the summary judgment de novo under FOIA, it also grants deference to the FBI regarding its invocation of Exemption 7(A). I am confused about how deference exists in a de novo review. Does deference prevent the court from independently analyzing the evidence, or does it weigh heavily during the de novo assessment? What is the doctrinal line between deferring on the agency's expertise and reviewing the application de novo?
Answer:
### Why does the D.C. Circuit grant deference yet review de novo?

The confusion stems from conflating the **standard of review** (how the court analyzes the legal claim) with the **weight given to evidence** (how the court views the agency's facts). In *Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit clarifies that these two concepts operate on different axes: the court reviews the summary judgment *de novo*, but affords the agency deference regarding its specialized determinations of law enforcement operations.

**1. The De Novo Review Standard**
Under FOIA, the court reviews the agency's grant of summary judgment independently. As the court states in Section III: "In FOIA cases, as in all others, we review a grant of summary judgment *de novo*." This means the court does not simply rubber-stamp the district court's decision; it must make its own determination on whether the statutory exemption applies. This standard originates from *Rep. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021), ensuring that the court retains the final authority over the legal rights.

**2. The Deference to Agency Expertise**
While the review is *de novo*, the court acknowledges that the agency possesses expertise beyond the court's own knowledge regarding ongoing investigations. In Section III, the court explicitly notes: "Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." This deference does not mean the court abdicates its responsibility; rather, it means the court gives the agency substantial weight on technical or specialized matters that fall within the agency's inherent expertise.

**3. The Doctrinal Line**
The doctrinal boundary between deference and de novo review is established in Section IV(A). While the court defers to the agency's determination that the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes," it maintains an independent requirement for the agency to satisfy specific legal thresholds. Specifically, "Still, its declarations must 'establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties.' Additionally, there must be a 'connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'"

Thus, deference applies to the *factual sufficiency* of the agency's claim (e.g., confirming that investigations are ongoing), provided the agency meets the statutory burden. The court does not defer on the legal principle itself; it respects the agency's judgment on the *facts* underlying the law. In *Farahi*, this was satisfied because the FBI's declarations "established" the ongoing investigations rather than merely alleging abstract pendency, allowing the court to rely on the agency's specialized assessment within the *de novo* framework.

### Why does the D.C. Circuit accept a 'conclusory' declaration that an investigation remains 'ongoing' despite the passage of significant time since the initial request?
Question:
The plaintiff argued that years have passed since the initial filing and that the FBI's assertion that investigations are 'ongoing' was too vague under the precedents. However, the D.C. Circuit found the declaration sufficient because of the context and prior declarations. Can you explain why 'vague' assertions regarding 'ongoing investigations' do not automatically expire an agency's ability to hide information under Exemption 7(A) months or years later? What factors 'counseled against' looking at the declaration in isolation?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Under *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit rejected the argument that an agency's declaration must regularly update its "ongoing" investigation status because the later declaration was not considered in isolation. Instead, the court viewed the September 2023 declaration as a procedural update to an earlier, more detailed public declaration that had already established a concrete pendency of enforcement proceedings. Because the underlying investigation involved serious counterterrorism activity and the later declaration merely confirmed continuity rather than creating new factual grounds, the "vague" nature of the update did not defeat the FOIA Exemption 7(A) protection against the agency having to prove the investigation still exists.

### Detailed Legal Explanation

**1. The Applicable Standard: Exemption 7(A) and Deferential Review**
Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings," provided those proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." (*CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014)). To secure this exception, an agency must show a nexus between the investigation and its law enforcement duties. In FOIA cases involving law enforcement, particularly national security, the court grants "a fair measure of deference" to the agency's determination. (*Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998)).

**2. Why Time Is Not Automatic Expiration (The "Context" Doctrine)**
The plaintiff in *Farahi* argued that the government's assertion that investigations were "ongoing" became implausible years after the initial filing, and that vague assertions do not suffice under *CREW*. However, the D.C. Circuit held that the declaration did not automatically "expir[e]" due to the passage of time. The court provided three specific reasons why the declaration was sufficient:

*   **The Declaration Was an Update, Not a Standalone Assertion:** The court reasoned that the public declaration the Facebook entry relied on (from Joseph Bender) was submitted not to establish the pendency of proceedings from scratch, but "to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending" (*Farahi*, D.C. Cir. at 364). The court explicitly stated that the "Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document."
*   **Preceding Detailed Declarations Founded Pendency:** The public declaration submitted initially (by David Hardy) established the existence of the investigations with specific detail regarding national security violations and potential pending proceedings. The subsequent declaration did not attempt to "establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch." (*Farahi*, D.C. Cir. at 365).
*   **Breadth of Underlying Investigation:** The court looked broadly at the FBI's underlying investigation to validate the "ongoing" status. Given Farahi's connection to "foreign terrorist organizations," "Al Qaeda members," and "convicted terrorists," the breadth of the investigation makes it plausible that a prospective criminal proceeding continued as of the later filing. (*Farahi*, D.C. Cir. at 366).

**3. Segregation is Secondary**
The court also noted that the agency has met its burden to show that non-exempt information is not reasonably "segregable." Because the federal government specializes in law enforcement, its determination on the scope of the withheld categories is entitled to deference. If the nonexempt portions of the records are "interspersed line-by-line" or otherwise meaningless outside the context of the still-pending investigation, they cannot be safely segregated even if the "ongoing" issue had been better explained. (*Meat Data Cent.*, Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977)).

**Conclusion**
The D.C. Circuit’s acceptance of the declaration rests on the principle that the agency’s burden does not require a constant update when the relevant law enforcement context has not materially changed. Where an agency’s initial detailed declaration establishes a high level of pendency of enforcement proceedings, a subsequent declaration confirming that the investigation remains "ongoing" is sufficient provided the underlying investigation remains sufficiently "concrete" to justify continued law enforcement withholding. The court effectively reasons that a declaration of "ongoing" status, while potentially vague in isolation, is legally standard and sufficient when supported by a history of specific factual findings regarding the nature of the investigation.

### What is the specific 'nexus' burden the government must meet to establish records were compiled for law enforcement purposes under Campbell?
Question:
In Section IV.A, the court quotes Campbell v. DOJ regarding the 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties.' In practice, how specific must the agency's declaration be regarding this nexus to satisfy Exemption 7(A)? Is it enough to state a person had 'connections' to violence, or does the agency need to detail how the investigation specifically fulfills a federal law enforcement duty?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Under *Campbell v. DOJ* as applied in *Farah v. FBI*, the agency must establish a **rational nexus** between the investigation and a specific **federal law enforcement duty**. It is not sufficient for the agency to merely claim that a person had "connections." The declaration must specifically explain that the investigation was conducted to fulfill a federal enforcement obligation and connect the individual or incident to a possible violation of federal law or security risk.

***

**Legal Analysis**

Your question addresses the specificity requirement for Exemption 7(A), which protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." In *Farah*, the D.C. Circuit clarifies that while the agency enjoys deference, it bears the burden of proof.

### 1. The Specificity of the "Nexus" Burden
In Section IV.A of the opinion, the court explicitly defines the standard derived from *Campbell*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998). The opinion states that the government's declaration must establish:

> "a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,' as well as a 'connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'" *Farah*, 2025 WL 5243, at *8 (quoting *Campbell*, 164 F.3d at 32); see also *Farah* at *9.

This standard serves as the answer to your specific query: **You cannot satisfy Exemption 7(A) with a generic assertion of "connections."** The agency must detail how the specific investigation fulfills a statutory or regulatory law enforcement obligation (e.g., preventing national security attacks or detecting terrorism) and why the individual's involvement poses a risk that links to federal law.

In *Farah*, the Court found the government satisfied this burden because the declaration linked the withheld records to investigations "of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject's association with individuals directly involved in such violations." *Id.* at *10. The court required the government to show that the investigation was not merely administrative but was directly tied to an ongoing or anticipated enforcement action.

### 2. Public vs. Ex Parte Declarations
While the *Campbell* nexus requires specificity, the burden of explaining this varies based on the type of declaration submitted.
*   **Public Declarations:** The government must provide a "reasonable specificity" in its public declarations to justify secrecy. In *Farah*, this meant the initial public declaration from David Hardy had to explain that the records were "compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations... pending law enforcement proceedings." *Farah* at *10.
*   **Supplemental/Ex Parte Declarations:** In cases where the government's burden involves potentially revealing details about ongoing threats to national security, the opinion allows for more detailed justifications to be submitted ex parte (outside of public record). In *Farah*, the Agency used an ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender and an update on the Hardy declaration to confirm that the investigation "remained ongoing" without disclosing the specific content of the ex parte justification.

### 3. Deference and Burden Sharing
Although the government submits declarations, the question remains whether the nexus exists. The *Campbell* principle established in *Farah* is that the agency bears the burden of proving the exemption applies. However, the court notes that the agency is entitled to "defence" in its determination. In *Farah*, the agency met this burden by "confirm[ing] that investigations remained ongoing" and detailing the specific nature of the alleged ties to foreign terrorist organizations. *Farah* at *11.

***

**Conclusion**

To be precise with the standard established in *Farah*: **A generic statement that a person "had connections" is likely insufficient.** The agency must detail the **specific investigation purpose** (e.g., counter-terrorism, national security) and articulate how the records were compiled to **fulfill a federal law enforcement duty** (e.g., investigating alliances, potential violations of federal law). The declaration must make it clear that the investigation targets an individual's ties to a recognized enforcement duty, as the court did not view the FBI's declaration sufficient without that explicit "nexus" of enforcement duty against a "connection to a security risk."

### Is a FOIA plaintiff required to oppose an agency's use of ex parte declarations, or does that create an automatic basis for respecting the agency's findings?
Question:
Regarding Section V, the court notes that Farahi forfeited his challenge to the district court's reliance on ex parte declarations because he did not oppose the agency's motion to file them under seal. I understand ex parte filings raise concerns under FOIA. Does a plaintiff's failure to object during the lawsuit effectively waive their ability to argue that the ex parte submission itself was the only basis for withholding agreement? Why does the court rely on affidavits given a separation regarding the standard?
Answer:
### Answer

Based on the holdings in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), yes, a FOIA plaintiff’s failure to object to an agency's motion to submit ex parte declarations under seal constitutes a waiver of the right to challenge the district court’s reliance on those specific documents. It effectively prevents the plaintiff from arguing that the district court should not have accepted the declarations as the sole basis for upholding an exemption.

**1. Procedural Waiver of Reliance on Ex Parte Declarations**
The central ruling on this issue is found in Section V of the opinion. While acknowledging that ex parte declarations generally "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," the appellate court held that Farahi forfeited his ability to challenge the process used by the district court. Specifically, the D.C. Circuit stated:

> "Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."
> (See *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep't of Veterans Affs.*, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C. Cir. 2023)).

The opinion explains that this limitation is inherent in FOIA litigation involving national security or enforcement materials. Because the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the secret material is through an ex parte declaration, allowing a plaintiff to challenge the declaration's admissibility without objecting would undermine the procedural mechanism necessary for adjudication.

**2. Substantive Burden vs. Reliance on Evidence**
While the failure to object waives procedural challenges to the *reliance* on the sealed documents, it does not automatically prevent the appellate court from conducting a substantive review of whether Exemption 7(A) applies on the record. The court's analysis of whether an exemption applies remains governed by the independent standards of law such as:

*   **Duty for Law Enforcement:** The agency must show the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." *Id.*
*   **Interference with Proceedings:** The agency must show release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated." *Id.*
*   **Deference:** Agencies specializing in enforcement receive deference on these determinations, provided the record shows a reasonable nexus. *Campbell v. DOJ*; *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ*.

Therefore, while the plaintiff lost the argument that the *method* of withholding (via ex parte declaration) was improper, the substantive burden of proof regarding whether the withholding was justified remained valid.

**3. Why Affidavits Were Sufficient (Section V)**
The court relied on the ex parte affidavits because they were the primary means for the district court to form findings on segregability without violating the secrecy of the records. The opinion notes that ex parte submissions allow courts to find that exempt material is "inextricably intertwined" with non-exempt information without publishing the rivalrous analysis on the public record. Since Farahi did not oppose the filing of these declarations initially, he may not attack the sufficiency of the agency's "meaningful information" to support the segregability determination later.

**Summary**
In *Farahi*, the failure to file a timely objection to the ex parte submission triggered a procedural forfeiture. Consequently, the court could not entertain arguments challenging the district court's reliance on those declarations. However, this was a procedural bar, not a substantive judgment of the agency's authority to withhold; the court independently deferred to the agency’s findings on Exemption 7(A) and aggregated the public claim evidence to determine if the withholding was proper.

### What statutory standard does the D.C. Circuit apply to distinguish between 'randomly interspersed' and 'inextricably intertwined' documents under the Mead test?
Question:
The court discusses segregability in Section V, citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. and noting that a 'small amount... interspersed line-by-line' is not likely to be reasonably segregable. How does the court determine when information is not 'randomly interspersed' versus when the exempt content creates an 'inextricable' mix? In FOIA cases, why is the 'focus' on information rather than just words, and does that distinguish the lines between releasable and withheld data?
Answer:
### Answer

**Short Answer**

Under the D.C. Circuit’s analysis in *Farahi*, the statutory standard for distinguishing between releasable "randomly interspersed" lines and withheld "inextricably intertwined" material is found in **5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)** and the segregability requirement under **5 U.S.C. § 552(b)**, applied through the framework established in **Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Air Force**.

The court determines that exempt information must be withheld in its entirety only when exempt and non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined," rather than when they are merely mixed. However, the court explicitly adopts from *Mead* a standard that treats **document mixing of a "line-by-line" nature as presumptively not segregable.** The "focus" on information rather than individual words prevents plaintiffs from releasing partial data that would not constitute meaningful information on its own.

### Detailed Explanation

**The Segregability Standard and *Mead Data Cent.* Application**
The D.C. Circuit reviews segregability under FOIA Exemption 7(A) to ensure agencies take "reasonable steps" to segregate nonexempt information. In Section V, the court cites *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977), establishing that withholding a record is permissible only when nonexempt portions are "reasonably segregable." If they are not, the agency may withhold the entire record.

Specifically, the court applies *Mead* to determine if material is "inextricably intertwined." The court notes that if nonexempt information is "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions," disclosure is barred. However, in determining whether segregability exists, the court relies on the government’s burden to demonstrate conclusively that non-exempt information cannot be separated without destroying the exempt nature of the material.

**Distinguishing "Randomly Interspersed" vs. "Inextricably Intertwined"**
The *Farahi* opinion clarifies *how* this distinction applies in practice, noting that the physical mechanics of the document do not always dictate release. Section V states:
> "But if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,' that material is not likely to be 'reasonably segregable.'" *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261.

This indicates that while the text signifies a distinction, the standard line-by-line interspersion is treated as **non-segregable**. This means the mere presence of words mixed within a singular document pattern is not enough to satisfy FOIA disclosure; if the relationship between the exempt and non-exempt content is so dense (interspersed line-by-line) that separation would be unreasonable, the court will find the information is not segregable. Conversely, a mix is only releasable if the exempt content and non-exempt content are distinct enough to be separated, or if the aggregator can articulate how the exempt content does not taint the unreleased portion.

**The Focus on Information vs. Words**
The *Farahi* opinion addresses why plaintiffs should not be able to demand the release of "words" without the surrounding "information." The court relies on the language from *Mead* defining the scope of what is discoverable. Section V states:
> "So, while Farahi is correct that individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI’s interests, that is neither here nor there. The focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55.

This establishes that a redacted release is limited by substantive utility. The "focus on information" means that plaintiffs may challenge withholding only if they can identify meaningful information separated from the exempt material. Terrestrial or isolated words that do not convey substantial information are excluded from the analysis of releasable data. If a word has no independent informational import, its inclusion or exclusion does not change the freeing or seizing of "information." Therefore, the decision to release depends on whether the *content* can be segregated, not just whether the string of characters can be typed.

**Conclusion**
According to *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit applies the *Mead* standard where the court must accept the government’s declarations regarding whether exempt material is inextricably intertwined. The court does not validate the segregation of random line-by-line interspersion unless the government demonstrates meaningful separability that preserves information integrity. This aligns with the goal of relief being based on meaningful information.

### Why did the plaintiff forfeit his arguments regarding 'overbroad' categories when withholding documents within Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
Section IV.C mentions that when an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are 'sufficiently distinct' to allow a court to determine exemptions. The plaintiff here conceded that the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad. On what grounds does the D.C. Circuit hold that a plaintiff forfeits these arguments, and is there a procedural timeline requirement for challenging the distinctness of withholding categories during summary judgment?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on the D.C. Circuit’s opinion in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the plaintiff forfeited his arguments regarding the overbreadth of the FBI's withholding categories because he failed to provide a specific explanation supporting those claims. The opinion does not establish a rigid procedural timeline (e.g., a specific filing date) as the reason for the forfeiture; rather, it emphasizes the **substantive requirement** to offer concrete reasoning to preserve the argument.

### Why the Plaintiff Forfeited the Argument
The plaintiff, Farahi, conceded that the FBI had seized the categorization of his files and argued they were overbroad. However, the appellate court found the argument defective on its merits and thus waived it entirely. According to Section IV.C of the opinion, the court held:

> "Farahi briefly objects that the government did not adequately justify how it defined the categories of documents... But Farahi provides **no specific explanation** for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad. Farahi accordingly **forfeited his arguments on this score.**"

The forfeiture was not due to a procedural error regarding when the objection was filed, but rather because the argument lacked the necessary detail. To avoid forfeiture, a plaintiff must articulate with specificity why the categories are constitutionally or statistically overbroad.

### Relevant Legal Standard & Precedent
The court clarified that under FOIA, when an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, the categories used must be **"sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine exemptions."**

*Foad Farahi v. FBI*, 10th Cir. (D.C. Cir. 2025), cited *CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088* (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB*, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)) for the distinctness requirement.
*   **Key Takeaway:** The categories placed on the record must allow the reviewing court to determine which specific exemptions apply to each portion of the withheld evidence.
*   **Forfeiture Rationale:** The court explicitly relied on **Khine v. DHS**, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019), to conclude that without a specific explanation, the plaintiff forfeits the claim. This implies that general assertions of overbreadth are insufficient to sustain an objection that is procedurally barred from further adjudication.

### On Procedural Timeline Requirements
You asked whether there is a procedural timeline requirement for challenging the distinctness of withholding categories during summary judgment. **The provided opinion does not impose a specific temporal deadline (such as a hearing or briefing cut-off) for this challenge.**

Instead, the forfeiture resulted from the **absence of substantive specificity**. While standard FOIA litigation typically requires parties to address segregability and categorization at the summary judgment stage to avoid surprise arguments on appeal, the D.C. Circuit here focused the forfeiture on the quality of the explanation ("no specific explanation") rather than the timing of the complaint. The opinion implies that if the agency's categories are challenged, the challenger must provide concrete legal or factual reasons for the overbreadth, regardless of the stage at which it is formally raised, to avoid losing the issue.

### Summary
The plaintiff lost the opportunity to argue regarding overbroad categories because he failed to provide a **specific explanation** as to *why* they were overbroad. The D.C. Circuit treats the lack of this supporting detail as a failure to substantiate the claim, effectively forfeiting the argument under *Khine*. While the specific procedural deadline for raising this is not detailed in the opinion, the practical effect is that an entirely conclusory challenge at the appellate stage was considered procedurally barred due to the lack of the requisite evidentiary support.
