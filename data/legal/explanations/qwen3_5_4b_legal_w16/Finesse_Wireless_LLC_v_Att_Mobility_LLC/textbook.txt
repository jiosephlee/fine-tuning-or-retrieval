Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## Appellate Jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)

To understand the review this court provides, one must first understand where the case originated and the statutory authority guiding this appellate review. The *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* opinion operates under the jurisdiction granted by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This statute empowers courts of appeals with specific jurisdiction over appeals in patent cases, distinguishing them from general civil appeals. By citing this specific provision, the court establishes that this is a non-ordinary patent appeal involving a decision by the District Court. This statutory hook is critical for any legal analyst because it dictates the scope of the appellate review. Unlike standard civil appeals where a district court’s award is often reviewed for abuse of discretion, patent appeals under this section trigger a specific subject-matter jurisdiction requirement. In this意见书, the court notes that they have this specific authority because the case arises from a district court ruling on a patent dispute, a common trigger for the Federal Circuit (or the relevant Court of Appeals in the geographic assignment). Understanding this jurisdiction allows students to anticipate that the court will be strictly adhering to the Federal Circuit review standards specific to intellectual property disputes, ensuring the legal question is handled under the specialized rules applicable to technology and patents.

## Summary of the Parties and Patents

The parties involved in this litigation represent a classic patent infringement scenario, with the named plaintiff and defendants defined by the patent assignment. The Appellee, Finesse Wireless LLC, holds the patents at issue. The Appellants consist of two entities: AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corporation. Both entities are the owners of the intellectual property in question and the appellants are collectively appealing against the Appellee. The patents asserted are U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775, which the plaintiff refers to as the '134 and '775 patents, respectively. These patents are central to the dispute because the district court determined they were infringed upon Finesse’s method for mitigating signal interference. The Appellants challenge this finding by asserting they do not infringe any of the patent claims. For a law student studying this opinion, it is essential to note who bears the burden of proof: the patent owner (Finesse) must sufficiently prove the accused product infringes the claims. The distinction between the Appellants and Appellee is vital for procedural posture because it establishes the roles in the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Appellants, having lost the case below, now argue before the appellate court regarding the validity of the jury’s verdict.

## Description of the Accused Radios and PIM Cancellation Features

Understanding the factual allegations requires familiarity with the technology at issue: radiofrequency modulation and interference cancellation. Finesse describes the patents as generally relating to methods for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. Radios generally operate by avoiding signal interference by using different frequencies; however, when channels encounter obstacles, transmit signals can combine to form new frequencies known as intermodulation products. This is referred to in the opinion as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). There is a distinction made between IMPs caused by active components and those passively generated by external obstacles like metal fences or loose connections. While passive obstacles generate “passive intermodulation products” (PIM), other intermodulations can stem from mechanical internal issues. The patents claim methods to handle these formations, specifically by isolating different signals and calculating estimates to cancel them out. The specific patent claims at issue involve isolating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and then canceling out those interference generating signals. To determine infringement, one must identify whether the radio being sued uses this method. The court notes that Finesse bears the burden of proving the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” This technological context is crucial for understanding the factual disputes at the District Court level, specifically regarding the technical details of the radios' internal function.

## Procedural History of the District Court Litigation

The underlying dispute began in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. Finesse initiated the lawsuit alleging that the accused radios infringed upon the '134 and '775 patents. The jury made a finding of fact, declaring all asserted claims valid and infringed. This jury finding led to a damages award of $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the remaining life of the patents. Upon receiving the jury verdict, the Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) moved for relief. Their requests included a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, a JMOL on damages, and a request for a new trial. The lower court, representing the District Court, denied all of these motions. This denial brings the case to the appellate level. The student must note the significance of the District Court's decision: the appellate court now reviews this denial. This review process is the primary focus of the opinion. The District Court had determined that the evidentiary record was sufficient for the jury's finding, a decision that the appellate court scrutinizes under the standards of law it applies to such motions.

## Appellants' Motions: JMOL on Noninfringement, JMOL on Damages, and New Trial

The procedural posture of the appeal is defined by the specific motions the Appellants exercised to avoid an adverse judgment. The Appellants appealed the denial of the JMOL of noninfringement and the denial of a new trial on damages. While the district court's damages decision was within its discretion, the denial of a claim finding (noninfringement) required a specific legal analysis of the district court's reasoning. The Appellants argued that the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support a finding that their products infringed the Finesse patents. Specifically, they contended that their radios do not sample the correct signals or perform the claimed multiplications to cancel the interference. The district court had previously removed the jury verdict by denying their motion, concluding that the jury's conclusion was supported by the evidence presented. Now, on appeal, the focus shifts to whether, after reviewing the evidence, the evidence pointed overwhelmingly to one party so that the court believes reasonable jurors could not arrive at a contrary conclusion. The Appellants' motions establish the scope of the review required from the appellate court. They specifically argue that technical limitations, such as the order of signals being sampled or the number of multiplications, were not met in the accused radio technology. Understanding these motions provides the student with the framework for the subsequent legal analysis, where the court will evaluate whether the District Court's rejection of the JMOL motion was legally sound.

# Standards of Review for Patent Infringement Appeals

## Chapter Description

This chapter defines the governing legal tests applied by the Federal Circuit to district court decisions in patent infringement cases, distinguishing between the standards for question of law (de novo) versus questions of fact (substantial evidence and local circuit standards).

## Standard of Review for District Court JMOL Deni als (De Novo / Fifth Circuit Loops)

When a litigant appeals a district court’s denial of a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the Appellate Court must apply the specific standard of review mandated by the regional circuit to which the federal court belongs. In this case, the Federal Circuit addressed the standard of review in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, explicitly tying its analysis to the law of the Fifth Circuit to ensure procedural consistency. The opinion states that the court reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit. In *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the court of appeals in the Fifth Circuit had already defined the standard for this specific Grievance: the court reviews denials of JMOL de novo.

This de novo review does not apply lightly. The law of the regional circuit mandates that the appellate court reverses a district court’s denial of JMOL only under a specific threshold: "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This "loop" of deference and de novo review requires the appellate court to look at the evidence through the lens of the jury's perspective. If the evidence in the record is so one-sided, the jury's assessment becomes moot. In *Finesse*, the court applied this lens when reviewing AT&T’s motion for noninfringement, finding that the expert testimony sufficiently failed under this high bar because it relied on self-contradictory testimony rather than overwhelming data to support the Jury’s verdict.

## Claim Construction Review (De Novo)

Claim construction is a legal question, and therefore, it receives de novo review on appeal distinct from factual findings. The Federal Circuit establishes that the court reviews claim construction de novo. However, while the appellate court reviews the construction decision itself from scratch, it must often consider any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction for clear error, though the standard remains primarily legal. In the *Finesse* opinion, the court noted that the standard applies the district court’s construction decision based on the agreed-upon construction and inventor testimony.

This distinction is vital for lawyers because it means the appellate court is not bound by lower court reasons for rulings unless they fail clearly. In *Finesse*, the district court had construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to be "separately identifiable," but not "unique." The court relied on expert testimony. On appeal, the court had to determine the correct scope. Because the construction is a question of law, the appellate court did not stay the construction based on the trial record alone. Instead, it re-evaluated the construction of claim terms like "signals of interest." This de novo review allows the appellate court to correct misconstructions that would otherwise lead to unjustified infringement findings. The appellate court in *Finesse* applied this standard to determine that the "interference generating signals" could not be claimed samples generated downstream of the ADC.

## Review of Jury Patent Findings (Substantial Evidence)

Once a question of fact arises, such as whether a jury's finding of infringement was reasonable, the standard of review shifts. Under *Wi-LAN* law and the Fifth Circuit's practice, a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial. This is the lowest standard allowed in patent appeals that still ensures high-quality jury decisions.

In *Finesse*, the court applied this standard to the noninfringement proceedings. The court held that the jury’s finding of infringement for the ’134 patent was not supported by substantial evidence because the expert testimony was "confusing and unclear." Even if the evidence was viewed in the light most favorable to the jury as permitted by the Fifth Circuit standard, it was not found substantial enough to prevent a reasonable jury from reaching a contrary conclusion. The court reasoned that the expert, Dr. Wells, had mapped certain inputs to "interference generating signals" incorrectly. The court highlighted that Finesse’s attorney pieced together contradictory testimony from across the record rather than relying on clear exposition. This application of the substantial evidence standard serves as a safeguard: it allows the jury decision to stand unless the evidence is so flimsy that no reasonable person could have reached that specific conclusion.

## Standard of Review for Denials of New Trial on Damages (Abuse of Discretion)

The standard of review for denials of motions for a new trial on damages is distinct from the JMOL and jury finding standards. Here, the appellate court reviews the district court’s decision for abuse of discretion. This grants the district court significant latitude to weigh the damages based on economic factors, jury instructions, and expert testimony, provided the decision is not arbitrary or unreasonable.

In *Finesse*, the Appellants appealed the denial of a new trial on damages. However, because the appellate court reversed the JMOL of noninfringement, the district court's entire award became invalid. The opinion explicitly states, "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award." While the new trial standard would be "abuse of discretion," the damages were not reviewed under this standard independently; they were vacated due to the fundamental reversal of the infringement issue. This illustrates the procedural posture: if the liability (infringement) is not established, the quantum (damages) calculation is moot.

## Application to the Fifth Circuit Standard cited in WI-LAN v. Apple Inc.

The Federal Circuit relies on regional circuit law to determine the appropriate standard of review for both infringement and damages in patent cases. In the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the standard applied is referenced to regional circuit law through the citation to *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). *Wi-LAN* serves as the governing authority for the Fifth Circuit's approach to patent jury reviews in the Federal Circuit.

The case in *Finesse* cites *Wi-LAN* specifically to establish the rules regarding JMOL and substantial evidence. The court applies the regional circuit law, here the Fifth Circuit, to distinguish between the "logic of local standards" and the "rule of law." In the *Finesse* opinion, the court cites that district court decisions are governed by the law of the regional circuit to ensure consistency across the appeals process. This citation anchors the appellate review, ensuring that the "light most favorable to the verdict" and "substantial evidence" standards are not solely Federal Circuit inventions but are part of the broader circuit law context. The court also notes that when a party moves for communication de novo, they do not require the district court to have erred. But when the question is factual as in *Finesse*, the "abuse of discretion" standard becomes relevant. The citation to Washington in *Wi-LAN* confirms that the appellate court does not substitute its judgment for the jury’s without substantial evidence.

# Analysis of the '134 Patent: Signal Sampling and Mapping

## Analysis of '134 Claims: 'Signals of Interest' and 'Interference Generating Signals'

When analyzing a patent opinion, the first step involves understanding the specific limitation language that defines the scope of protection. In *Finesse Wireless LLC*, Claim 1 of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 outlines a method for reducing interference in radio systems. A critical element of Claim 1 is the requirement to over-sample a passband of received signals, where those signals must include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." To establish infringement under this limitation, Finesse must demonstrate that the accused Nokia radios sample a passband containing both types of signals simultaneously. The "interference generating signals" are defined as signals capable of generating intermodulation products inband of the signals of interest. This claim construction dictates that the infringement is not established simply by the presence of noise or interference in a general sense; the accused device must physically sample a passband that incorporates the signals listed as the sources of that interference. If the hardware architecture prevents the simultaneous sampling of these specific signals at the claimed frequency, the claim is not met. The court reviews the evidence presented at trial to determine whether a reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios satisfied this specific signal mapping requirement.

## The Burden of Proof on Plaintiff (Finesse)

In patent infringement cases, the plaintiff bears the burden of proof to show that the accused product infringes every step of the asserted claims. During a trial, this burden translates into the requirement that the plaintiff present evidence sufficient to support a finding of infringement. In the appellate context, when addressing the denial of a motion for JMOL, the standard of review is de novo, but the underlying review of fact focuses on whether there is sufficient evidence to uphold the jury's verdict. Finesse, as the movant for JMOL of noninfringement, was required to provide competent evidence that the jury's finding despite its doubts was supported by the record. The court noted that Finesse bore the burden of proving the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." If the plaintiff's evidence fails to demonstrate that the specific signals required by the claim are physically capable of being sampled at the time of the infringing act, the burden cannot be met. The court emphasized that the plaintiff cannot simply suggest a technical argument; the evidence must be clear enough that a jury could reasonably rely upon it to convict for infringement.

## Evaluation of Expert Testimony (Dr. Wells) and the 'Red Path'

The sufficiency of the evidence in *Finesse* hinged largely on the testimony of Dr. Wells, Finesse's expert witness. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the claimed limits by mapping specific technical signals from the Nokia technical document onto the claim terminology. He identified the "downlink transmit reference" as the "signals of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" as the "interference generating signals." However, a significant portion of the trial testimony and cross-examination involved the identification of signals on a red path depicted in the technical document. Dr. Wells consistently testified that the red path was represented by the "downlink TX reference" and the "modeled PIM path." This testimony creates confusion for the reader and the appellate court regarding what specific signals were actually present in the passband. When challenged on cross-examination, Dr. Wells indicated that the two inputs represented by the signals could be x1 and x2, yet he retained his mapping of the "red path" to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM. The appellate court noted that piecing together different parts of the testimony over ninety pages apart did not constitute clear evidence of infringement. This lack of clarity impacted the J.M.L. argument because the court must be satisfied that the testimony was not merely ambiguous, but sufficient for a jury to be convinced.

## Finding of Insufficiency: 'Modeled PIM Path Generation After Sampling'

A pivotal objection raised by the appellate court centered on the technical timeline of how the signals were generated. The claimed infringement requires that the "interference generating signals" exist and are sampled within the passband of received signals. However, the technical testimony established that the "modeled PIM path," which Dr. Wells identified as the "interference generating signals," was generated downstream in the GROOT FPGA after the analog-to-digital conversion occurred. This created a temporal contradiction: the signals could not be sampled before they were generated. Dr. Wells acknowledged that the modeled PIM signal was created by the system, but failed to explain how these signals existed at the point of sampling in the passed stop positions. The court reasoned that the receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes interference generating signals since those signals are not generated until after sampling occurs. Consequently, the limitation requiring the sampling of these signals could not be met physically by the accused device. This technical impossibility means that the jury's finding that the signals were sampled in the required manner could not be supported by the evidence, requiring a reversal of the JMOL denial.

## Determination on Substantial Evidence for the '134 Patent Infringement'

The final step in the *Finesse* appeal was the application of the substantial evidence standard to the expert testimony. The appellate court reviews district court denials of JMOL de novo, but must decide if there is substantial evidence to support the court's decision to uphold the infringement verdict. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence. In *Finesse*, the court concluded that the evidence was insufficient to satisfy this standard because the testimony was self-contradictory and clear. Dr. Wells continued to testify that the modeled PIM path was the "interference generating signals" and never clarified that it was referring to x1 and x2 as required by the claim construction inference. The court noted that this sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict, citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*. Because the plaintiff rested its case on an expert's testimony that could not logically support the claim mapping, the jury's verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. The appellate court therefore reversed the district court's denial of JMOL, holding that the evidence was insufficient to uphold the finding of infringement for the asserted claims of the '134 patent.

## Subtopic 1: Analysis of '775 Claims: Seven Multiplication Requirement

In Patent No. 775, the central method claim at issue was for performing interference cancellation in a receiver. A key limitation in Claim 1 required that the method achieve this by "generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 and S1×S2×S3 and S1×S1×S3 and S2×S2×S3 and S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3." This limitation explicitly required seven specific third-order multiplications to be performed on the three signals to produce the interference cancellation signals. For a student reading this opinion, it is crucial to note that under federal patent law, infringement of a patent claim occurs when every element of the claim is present in the accused device and performed in order. Here, the plurality of the judicial review focused heavily on whether the accused Nokia radios isolated and substituted these exact seven multiplications. Appellants argued that it was impossible for the accused radios to perform seven multiplications given their architecture. Finesse, the owner of the patent, argued that Dr. Wells, the expert witness for Finesse, identified that the accused radios did, in fact, perform these multiplications based on a technical document provided to the jury. The court held that establishing infringement requires clear mapping from the technical functionality of the accused device to the limitations of the patent claim. When the limited number of distinct mathematical operations in the claims becomes a point of contention, the jury's verdict must be supported by evidence that demonstrates the actual computational steps taken by the circuit are identical to those described in the claim. In this instance, the argument turned on whether there was sufficient evidence to support the finding that not just three, but seven specific multiplications were generated.

## Subtopic 2: Claim Construction: 'Separately Identifiable' vs 'Unique' Signals

Claim construction establishes the legal definition of patent claim terms. In the '775 patent analysis, confidence in the factual determination relied on two critical assumptions regarding the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3." First, the district court constructed these signals as "separately identifiable," meaning they can be distinguished, even if they are the same type of signal. Second, the district court credited both intrinsic evidence (the patent documents) and inventor (extrinsic) testimony, both suggesting that S1, S2, and S3 did not have to be unique or distinct from the source. At the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) stage, Appellants raised an argument that "three signals" implies "three unique signals." Appellants failed to challenge this specific construction at the appropriate Rule 50(a) stage regarding the lien of noninfringement. Because of this procedural failure, the Fifth Circuit likely viewed the waiver of the unique argument by the district court. However, regarding the "seven multiplications," the district court did not reach the issue of the quantity of multiplications at summary judgment in the district. The district court for the '775 patent rejected the argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be unique signals, but the more significant failure was the documentary evidence required to support the seven-multiplication factorial. The court found that there was no agreement or evidence regarding how the three signals mapped onto the required multiplications.

## Subtopic 3: Evidence Review: The Nokia Technical Document vs. Claim Limitation

To prove infringement, a party must show that the accused device performs every element of the claim. Dr. Wells, the Finesse expert, testified that the accused Nokia radios infringed '775 Patent Claim 1. He relied on a Nokia technical document to support his testimony. This document mapped the internal operations of the accused radios to the mathematical requirements of the patent. Dr. Wells argued that the document showed the accused radios multiplied the three distinct multiplications listed within it, arguing that each of those distinct multiplications corresponded to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. The court found that Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to show that the accused radios perform the claimed multiplications in real-time. However, the court examined the document closely and observed that it involved "modulus and complex conjugate operations." In patent infringement litigation, the record must clearly show how the specific steps in the claim were executed. Dr. Wells identified three multiplications in the Nokia document: the product of x1 and x1, x1 and x1, and x1 and x2, for example. The issue was whether these represented the seven required multiplications claimed in the '775 patent. The court noted that to satisfy the claim limitation, the expert or plaintiff must map the documents to the claims explicitly. The evidence presented did not clearly establish how the claimed multiplications were calculated from the technical document presented.

## Subtopic 4: Discovery of Missing Multiplications in the Document

A critical failure in the '775 infringement finding was the lack of mapping between the claimed architecture and the actual implementation described in evidence. The '775 Claim 1 limitation specifies a particular digit configuration: seven specific multiplication chains. For instance, S1×S1×S2, followed by S1×S2×S2, and so on. Appellants pointed out that the Nokia document they offered at trial only listed three distinct multiplications (e.g., x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|). There was no evidence that the accused radios performed all seven distinct multiplications as required by the claim limitation. Even if the argument was made on appeal that the accused radios performed ten multiplications based on other mathematical operations, Finesse had to explain how the seven claimed multiplications were specifically identified within that list. The court found that nowhere in the expert testimony or the record was there an explanation of which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications. Furthermore, because the document did not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse's proposed mapping was inconsistent. The claim required specific mathematical operations to be performed. If the evidence only showed other mathematical operations or fewer distinct operations, the jury could not find substantial evidence of infringement.

## Subtopic 5: Determination on Substantial Evidence for the '775 Patent Infringement

The appellate court reviewed the district court's denial of JMOL under the standard that a "factual finding is supported by substantial evidence." This standard asks whether a reasonable jury could have found infringement in light of the evidence presented at trial. The court concluded that, regarding the limitation for seven multiplications, no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios performed these steps. The evidence provided only showed three distinct multiplications and involved complex conjugate and modulus operations that were not mapped to the specific S1, S2, S3 multiplication chains required by Claim 1[d]. Since the plaintiff Finesse failed to provide a clear mapping of how the accused device met the seven-multiplication limitation, the fact-finding below lacked the requisite evidentiary support. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL and judgment as a matter of law for the '775 patent. This decision highlights that experts cannot draw inferences from technical data that prove they did not perform every element of the claim, particularly when the required number of distinct configurations is not met. The damages award was vacated as a result of this reversal.

# Damages Review, Vacatur, and Final Disposition

## Motion for New Trial on Damages: Standard of Review and Hearing

On appeal, the primary procedural posture concerns the district court's handling of post-verdict motions. Beyond the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) addressed in prior sections, the district court for the Eastern District of Texas also denied a motion for a new trial on damages. Prior to this, the party with the burden of liability or compensation argued that the jury’s specifications for damage were unsupported by credible evidence. In federal patent appeals, particularly regarding monetary awards, the parties may seek review for errors made by the jury or specific legal miscalculations contained in the award. In this instance, Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) moved for JMOL on damages as well as a new trial to partly correct the award. This motion constitutes a distinct appellate issue because it does not challenge the substantive infringement but rather the quantification of redress for liability already found by the court below.

The Federal Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of a new trial on damages for abuse of discretion. This standard of review affords the district court significant deference in determining whether to allow a party to retry the damages issue. As the court notes, the standard aligns with regional circuit law and Fed. Cir. case law regarding the propriety of abstention of the district court's discretion in the property and money aspect of the verdict. The Fifth Circuit, the circuit with jurisdiction to review the Eastern District of Texas Judge's decision, maintains that damages awards are determined by the jury, and thus the district court's exercise of discretion to deny a new trial is subject to appellate review only if the decision is clearly arbitrary or capricious. The appellate court does not conduct a *de novo* re-examination of the math or the calculations unless there is a clear indication that the rule was not followed or the calculation was legally infeasible.

## Effect of Reversed Infringement on the Damages Award

Determining the effect of the reversal of the infringed finding is central to understanding the disposition. The core principle of patent law is that a patent holder may not recover damages for infringement because there was no infringement; damages are a correlative finding of liability, not liability without infringement. In this case, the Federal Circuit has determined that the jury found the infringing design of the accused radios to infringe on at least 51 key limitations of the asserted patents. However, the Federal Circuit found in the prior section that the evidence did not meet the high standard of substantive evidence to sustain a finding of infringement. When a court reverses the finding of infringement, the underlying damage award cannot stand as a legal basis for the plaintiff's recovery.

The court reasoned that a party cannot claim a return of funds for conduct that is not legally actionable. Since we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, the legal basis for the monetary award is eliminated. This is a procedural maneuver that relies on the substantive non-infringement holding to render the damages award a lone claim without relying on a finding of infringement. The appellate court did not re-express the new trial on damages specifically but vacated the entire award because the前提 (premise) on which it was built—the infringement—was legally removed. The court noted that the damages award was a transaction of the underlying claim, and because the claim was not established, the award could not remain on its face.

## Rationale for Vacating the Lump-Sum Damages

However, in patent practice, it is sometimes beneficial to determine if a district court's finding of infringement is valid, and in some cases, the damages could survive if the original verdict was partially correct under a different legal standard. But the prevailing principle here was that the entire verdict could not prevail because the findings of infringement were not supported by substantial evidence. For the Federal Circuit to instruct that the district court vacate the damages award, it must first be established that the reversal of infringement is complete and leaves the plaintiff with no legal basis to recover the specified amount.

In this instance, the Federal Circuit emphasized the structural relationship between the infringement and the damages. The district court provided a lump-sum damages award for the remaining life of the patents. Because the Federal Circuit reversed the noninfringement, the Federal Circuit was compelled to vacate the damages award, not merely to remand for a remand or reduction. The rationale is simply that an award for the reasonable value of a patent that does not include infringement claims must be struck because no impairment of rights is occurring. The court did not need to address the damages issue due to the determination of the noninfringement. This ensures that the lower court does not enter a judgment for a plaintiff in a case where the core right is not infringed.

## Final Order and Costs Enforcement

Finally, the appellate court must provide a final order of the appeal. The Federal Circuit issued an order reversing and vacating the district court's decisions. Specifically, the court ruled to reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents. Because the damages award is vacated upon the void of the infringing verdict, the final disposition is to vacate the damages award. This allows the parties to proceed to the next remedy, often a remand to the district court for consideration of the damages if the denial of JMOL finds that the evidence supports a finding of non-infringement or invalidity, or the parties might negotiate a settlement.

In terms of the procedural costs of the appeal, Federal Circuit law requires the economic costs of pending litigation to be allocated to the losing party or the party that suffered the loss from the reversal. In this determination, the Federal Circuit ordered costs to be awarded to the Defendants-Appellants. This allocation reflects the fact that the District Court made errors in the substantive findings of infringement and that the corporate appellants were the ones billed for the costs of the appeal on the damages motion and the JMOL denial. This final order ensures that the appellate decision is procedurally complete and resolves the financial consequences of the reversal.
