## The Red Path Confusion: When Contradictory Expert Testimony Destroys a Patent Verdict

The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* arrives at a critical juncture in patent litigation: the effective cost of winning a judgment in a future tense, where the Federal Circuit *Wi-LAN* standard for substantial evidence is applied to factual findings regarding infringement. With the District Court’s $166 million jury award vacated, the Federal Circuit has issued a stinging rebuke to plaintiffs who cannot support their claims through unimpeachable technical evidence. This case matters because it curbs the doctrine of the "Battle of Experts." A firm investigation into intermodulation product interference is not retroactive to the technical measurement of an ADC, and a jury verdict is not sacred if the underlying expert testimony is legally incapable of sustaining the claim at issue. *We* found the dissent in the *Wi-LAN* era still applies to the threshold standard of evidence.

The court’s reasoning hinged on two distinct layers of confusion: the '134 patent (interference cancellation) and the '775 patent. For the '134 patent, the Federal Circuit noted a substantial lack of clarity in Dr. Wells’ testimony regarding the "red path" in a Nokia technical document. This path represented the inputs to the RF ADC. Wells testified that the system samples "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." However, the defense highlighted a physical impossibility: the modeled PIM path, according to the red path being sampled, was generated downstream of the sampling point. When Dr. Wells was questioned on cross-examination regarding whether x1 and x2 on the diagram were the signals of interest or the modeled PIM, he failed to clarify his mapping. The Federal Circuit found this "confusing change of course" insufficient to satisfy the jury. *We* can only conclude the "substantial evidence" standard requires a clear, consistent link between claim limitation and the physical reality of the accused device, rather than a pieced-together tolerance for conflicting testimony on the stand.

The court was equally unconvinced by the Dr. Wells claim regarding the '775 patent, which required seven specific multiplications of three signals S1, S2, and S3. The jury verdict was based on the assertion that the accused device performed these multiplications, but Dr. Wells only identified three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document. Even more damning was the lack of proof that any of the claimed seven operations were actually performed, as the document relied on modulus and complex conjugate operations which are distinct entities under the claim. On appeal, even more evidence was offered, none of which clarified the mapping of the ten operations in the Nokia document to the seven required by the claim. The court noted plainly: "Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury." The court found no reasonable jury could find the seven multiplications were performed. This reinforces the proposition from the *Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.* line of cases: when a plaintiff cannot clear the record with the "clear and unambiguous" evidence for their mapping, they cannot assume the jury's finding of 'infringement' is legally valid.

From a doctrinal perspective, this opinion solidifies a narrow window for factual findings in patent cases. The Fifth Circuit’s "strongly and overwhelmingly" test for substantial evidence is still the guiding light for the Federal Circuit, and *Wi-LAN* clarifies that it is not enough for an expert to be technically correct. They must be legally correct at the time of testimony. This puts immense weight on the drafting of expert reports and the robustness of the testimony when challenges are raised about diagrammatic elements. If evidence is self-contradictory or fails to show clear mapping on contemporaneous challenges, the Federal Circuit may strike that evidence as legally insufficient. This warns of a heightened risk for plaintiffs who cannot produce a clear, unified record, especially in cases where the accused device relies on a specific logic that the patent claims require more than a "red path."



## Three Signals, Four Inputs, Seven Multiplications: The Struggle to Match Patent Claims to Technical Reality

In the world of telecommunications patent disputes, one of the most perilous gambles a litigant takes is trying to translate complex signal processing into a binary "yes or no" jury verdict. United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* highlights just how costly that gamble can be. The Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reversed a lower court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law, vacating a massive damages award of roughly $166 million for AT&T and Nokia. While the case involves broad claims of method for mitigating intermodulation interference, the prevailing lesson is that an infringement verdict cannot rest on expert testimony that ignores fundamental physical realities or oversights in the mathematical transformation of data.

The Federal Circuit confirmed that inference theory—using a single piece of evidence or testimony to bridge gaps in complex technical facts—runs the risk of failing the "substantial evidence" standard. **## The Struggle for Technical Accuracy**

Technically, the court found that the two inputs were insufficient to generate the required seven multiplications. The accused radios only used two inputs (x1, x2), and the expert identified only three distinct multiplications. The "claims were ambiguous" regarding the count of multiplications. The court found the "substantial evidence" standard was not met because the evidence at best supported a finding that the accused devices performed three multiplications, not seven.

Furthermore, the Court noted that the claims require three *signals* to be multiplied in seven *wises*. This distinction is significant. The jury was asked to find that the accused device performed a specific, highly complex mathematical transformation on the signals. The expert testimony provided no clear mapping between the three distinct multiplications on the Nokia technical document and the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse attempted to clarify the mapping on appeal, but because that explanation was never presented to the jury, the district court’s denial of motion for judgment as a matter of law could not be upheld.

**## Implications for the Patent Landscape**

This decision underscores the importance of grounding patent claims in the actual capabilities of the accused product, particularly in method-of-use patents involving radio frequency processing. While the doctrine of equivalents and claim construction are tools to interpret the scope of patent scope, they cannot be used to cloth a patent claim over technical impossibilities. The Court emphasized that when the party with the burden of proof presents an expert whose testimony contradicts physical laws or technical realities, the court should not assume the jury can overlook it.

A key takeaway for patentees is the necessity of aligning technical expert testimony with specific identifiers in the claim limitations. In the '134 patent, the mapping of "DL(TX) reference" and "modeled PIM path" to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" was crucial. Since the PIM path is generated downstream of the ADC, sampling it requires a forward signal, which is temporally impossible in the claimed method. By allowing evidence to rest on self-contradictory testimony, the Federal Circuit has set a precedent that entity-juries or district courts cannot rely on mere inferential leaps to establish infringement in complex signal processing.

**## Conclusion: The Limits of Paper Rulings**

The Federal Circuit’s decision to vacate the damages award is a reminder that the jury's role ends when the evidence cannot support a rational inference of infringement. The case demonstrates that proof of infringement in engineering challenges is not just a matter of counting signals or distinguishing "signals" from "products." It is often a rigorous forensic inspection of what physical architectures actually permit. For companies facing patent litigation in the communications industry, this case may be a vote against broad claim constructions that do not reflect the actual mathematical operations performed by the accused device. Ultimately, the Federal Circuit has reminded us that when a jury awards a verdict based on evidence that defies basic logic, the ultimate remedy is a reversal, not a confirmation.



# Reversing Facts, Not Law: The Federal Circuit's High Bar for "Substantial Evidence"

The commercial stakes in wireless communications patents are rarely higher than those involved in cellular radio interference. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit delivered a sharp reminder to patent owners that even when they sit at the jury side of the flag, the evidence must hold up to substantial scrutiny. The case, originating in the E.D. Tex with looming damages of over $166 million, serves as a practical guide to the Fifth Circuit’s specific—and high—standard for reviewing denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). For patentees, it is a clear message: Do not allow your expert testimony to wobble, and do not assume a reasonable jury can follow your technical roadmap when your own expert has made contradictory maps.

The Federal Circuit’s reasoning centers on the Fifth Circuit’s established standard for reviewing a denial of JMOL regarding a jury verdict. Under this standard, a court will reverse the district court's decision to uphold a verdict only if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in one party's favor that reasonable jurors could not reach a contrary conclusion. In cases where the patentee bears the burden of proving infringement, this places Finesse at a significant disadvantage. The court found that Dr. Wells, the principal infringement expert, did not map the signals correctly, creating a factual ambiguity so significant that the jury could not have knowingly proven infringement. The Federal Circuit emphasized that confusing testimony from a plaintiff’s burden-bearer is not sufficient to sustain a verdict.

This deficiency was most evident in the '134 patent, which required sampling of both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios sampled both signals via a red path, but on cross-examination, the defense arguments highlighted that the modeled PIM path was generated *after* sampling occurred, making it impossible for the sampled "passband" to include it. More critically, Dr. Wells later testified that the red path included two signals (x1 and x2), but he never explicitly mapped those to the claim limitations. Finesse argued that Dr. Wells had a "misimpression," but the court found that piecing together testimony from over ninety pages of the record did not meet the evidentiary bar for a substantial finding. This highlights a delicate balance: a jury finding is not just about meeting the legal standard, but about meeting the evidentiary threshold of factual clarity.

In the '775 patent case, the court similarly found the evidence failed to support the complexity of the claim elements. The claims required seven multiplications of three distinct signals, but the technical documents presented at trial showed only three distinct multiplications. The court noted that while Finesse had the burden of showing the multiplicative process occurred, the evidence provided did not track the specific math requirements of the claim. Unlike claim construction, which remains a matter of law review, the court found that the technical mapping failed at the factual level required to educate the jury. The court did not simply view the evidence as insufficient for the patentee to win; it found that the patentee provided no sufficient evidence for the jury to find the infringement allegedly occurred.

The decision vacates the lump sum damages against AT&T, but it exposes the fragility of jury findings when expert testimony is circular. The Federal Circuit’s statement that the verdict is not supported by substantial evidence if "no reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented" suggests that the Fifth Circuit is heavily skeptical of relying on technical experts who cannot explain contradictory bits of their own testimony. For litigators, the implication is that a JMOL motion should be treated as a threshold standard. If the plaintiff cannot clearly map their patent elements to the hardware, the court may simply strike the claim.

### Implications for Expert Testimony and Claim Construction

The distinction here is crucial for patent litigation. While claim construction—interpreting what terms like "three signals" mean—is reviewed de novo by the appellate court, factual findings supporting the infringement are reviewed for substantial evidence. In *Finesse*, the court balanced these two concepts but ultimately ruled in favor of the appellants (the accused infringers) because the evidentiary support for the infringement imperatively failed. The Federal Circuit’s opinion, citing *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, suggests that the standard of "substantial evidence" acts as a gatekeeper ensuring that the jury’s verdict is not just an invention but a fact grounded in a clear, coherent evidence trail.

This sets a high bar for patentees to prove infringement when faced with complex technical rebuttals. It means that even if the patent is valid, if the plaintiff's evidence is weak or contradictory, the court can strike down the judgment as a matter of law without legally overturning the patent itself. Furthermore, the opinion suggests that if a party presents conflicting expert testimony, the court may not allow a jury to fill in the gaps.

### Guidance for Litigators

This case offers a two-part warning for patent owners. First, ensure your expert testifies in a way that directly addresses claim limitations. If you need a jury to believe that a device samples specific signal paths, ensure the expert clearly maps the device mechanics to the claim language rather than relying on secondary testimony or implication. Second, understand that the Federal Circuit is not a rubber stamp for jury findings; it can reverse if the evidence is not substantial. This suggests that a strategic move to file JMOL motions, not just at the trial level but whenever the evidence is muddy, should be used more aggressively.

Ultimately, the Federal Circuit reinforced that in patent litigation, the burden of proof remains on the prosecutor. If the evidence does not meet the substantial threshold required to support the jury's conclusion, the judgments of law must be reversed.
