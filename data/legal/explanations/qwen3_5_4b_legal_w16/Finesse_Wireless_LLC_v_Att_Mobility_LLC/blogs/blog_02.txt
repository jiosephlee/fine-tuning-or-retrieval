## Three Signals, Four Inputs, Seven Multiplications: The Struggle to Match Patent Claims to Technical Reality

In the world of telecommunications patent disputes, one of the most perilous gambles a litigant takes is trying to translate complex signal processing into a binary "yes or no" jury verdict. United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* highlights just how costly that gamble can be. The Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reversed a lower court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law, vacating a massive damages award of roughly $166 million for AT&T and Nokia. While the case involves broad claims of method for mitigating intermodulation interference, the prevailing lesson is that an infringement verdict cannot rest on expert testimony that ignores fundamental physical realities or oversights in the mathematical transformation of data.

The Federal Circuit confirmed that inference theory—using a single piece of evidence or testimony to bridge gaps in complex technical facts—runs the risk of failing the "substantial evidence" standard. The Court noted that Finesse bore the burden of proving infringement, which required demonstrating that accused Nokia radios performed the exact steps outlined in the patent claims. **## The Struggle for Technical Accuracy**

Technically, the court found that the two inputs were insufficient to generate the required seven multiplications. The accused radios only used two inputs (x1, x2), and the expert identified only three distinct multiplications. The "claims were ambiguous" regarding the count of multiplications. The court found the "substantial evidence" standard was not met because the evidence at best supported a finding that the accused devices performed three multiplications, not seven.

Furthermore, the Court noted that the claims require three *signals* to be multiplied in seven *wises*. This distinction is significant. The jury was asked to find that the accused device performed a specific, highly complex mathematical transformation on the signals. The expert testimony provided no clear mapping between the three distinct multiplications on the Nokia technical document and the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse attempted to clarify the mapping on appeal, but because that explanation was never presented to the jury, the district court’s denial of motion for judgment as a matter of law could not be upheld.

**## Implications for the Patent Landscape**

This decision underscores the importance of grounding patent claims in the actual capabilities of the accused product, particularly in method-of-use patents involving radio frequency processing. While the doctrine of equivalents and claim construction are tools to interpret the scope of patent scope, they cannot be used to cloth a patent claim over technical impossibilities. The Court emphasized that when the party with the burden of proof presents an expert whose testimony contradicts physical laws or technical realities, the court should not assume the jury can overlook it.

A key takeaway for patentees is the necessity of aligning technical expert testimony with specific identifiers in the claim limitations. In the '134 patent, the mapping of "DL(TX) reference" and "modeled PIM path" to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" was crucial. Since the PIM path is generated downstream of the ADC, sampling it requires a forward signal, which is temporally impossible in the claimed method. By allowing evidence to rest on self-contradictory testimony, the Federal Circuit has set a precedent that entity-juries or district courts cannot rely on mere inferential leaps to establish infringement in complex signal processing.

**## Conclusion: The Limits of Paper Rulings**

The Federal Circuit’s decision to vacate the damages award is a reminder that the jury's role ends when the evidence cannot support a rational inference of infringement. The case demonstrates that proof of infringement in engineering challenges is not just a matter of counting signals or distinguishing "signals" from "products." It is often a rigorous forensic inspection of what physical architectures actually permit. For companies facing patent litigation in the communications industry, this case may be a vote against broad claim constructions that do not reflect the actual mathematical operations performed by the accused device. Ultimately, the Federal Circuit has reminded us that when a jury awards a verdict based on evidence that defies basic logic, the ultimate remedy is a reversal, not a confirmation.
