Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## The Red Path Confusion: When Contradictory Expert Testimony Destroys a Patent Verdict

The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* arrives at a critical juncture in patent litigation: the effective cost of winning a judgment in a future tense, where the Federal Circuit *Wi-LAN* standard for substantial evidence is applied to factual findings regarding infringement. With the District Court’s $166 million jury award vacated, the Federal Circuit has issued a stinging rebuke to plaintiffs who cannot support their claims through unimpeachable technical evidence. This case matters because it curbs the doctrine of the "Battle of Experts." A firm investigation into intermodulation product interference is not retroactive to the technical measurement of an ADC, and a jury verdict is not sacred if the underlying expert testimony is legally incapable of sustaining the claim at issue. *We* found the dissent in the *Wi-LAN* era still applies to the threshold standard of evidence.

The court’s reasoning hinged on two distinct layers of confusion: the '134 patent (interference cancellation) and the '775 patent. For the '134 patent, the Federal Circuit noted a substantial lack of clarity in Dr. Wells’ testimony regarding the "red path" in a Nokia technical document. This path represented the inputs to the RF ADC. Wells testified that the system samples "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." However, the defense highlighted a physical impossibility: the modeled PIM path, according to the red path being sampled, was generated downstream of the sampling point. When Dr. Wells was questioned on cross-examination regarding whether x1 and x2 on the diagram were the signals of interest or the modeled PIM, he failed to clarify his mapping. The Federal Circuit found this "confusing change of course" insufficient to satisfy the jury. *We* can only conclude the "substantial evidence" standard requires a clear, consistent link between claim limitation and the physical reality of the accused device, rather than a pieced-together tolerance for conflicting testimony on the stand.

The court was equally unconvinced by the Dr. Wells claim regarding the '775 patent, which required seven specific multiplications of three signals S1, S2, and S3. The jury verdict was based on the assertion that the accused device performed these multiplications, but Dr. Wells only identified three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document. Even more damning was the lack of proof that any of the claimed seven operations were actually performed, as the document relied on modulus and complex conjugate operations which are distinct entities under the claim. On appeal, even more evidence was offered, none of which clarified the mapping of the ten operations in the Nokia document to the seven required by the claim. The court noted plainly: "Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury." The court found no reasonable jury could find the seven multiplications were performed. This reinforces the proposition from the *Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.* line of cases: when a plaintiff cannot clear the record with the "clear and unambiguous" evidence for their mapping, they cannot assume the jury's finding of 'infringement' is legally valid.

From a doctrinal perspective, this opinion solidifies a narrow window for factual findings in patent cases. The Fifth Circuit’s "strongly and overwhelmingly" test for substantial evidence is still the guiding light for the Federal Circuit, and *Wi-LAN* clarifies that it is not enough for an expert to be technically correct. They must be legally correct at the time of testimony. This puts immense weight on the drafting of expert reports and the robustness of the testimony when challenges are raised about diagrammatic elements. If evidence is self-contradictory or fails to show clear mapping on contemporaneous challenges, the Federal Circuit may strike that evidence as legally insufficient. This warns of a heightened risk for plaintiffs who cannot produce a clear, unified record, especially in cases where the accused device relies on a specific logic that the patent claims require more than a "red path."
