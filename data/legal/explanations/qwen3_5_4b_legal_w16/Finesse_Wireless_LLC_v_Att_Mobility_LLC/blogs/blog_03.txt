Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Reversing Facts, Not Law: The Federal Circuit's High Bar for "Substantial Evidence"

**By [Your Name]**

The commercial stakes in wireless communications patents are rarely higher than those involved in cellular radio interference. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit delivered a sharp reminder to patent owners that even when they sit at the jury side of the flag, the evidence must hold up to substantial scrutiny. The case, originating in the E.D. Tex with looming damages of over $166 million, serves as a practical guide to the Fifth Circuit’s specific—and high—standard for reviewing denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). For patentees, it is a clear message: Do not allow your expert testimony to wobble, and do not assume a reasonable jury can follow your technical roadmap when your own expert has made contradictory maps.

The Federal Circuit’s reasoning centers on the Fifth Circuit’s established standard for reviewing a denial of JMOL regarding a jury verdict. Under this standard, a court will reverse the district court's decision to uphold a verdict only if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in one party's favor that reasonable jurors could not reach a contrary conclusion. In cases where the patentee bears the burden of proving infringement, this places Finesse at a significant disadvantage. The court found that Dr. Wells, the principal infringement expert, did not map the signals correctly, creating a factual ambiguity so significant that the jury could not have knowingly proven infringement. The Federal Circuit emphasized that confusing testimony from a plaintiff’s burden-bearer is not sufficient to sustain a verdict.

This deficiency was most evident in the '134 patent, which required sampling of both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios sampled both signals via a red path, but on cross-examination, the defense arguments highlighted that the modeled PIM path was generated *after* sampling occurred, making it impossible for the sampled "passband" to include it. More critically, Dr. Wells later testified that the red path included two signals (x1 and x2), but he never explicitly mapped those to the claim limitations. Finesse argued that Dr. Wells had a "misimpression," but the court found that piecing together testimony from over ninety pages of the record did not meet the evidentiary bar for a substantial finding. This highlights a delicate balance: a jury finding is not just about meeting the legal standard, but about meeting the evidentiary threshold of factual clarity.

In the '775 patent case, the court similarly found the evidence failed to support the complexity of the claim elements. The claims required seven multiplications of three distinct signals, but the technical documents presented at trial showed only three distinct multiplications. The court noted that while Finesse had the burden of showing the multiplicative process occurred, the evidence provided did not track the specific math requirements of the claim. Unlike claim construction, which remains a matter of law review, the court found that the technical mapping failed at the factual level required to educate the jury. The court did not simply view the evidence as insufficient for the patentee to win; it found that the patentee provided no sufficient evidence for the jury to find the infringement allegedly occurred.

The decision vacates the lump sum damages against AT&T, but it exposes the fragility of jury findings when expert testimony is circular. The Federal Circuit’s statement that the verdict is not supported by substantial evidence if "no reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented" suggests that the Fifth Circuit is heavily skeptical of relying on technical experts who cannot explain contradictory bits of their own testimony. For litigators, the implication is that a JMOL motion should be treated as a threshold standard. If the plaintiff cannot clearly map their patent elements to the hardware, the court may simply strike the claim.

### Implications for Expert Testimony and Claim Construction

The distinction here is crucial for patent litigation. While claim construction—interpreting what terms like "three signals" mean—is reviewed de novo by the appellate court, factual findings supporting the infringement are reviewed for substantial evidence. In *Finesse*, the court balanced these two concepts but ultimately ruled in favor of the appellants (the accused infringers) because the evidentiary support for the infringement imperatively failed. The Federal Circuit’s opinion, citing *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, suggests that the standard of "substantial evidence" acts as a gatekeeper ensuring that the jury’s verdict is not just an invention but a fact grounded in a clear, coherent evidence trail.

This sets a high bar for patentees to prove infringement when faced with complex technical rebuttals. It means that even if the patent is valid, if the plaintiff's evidence is weak or contradictory, the court can strike down the judgment as a matter of law without legally overturning the patent itself. Furthermore, the opinion suggests that if a party presents conflicting expert testimony, the court may not allow a jury to fill in the gaps.

### Guidance for Litigators

This case offers a two-part warning for patent owners. First, ensure your expert testifies in a way that directly addresses claim limitations. If you need a jury to believe that a device samples specific signal paths, ensure the expert clearly maps the device mechanics to the claim language rather than relying on secondary testimony or implication. Second, understand that the Federal Circuit is not a rubber stamp for jury findings; it can reverse if the evidence is not substantial. This suggests that a strategic move to file JMOL motions, not just at the trial level but whenever the evidence is muddy, should be used more aggressively.

Ultimately, the Federal Circuit reinforced that in patent litigation, the burden of proof remains on the prosecutor. If the evidence does not meet the substantial threshold required to support the jury's conclusion, the judgments of law must be reversed.
