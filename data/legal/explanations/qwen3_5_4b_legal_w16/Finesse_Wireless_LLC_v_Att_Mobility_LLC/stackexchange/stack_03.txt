Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What constitutes a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope?
Question:
The amended claims were changed to say 'three signals' instead of 'two or three'. The appellee argued this is a clear disavowal of the accused radios because they only use two signals. But the court said this was not disavowal sufficient to narrow the scope. What is the legal test for when a claim amendment counts as disavowing a specific application of the claim scope?
Answer:
### Answer

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 U.S. CLLP (Fed. Cir.), the Federal Circuit applied the standard of "clear and unambiguous" disavowal to determine when a claim amendment during prosecution narrows the scope of a patent to exclude a specific accused application. Based on the opinion provided, a claim amendment constitutes a sufficient disavowal of scope over a specific application only if it necessarily excludes that application; if the amendment permits interpretations that include the accused application, it is not disavowing the scope.

**The Legal Standard**

The court cites *Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), for the principle that a claim amendment must not merely add subject matter requirements, but must rise to the level of a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal. In *Finesse*, the court explicitly found that the amendment regarding the '775 patent failed this test. The court held that the applicant’s change from the original "two or three signals" to "three signals" did not rise to the level of "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals.

**Application to the Specific Facts**

The court's reasoning established that for an amendment to disavow a specific application (in this case, a product using two unique signals), the new limitation must logically necessitate that specific feature, thereby excluding the prior scope. In the *'775* patent section, the amendment added a limitation of "digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3 in seven multiplications." The court reasoned that while this addition required "three separately identifiable signals," it did not require "any specific number of unique input signals."

Consequently, the court found that "at most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals." Because the skilled artisan could interpret "three signals" in the context of the claim as the three separately identifiable inputs (which could be the same signal repeated or duplicates of different signals) rather than strictly three distinct unique sources, the amendment did not strip away the scope of radios using two signals. Since the amendment did not clearly narrow the scope of the patent to require a feature that the accused radios lacked (specifically three unique inputs), the scope did not shrink in a way that constituted a valid disclaimer of precedent.

**Conclusion**

Therefore, under the test established by the Federal Circuit in *Finesse Wireless LLC*, a claim amendment only disavows a specific application of claim scope if the language changes are sufficient to exclude that application and its use. If the amendment leaves open the possibility that the claim covers the scope the applicant originally claimed (e.g., allowing two signals to be used as three "separately identifiable" inputs), the disavowal is insufficient to narrow the scope to exclude that application.
