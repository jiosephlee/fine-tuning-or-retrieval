Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Standards of Review for Patent Infringement Appeals

## Chapter Description

This chapter defines the governing legal tests applied by the Federal Circuit to district court decisions in patent infringement cases, distinguishing between the standards for question of law (de novo) versus questions of fact (substantial evidence and local circuit standards).

## Standard of Review for District Court JMOL Deni als (De Novo / Fifth Circuit Loops)

When a litigant appeals a district court’s denial of a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the Appellate Court must apply the specific standard of review mandated by the regional circuit to which the federal court belongs. In this case, the Federal Circuit addressed the standard of review in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, explicitly tying its analysis to the law of the Fifth Circuit to ensure procedural consistency. The opinion states that the court reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit. In *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the court of appeals in the Fifth Circuit had already defined the standard for this specific Grievance: the court reviews denials of JMOL de novo.

This de novo review does not apply lightly. The law of the regional circuit mandates that the appellate court reverses a district court’s denial of JMOL only under a specific threshold: "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This "loop" of deference and de novo review requires the appellate court to look at the evidence through the lens of the jury's perspective. If the evidence in the record is so one-sided, the jury's assessment becomes moot. In *Finesse*, the court applied this lens when reviewing AT&T’s motion for noninfringement, finding that the expert testimony sufficiently failed under this high bar because it relied on self-contradictory testimony rather than overwhelming data to support the Jury’s verdict.

## Claim Construction Review (De Novo)

Claim construction is a legal question, and therefore, it receives de novo review on appeal distinct from factual findings. The Federal Circuit establishes that the court reviews claim construction de novo. However, while the appellate court reviews the construction decision itself from scratch, it must often consider any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction for clear error, though the standard remains primarily legal. In the *Finesse* opinion, the court noted that the standard applies the district court’s construction decision based on the agreed-upon construction and inventor testimony.

This distinction is vital for lawyers because it means the appellate court is not bound by lower court reasons for rulings unless they fail clearly. In *Finesse*, the district court had construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to be "separately identifiable," but not "unique." The court relied on expert testimony. On appeal, the court had to determine the correct scope. Because the construction is a question of law, the appellate court did not stay the construction based on the trial record alone. Instead, it re-evaluated the construction of claim terms like "signals of interest." This de novo review allows the appellate court to correct misconstructions that would otherwise lead to unjustified infringement findings. The appellate court in *Finesse* applied this standard to determine that the "interference generating signals" could not be claimed samples generated downstream of the ADC.

## Review of Jury Patent Findings (Substantial Evidence)

Once a question of fact arises, such as whether a jury's finding of infringement was reasonable, the standard of review shifts. Under *Wi-LAN* law and the Fifth Circuit's practice, a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial. This is the lowest standard allowed in patent appeals that still ensures high-quality jury decisions.

In *Finesse*, the court applied this standard to the noninfringement proceedings. The court held that the jury’s finding of infringement for the ’134 patent was not supported by substantial evidence because the expert testimony was "confusing and unclear." Even if the evidence was viewed in the light most favorable to the jury as permitted by the Fifth Circuit standard, it was not found substantial enough to prevent a reasonable jury from reaching a contrary conclusion. The court reasoned that the expert, Dr. Wells, had mapped certain inputs to "interference generating signals" incorrectly. The court highlighted that Finesse’s attorney pieced together contradictory testimony from across the record rather than relying on clear exposition. This application of the substantial evidence standard serves as a safeguard: it allows the jury decision to stand unless the evidence is so flimsy that no reasonable person could have reached that specific conclusion.

## Standard of Review for Denials of New Trial on Damages (Abuse of Discretion)

The standard of review for denials of motions for a new trial on damages is distinct from the JMOL and jury finding standards. Here, the appellate court reviews the district court’s decision for abuse of discretion. This grants the district court significant latitude to weigh the damages based on economic factors, jury instructions, and expert testimony, provided the decision is not arbitrary or unreasonable.

In *Finesse*, the Appellants appealed the denial of a new trial on damages. However, because the appellate court reversed the JMOL of noninfringement, the district court's entire award became invalid. The opinion explicitly states, "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award." While the new trial standard would be "abuse of discretion," the damages were not reviewed under this standard independently; they were vacated due to the fundamental reversal of the infringement issue. This illustrates the procedural posture: if the liability (infringement) is not established, the quantum (damages) calculation is moot.

## Application to the Fifth Circuit Standard cited in WI-LAN v. Apple Inc.

The Federal Circuit relies on regional circuit law to determine the appropriate standard of review for both infringement and damages in patent cases. In the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the standard applied is referenced to regional circuit law through the citation to *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). *Wi-LAN* serves as the governing authority for the Fifth Circuit's approach to patent jury reviews in the Federal Circuit.

The case in *Finesse* cites *Wi-LAN* specifically to establish the rules regarding JMOL and substantial evidence. The court applies the regional circuit law, here the Fifth Circuit, to distinguish between the "logic of local standards" and the "rule of law." In the *Finesse* opinion, the court cites that district court decisions are governed by the law of the regional circuit to ensure consistency across the appeals process. This citation anchors the appellate review, ensuring that the "light most favorable to the verdict" and "substantial evidence" standards are not solely Federal Circuit inventions but are part of the broader circuit law context. The court also notes that when a party moves for communication de novo, they do not require the district court to have erred. But when the question is factual as in *Finesse*, the "abuse of discretion" standard becomes relevant. The citation to Washington in *Wi-LAN* confirms that the appellate court does not substitute its judgment for the jury’s without substantial evidence.
