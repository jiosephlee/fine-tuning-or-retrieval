Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of the '134 Patent: Signal Sampling and Mapping

## Analysis of '134 Claims: 'Signals of Interest' and 'Interference Generating Signals'

When analyzing a patent opinion, the first step involves understanding the specific limitation language that defines the scope of protection. In *Finesse Wireless LLC*, Claim 1 of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 outlines a method for reducing interference in radio systems. A critical element of Claim 1 is the requirement to over-sample a passband of received signals, where those signals must include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." To establish infringement under this limitation, Finesse must demonstrate that the accused Nokia radios sample a passband containing both types of signals simultaneously. The "interference generating signals" are defined as signals capable of generating intermodulation products inband of the signals of interest. This claim construction dictates that the infringement is not established simply by the presence of noise or interference in a general sense; the accused device must physically sample a passband that incorporates the signals listed as the sources of that interference. If the hardware architecture prevents the simultaneous sampling of these specific signals at the claimed frequency, the claim is not met. The court reviews the evidence presented at trial to determine whether a reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios satisfied this specific signal mapping requirement.

## The Burden of Proof on Plaintiff (Finesse)

In patent infringement cases, the plaintiff bears the burden of proof to show that the accused product infringes every step of the asserted claims. During a trial, this burden translates into the requirement that the plaintiff present evidence sufficient to support a finding of infringement. In the appellate context, when addressing the denial of a motion for JMOL, the standard of review is de novo, but the underlying review of fact focuses on whether there is sufficient evidence to uphold the jury's verdict. Finesse, as the movant for JMOL of noninfringement, was required to provide competent evidence that the jury's finding despite its doubts was supported by the record. The court noted that Finesse bore the burden of proving the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." If the plaintiff's evidence fails to demonstrate that the specific signals required by the claim are physically capable of being sampled at the time of the infringing act, the burden cannot be met. The court emphasized that the plaintiff cannot simply suggest a technical argument; the evidence must be clear enough that a jury could reasonably rely upon it to convict for infringement.

## Evaluation of Expert Testimony (Dr. Wells) and the 'Red Path'

The sufficiency of the evidence in *Finesse* hinged largely on the testimony of Dr. Wells, Finesse's expert witness. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the claimed limits by mapping specific technical signals from the Nokia technical document onto the claim terminology. He identified the "downlink transmit reference" as the "signals of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" as the "interference generating signals." However, a significant portion of the trial testimony and cross-examination involved the identification of signals on a red path depicted in the technical document. Dr. Wells consistently testified that the red path was represented by the "downlink TX reference" and the "modeled PIM path." This testimony creates confusion for the reader and the appellate court regarding what specific signals were actually present in the passband. When challenged on cross-examination, Dr. Wells indicated that the two inputs represented by the signals could be x1 and x2, yet he retained his mapping of the "red path" to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM. The appellate court noted that piecing together different parts of the testimony over ninety pages apart did not constitute clear evidence of infringement. This lack of clarity impacted the J.M.L. argument because the court must be satisfied that the testimony was not merely ambiguous, but sufficient for a jury to be convinced.

## Finding of Insufficiency: 'Modeled PIM Path Generation After Sampling'

A pivotal objection raised by the appellate court centered on the technical timeline of how the signals were generated. The claimed infringement requires that the "interference generating signals" exist and are sampled within the passband of received signals. However, the technical testimony established that the "modeled PIM path," which Dr. Wells identified as the "interference generating signals," was generated downstream in the GROOT FPGA after the analog-to-digital conversion occurred. This created a temporal contradiction: the signals could not be sampled before they were generated. Dr. Wells acknowledged that the modeled PIM signal was created by the system, but failed to explain how these signals existed at the point of sampling in the passed stop positions. The court reasoned that the receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes interference generating signals since those signals are not generated until after sampling occurs. Consequently, the limitation requiring the sampling of these signals could not be met physically by the accused device. This technical impossibility means that the jury's finding that the signals were sampled in the required manner could not be supported by the evidence, requiring a reversal of the JMOL denial.

## Determination on Substantial Evidence for the '134 Patent Infringement'

The final step in the *Finesse* appeal was the application of the substantial evidence standard to the expert testimony. The appellate court reviews district court denials of JMOL de novo, but must decide if there is substantial evidence to support the court's decision to uphold the infringement verdict. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence. In *Finesse*, the court concluded that the evidence was insufficient to satisfy this standard because the testimony was self-contradictory and clear. Dr. Wells continued to testify that the modeled PIM path was the "interference generating signals" and never clarified that it was referring to x1 and x2 as required by the claim construction inference. The court noted that this sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict, citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*. Because the plaintiff rested its case on an expert's testimony that could not logically support the claim mapping, the jury's verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. The appellate court therefore reversed the district court's denial of JMOL, holding that the evidence was insufficient to uphold the finding of infringement for the asserted claims of the '134 patent.
