Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## Appellate Jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)

To understand the review this court provides, one must first understand where the case originated and the statutory authority guiding this appellate review. The *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* opinion operates under the jurisdiction granted by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This statute empowers courts of appeals with specific jurisdiction over appeals in patent cases, distinguishing them from general civil appeals. By citing this specific provision, the court establishes that this is a non-ordinary patent appeal involving a decision by the District Court. This statutory hook is critical for any legal analyst because it dictates the scope of the appellate review. Unlike standard civil appeals where a district court’s award is often reviewed for abuse of discretion, patent appeals under this section trigger a specific subject-matter jurisdiction requirement. In this意见书, the court notes that they have this specific authority because the case arises from a district court ruling on a patent dispute, a common trigger for the Federal Circuit (or the relevant Court of Appeals in the geographic assignment). Understanding this jurisdiction allows students to anticipate that the court will be strictly adhering to the Federal Circuit review standards specific to intellectual property disputes, ensuring the legal question is handled under the specialized rules applicable to technology and patents.

## Summary of the Parties and Patents

The parties involved in this litigation represent a classic patent infringement scenario, with the named plaintiff and defendants defined by the patent assignment. The Appellee, Finesse Wireless LLC, holds the patents at issue. The Appellants consist of two entities: AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corporation. Both entities are the owners of the intellectual property in question and the appellants are collectively appealing against the Appellee. The patents asserted are U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775, which the plaintiff refers to as the '134 and '775 patents, respectively. These patents are central to the dispute because the district court determined they were infringed upon Finesse’s method for mitigating signal interference. The Appellants challenge this finding by asserting they do not infringe any of the patent claims. For a law student studying this opinion, it is essential to note who bears the burden of proof: the patent owner (Finesse) must sufficiently prove the accused product infringes the claims. The distinction between the Appellants and Appellee is vital for procedural posture because it establishes the roles in the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Appellants, having lost the case below, now argue before the appellate court regarding the validity of the jury’s verdict.

## Description of the Accused Radios and PIM Cancellation Features

Understanding the factual allegations requires familiarity with the technology at issue: radiofrequency modulation and interference cancellation. Finesse describes the patents as generally relating to methods for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. Radios generally operate by avoiding signal interference by using different frequencies; however, when channels encounter obstacles, transmit signals can combine to form new frequencies known as intermodulation products. This is referred to in the opinion as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). There is a distinction made between IMPs caused by active components and those passively generated by external obstacles like metal fences or loose connections. While passive obstacles generate “passive intermodulation products” (PIM), other intermodulations can stem from mechanical internal issues. The patents claim methods to handle these formations, specifically by isolating different signals and calculating estimates to cancel them out. The specific patent claims at issue involve isolating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and then canceling out those interference generating signals. To determine infringement, one must identify whether the radio being sued uses this method. The court notes that Finesse bears the burden of proving the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” This technological context is crucial for understanding the factual disputes at the District Court level, specifically regarding the technical details of the radios' internal function.

## Procedural History of the District Court Litigation

The underlying dispute began in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. Finesse initiated the lawsuit alleging that the accused radios infringed upon the '134 and '775 patents. The jury made a finding of fact, declaring all asserted claims valid and infringed. This jury finding led to a damages award of $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the remaining life of the patents. Upon receiving the jury verdict, the Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) moved for relief. Their requests included a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, a JMOL on damages, and a request for a new trial. The lower court, representing the District Court, denied all of these motions. This denial brings the case to the appellate level. The student must note the significance of the District Court's decision: the appellate court now reviews this denial. This review process is the primary focus of the opinion. The District Court had determined that the evidentiary record was sufficient for the jury's finding, a decision that the appellate court scrutinizes under the standards of law it applies to such motions.

## Appellants' Motions: JMOL on Noninfringement, JMOL on Damages, and New Trial

The procedural posture of the appeal is defined by the specific motions the Appellants exercised to avoid an adverse judgment. The Appellants appealed the denial of the JMOL of noninfringement and the denial of a new trial on damages. While the district court's damages decision was within its discretion, the denial of a claim finding (noninfringement) required a specific legal analysis of the district court's reasoning. The Appellants argued that the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support a finding that their products infringed the Finesse patents. Specifically, they contended that their radios do not sample the correct signals or perform the claimed multiplications to cancel the interference. The district court had previously removed the jury verdict by denying their motion, concluding that the jury's conclusion was supported by the evidence presented. Now, on appeal, the focus shifts to whether, after reviewing the evidence, the evidence pointed overwhelmingly to one party so that the court believes reasonable jurors could not arrive at a contrary conclusion. The Appellants' motions establish the scope of the review required from the appellate court. They specifically argue that technical limitations, such as the order of signals being sampled or the number of multiplications, were not met in the accused radio technology. Understanding these motions provides the student with the framework for the subsequent legal analysis, where the court will evaluate whether the District Court's rejection of the JMOL motion was legally sound.
