## Subtopic 1: Analysis of '775 Claims: Seven Multiplication Requirement

In Patent No. 775, the central method claim at issue was for performing interference cancellation in a receiver. A key limitation in Claim 1 required that the method achieve this by "generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 and S1×S2×S3 and S1×S1×S3 and S2×S2×S3 and S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3." This limitation explicitly required seven specific third-order multiplications to be performed on the three signals to produce the interference cancellation signals. For a student reading this opinion, it is crucial to note that under federal patent law, infringement of a patent claim occurs when every element of the claim is present in the accused device and performed in order. Here, the plurality of the judicial review focused heavily on whether the accused Nokia radios isolated and substituted these exact seven multiplications. Appellants argued that it was impossible for the accused radios to perform seven multiplications given their architecture. Finesse, the owner of the patent, argued that Dr. Wells, the expert witness for Finesse, identified that the accused radios did, in fact, perform these multiplications based on a technical document provided to the jury. The court held that establishing infringement requires clear mapping from the technical functionality of the accused device to the limitations of the patent claim. When the limited number of distinct mathematical operations in the claims becomes a point of contention, the jury's verdict must be supported by evidence that demonstrates the actual computational steps taken by the circuit are identical to those described in the claim. In this instance, the argument turned on whether there was sufficient evidence to support the finding that not just three, but seven specific multiplications were generated.

## Subtopic 2: Claim Construction: 'Separately Identifiable' vs 'Unique' Signals

Claim construction establishes the legal definition of patent claim terms. In the '775 patent analysis, confidence in the factual determination relied on two critical assumptions regarding the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3." First, the district court constructed these signals as "separately identifiable," meaning they can be distinguished, even if they are the same type of signal. Second, the district court credited both intrinsic evidence (the patent documents) and inventor (extrinsic) testimony, both suggesting that S1, S2, and S3 did not have to be unique or distinct from the source. At the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) stage, Appellants raised an argument that "three signals" implies "three unique signals." Appellants failed to challenge this specific construction at the appropriate Rule 50(a) stage regarding the lien of noninfringement. Because of this procedural failure, the Fifth Circuit likely viewed the waiver of the unique argument by the district court. However, regarding the "seven multiplications," the district court did not reach the issue of the quantity of multiplications at summary judgment in the district. The district court for the '775 patent rejected the argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be unique signals, but the more significant failure was the documentary evidence required to support the seven-multiplication factorial. The court found that there was no agreement or evidence regarding how the three signals mapped onto the required multiplications.

## Subtopic 3: Evidence Review: The Nokia Technical Document vs. Claim Limitation

To prove infringement, a party must show that the accused device performs every element of the claim. Dr. Wells, the Finesse expert, testified that the accused Nokia radios infringed '775 Patent Claim 1. He relied on a Nokia technical document to support his testimony. This document mapped the internal operations of the accused radios to the mathematical requirements of the patent. Dr. Wells argued that the document showed the accused radios multiplied the three distinct multiplications listed within it, arguing that each of those distinct multiplications corresponded to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. The court found that Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to show that the accused radios perform the claimed multiplications in real-time. However, the court examined the document closely and observed that it involved "modulus and complex conjugate operations." In patent infringement litigation, the record must clearly show how the specific steps in the claim were executed. Dr. Wells identified three multiplications in the Nokia document: the product of x1 and x1, x1 and x1, and x1 and x2, for example. The issue was whether these represented the seven required multiplications claimed in the '775 patent. The court noted that to satisfy the claim limitation, the expert or plaintiff must map the documents to the claims explicitly. The evidence presented did not clearly establish how the claimed multiplications were calculated from the technical document presented.

## Subtopic 4: Discovery of Missing Multiplications in the Document

A critical failure in the '775 infringement finding was the lack of mapping between the claimed architecture and the actual implementation described in evidence. The '775 Claim 1 limitation specifies a particular digit configuration: seven specific multiplication chains. For instance, S1×S1×S2, followed by S1×S2×S2, and so on. Appellants pointed out that the Nokia document they offered at trial only listed three distinct multiplications (e.g., x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|). There was no evidence that the accused radios performed all seven distinct multiplications as required by the claim limitation. Even if the argument was made on appeal that the accused radios performed ten multiplications based on other mathematical operations, Finesse had to explain how the seven claimed multiplications were specifically identified within that list. The court found that nowhere in the expert testimony or the record was there an explanation of which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications. Furthermore, because the document did not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse's proposed mapping was inconsistent. The claim required specific mathematical operations to be performed. If the evidence only showed other mathematical operations or fewer distinct operations, the jury could not find substantial evidence of infringement.

## Subtopic 5: Determination on Substantial Evidence for the '775 Patent Infringement

The appellate court reviewed the district court's denial of JMOL under the standard that a "factual finding is supported by substantial evidence." This standard asks whether a reasonable jury could have found infringement in light of the evidence presented at trial. The court concluded that, regarding the limitation for seven multiplications, no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios performed these steps. The evidence provided only showed three distinct multiplications and involved complex conjugate and modulus operations that were not mapped to the specific S1, S2, S3 multiplication chains required by Claim 1[d]. Since the plaintiff Finesse failed to provide a clear mapping of how the accused device met the seven-multiplication limitation, the fact-finding below lacked the requisite evidentiary support. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL and judgment as a matter of law for the '775 patent. This decision highlights that experts cannot draw inferences from technical data that prove they did not perform every element of the claim, particularly when the required number of distinct configurations is not met. The damages award was vacated as a result of this reversal.
