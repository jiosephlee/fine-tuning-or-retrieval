Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the standard of review for a district court's denial of JMOL in the Federal Circuit?
Question:
In this opinion, the court mentions reviewing a denial of JMOL of noninfringement de novo. But later it says it reviews a jury's finding of infringement for substantial evidence. Can someone explain why there's a difference between the standard for JMOL of noninfringement (law/civil) vs. the standard for infringement findings (factual)? Doesn't JMOL cover both?
Answer:
### The distinction between JMOL of noninfringement and infringement findings relies on the nature of the conclusion being reviewed: legal versus factual standards.

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit distinguishes these standards based on the underlying subject matter. The court clarifies that it reviews a district court's denial of JMOL **de novo** (law of the regional circuit), meaning the application of legal principles is reviewed fresh. However, it reviews a jury's finding of infringement for **substantial evidence** (factual findings).

The Federal Circuit applies this dual standard to ensure it gives independent deference to factual determinations while retaining control over legal interpretations. As the court notes, a JMOL "of noninfringement" challenges whether the court properly applied the law to the evidence, which warrants *de novo* review. Conversely, an "infringement finding" is a conclusion of fact regarding how the accused device operated.

While these standards appear at first to apply to the same motion, the court employs them separately:
1.  **JMOL Denial (Legal):** "We review a district court's denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit." This is *de novo* review because it tests whether the district court correctly applied the legal standard to the facts.
2.  **Infringement Finding (Factual):** "We review a jury's finding of infringement for substantial evidence." As the court cites *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*, a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence."

In this case, the Federal Circuit reversed the *denial* of JMOL not on a novel legal point, but because the jury's factual findings of infringement were not supported by substantial evidence (*'134 and '775 patents*). Because the jury's verdict relied on conflicting expert testimony (Dr. Wells' mapping of the "red path"), the court applied the *substantial evidence* standard to the factual record rather than merely a *de novo* legal check. Thus, while JMOL motions possess a legal (de novo) component that supersedes factual reviews, the actual *finding* of infringement relies on the substantial evidence standard for factual sufficiency.

### How does expert testimony that "changes course" affect a JMOL motion?
Question:
For the '134 patent, Dr. Wells testified that the 'DL(TX) reference' was one signal and the 'modeled PIM path' was another, mapping them to the claim limitations 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals'. But on cross-examination, he clarified (allegedly) that 'x1' and 'x2' were the inputs. How does this confusion impact the 'substantial evidence' standard? I think if an expert or lawyer was confused for 90 pages, that just supports the jury, does it not?
Answer:
### Answer

Based on the reasoning in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, **expert testimony that "changes course" or contradicts itself does not support a jury verdict and may instead lead to a grant of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL)** when the conflicting testimony fails to meet the standard of "substantial evidence."

Your intuition that confusing testimony "supports the jury" is incorrect under the standards applied in this opinion. While the jury listened to Dr. Wells for 90 pages, the Federal Circuit held that "This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict." Because the expert rested the patent's infringement claims on testimony that did not clearly map to the claim language, no reasonable jury could have found infringement.

To understand the legal mechanism here, we must look at the applicable standards and how the court applied them to the expert's inconsistency.

#### 1. The Standard for Substantial Evidence
When reviewing a jury's finding of infringement, the Federal Circuit falls under the "substantial evidence" standard. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence only if "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022) [cited in *Finesse*].

Crucially, this is not a sliding scale of "confusion." The plaintiff must clearly provide enough evidence to allow a jury to definitively find infringement. In *Finesse*, the court found the evidence insufficient because the expert could not satisfy the jury that the accused device met the specific claim limitations.

#### 2. How the "Change of Course" Destroys Evidence
In *Finesse*, Dr. Wells testified that the "DL (TX) reference" was the "signal of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" was the "interference generating signal." When counsel pointed out the technical impossibility of sampling a signal generated after the ADC, Dr. Wells pivoted, stating, "there's two signals on that red path because we know that because there's the x1 and the x2." 

However, he did not clarify that "x1 and x2" were the signals of interest; he continued to refer to the "red path" as the DL (TX) reference and modeled PIM path. This created a "piecing together" of evidence over ninety pages of contradictory testimony. The Court noted that the party with the burden (the Plaintiff) "rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony."

The opinion explicitly states:
> "When the party with the burden of proof, such as Finesse, rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard." *Finesse*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 187 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) (citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008)).

Because the Plaintiff could not offer a consistent narrative that directly resulted in a finding of infringement, the "90 pages" of testimony were deemed insufficient to sustain the jury's verdict.

#### 3. The Effect on the JMOL Motion
Since Plaintiff's case failed the substantial evidence test due to the expert's conflicting testimony, the trial court's denial of the defendant's JMOL motion was improper. The appellate court "reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement" and "vacated the damages award."

**The Key Takeaway:** Confusing or contradictory expert testimony does not make a case for the jury stronger; in the context of a JMOL motion based on insufficient evidence, it serves as a fatal flaw. As the amici noted in oral argument, "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused device literally met this limitation based on the expert's opinion, given his contradictory testimony."

If you are preparing for a motion to JMOL, remember that while the length of the trial record does not distinguish the strength of the verdict, the *clarity* of the evidence presented by experts is paramount. If a witness "changes course" or relies on a theory that is internally inconsistent, they have not provided the "substantial evidence" required to keep a jury verdict standing.

### What constitutes a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope?
Question:
The amended claims were changed to say 'three signals' instead of 'two or three'. The appellee argued this is a clear disavowal of the accused radios because they only use two signals. But the court said this was not disavowal sufficient to narrow the scope. What is the legal test for when a claim amendment counts as disavowing a specific application of the claim scope?
Answer:
### Answer

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 U.S. CLLP (Fed. Cir.), the Federal Circuit applied the standard of "clear and unambiguous" disavowal to determine when a claim amendment during prosecution narrows the scope of a patent to exclude a specific accused application. Based on the opinion provided, a claim amendment constitutes a sufficient disavowal of scope over a specific application only if it necessarily excludes that application; if the amendment permits interpretations that include the accused application, it is not disavowing the scope.

**The Legal Standard**

The court cites *Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), for the principle that a claim amendment must not merely add subject matter requirements, but must rise to the level of a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal. In *Finesse*, the court explicitly found that the amendment regarding the '775 patent failed this test. The court held that the applicant’s change from the original "two or three signals" to "three signals" did not rise to the level of "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals.

**Application to the Specific Facts**

The court's reasoning established that for an amendment to disavow a specific application (in this case, a product using two unique signals), the new limitation must logically necessitate that specific feature, thereby excluding the prior scope. In the *'775* patent section, the amendment added a limitation of "digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3 in seven multiplications." The court reasoned that while this addition required "three separately identifiable signals," it did not require "any specific number of unique input signals."

Consequently, the court found that "at most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals." Because the skilled artisan could interpret "three signals" in the context of the claim as the three separately identifiable inputs (which could be the same signal repeated or duplicates of different signals) rather than strictly three distinct unique sources, the amendment did not strip away the scope of radios using two signals. Since the amendment did not clearly narrow the scope of the patent to require a feature that the accused radios lacked (specifically three unique inputs), the scope did not shrink in a way that constituted a valid disclaimer of precedent.

**Conclusion**

Therefore, under the test established by the Federal Circuit in *Finesse Wireless LLC*, a claim amendment only disavows a specific application of claim scope if the language changes are sufficient to exclude that application and its use. If the amendment leaves open the possibility that the claim covers the scope the applicant originally claimed (e.g., allowing two signals to be used as three "separately identifiable" inputs), the disavowal is insufficient to narrow the scope to exclude that application.

### Why was the jury's finding on 'seven multiplications' insufficient?
Question:
For the '775 patent, the claims require multiplying 'three signals' in seven specific multiplications. Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications from a Nokia document. The Appellants didn't explain how the three multiplications map to seven multiplications. Can a jury find infringement when the expert only identifies three components that seem to match the term? What burden of proof is required for this specific element?
Answer:
In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 WL 6497813 (Fed. Cir. 2025) (No. 2024-1039), the Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding the seventh-order multiplications requirement in the '775 patent. The court held that the jury's finding was legally insufficient because the evidence presented did not provide a factual basis to support that the accused device performed the specific number of multiplications claimed.

To understand why the finding was insufficient, one must first look at the standard of review the Federal Circuit applies. When reviewing the denial of a motion for JMOL, the Federal Circuit reviews the jury's factual findings for *substantial evidence*. As the court noted, a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence "if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented." *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). On the *Finesse* record, this standard requires that the plaintiff's expert testimony must clearly establish that every limitation of the claim was met.

In the '775 patent dispute, Claim 1 explicitly limits the accused method to "generating an n-th order ICS... and includes generating... seven third order multiplications." While the plaintiff's expert, Dr. Wells, testified that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, the primary evidence relied upon to prove this infringement was a Nokia technical document. The court found that this document involved complex operations (modulus and complex conjugate) but *did not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.* Instead, the document as explicated by Dr. Wells listed only *three distinct multiplications.* $\ Sield之處$

The critical failure in the jury's finding was the inability to map the evidence to the claim language. The court specifically identified the evidentiary gap: "Dr. Wells did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications." $J.A. 14966$–$79$. Although Finesse attempted to clarify the mapping on appeal, "that evidence was never presented to the jury." Therefore, the expert's testimony rested on an unexplained inference. Because the document does not disclose repeated multipillions such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed, and because the expert offered no specific link between the Nokia document's three distinct multiplications and the seven required by Claim 1, the Federal Circuit concluded that "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications."

Consequently, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL, holding that the evidence was not substantial enough to support a verdict that the accused device met the 'seven' requirement. This illustrates that even when an expert testifies generally about a mechanism's operation, specific claim limitations require specific evidentiary support that the jury must be able to rely upon material cases to a find infringement.

### Why does the court vacate the damages award upon reversing the JMOL?
Question:
The court reversed the denial of JMOL of non-infringement, but the jury had already awarded $166 million in damages. Why does the court 'vacate' the entire damages award? Does the fact that the product might not be infringing mean the value cannot stand regardless of which damages calculation the jury used, or is there a specific post-judgment timing issue?
Answer:
The Court vacated the damages award because the award's validity is predicated entirely on the existence of the patent infringement finding. Since the Federal Circuit has ruled that the jury did not receive substantial evidence to support infringement, the entire damages judgment is legally nullified because damages cannot be awarded absent infringement.

**Damages Prerequisite**
Inpatent litigation, a finding of infringement is a threshold requirement for any recovery of damages. Under the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit's review of this appeal, the Federal Circuit structured its decision to establish that liability (infringement) is a necessary condition precedent to the remedy (damages). Specifically, Section II ("Damages") of the opinion states, in control of the final judgment: "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, we vacate the damages award." This dictates that the damages award is not independent; it is contingent upon the affirmative finding that was just reversed.

**Logical Sequence of Vacatur**
The Court does not see an issue regarding "calculated values" or the mathematical validity of the $166 million figure. Rather, the legal logic is that if the jury's finding of infringement is legally reversed due to a lack of evidence (as established in Section I regarding the substantial evidence standard), the fact pattern supporting the damages calculation collapses. A jury cannot award damages for an alleged act they have not established, nor can a court sustain a judgment found solely on an opinion that failed a standard of proof. When the possibility of infringement is negated by a successful motion for JMOL/Noninfringement, the damages table has no legal foundation.

**Conclusion**
The vacatur is procedural and substantive: the reversing of the False Finding of Infringement specifically renders the specific damages award unenforceable. To allow the damages money to stand would violate the order of operations in patent adjudication, thereby effectively meaning that once the foundational guilt (infringement) is removed, the punishment (damages) must follow suit.
