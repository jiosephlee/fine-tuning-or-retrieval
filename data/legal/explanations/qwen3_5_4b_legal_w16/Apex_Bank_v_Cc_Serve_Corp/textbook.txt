Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural History

## CC Serve and Apex Bank: Ownership of Related Credits Card and Banking Services

To understand the substantive rights at stake in this trademark dispute, one must first identify the registrants and the nature of the confusion they claim to be at risk. In this case, CC Serve is a company that manages credit card services, entering into partnerships with banks to issue branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers. It functions as the service provider servicing these accounts. By contrast, Apex Bank is a traditional retail bank chartered in Tennessee with branches and standard banking products, including checking, savings, and loans. While Apex Bank does not currently offer credit card services, it intended to introduce an internet banking service under the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. The core factual context required for the trademark analysis involves the overlap between these distinct service offerings. Although CC Serve does not issue the physical credit cards itself, it manages the credit card accounts for the banks that do. Apex Bank sought to register its marks in connection with banking and financing services. The Board later determined that credit card services and banking services are related in a manner that allows consumers to mistakenly believe they emanate from a single source.

## The Applications: Prior Registration and Intent-to-Use Applications

The procedural timeline and the marks at issue were established through specific filings with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). CC Serve held a registration for the word mark ASPIRE, issued in 1998, which covers credit card services. This registration includes an effective priority date of October 17, 1996, establishing CC Serve's prior rights to the mark. In 2019, Apex Bank filed intent-to-use applications with the USPTO to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. This filing followed Apex Bank's discovery of CC Serve's existing mark. The examining attorney initially approved the publication of the Apex Bank marks on December 17, 2019. However, CC Serve intervened in this process. Technical decisions regarding the meaning of identifiers, such as whether "credit card services" encompasses broader banking services, were critical in defining the scope of the existing ASPIRE mark. This initial filing by Apex and the subsequent registration status of CC Serve set the stage for the formal opposition filed by CC Serve, creating a constitutional framework for reviewing the likely confusion between these two distinct service providers.

## The Opposition Process and Board Ruling on Section 2(d)

Following the publication of Apex Bank's application, CC Serve initiated a Section 2(d) opposition under the Lanham Act. Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), serves as the primary mechanism for refusing registrations that are likely to cause confusion with prior ® federally registered marks. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) examined the opposition and sustained it, concluding that consumer confusion was likely based on concurrent use of the marks under their respective goods. Apex Bank subsequently appealed this decision to the Federal Circuit. The dispute centers on the Board's application of the Lanham Act's provisions and the TTAB's interpretation of the Likelihood of Confusion standard. The Board's primary Finding was that the marks and services were confusingly similar enough to warrant a refusal of registration. This establishment of the opposition and the Board's decision is the procedural pivot point of the Federal Circuit appeal, as the Court's review of the Board's decision determines whether the registration should be refused or whether the application should proceed to publication.

## Jurisdiction and Standard of Review for the Federal Circuit

The legal posture of this appeal hinges on the Court's jurisdiction and the appropriate standard for reviewing the Board's decision. The Federal Circuit has statutory jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B) to hear appeals from decisions by the TTAB. This specific jurisdiction is crucial because it grants the court authority to review the Board's decision. Once the appeal is brought to the Federal Circuit, the standard of review restates the position taken by the Board regarding the likelihood of confusion. The board's legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, meaning the court examines the issue anew without deference to the Board's legal interpretation. However, the Board's findings of fact are reviewed for substantial evidence, which means the court considers whether a reasonable mind would accept the evidence as adequate to support the Board's conclusion. This standard allows the court to affirm the Board's decision on the strength of the reasons adduced, provided it is not legally erroneous or unsupported by the record. This distinction between law and fact is fundamental to understanding how the Court will later evaluate the parties' arguments regarding the DuPont factors.

# The Legal Framework: Lanham Act Authority and Review Standards

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act and the Threshold of Likelihood of Confusion

A trademark opposition, such as the one initiated by CC Serve against Apex Bank, is governed by the statutory provisions of the Lanham Act. Section 2(d) of the Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), requires the Board to refuse registration when it finds that confusion is likely to arise from the concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board sustained the opposition under this section, concluding that consumer confusion was likely based on the similarities of the marks and services involved. This establishes the threshold for refusal: the requesting party does not need to prove actual confusion, only that the likelihood of confusion is probable under the circumstances. The opinion clarifies that this is not merely a question of the parties' intent but requires an objective assessment of the relevant factors. Consequently, the opposition process serves as a preventative mechanism to maintain a clear marketplace, prohibiting the registration of marks that would mislead consumers into believing the goods or services originate from a single source despite an alternative proprietor's interest.

In analyzing the opposition, the court notes that the likelihood of confusion is the primary predicate for any trade dress or brand claim under the Lanham Act. This approach ensures that the legal interest at stake—consumer protection regarding the source of goods—is protected before the Board commits to a registration that may cause market harm. The appeal in *Apex Bank* focused on whether the Board correctly applied this threshold, as the Board's initial decision hinged on whether association between the ASPIRE marks of the two banks was likely. The court reaffirmed that the Board's duty under Section 2(d) is to scrutinize both the marks and the services offered. By focusing on the likelihood of confusion, the Board ensures that a high degree of similarity does not automatically lead to cancellation but serves as the default peremptory ground for refusal unless the applicant successfully argues otherwise.

## The DuPont Factors: Fact-Finding and Discretion in Board Application

To determine likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d), the Board applies a multi-factor test commonly known as the DuPont analysis. The opinion specifies that likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts derived from these factors. While the Board is not required to consider every DuPont factor, it must focus on those that are relevant and of record. In *Apex Bank*, the Board assessed aspects of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors to reach its decision. These factors generally encompass the similarity of the marks, the similarity of goods and services, and the number of similar marks in use. The opinion highlights that the Board must exercise discretion in applying these factors to the evidence presented during the prosecution phase of the application.

The Board's role is to weigh these experts from where the opinion reflects the factors: the second factor examines the similarity of the specific services provided or the extent to which marketing circumstances give rise to mistaken belief. In *Apex*, the Board determined that credit card services and banking and financing services were related enough to warrant a likelihood of confusion because they are legally identical in part. This illustrates that the goods or services need not be identical to create confusion; the relationship is the legal test. The opinion acknowledges that factors are inquiries into fact which the applicant must support with evidence. The Board applies them in a way that balances the commercial association and potential harm to the consumer. However, the Board must not allow the application of the DuPont factors to become a formality; rather, it must actively engage with the specific details of the conflicting marks and business activities. The opinion suggests that the Board's ability to weigh the factors depends on the distinctness of the marks and the actual market usage of the parties involved.

## Federal Circuit Standard of Review: De Novo Application of Law vs. Substantial Evidence of Fact

Once the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board issues its decision, the Federal Circuit reviews the legal conclusions de novo. The opinion states explicitly, "We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." This division is critical for law students understanding the appellate process. Legal conclusions are the Board's application of the law to the facts, such as whether the DuPont factors legally support a finding of confusion. Because these are matters of legal interpretation, the appellate court reviews them with no deference, deciding the correct legal standard from the start. However, the Board's factual findings—such as whether the services are similar based on the record—are given deference and only reversed if they lack substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This distinction allows the court to correct legal errors while respecting the Board's specialized appellate function in trademark proceedings.

The standard of review ensures that the Federal Circuit maintains uniformity in trademark law while allowing the Board to function as the first instance of fact-finding. The opinion notes that the court reviews the Board's analysis through the lens of these standards, where "legal conclusions" received the higher scrutiny. In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit found the Board's analysis of the sixth DuPont factor was legally flawed, thus requiring a de novo analysis of the legal scope of the relevant public. Conversely, for other disputes, factual findings stand unless no reasonable mind could accept them as sufficient. This bifurcated approach forces the appellant to identify precisely where the error lies: whether it is a failure to apply the law correctly or a failure to rely on adequate evidence in the initial fact-finding.

## The Necessity to Consider Only Relevant Factors Within the Record

The appellate court enforces a constraint that the Board cannot consider facts not present in the record, and that it must consider relevant factors to the totality of its analysis. The opinion states, "The Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.'" This principle is foundational to appellate review because it prevents a Board from inventing complexities that do not exist in the administrative proceeding. In *Apex Bank*, the Board erred in limiting its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor to marks related to credit card services, having previously found those services to be "legally identical" to banking services under the second DuPont factor. The court found that when the second factor establishes a factual finding of service similarity, the Board must maintain the same scope of relevance when analyzing the sixth factor regarding the number of similar marks.

The court vacated the Board's finding on the sixth DuPont factor because it was too narrow regarding the scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration. The Board failed to consider 42 other marks using "Aspire" which were relevant because the services were highly similar. This illustrates that the analysis of DuPont factors is not static; it requires an internal consistency from one factor to another. If the facts support a broad interpretation of service similarity in one factor, the Board must allow relevant similar marks to be considered in another factor to ensure the final determination of likelihood of confusion is accurate. The opinion concludes that the Board did not err in its legal analysis regarding the second factor but corrected its finding on the first, indicating the formal constraints that the Board must maintain when defining the record of relevant factors.

# Affirmance: Analysis of Similarity of Goods and Services (Second Factor)

## Board's Definition of Credit Card Services vs. Banking Services

To understand the court's affirmation of the Board's finding on the second DuPont factor, students must first grasp how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) interpreted the services involved. The second DuPont factor assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and/or services, examining whether the respective products are related in a manner such that they could give rise to a mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source. On appeal, the Federal Circuit looked specifically at the services offered by CC Serve—credit card services—and Apex Bank—banking and financing services. As outlined in the opinion, the Board began by analyzing the meaning of these service categories. Because the Trademark Manual for Examiners had deleted the entry for "credit card services" after CC Serve's registration, the Board first determined the prevailing meaning of the identified services. The Board defined "credit card services" as issuing credit cards for finance and debt counseling, processing payments, authorization, monitoring, and accessing credit scores.

For the second DuPont factor, the Federal Circuit agreed with the Board that the services provided by the two parties fell within the relevant universe. While the parties disputed whether their services were identical, the standard is not strict identity; the evidence must only establish that the products are related in some manner. The Board determined that the definitions of "banking" and "finance" encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. Consequently, the Board concluded that Apex's services (banking/financing) and CC Serve's services (credit cards) are "legally identical, in part." The appellate court noted that the Board did not have to define the services in identical terms for every fact, only that the definitions of the relevant goods provided substantial evidence to the Board that the services were sufficiently related to warrant consideration under the second DuPont factor.

## Economic Integration: The Link Between Credit Cards and Lending Funds

A critical aspect of the resemblance analysis in this case was the economic integration between the parties' activities. CC Serve initiated litigation as the entity that issues credit cards under the name ASPIRE, partnering with a bank to issue branded cards. On the other hand, Apex Bank is a retail bank chartered in Tennessee that offers various financial products, including loans and accounts, but did not offer credit cards. However, Apex planned to offer an internet bank under the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for "banking and financing services." The opinion highlights that the economic fallout of these services requires consideration of the relationship between issuing cards and banking.

The Board determined that because extending credit or providing funds is the essence of credit card services and banking services, there is a strong link between the two. The Board reasoned that if a customer sees the credit card, they could be misled into believing that the credit card funds are issued by the bank provider, and vice versa. The Board recognized that the service descriptions implied that the services offered by each party were of a type that could emanate from a single source. The Federal Circuit confirmed that this economic integration, analyzed through the lens of the Board's definitions and dictionaries, creates a relationship strong enough to sustain a finding of similarity under the second DuPont factor. The court emphasized that evidence must only show a relatedness in marketing or function, not necessarily legal identity of the specific product.

## The Board's Conclusive Reasoning on 'Legal Identity' and Substantial Evidence

When the Federal Circuit reviews the Board's analysis, it generally applies a mixed standard: legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, while findings of fact must be supported by substantial evidence. To understand the affirmation in this section, students should note how the court defined the threshold standard used. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." In this specific case, the Board presented a detailed analysis of the service definitions supported by third-party registrations. The opinion indicates that the Board supported its finding by citing (J.A. 33–34), noting that "the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark."

The Federal Circuit acted as the ultimate arbiter of whether this evidence met the substantial evidence threshold. The court reasoned that because the Board's conclusion was supported by dictionary definitions and third-party registrations, the evidence was adequate to uphold the Board's conclusion that services were highly similar. The appellate court found that it did not err in applying the substantial evidence standard to the second DuPont factor. The opinion confirms that individual findings under the DuPont factors are questions of fact, and the Federal Circuit tests these for substantial evidence, deferring to the Board's weight assignment unless that weight assignment lacks support. Therefore, the Board's assessment that services were "legally identical, in part" held up under scrutiny, leading to the affirmation of that specific factor.

## Why the Court Found No Error in Determining High Service Similarity

Finally, the court addressed the appellant's specific objections to the Board's determination. Apex argued on appeal that the parties' services were not the same and were not directly competitive, particularly noting that CC Serve was not providing banking services directly but was partnering with banks to manage credit card programs. The Federal Circuit rejected this argument as unavailing. The court stated that the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services, and substantial evidence supports the Board's finding. Apex's key reliance on the fact that CC Serve joins with a bank versus Apex being the bank itself did not negate the finding of related services.

The court held that the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar was supported by relevant evidence and appealed to the authority of the Board's detailed reasoning. By affirming the Board's analysis, the court clarified that the second DuPont factor does not demand identical goods, but rather a demonstrated relatedness that could cause confusion. The opinion explicitly notes that the Board's analysis should be given deference since the members of the board were the first to make these findings and were tasked with applying the relevant standards. Consequently, the court found no merit in Apex's claim that the Board erred in concluding that the second DuPont factor weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion, resulting in an affirmation of that specific factor while requiring remand for other factors where similar marks defined differently.

# Vacatur and Remand: Scope of Similar Marks and Factor Consistency

## The Sixth Factor: Effectiveness of Similar Marks and the Definition of 'Similar Goods'

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) applies a multi-factor test to determine likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). The federal circuit court in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* reviewed whether the Board applied the DuPont factors correctly. Specifically, the Court of Appeals examined the application of the sixth DuPont factor, which asks whether the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods will counter the effect of the non-similarity of the parties' marks. A prior use of similar marks can imply a relatively weak mark, suggesting that the primary mark is not entitled to a broad scope of protection because the relevant market is crowded with competitors using similar branding.

In this case, CC Serve already held a registration for the word mark ASPIRE in connection with credit card services. Apex intended to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board found that because Apex's methods involved "banking and financing," but CC Serve's services involved "credit card services," the services were related. However, when evaluating the sixth DuPont factor, the Board isolated the universe of third-party marks to only those found on credit card services. This error creates a specific legal teaching moment: the scope of factors is linked by factual findings rather than legal categorization. The sixth DuPont factor requires the analysis of similar marks relevant to the similarity of goods found in the second DuPont factor. If the goods are similar, as established under the second factor, the relevant public—and thus the universe of similar marks—is defined by that same similarity, not a narrower subset grounded strictly in product classification definitions.

## Procedural Error: Limiting Similar Marks to Credit Card Clients Only

The Board adjudicated that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks, concluding it was not industrially or commercially weak. The Board countenanced that the evidence regarding third-party usage of "Aspire" marks for credit card services was relevant, but explicitly excluded marks related to other banking and financing services. This procedural error was grounded in the Board's earlier determination that Apex's services were different enough to require a distinct scope review. However, the Board reversed itself when it cited cases like *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.*, 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015), which establish that consumer education dampens confusion when there is substantial overlap.

The Federal Circuit noted that limiting the "similar marks" analysis solely to credit card services, despite the finding of service similarity, failed to account for the broader marketing environment. Apex had submitted 42 other uses of "Aspire" in connection with banking and financing. By excluding these, the Board artificially reduced the density of the relevant public. The legal principle established here is that the Board cannot apply a honeycomb of their own variable to the DuPont factors. If the Board decides services are "highly similar" in the second fact finding, the third-party evidence regarding similar marks must be considered under that same scope. The Board restricted the thought process to only those where credit card clients made a difference, yet the overall impression is that of a financial consumer base. The error lay in defining the market narrowness to serve the Board's economic conclusion rather than the factual finding of service overlap.

## The Requirement for Consistency: How a Broad Finding of Service Similarity Impacts Factor Counters

A unified reading of trademark section requires consistency in the scope of goods and services across all DuPont factors. The Board found partial legal identity between Apex's and CC Serve's services in the second DuPont factor analysis. If services are "legally identical, in part," the relevant universe of goods for subsequent factors cannot be narrowed by redefining the goods once the similarity is established as a matter of fact. The Sixth Factor requires that when a field is crowded with similar marks, customers are more adept at distinguishing between them. This "crowded field" reasoning is central to assessing commercial weakness.

The court held that the definition of service similarity applied in the second DuPont factor set a strict standard for all other factors. When the Board determined the parties' services are highly similar, it must apply that same definition when construing the "similar goods" in the sixth DuPont factor. It is procedurally unsound to confine the analysis to credit card services for Factor Six while acknowledging the services overlap in banking for Factor Two. This inconsistency undermines the logical chain of the confusion analysis. In trademark law, the fear of confusion must be balanced against the evidence of commercial weakness. By narrowing the Field of evidence in the sixth factor, the Board allowed the marks to appear less crowded in relevant markets, inadvertently weakening the counter-argument against likelihood of confusion. Therefore, the scope of similarity in Factor One (the commercial impression) and Factor Six must align to reflect the factual reality of the parties' related marketing services.

## Reconsideration of the First Factor: Overall Commercial Impression and Rebuttal

The court reasoned that the vacature of the sixth DuPont factor necessitated a reconsideration of the first DuPont factor, which assesses the similarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression. The Board had concluded that the first DuPont factor weighed against confusion based on the impression that ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK were related. However, the assignment of commercial strength to a mark is inextricably linked to the degree of institutional recognition, which is influenced by the size of the commercial impression.

As stated in *In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017), commercial impression can dictate the scope of protection regarding the perceived differentiation of the mark. If the Board had correctly identified a broader scope of third-party marks under the sixth DuPont factor, it likely would have found the ASPIRE mark to be less commercially strong or entitled to a broader scope. This finding directly affects the commercial impression in the first DuPont factor. If the board did not consider the broader use of similar ASPIRE marks, it failed to accurately assess the commercial impression and how others respond to such marks. Reexamining the first factor is required to ensure the overall analysis of likelihood of confusion is not skewed by a procedural oversight in the sixth factor. The court vacated the findings in the first factor to ensure the Board re-evaluates whether the commercial impression supports a likelihood of confusion under the corrected factual findings.

## Disposition: Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remand to the TTA Board

The standard of review dictates that the Court of Appeals reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo, while findings of fact are reviewed under substantial evidence. In this chapter, we see the application of these standards in the remand process. The Court affirmed the Board's analysis regarding the second DuPont factor because substantial evidence supported the finding that Apex and CC Serve's services were highly similar. However, regarding the sixth and first DuPont factors, the Board erred in its legal analysis and applied a conflicting definition of similarity. The Court vacated these findings.

The disposition in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is to affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand. The remand is crucial because the Board must reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration. It must apply the same scope of similarity used to define the services under the second factor when analyzing the sixth and first DuPont factors. This ensures that the relevant public remains consistent throughout the likelihood of confusion inquiry. The ruling teaches students that disregarding a prior factual finding of service similarity to narrow the scope of a subsequent factor constitutes a reversible error. The costs of this appeal were borne by the appellant, Apex Bank, fund to the appellant.
