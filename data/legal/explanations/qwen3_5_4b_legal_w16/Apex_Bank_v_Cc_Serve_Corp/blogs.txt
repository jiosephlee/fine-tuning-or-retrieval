Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## The Consistency Ratchet: How Factor Two Constrain Factor Six

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* offers a timely correction to a particularly messy vein of trademark doctrine: the consistent application of the multi-factor DuPont test for likelihood of confusion. This case matters because of the specific way it constrains the TTAB from artificially narrowing the scope of comparison in subsequent factual findings. While the court reaffirmed the doctrine that services are balanced when determining whether goods are "similar," it struck down a circular logic used by the Examiner that effectively allowed a party to pick which factor to use. If the TTAB determines that the goods are nearly identical under the second factor, they cannot pretend that the field of competition under the sixth factor is entirely different to restrict the comparison of "weak" prior marks. In short, the court established a "consistency ratchet" that binds factual findings across different factors of the test to prevent the TTAB from cherry-picking data that compromises the integrity of the confusion standard.

The facts of the case involve a direct conflict over the ASPIRE mark in the financial services space. CC Serve, the established registrant, owns ASPIRE for credit card services. Apex Bank, the challenger, sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. Apex argued that the dispute over the "commercial strength" of the mark should allow more comprehensive consideration of existing ASPIRE-branded marks in competing industries. The TTAB agreed with Apex that CC Serve's mark was similar enough to the goods at issue, but then the Board reasoned that its analysis of the "number and nature of similar marks" (Factor Six) was incomplete. It restricted its review of existing third-party marks to only those used specifically for credit card services. By doing so, the TTAB found that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to normal protection because there weren't enough other banks using it in the specific *credit card* service niche they selected as relevant.

The Federal Circuit held that this boundary for analyzing Factor Six was legally flawed. The opinion scrutinized this under the first factor too, which asks whether the marks create a commercial impression. Crucially, the court reasoned that when the Board makes a factual finding that the services are "highly similar" under the second DuPont factor—concluding that banking and credit card services are partially legally identical—that finding Controls the universe of comparison for all subsequent factors. To argue that there are similar marks in other areas when the services overlap means you must look at the broader field, not just the subset the Board liked. This is a significant correction. It ensures that when determining if a field is crowded to the point of commercial weakness, the Board cannot use the second factor to decide that two goods are distinct enough to ignore all but one type of competitor's mark.

From a doctrinal standpoint, the decision reinforces the holistic nature of likelihood-of-confusion analysis. Trademark law does not function as a series of isolated checkboxes where a favorable finding on one factor automatically nullifies a less favorable finding on another in the same analysis. By vacating the Board’s restricted analysis on Factor Six and Factor One, the court signals that the importer of evidence regarding the state of the market must survive the scrutiny of the priming factual finding. It prevents the TTAB from engaging in the "double-dipping" process: affirming that goods are the same, then treating the goods as different for the sake of determining the listener, public's ability to distinguish the mark.

The downstream effects of this ruling suggest increased scrutiny on the TTAB's scope when prior users challenge weak or non-exclusive marks. If a registrant argues their mark is weak because there are dozens of others in the 2024 financial services industry, the Board will be required to consider all those uses. It will not be permitted to thin the data by retreating to a specific sub-segment of the market that has established that usage. This creates a more rigid testing framework that makes it harder for applicants to argue that a prior mark's protection should be limited to the "niche" needs of a specific product line when the goods are deemed identical. The court will likely see the Board remand to reconsider the entire scope of ASPIRE usage across all banking and credit services. Ultimately, this case ensures that the consistency ratchet works in favor of the comprehensive review of the confusion test.



# Legal Identification of Financial Services: When 'Banking' and 'Credit Cards' Become One Mark

The landscape of financial branding is often fractured by transactional models that blur the lines between direct service provision and franchise-style partnerships. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit addressed a critical theoretical tension within trademark law: whether distinct service descriptions constitute separate markets or a single "legal" sphere of confusion when applied to financial products that rely on interconnected infrastructure. This decision underscores a fundamental dynamic in the current financial sector—where a credit card issuer (CC Serve) partners with banks (Apex) versus a bank launching its own subsidiary service (Apex)—raising the stakes for trademark scope protection in the post-2010 Zala and Porter era.

The core dispute centered on Apex's intention to register as "ASPIRE BANK" for banking and financing services. CC Serve, the owner of the well-known "ASPIRE" mark for credit card services, argued that Apex's branding would likely cause confusion because both entities would extend credit and manage financial accounts. Although the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board initially refused Apex's registration based on likelihood of confusion under 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), the Federal Circuit found two of the thirteen *DuPont* factors to warrant afimrmance and vacated a third, with significant ramifications for how similar goods are defined in multi-service sectors.

## The Significance of the Blanket Financial Mark

The Federal Circuit’s analysis began by agreeing with the Board’s determination on the second *DuPont* factor, which assesses the "relatedness of the respective products." In this case, the Board found that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" were "legally identical, in part." The reasoning relied on dictionary definitions and purpose resulting in the conclusion that both services extend credit or extend funds. The court affirmed this finding, acknowledging substantial evidence that financial offerings are deeply intertwined. This confirms that in the modern financial ecosystem, establishing a credit card service portfolio alongside general banking services creates a commercially cohesive impression that consumers often perceive as a single bank.

However, this affirmation created a necessary, albeit legally confusing, foundation for the vacated decisions on the sixth factor. The Board had previously certified the sixtieth *DuPont* factor regarding the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." Under the Board’s original interpretation, Apex could only count marks used for "credit card services." The court rejected this restriction, holding that when the *goods* and *services* themselves are legally identical or highly related under the second factor, the *universe* of competing marks must be evaluated in the same scope.

The court reasoned that imposing a "narrower standard for similarity" for the sixth factor would be inconsistent with the factual findings made regarding the second factor. Essentially, if the products are so similar that they are legally linked, the consumer cannot reasonably distinguish between competing marks in that specific field even if some are outside the immediate credit card ecosystem. By choosing to vacate the Board's limitation on the sixth factor, the court emphasized that the scope of similarity must be coextensive with the scope of relationship established by the second factor.

## The Mandatory Cascading Effect of Similar Goods

The second and sixth factors are not strictly isolated from the first *DuPont* factor, which evaluates the "similarity of the marks in their entireties." The court noted that reconsidering the scope of similar marks under the sixth factor directly impacts the overall commercial impression. If the relevant universe of similar marks expands—as it did in the remand order—it inevitably alters the commercial strength or weakness of CC Serve's mark. Consequently, the Board is required to reconsider the first factor not in isolation, but in light of the broader findings regarding the financial services landscape.

This reinforces a key principle in trademark litigation: the *DuPont* factor analysis is holistic, even when conducted in sequential order. The vacating of the first factor does not signal a flaw in the Board's analysis of the first factor per se, but rather a procedural necessity to ensure the "overall commercial impression" reflects the reality of the remanded universe of similar marks. This suggests that lower courts and the TTAB must avoid creating silos when analyzing the "nature of similar marks." If a specific product line (credit cards) is deemed legibly identical to another (banking), the Board cannot treat competing marks within the credit card niche as irrelevant to the broader financial service landscape.

## Implications for Partnership-Based Models

Beyond the black-letter law, the decision sends a signal to the partnership models dominant in fintech and retail banking. CC Serve operates a credit card program in partnership with banks, managing the accounts but not issuing the funds directly. If this model is treated as having distinct "banking and financing services" from the bank provider itself, it faces inherent confusion risks with banks trying to launch their competing brand names in the same services.

The bias in the court toward a broader interpretation of similar goods suggests that financial partners should expect heightened scrutiny regarding their trademark registrations. Apex Bank argued that it was a pure bank while CC Serve was a facilitator. However, the court’s affirmation of the second factor rejected that distinction in the context of the "DuPont" framework. This means that for a financial service provider to register a mark that is nationally recognized in a "Bank" name, it must account for the concentration of marks in the "Credit Card" service, which serves as a proxy for the broader banking relationship.

Downstream, this opinion forces the TTAB to align its fact-finding on similarity across the *DuPont* factors rather than compartmentalizing them by service type. This could lead to more uniformity in registration refusals within the financial services sector. If the TTAB begins to treat "banking," "lending," and "credit card management" as a single continuum for likelihood-of-confusion purposes, the cost of entering the branded financial marketplace will become steeper. Companies that rely on descriptive ports of service to avoid litigation may find that the "commercial strength" of a mark is diluted by the sheer volume of registration in the broader financial services corpus. Ultimately, the *Apex Bank* decision serves as a cautionary tale that the legal boundary between "credit card services" and "banking services" is, for trademark law purposes, increasingly porous.



# The Domino Effect of Vacatur: When a Single DuPont Error Cascades Across Factors

Trademark oppositions between competing financial services providers often hinge on the interpretation of broad but standardized legal frameworks. *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* demonstrates why the Federal Circuit’s adherence to doctrinal consistency matters more than it initially appears. This case matters not merely because it resolves a specific conflict over the use of the word "Aspire," but because it establishes a clear corrective mechanism who the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board operates within. While Section 2(d) objections are designed to prevent consumer confusion, the Federal Circuit recently issued a reminder that the DuPont factors cannot be treated as isolated data points. The Federal Circuit's decision reinforces that when the Second DuPont factor establishes a high degree of similarity between goods, the Sixth DuPont factor cannot logically restrict its universe of evidence to a narrower subset without contradicting its own factual findings. This creates a ripple effect through the analysis, underscoring the interconnected nature of trademark evidence.

## The Board's Narrow Analysis of Commercial Strength

The Federal Circuit’s review focused on the Board's handling of two specific factors found in the DuPont test, followed by an affirmation of the Second Factor where the goods were indeed legally similar. The critical analytical error occurred in the Board’s consideration of the Sixth DuPont factor regarding the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor is intended to identify whether a particular mark is strong or weak; a crowded marketplace indicates a weaker mark entitled to protection only within that niche. The Board reasoned that since the Second Factor found Apex and CC Serve's services were heavily similar, the relevant public was consumers of credit card services. However, the Board then arbitrarily confined the Sixth Factor analysis to only nine uses of "Aspire" within the credit card category, dismissing other "Aspire" marks relating to broader banking and financing services as irrelevant.

This reasoning is fundamentally flawed because it ignores the precedent set by the Board's own findings. The Board had previously accepted that credit card services and banking/financing services were legally identical under Section 2(d). Once the Second Factor is affirmed as showing a high overlap, the Sixth Factor must consider the full range of potential similarities, not just the sector the Board has already defined as the same. As noted in the opinion, the Fifth Circuit has held that the Sixth Factor requires the consideration of marks on similar goods, not identical ones, and consistency is required across the analytical factors. By silencing the broader pool of similar marks used in the banking industry, the Board artificially strengthened the position of CC Serve's mark, effectively determining its scope of protection when the relevant evidence for its similarity was incomplete.

## The Cascading Vacatur Doctrine

The vacatur on the Sixth Factor did not stand alone; the Federal Circuit's decision explicitly vacated the analysis of the First DuPont factor, which concerns the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation." This creates the "cascade" effect highlighted in the blog post. The Federal Circuit reasoned that because the Board's analysis of the Sixth Factor was legally flawed, it could not properly inform the assessment of commercial impression under the First Factor. In trademark law, the "commercial impression" often relies on the underlying strength of the mark, which is the very thing the Sixth Factor is meant to clarify. If a board fails to consider a broader universe of similar marks, the commercial impression analysis remains skewed because the premise of "weakness" varies significantly from "strength."

This cascading vacatur forces the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board to revisit its conclusions holistically rather than as a checklist. The Federal Circuit has confirmed that while findings of fact are tested for substantial evidence, legal conclusions under the DuPont factors are reviewed de novo. That means a metric-based error in one factor invalidates the legal conclusion of the next. In practical terms, this prevents officials from patching a niche analysis to avoid a broader challenge to trademark rights. When the Second Factor establishes a conceptual identity between credit card and general finance, the logical universe of comparison cannot shrink. By forcing a remand for reconsideration of the First and Sixth factors in light of the broad finding of service similarity, the court ensures that the Board re-evaluates the strength of the opposing mark within the full context of the financial services marketplace.

## Doctrinal Implications for Future Oppositions

The broader implication of this decision is the necessity of doctrinal consistency in trademark opposition analysis across Federal Circuit appeals. It reinforces that the DuPont factors are not a scoring system where each element can be weighed independently without regard for others. In this case, the Seventh Circuit required the Board to revise its approach, preventing the application of a "weak mark" theory to a mark that the analysis of service similarity suggests operates in a crowded field. Downstream effects for oppositional filers could be subtle but significant; corporations will increasingly need to ensure that evidence of third-party similar marks is comprehensive across all relevant services, not just the direct competitor's specific line of business. Operators will also need to be more vigilant in ensuring their evidence of service overlap is defined broadly enough to withstand appellate scrutiny that views the Second and Sixth factors as reflections of the same reality. For trademark counsel, this case serves as a reminder that the "relevant public" is not a variable that can be fixed and then ignored; rather, as the scope of similarity grows, the scope of evidence regarding similar marks must grow accordingly.
