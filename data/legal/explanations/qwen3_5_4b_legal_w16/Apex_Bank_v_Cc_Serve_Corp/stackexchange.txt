Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What is the standard of review when appealing a specific Trademark opposition refusal in the Federal Circuit?
Question:
The opinion states that legal conclusions are reviewed "de novo," but factual findings are reviewed for "substantial evidence." Can someone break down the specific roles of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) versus the Court of Appeals here? Does the appeals court retry the facts or just see if the Board's conclusions make sense?
Answer:
### Answer

Based on the court opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit applies a bifurcated standard of review when appealing a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) decision. The standard hinges on the distinction between the TTAB's **findings of fact** and the **legal conclusions** drawn from those findings.

#### 1. The Standard of Review
As the court explicitly states in the appellate opinion:
> "We review the [B]oard's legal conclusions *de novo*, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." (Opinion at 5).

This establishes two distinct tiers of deference depending on the issue presented:

*   **De Novo Review for Legal Conclusions:** The Federal Circuit acts as the final tribunal for legal interpretation. It applies this standard to any legal question, including whether the TTAB was correct in identifying relevant DuPont factors as questions of law or fact.
*   **Substantial Evidence Review for Factual Findings:** The standard is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." (Opinion at 5, citing *Cons. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*). This is a highly deferential standard; the court does not reweigh the evidence but checks if any non-frivolous evidence exists to support the Board's finding.

#### 2. TTAB vs. Federal Circuit Roles
This case illustrates that the FCircuit does not "retry the facts." Instead, it determines whether the Board’s factual findings, supported by the evidence in the record, were sufficient to sustain its legal conclusions.

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit recognized this dual role:
*   **The TTAB:** "Determined the meaning of the identified services... and [considered third-party registrations] to support its finding that 'the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.'" (Opinion at 5).
*   **The Court:** Reviewed whether those service descriptions and evidence truly warranted the factual conclusion.

The opinion notes that specific issues might trigger remand rather than a new factual hearing. For instance, under the Sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited the scope of similar marks to credit card services. However, because the TTAB had already factually found the party services were "highly similar" (Second Factor), the Court determined the Board's *legal application* of that first fact was too narrow. The Court did not ask the Board to gather new evidence, but rather directed the Board to "reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board's factual finding." (Opinion at 11).

#### 3. The Hybrid Nature of the DuPont Factors
While the overall standard is split between law and fact, the likelihood-of-confusion analysis creates a complex hybrid. The opinion clarifies:
> "Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors." (Opinion at 5).

Thus, the Federal Circuit reviews the strength of the factual findings under the DuPont factors to determine the ultimate legal legal conclusion on confusion.

*   **Question of Fact:** The Court held that "Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal." (Opinion at 5, citing *M2 Software*). Therefore, the Court asked if the Board had "substantial evidence" for its finding regarding the similarity of goods or the commercial strength of a prior mark.
*   **Question of Law:** The Second Factor's conclusion was "affirmed" because the Board carefully considered the descriptions and had "substantial evidence to support the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar." (Opinion at 6).
*   **Vacating for Legal Error:** However, where the Court found the Board had applied the law incorrectly *to* the facts (such as limiting the scope of similar goods despite a factual finding of service similarity), the Court remanded. As the Court held: "The Board's analysis was legally flawed... We thus vacate the Board's finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand." (Opinion at 11–12).

#### Conclusion
In summary, under the controlling precedent in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit **does not retry factual findings** but determines if those findings are supported by substantial evidence. Legal conclusions regarding the applicability of the Lanham Act and the DuPont factors are reviewed **de novo**, meaning the Court will correct errors where the TTAB misapplied the law to the accepted facts. When the Court finds the Board's analysis of the law essential to the outcome (e.g., defining the universe of marks), it vacates the finding and remands the specific issue for the Board to apply the correct legal standard to the prior-identified facts.

### Why did the Court consider 'banking and financing services' legally 'identical' to 'credit card services'?
Question:
Apex Bank didn't currently offer credit cards, only checking/savings/mortgages, while CC Serve offered credit card services. The Board found the services were highly similar. How does the Court justify finding that the dictionary definitions for 'banking' encompass 'credit cards' for the purpose of the 2nd DuPont Factor? I was under the impression that unrelated goods/services get less weight.
Answer:
**Title:** The Evidentiary Burden for the Second DuPont Factor: Similarity vs. Identity Under the Dictionary Definition

**Answer:**

Despite your impression that weight for unrelated goods is low, the Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) finding that Apex’s "banking and financing services" are highly similar to CC Serve’s "credit card services." This finding rested on the Board’s interpretation of dictionary definitions and the legal standard for the second DuPont factor, rather than a strict requirement for functional identity.

First, it is necessary to clarify the procedural roles. While the Board made the factual finding regarding dictionary definitions, the Federal Circuit affirmed that finding based on substantial evidence. As the opinion states, the Board determined that because the dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance" "encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards" (J.A. 27–28), the parties' services are "legally identical, in part" (J.A. 27–28).

Second, the Federal Circuit looked to the standard for the second DuPont factor. Under *Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, 668 F.3d at 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012), "The services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that 'the respective products are related in some manner.'" The Court found that Apex's argument regarding the services' unrelatedness was unavailing (J.A. 27–28). The Court noted that substantial evidence supported the Board's determination that the high degree of similarity between agencies' services weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion (J.A. 35).

Finally, regarding weight: although unrelated goods generally receive less weight under the second DuPont factor because consumers are less likely to be confused, theweightier a factor must be taken into account (M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382). Because the Board found the services were related under the dictionary definition of "banking," the Court held that the services were sufficiently related for the services not to be unrelated. Thus, the Court upheld the finding that the services were legally similar in part (J.A. 27–28) and affirmed that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion (J.A. 35).

The Court did not independently justify the dictionary definitions but rather affirmed the Board's application of the definitions to the DuPont standard that similarity requires *relatedness*, not strict identity. Consequently, the Court found the Board's logic regarding the core financial function of extending credit supported "highly similar" services under the second DuPont factor. We affirm the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor.

### Why was the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's definition of the 'relevant public' for the Sixth DuPont Factor legally flawed?
Question:
The Board only counted third-party marks related to credit card services when applying the Sixth Factor (number of similar marks). Why did the Court say the Board made an error by excluding marks using 'Aspire' for other banking/financing services? If the Court already found in the Second Factor that the goods/services are similar, shouldn't the Sixth Factor scope remain broader rather than narrower to just credit cards?
Answer:
### Why the Sixth Factor Analysis was Legally Flawed: Nexus Between Identical Goods and Multi-Factor Scope

**Short Answer**
The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board erred because it failed to maintain a consistent definition of "similar goods" across the DuPont factors. Although the Board determined in **Factor Two** that Apex’s banking services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar, it artificially narrowed the scope for **Factor Six** to exclude marks used on broader banking services. The Federal Circuit held that once a factual finding establishes service similarity under Factor Two, the Board must apply that same broad scope when counting third-party marks in Factor Six.

**Analysis**
The Sixth DuPont Factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use) asks whether a mark has commercial strength or if the market is crowded, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. Limiting this analysis requires a precise definition of the "relevant public" and the pool of "similar marks."

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board found CC Serve’s "Aspire" registration entitled to "normal scope of protection" and excluded third-party marks using "Aspire" for other financial services (e.g., general banking/financing) because CC Serve only offered credit card services. The Board reasoned that the "relevant public" should be strictly "consumers of ‘credit card services’."

However, the Federal Circuit vacated this finding because it logiquecally conflicted with the Board's own judicial conduct of **Factor Two** (similarity of goods or services).

As the Court noted, Factor Two analysis concluded that Apex’s "banking and financing services" and CC Serve’s "credit card services" were not only similar but "partially legally identical." When the Board established this high degree of similarity, it inadvertently expanded the universe of relevant goods beyond just credit cards. The Court reasoned:

> **"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor."**

**Why Narrowing the Scope Was Flawed**
1.  **Circular Logic:** If Acknowledged (Factor 2) that "banking service" and "credit card services" can emanate from a single source, then third-party marks on "banking services" (Factually similar to credit card services) are relevant evidence of mark crowding in that "financial services industry."
2.  **Inconsistent Standard:** The Federal Circuit noted there is no legal basis to treat the Sixth Factor as requiring identical goods, as precedent such as *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation* demonstrates. Yet, by isolating the "relevant public" to credit-card-only consumers, the Board applied a stricter threshold than the Second Factor required.
3.  **Market Education:** Expert testimony often relies on the broader industry to show customer awareness. By excluding 42 other "Aspire" marks that served banking customers but were deemed irrelevant in the Sixth Factor, the Board ignored evidence of the industry's crowded nature.

**Remedy**
The Federal Circuit vacated the specific finding on the Sixth Factor and remanded the case to the Board. The Board must reconsider whether third-party marks using "Aspire" for general banking/financing services count as "similar marks" on "similar goods." Given the Factor Two finding that these goods are "legally identical, in part," the Board must count those 42 additional third-party marks toward the analysis of commercial weakness and confusion risk.

### What is the doctrinal link between the Sixth Factor (Strength) and the First Factor (Appearance) that necessitates remanding both?
Question:
The opinion vacates both the Sixth and First DuPont factors. Since the Sixth Factor considers commercial strength (based on crowded fields like 'Aspire'), why does assessing the First Factor (similarity of impression) depend on recalculating the Sixth Factor? The opinion notes reconsidering strength might affect commercial impression, but can someone explain how these factors are legally interdependent for confusion analysis?
Answer:
### Why the Sixth and First Factors Are Linked in *Apex Bank*

The doctrinal link between the Sixth Factor (Strength) and the First Factor (Appearance/Impression) in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is that the **overall commercial impression** of the mark, which the First Factor requires the Board to evaluate, cannot be accurately determined without a correct factual assessment of the **commercial strength** of the prior mark, which is the specific inquiry of the Sixth Factor.

The Court reasoned that these factors are legally interdependent because a mistake in calculating the number and nature of similar marks (Sixth Factor) alters the factual record upon which the commercial impression is based. Specifically, the opinion establishes this causal chain in Section III.C:

1.  **Strength Determines Impression:** The Court explicitly stated that the First Factor considers marks "as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." Because the Court held that "commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor," it found that an error in the Sixth Factor "may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."
2.  **Scope Error Cascades:** The Board erred in the Sixth Factor analysis because it improperly narrowed the relevant public. While the Second Factor (Goods/Services) found the parties' services were "highly similar," the Board restricted the Sixth Factor (similar marks in use) to only "credit card services" marks. As the Court noted in Section III.B, the Sixth Factor requires consideration of similar marks for "similar goods and services," not identical ones. By ignoring marks related to other banking services, the Board skewed the count of competing marks, thereby correctly ignoring the Board's failure to show the mark's relative weakness.
3.  **Remand Necessity:** Because the scope of the "similar marks" determines the "commercial strength," and commercial strength impacts the "commercial impression," the Board could not properly analyze the First Factor on remand. As the Court wrote in Section III.C, the Board "must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here" regarding the First Factor to ensure the commercial impression is calculated based on the corrected commercial strength analysis.

In summary, the First Factor is remanded alongside the Sixth Factor because the "commercial impression" is a weighing factor that relies on the factual foundation laid by the "market strength"—once that foundation is corrected, the weighing of the commercial impression must likewise be reset to say it potentially supports a different finding on likelihood of confusion.

### How do 'Considerable' and 'Ubiquitous' uses of a mark impact the likelihood of confusion analysis?
Question:
The Board mentioned a certain threshold for 'voluminous evidence' for commercial strength. If evidence of third-party use shows 'voluminous' registration and use, does that indicate the original registrant's mark is weak? Conversely, if it's rare, does that mean they have rightful stronger protection? How does the presence or absence of these 'crowded' third-party marks influence the likelihood of confusion standards?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed the sixth DuPont factor in a likelihood-of-confusion analysis, specifically the question of how third-party use impacts a registered mark's commercial strength and protection scope.

**Impact of Third-Party Use on Commercial Strength**
Under the sixth DuPont factor, the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods," the presence of voluminous third-party use generally indicates that the original registrant's mark is **weak** and entitled to a narrower scope of protection. Conversely, a lack of such third-party use relative to the relevant goods supports a stronger claim for protection.

The Federal Circuit cited *Palm Bay Imps.*, Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot and *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters.* to establish that "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak." Specifically, the court noted that when a field is "crowded with similar marks," customers are "more adept at distinguishing marks from each other," which "tends to indicate a lack of commercial strength." Thus, voluminous evidence of crowded third-party usage tends to weigh against a prior registrant's ability to claim broad protection.

However, the opinion clarifies that the registrant must demonstrate whether the use rises to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" to demonstrate weakness in protection. In *Apex Bank*, the Board initially found that nine existing uses of "ASPIRE" in credit card services did not meet this threshold for weakness. However, the Federal Circuit vacated this finding because the Board improperly limited the analysis to credit card services alone.

**Effect on Likelihood of Confusion Standards**
The crowding of third-party marks influences the likelihood of confusion analysis by constraining the "scope of protection." A weak mark (shown by crowding) allows for a narrower scope of protection, whereas a strong mark (shown by rarity) warrants the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks."

In *Apex Bank*, the Court emphasized that the Board's evaluation of the second DuPont factor (similarity of goods) and the sixth DuPont factor (similarity of marks) must align in scope. Because the Board previously determined that "Apex" and "CC Serve's services are highly similar," including banking and credit card services, the Board must retain that "same scope" when considering similar marks under the sixth DuPont factor. By narrowing its universe of evidence to credit card services while asserting a high degree of service similarity, the Board erred.

**Remand and Standard**
The Court vacated the analysis for factors one and six, remanding for the Board to reconsider the universe of eligible third-party marks. The central legal takeaway is that the analysis of commercial strength under Factor 6 depends entirely on defining the relevant goods correctly. Voluminous evidence of use in the broader financial services field (not just credit cards) would likely be deemed relevant to show commercial weakness, whereas sparse evidence supports the registrant's stronger protection entitlement. Ultimately, the presence of crowded third-party marks serves to presume a lower standard of protection for the registrant's mark, though the defendant must prove such weakness to alter the scope of potential confusion.

### What does the outcome 'Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remanded' mean for the parties' case moving forward?
Question:
I see the Court said 'Amend the Board's analysis' on the Second Factor (Affirmed) but 'remand for reconsideration' on Factors 1 and 6 (Vacated). Does this mean the Board just re-analyzes the contested factors for the defense, or does the Bank still have an obligation to file a petition? Essentially, do the parties need to pay anything for these specific costs mentioned at the bottom, or are they solely responsible to the Client?
Answer:
### Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remanded: Continuing Litigation and Costs

The outcome of *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* indicates that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) must reconsider specific parts of the original decision, but the litigation does not end immediately. The Federal Circuit's use of "Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remanded" specifically dictates the Board’s next procedural steps and cost allocation.

**1. Procedural Status of the Parties (Remand)**
The Federal Circuit did not resolve the case to a final judgment on all points. Instead, it preserved one result and sent two back to the TTAB.
*   **What happens next:** Because the court "vacate[d]" the Board's findings on **Facto 1 and Factor 6**, the Board is legally required to re-evaluate those specific indices. The Federal Circuit states: *"We vacate the Board’s findings... and remand for the Board to consider..."* Thus, the Board will not re-analyze the facts; it will conduct its own analysis based on court instructions, likely resulting in a new decision on those factors.
*   **No new petition required:** The District does not require the Bank (Apex) to file a new petition to the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit has already issued its judgment. The "Remand" orders the case back to the TTAB, not to a higher court. If the TTAB issues a final refusal after this remand, the opposing party (CC Serve) would file an appeal, *not* the Bank (unless the outcome changes), to the Federal Circuit.

**2. What Factor 2 Status Means**
The court determined that the Board's analysis of the **second DuPont factor** (Question of Confusion/Sponsorship similarity) was legal and supported by evidence. The court "affir[d] the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor." This determination stays in effect. The parties do not need to re-litigate this specific factor unless the Board errs again during its remand proceedings.

**3. Allocation of Costs**
The costs listed at the bottom of the opinion are determined by the outcome of the appeal.
*   **Who pays:** The opinion orders *"Costs to appellant."* Apex Bank was the party who filed the appeal (Appeellant). Therefore, under this specific order, Apex Bank bears the cost of the appeal to the Federal Circuit.
*   **Client responsibility:** The opinion does not allocate "attorney fees" or client reimbursement, only "Costs to appellant." This means the Bank's liability for these specific operational costs falls on the Appellant (Apex Bank). Whether this is borne by the Bank directly or charged to a Client depends on the internal fee agreement between the Bank and its counsel; the Court's opinion does not dictate who pays the Client.

**Summary**
The case moves back to the TTAB for re-litigation of Factors 1 and 6 based on the court's vacated finding. The Bank does not need to file another petition with the Federal Circuit, as the appellate process is complete for this round—instead, the Board will issue a new decision on the vacated points. Financially, Apex Bank is ordered to pay the court costs associated with this appeal.
