Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 2: # The Legal Framework: Lanham Act Authority and Review Standards

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act and the Threshold of Likelihood of Confusion

A trademark opposition, such as the one initiated by CC Serve against Apex Bank, is governed by the statutory provisions of the Lanham Act. Section 2(d) of the Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), requires the Board to refuse registration when it finds that confusion is likely to arise from the concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board sustained the opposition under this section, concluding that consumer confusion was likely based on the similarities of the marks and services involved. This establishes the threshold for refusal: the requesting party does not need to prove actual confusion, only that the likelihood of confusion is probable under the circumstances. The opinion clarifies that this is not merely a question of the parties' intent but requires an objective assessment of the relevant factors. Consequently, the opposition process serves as a preventative mechanism to maintain a clear marketplace, prohibiting the registration of marks that would mislead consumers into believing the goods or services originate from a single source despite an alternative proprietor's interest.

In analyzing the opposition, the court notes that the likelihood of confusion is the primary predicate for any trade dress or brand claim under the Lanham Act. This approach ensures that the legal interest at stake—consumer protection regarding the source of goods—is protected before the Board commits to a registration that may cause market harm. The appeal in *Apex Bank* focused on whether the Board correctly applied this threshold, as the Board's initial decision hinged on whether association between the ASPIRE marks of the two banks was likely. The court reaffirmed that the Board's duty under Section 2(d) is to scrutinize both the marks and the services offered. By focusing on the likelihood of confusion, the Board ensures that a high degree of similarity does not automatically lead to cancellation but serves as the default peremptory ground for refusal unless the applicant successfully argues otherwise.

## The DuPont Factors: Fact-Finding and Discretion in Board Application

To determine likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d), the Board applies a multi-factor test commonly known as the DuPont analysis. The opinion specifies that likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts derived from these factors. While the Board is not required to consider every DuPont factor, it must focus on those that are relevant and of record. In *Apex Bank*, the Board assessed aspects of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors to reach its decision. These factors generally encompass the similarity of the marks, the similarity of goods and services, and the number of similar marks in use. The opinion highlights that the Board must exercise discretion in applying these factors to the evidence presented during the prosecution phase of the application.

The Board's role is to weigh these experts from where the opinion reflects the factors: the second factor examines the similarity of the specific services provided or the extent to which marketing circumstances give rise to mistaken belief. In *Apex*, the Board determined that credit card services and banking and financing services were related enough to warrant a likelihood of confusion because they are legally identical in part. This illustrates that the goods or services need not be identical to create confusion; the relationship is the legal test. The opinion acknowledges that factors are inquiries into fact which the applicant must support with evidence. The Board applies them in a way that balances the commercial association and potential harm to the consumer. However, the Board must not allow the application of the DuPont factors to become a formality; rather, it must actively engage with the specific details of the conflicting marks and business activities. The opinion suggests that the Board's ability to weigh the factors depends on the distinctness of the marks and the actual market usage of the parties involved.

## Federal Circuit Standard of Review: De Novo Application of Law vs. Substantial Evidence of Fact

Once the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board issues its decision, the Federal Circuit reviews the legal conclusions de novo. The opinion states explicitly, "We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." This division is critical for law students understanding the appellate process. Legal conclusions are the Board's application of the law to the facts, such as whether the DuPont factors legally support a finding of confusion. Because these are matters of legal interpretation, the appellate court reviews them with no deference, deciding the correct legal standard from the start. However, the Board's factual findings—such as whether the services are similar based on the record—are given deference and only reversed if they lack substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This distinction allows the court to correct legal errors while respecting the Board's specialized appellate function in trademark proceedings.

The standard of review ensures that the Federal Circuit maintains uniformity in trademark law while allowing the Board to function as the first instance of fact-finding. The opinion notes that the court reviews the Board's analysis through the lens of these standards, where "legal conclusions" received the higher scrutiny. In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit found the Board's analysis of the sixth DuPont factor was legally flawed, thus requiring a de novo analysis of the legal scope of the relevant public. Conversely, for other disputes, factual findings stand unless no reasonable mind could accept them as sufficient. This bifurcated approach forces the appellant to identify precisely where the error lies: whether it is a failure to apply the law correctly or a failure to rely on adequate evidence in the initial fact-finding.

## The Necessity to Consider Only Relevant Factors Within the Record

The appellate court enforces a constraint that the Board cannot consider facts not present in the record, and that it must consider relevant factors to the totality of its analysis. The opinion states, "The Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.'" This principle is foundational to appellate review because it prevents a Board from inventing complexities that do not exist in the administrative proceeding. In *Apex Bank*, the Board erred in limiting its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor to marks related to credit card services, having previously found those services to be "legally identical" to banking services under the second DuPont factor. The court found that when the second factor establishes a factual finding of service similarity, the Board must maintain the same scope of relevance when analyzing the sixth factor regarding the number of similar marks.

The court vacated the Board's finding on the sixth DuPont factor because it was too narrow regarding the scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration. The Board failed to consider 42 other marks using "Aspire" which were relevant because the services were highly similar. This illustrates that the analysis of DuPont factors is not static; it requires an internal consistency from one factor to another. If the facts support a broad interpretation of service similarity in one factor, the Board must allow relevant similar marks to be considered in another factor to ensure the final determination of likelihood of confusion is accurate. The opinion concludes that the Board did not err in its legal analysis regarding the second factor but corrected its finding on the first, indicating the formal constraints that the Board must maintain when defining the record of relevant factors.
