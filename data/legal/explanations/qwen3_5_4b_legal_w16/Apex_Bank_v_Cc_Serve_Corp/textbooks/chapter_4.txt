Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # Vacatur and Remand: Scope of Similar Marks and Factor Consistency

## The Sixth Factor: Effectiveness of Similar Marks and the Definition of 'Similar Goods'

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) applies a multi-factor test to determine likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). The federal circuit court in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* reviewed whether the Board applied the DuPont factors correctly. Specifically, the Court of Appeals examined the application of the sixth DuPont factor, which asks whether the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods will counter the effect of the non-similarity of the parties' marks. A prior use of similar marks can imply a relatively weak mark, suggesting that the primary mark is not entitled to a broad scope of protection because the relevant market is crowded with competitors using similar branding.

In this case, CC Serve already held a registration for the word mark ASPIRE in connection with credit card services. Apex intended to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board found that because Apex's methods involved "banking and financing," but CC Serve's services involved "credit card services," the services were related. However, when evaluating the sixth DuPont factor, the Board isolated the universe of third-party marks to only those found on credit card services. This error creates a specific legal teaching moment: the scope of factors is linked by factual findings rather than legal categorization. The sixth DuPont factor requires the analysis of similar marks relevant to the similarity of goods found in the second DuPont factor. If the goods are similar, as established under the second factor, the relevant public—and thus the universe of similar marks—is defined by that same similarity, not a narrower subset grounded strictly in product classification definitions.

## Procedural Error: Limiting Similar Marks to Credit Card Clients Only

The Board adjudicated that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks, concluding it was not industrially or commercially weak. The Board countenanced that the evidence regarding third-party usage of "Aspire" marks for credit card services was relevant, but explicitly excluded marks related to other banking and financing services. This procedural error was grounded in the Board's earlier determination that Apex's services were different enough to require a distinct scope review. However, the Board reversed itself when it cited cases like *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.*, 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015), which establish that consumer education dampens confusion when there is substantial overlap.

The Federal Circuit noted that limiting the "similar marks" analysis solely to credit card services, despite the finding of service similarity, failed to account for the broader marketing environment. Apex had submitted 42 other uses of "Aspire" in connection with banking and financing. By excluding these, the Board artificially reduced the density of the relevant public. The legal principle established here is that the Board cannot apply a honeycomb of their own variable to the DuPont factors. If the Board decides services are "highly similar" in the second fact finding, the third-party evidence regarding similar marks must be considered under that same scope. The Board restricted the thought process to only those where credit card clients made a difference, yet the overall impression is that of a financial consumer base. The error lay in defining the market narrowness to serve the Board's economic conclusion rather than the factual finding of service overlap.

## The Requirement for Consistency: How a Broad Finding of Service Similarity Impacts Factor Counters

A unified reading of trademark section requires consistency in the scope of goods and services across all DuPont factors. The Board found partial legal identity between Apex's and CC Serve's services in the second DuPont factor analysis. If services are "legally identical, in part," the relevant universe of goods for subsequent factors cannot be narrowed by redefining the goods once the similarity is established as a matter of fact. The Sixth Factor requires that when a field is crowded with similar marks, customers are more adept at distinguishing between them. This "crowded field" reasoning is central to assessing commercial weakness.

The court held that the definition of service similarity applied in the second DuPont factor set a strict standard for all other factors. When the Board determined the parties' services are highly similar, it must apply that same definition when construing the "similar goods" in the sixth DuPont factor. It is procedurally unsound to confine the analysis to credit card services for Factor Six while acknowledging the services overlap in banking for Factor Two. This inconsistency undermines the logical chain of the confusion analysis. In trademark law, the fear of confusion must be balanced against the evidence of commercial weakness. By narrowing the Field of evidence in the sixth factor, the Board allowed the marks to appear less crowded in relevant markets, inadvertently weakening the counter-argument against likelihood of confusion. Therefore, the scope of similarity in Factor One (the commercial impression) and Factor Six must align to reflect the factual reality of the parties' related marketing services.

## Reconsideration of the First Factor: Overall Commercial Impression and Rebuttal

The court reasoned that the vacature of the sixth DuPont factor necessitated a reconsideration of the first DuPont factor, which assesses the similarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression. The Board had concluded that the first DuPont factor weighed against confusion based on the impression that ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK were related. However, the assignment of commercial strength to a mark is inextricably linked to the degree of institutional recognition, which is influenced by the size of the commercial impression.

As stated in *In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017), commercial impression can dictate the scope of protection regarding the perceived differentiation of the mark. If the Board had correctly identified a broader scope of third-party marks under the sixth DuPont factor, it likely would have found the ASPIRE mark to be less commercially strong or entitled to a broader scope. This finding directly affects the commercial impression in the first DuPont factor. If the board did not consider the broader use of similar ASPIRE marks, it failed to accurately assess the commercial impression and how others respond to such marks. Reexamining the first factor is required to ensure the overall analysis of likelihood of confusion is not skewed by a procedural oversight in the sixth factor. The court vacated the findings in the first factor to ensure the Board re-evaluates whether the commercial impression supports a likelihood of confusion under the corrected factual findings.

## Disposition: Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remand to the TTA Board

The standard of review dictates that the Court of Appeals reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo, while findings of fact are reviewed under substantial evidence. In this chapter, we see the application of these standards in the remand process. The Court affirmed the Board's analysis regarding the second DuPont factor because substantial evidence supported the finding that Apex and CC Serve's services were highly similar. However, regarding the sixth and first DuPont factors, the Board erred in its legal analysis and applied a conflicting definition of similarity. The Court vacated these findings.

The disposition in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is to affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand. The remand is crucial because the Board must reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration. It must apply the same scope of similarity used to define the services under the second factor when analyzing the sixth and first DuPont factors. This ensures that the relevant public remains consistent throughout the likelihood of confusion inquiry. The ruling teaches students that disregarding a prior factual finding of service similarity to narrow the scope of a subsequent factor constitutes a reversible error. The costs of this appeal were borne by the appellant, Apex Bank, fund to the appellant.
