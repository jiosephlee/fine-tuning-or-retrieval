Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What evidence can be considered under Rule 12(b)(6) for copyright fair use claims?
Question:
The Second Circuit states it can look at 'facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached... and documents incorporated by reference.' But does this include judicial notice of copyright registrations? Is it fair use a substantive case or a procedural pleading issue, and what standard of proof applies at this stage?
Answer:
In the Second Circuit opinion *Santos v. Kimmel*, 24-2196-cv (Oct. 6, 2025) (Lohier, C.J.), the court addresses the evidentiary scope and procedural standards applicable to a Rule 12(b)(6) motion regarding copyright fair use.

**Scope of Evidence for Judicial Notice**
Yes, judicial notice of copyright registrations is permitted when assessing a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. While the Second Circuit generally reviews complaints for "facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference," this opinion clarifies that the court may also take judicial notice of "copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office's registry." *Santos v. Kimmel*, 153 F. Supp. 3d ___ (2d Cir. 2025) (quoting *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005)). Thus, public copyright registry documents are considered alongside the pleadings themselves.

**Fair Use as a Substantive Defense at the Pleading Stage**
Although fair use is a substantive defense that "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior," the Second Circuit treats it as accessible at the pleading stage if the application is sufficiently clear. The court notes that the dismissal of a plaintiff’s claim requires a finding that fair use bars the action. However, this does not mandate a full evidentiary trial. The court may decide that fair use bars the claim at the dismissal stage if it is "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage. *Santos v. Kimmel* (citing *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 (2d Cir. 2016)).

**Standard of Analysis**
The standard for dismissal hinges on whether the legal conclusion of fair use can be "clearly established" from the documents and public record available at that stage. The court states that it "consider[s] only 'the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue'" (*Cariou v. Prince*, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013)). Consequently, the sufficiency of the fair use claim relies on the face of the complaint, attached exhibits, judicial notice of registrations, and the court’s interpretation of the Fourth Factor (market effect) without a trial. The court finds that a court must decide if the fair use defense "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior—and in turn, whether the plaintiff’s claim fails to state a claim for relief." *Santos v. Kimmel* (quoting *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024)).

In summary, *Santos v. Kimmel* allows the Second Circuit to determine fair use issues under a Rule 12(b)(6) motion by looking at pleadings and public copyright records where the transformative nature and fair use failure are "clearly established" without needing to resolve disputed factual issues at trial.

### Why is the subjective intent of the user irrelevant for transformative use?
Question:
Under Section 107, the court cites *Andy Warhol Found.* regarding 'purpose and character' of the use. Does the court mean that the 'purpose' is determined by an external observer, not the copyright holder? How does this align with Kimmel's argument that he initiated the creation?
Answer:
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit addresses the determination of the "purpose and character of the use" factor under Section 107 of the Copyright Act to dismiss copyright infringement claims involving parody and commentary. The Court clarifies that the "purpose" of a secondary use is determined objectively by a reasonable observer, rather than subjectively by the intent of the copyright holder or the creator of the derivative work.

Under the fair use doctrine, particularly when considering transformative use, the Court relies on the Supreme Court’s holdings in *Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith* to establish that subjective intent is irrelevant. The opinion states that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." By directing the inquiry to the objective perspective of a reasonable observer, the Court avoids the volatility of investigating the internal motivations of the parties.

This objective standard directly counters Kimmel’s argument that his subjective intent as the initiator of the project made the secondary work non-transformative. Kimmel argued he was the designer of the original works and their original purpose. However, addressing this, the Court notes that the secondary use was transformative only if examined through the lens of its communicative value rather than its creation timeline. Since "Santos's original allegation acknowledges[s] that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was 'to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money,'" the Court looks to the reasonable observation of these sentiments. The 2nd Circuit finds that this commentary was not a "supplantation of objects of the original creation," but rather an "addition of something new, with a further purpose."

Consequently, the subjective intent of Kimmel—whether he initiated the creation or his commercial interaction with Santos—does not determine the legal purpose of the work. Instead, the legal purpose is defined by what external observers perceive: "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity," not the internal commercial arrangements proposed by the copyright holder. This external standard ensures that transformative works like parody and criticism are assessed on their expressive impact rather than the intent of their authors.

**Bottom Line:** The "purpose and character" of a fair use is an objective inquiry focused on the transformative effect of the work as perceived by a reasonable observer, ensuring that the subjective motivations of the litigants do not control the outcome of the analysis.

### Why restrict fair use market harm to 'competing substitutes' rather than 'commercial damage'?
Question:
The court cites *Hachette Book* saying 'we ask not whether the second work would damage the market... but whether it usurps the market.' Why does the Fourth Factor focus on market substitution and not just harm caused by parody or criticism?
Answer:
Based on the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the limitation of the Fourth Factor to "market substitution" rather than general "commercial damage" is intended to distinguish between transformative, critical uses of copyright and direct commercial competition. This distinction ensures that courts do not discount fair use simply because parody or criticism might decrease the perceived value of an original work, provided that use does not serve as a direct competitor.

### The Distinction Between Substitution and Degradation

Under the Copyright Act (§ 107), the Fourth Factor asks about the effect of the secondary work upon the potential market for the copyrighted work. *Santos* clarifies that the inquiry is not a general inquiry into whether the use "devalued" the original through critical commentary, but rather whether the new work "usurps" the market by offering a competing substitute.

The Court expressly adopts the language from *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* to articulate this principle:

> “we ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” (*Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025)

The Court references *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* to support this limitation, noting that:

> “[t]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” (*Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025)

### Why "Comparative Damage" is Insufficient

The rationale for restricting the focus to competition rather than commercial harm stems from the nature of transformative uses like parody or criticism. These uses typically rely on the original work for commentary but do not create a proprietary revenue stream. As *Santos* observes, if a creator of an original work would not license critical reviews, then a failure to prevent criticism through a licensing fee is not a "potential market" that fair use analysis must protect.

If the court were to evaluate commercial harm broadly (e.g., does *Santos* earn less for his videos?), it would impermissibly penalize transformative works that rely on the *originality* of the initial content rather than a willingness to license it.

### Application in *Santos*

In this specific case, *Santos* recognized that the videos were "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Under the Second Circuit’s interpretation of the Fourth Factor derived from *Hachette*, the Defendants' use of the videos did not "upforce" a licensing market; they merely "usurped" the market by appearing as "competing substitutes." Because the purpose was expressive criticism and the videos did not compete with the original videos in a commercial marketplace, the Fourth Factor supported a finding of fair use.

### Does *Harper & Row* mean 'bad faith' automatically bars fair use?
Question:
The opinion quotes *Harper & Row* that 'fair use presupposes good faith.' But it also says the Second Circuit has 'distanced itself' from this interpretation. So, if Kimmel used bad faith motives regarding false representations, does that invalidate the fair use defense?
Answer:
**Answer:**

No, based on the reasoning in *Santos v. Kimmel*, bad faith motives regarding false representations do not automatically invalidate the fair use defense.

While the opinion acknowledges the Supreme Court's statement in *Harper & Row* that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," the Second Circuit in this case explicitly noted that "[T]he Court has since distanced itself from this statement." Consequently, the court was not bound to treat bad faith as an automatic bar to fair use at the pleading stage.

Instead, the court looked at the first factor of the four statutory fair use factors: "the purpose and character of the use." The court emphasized that whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, rather than the subjective intent of the user. Specifically, the court found that "Santos’s complaint contradicts any claim of a purpose on the Defendants’ part to 'supplant' Santos's 'commercially valuable right'" in the videos. Rather than finding bad faith, the complaint "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Thus, the court determined that the objective transformative nature of the use, as judged by a reasonable observer, overcame the alleged bad faith motive.

### Why avoid ruling on preemption claims when a claim fails on its own merits?
Question:
The court states it 'needs not resolve' the argument about whether the state law claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, choosing instead to dismiss them because they 'failed to state a claim.' What is the procedural significance of saving preemption for the state court vs dismissing on the pleadings?
Answer:
Based on the holding in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit avoided a direct ruling on preemption because the state-law claims were procedurally defective under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. The procedural significance of saving the preemption issue for the state court rather than resolving it immediately on the pleadings rests on the hierarchy of legal defenses and the requirements for preemption analysis.

The court avoided ruling on preemption because preemption is a substantive defense that presupposes a valid state-law claim. The opinion explicitly states: "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." By dismissing the state claims under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim, the court determined that the plaintiff had not even satisfied the basic threshold of state law, rendering preemption moot.

The legal doctrine at play is the "pleading standard" versus the "merits standard." Generally, a federal court will not decide whether federal law preempts a state claim unless the plaintiff has suggested that the state claim is viable under state law. If the plaintiff's complaint is inadequate on its face, the court need not engage in the complex judicial determination of whether the Copyright Act displaces state law. As the Second Circuit noted, the claim failed on the facial level.

The procedural significance of this approach lies in judicial efficiency and the avoidance of unnecessary federal preemption analysis. If the court had attempted to rule on preemption, it would have required a determination of whether the state claim was viable to begin with. Since it was not, ruling on preemption would have been superfluous. Furthermore, by dismissing on the pleadings *before* or *without* addressing preemption, the court stayed within the bounds of a procedural ruling rather than committing to a substantive preemption ruling that might interfere with state court authority or policy considerations regarding state law distinctiveness. Essentially, the state claim was dismissed because it was weak on the pleadings, not because it was overruled by the Copyright Act.

### Why is the Illinois presumption against third parties relevant when suing under the Terms of Service?
Question:
Under Illinois law, the court presumes against benefits to noncontracting third parties without an 'express declaration.' How does this interact with the allegation that Kimmel must have agreed to the Terms of Service as a user to sue as a third-party beneficiary?
Answer:
The Illinois presumption against third parties is relevant because it acts as a dispositive legal barrier to Santos’s state law breach of contract claim against the Terms of Service (TOS). Specifically, for a non-contracting third party to enforce TOS provisions, Illinois law requires more than a mere implication; it demands an "express declaration."

Under Illinois contract law, as cited in *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016), there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The presumption indicates that "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration." This doctrine prevents courts from extending contractual rights to individuals who are outside the agreement unless the contracting parties blatantly intended to do so.

Santos sought to sue based on the TOS, which he contradistinguished the TOS provisions. The Second Circuit noted that Santos is "not party to the Terms of Service to which users (like Kimmel) must agree," meaning he falls squarely within the class of third parties protected by the presumption. Because Santos "identifi[es] nothing close to an ‘express declaration’ that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him or other noncontracting parties," the presumption renders the claim impossible to state under state law. The opinion further distinguishes between this lack of explicit intent and other TOS provisions that do contain third-party beneficiary language, highlighting that where intent to conferring rights is lacking, the presumption bars enforcement.

Consequently, the Illinois presumption is relevant because it requires clear manifestation of intent to waive that general rule. Since no such "express declaration" existed, the Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that the complaint failed to state a claim for relief under state law.

### What is the specific injury requirement for fraudulent inducement under NY law?
Question:
The court affirms the dismissal for failure to allege 'out-of-pocket loss' as required under *Lama Holding Co.* or *Connaughton*. Is this strictly a New York requirement for contract damages, and why does the lack of specific loss lead to a failure to state a claim?
Answer:
### Answer

**The specific injury requirement for a fraudulent inducement claim under New York law, as applied by the Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel*, is that the plaintiff must allege they suffered an "out-of-pocket loss."**

As the Second Circuit affirmatively determines in *Santos v. Kimmel*, 24-2196-cv (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), dismissal for failure to allege this specific loss constitutes a failure to state a claim for relief under state law. The Court explicitly grounds this pleading requirement in New York jurisprudence.

**The Legal Standard**
Under the legal standards provided in the opinion, a plaintiff is required to allege specific factual bits to plausibly support the claim. For fraudulent inducement, the applicable New York law requirement is the allegation of "out-of-pocket loss." The Second Circuit states:

> "...Santos challenges the District Court's dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law. See *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996).

Upon this requirement, the Court affirms the District Court's conclusion:

> "...We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud. See *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017)."

**Application to *Santos***
The lack of specific loss leads to a failure to state a claim because the complaint, as admitted by the record, does not identify actual damages of this nature. The opinion notes that the complaint failed to allege essential terms or causation sufficient to this extent:

> "See Appel’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (failing to plead the essential terms of the putative implied contract... Nor does Santos plausibly allege the essential terms of the putative implied contract...)."

More specifically regarding the fraudulent claim, the lack of a pleading regarding out-of-pocket loss means the claim does not sufficiently allege the necessary injury to withstand the pleading standards established by *Lama* and *Connaughton*. Consequently, the Second Circuit held that the indictment failed to state a claim for relief under the state law governing the parties' agreement and conduct in New York.

In summary, under the holdings referenced in *Santos*, pleading an out-of-pocket loss is not just a damages argument after trial but a specific pleading requirement essential to establishing the claim at the motion to dismiss stage. Without such an allegation, the claim fails as a matter of New York substantive law, which the Second Circuit applies at the federal court level.
