Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why is the subjective intent of the user irrelevant for transformative use?
Question:
Under Section 107, the court cites *Andy Warhol Found.* regarding 'purpose and character' of the use. Does the court mean that the 'purpose' is determined by an external observer, not the copyright holder? How does this align with Kimmel's argument that he initiated the creation?
Answer:
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit addresses the determination of the "purpose and character of the use" factor under Section 107 of the Copyright Act to dismiss copyright infringement claims involving parody and commentary. The Court clarifies that the "purpose" of a secondary use is determined objectively by a reasonable observer, rather than subjectively by the intent of the copyright holder or the creator of the derivative work.

Under the fair use doctrine, particularly when considering transformative use, the Court relies on the Supreme Court’s holdings in *Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith* to establish that subjective intent is irrelevant. The opinion states that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." By directing the inquiry to the objective perspective of a reasonable observer, the Court avoids the volatility of investigating the internal motivations of the parties.

This objective standard directly counters Kimmel’s argument that his subjective intent as the initiator of the project made the secondary work non-transformative. Kimmel argued he was the designer of the original works and their original purpose. However, addressing this, the Court notes that the secondary use was transformative only if examined through the lens of its communicative value rather than its creation timeline. Since "Santos's original allegation acknowledges[s] that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was 'to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money,'" the Court looks to the reasonable observation of these sentiments. The 2nd Circuit finds that this commentary was not a "supplantation of objects of the original creation," but rather an "addition of something new, with a further purpose."

Consequently, the subjective intent of Kimmel—whether he initiated the creation or his commercial interaction with Santos—does not determine the legal purpose of the work. Instead, the legal purpose is defined by what external observers perceive: "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity," not the internal commercial arrangements proposed by the copyright holder. This external standard ensures that transformative works like parody and criticism are assessed on their expressive impact rather than the intent of their authors.

**Bottom Line:** The "purpose and character" of a fair use is an objective inquiry focused on the transformative effect of the work as perceived by a reasonable observer, ensuring that the subjective motivations of the litigants do not control the outcome of the analysis.
