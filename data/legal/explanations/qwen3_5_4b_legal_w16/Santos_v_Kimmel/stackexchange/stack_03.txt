Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why restrict fair use market harm to 'competing substitutes' rather than 'commercial damage'?
Question:
The court cites *Hachette Book* saying 'we ask not whether the second work would damage the market... but whether it usurps the market.' Why does the Fourth Factor focus on market substitution and not just harm caused by parody or criticism?
Answer:
Based on the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the limitation of the Fourth Factor to "market substitution" rather than general "commercial damage" is intended to distinguish between transformative, critical uses of copyright and direct commercial competition. This distinction ensures that courts do not discount fair use simply because parody or criticism might decrease the perceived value of an original work, provided that use does not serve as a direct competitor.

### The Distinction Between Substitution and Degradation

Under the Copyright Act (§ 107), the Fourth Factor asks about the effect of the secondary work upon the potential market for the copyrighted work. *Santos* clarifies that the inquiry is not a general inquiry into whether the use "devalued" the original through critical commentary, but rather whether the new work "usurps" the market by offering a competing substitute.

The Court expressly adopts the language from *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* to articulate this principle:

> “we ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” (*Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025)

The Court references *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* to support this limitation, noting that:

> “[t]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” (*Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025)

### Why "Comparative Damage" is Insufficient

The rationale for restricting the focus to competition rather than commercial harm stems from the nature of transformative uses like parody or criticism. These uses typically rely on the original work for commentary but do not create a proprietary revenue stream. As *Santos* observes, if a creator of an original work would not license critical reviews, then a failure to prevent criticism through a licensing fee is not a "potential market" that fair use analysis must protect.

If the court were to evaluate commercial harm broadly (e.g., does *Santos* earn less for his videos?), it would impermissibly penalize transformative works that rely on the *originality* of the initial content rather than a willingness to license it.

### Application in *Santos*

In this specific case, *Santos* recognized that the videos were "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Under the Second Circuit’s interpretation of the Fourth Factor derived from *Hachette*, the Defendants' use of the videos did not "upforce" a licensing market; they merely "usurped" the market by appearing as "competing substitutes." Because the purpose was expressive criticism and the videos did not compete with the original videos in a commercial marketplace, the Fourth Factor supported a finding of fair use.
