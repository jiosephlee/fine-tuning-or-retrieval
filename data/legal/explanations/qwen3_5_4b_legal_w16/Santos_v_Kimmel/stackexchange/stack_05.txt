Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why avoid ruling on preemption claims when a claim fails on its own merits?
Question:
The court states it 'needs not resolve' the argument about whether the state law claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, choosing instead to dismiss them because they 'failed to state a claim.' What is the procedural significance of saving preemption for the state court vs dismissing on the pleadings?
Answer:
Based on the holding in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit avoided a direct ruling on preemption because the state-law claims were procedurally defective under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. The procedural significance of saving the preemption issue for the state court rather than resolving it immediately on the pleadings rests on the hierarchy of legal defenses and the requirements for preemption analysis.

The court avoided ruling on preemption because preemption is a substantive defense that presupposes a valid state-law claim. The opinion explicitly states: "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." By dismissing the state claims under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim, the court determined that the plaintiff had not even satisfied the basic threshold of state law, rendering preemption moot.

The legal doctrine at play is the "pleading standard" versus the "merits standard." Generally, a federal court will not decide whether federal law preempts a state claim unless the plaintiff has suggested that the state claim is viable under state law. If the plaintiff's complaint is inadequate on its face, the court need not engage in the complex judicial determination of whether the Copyright Act displaces state law. As the Second Circuit noted, the claim failed on the facial level.

The procedural significance of this approach lies in judicial efficiency and the avoidance of unnecessary federal preemption analysis. If the court had attempted to rule on preemption, it would have required a determination of whether the state claim was viable to begin with. Since it was not, ruling on preemption would have been superfluous. Furthermore, by dismissing on the pleadings *before* or *without* addressing preemption, the court stayed within the bounds of a procedural ruling rather than committing to a substantive preemption ruling that might interfere with state court authority or policy considerations regarding state law distinctiveness. Essentially, the state claim was dismissed because it was weak on the pleadings, not because it was overruled by the Copyright Act.
