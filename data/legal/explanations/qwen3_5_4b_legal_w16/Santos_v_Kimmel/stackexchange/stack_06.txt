Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why is the Illinois presumption against third parties relevant when suing under the Terms of Service?
Question:
Under Illinois law, the court presumes against benefits to noncontracting third parties without an 'express declaration.' How does this interact with the allegation that Kimmel must have agreed to the Terms of Service as a user to sue as a third-party beneficiary?
Answer:
The Illinois presumption against third parties is relevant because it acts as a dispositive legal barrier to Santos’s state law breach of contract claim against the Terms of Service (TOS). Specifically, for a non-contracting third party to enforce TOS provisions, Illinois law requires more than a mere implication; it demands an "express declaration."

Under Illinois contract law, as cited in *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016), there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The presumption indicates that "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration." This doctrine prevents courts from extending contractual rights to individuals who are outside the agreement unless the contracting parties blatantly intended to do so.

Santos sought to sue based on the TOS, which he contradistinguished the TOS provisions. The Second Circuit noted that Santos is "not party to the Terms of Service to which users (like Kimmel) must agree," meaning he falls squarely within the class of third parties protected by the presumption. Because Santos "identifi[es] nothing close to an ‘express declaration’ that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him or other noncontracting parties," the presumption renders the claim impossible to state under state law. The opinion further distinguishes between this lack of explicit intent and other TOS provisions that do contain third-party beneficiary language, highlighting that where intent to conferring rights is lacking, the presumption bars enforcement.

Consequently, the Illinois presumption is relevant because it requires clear manifestation of intent to waive that general rule. Since no such "express declaration" existed, the Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that the complaint failed to state a claim for relief under state law.
