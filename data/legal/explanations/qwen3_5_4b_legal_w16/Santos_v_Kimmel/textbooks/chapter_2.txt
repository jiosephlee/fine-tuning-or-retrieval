Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # II. Copyright Infringement Claims: Fair Use Analysis

This chapter examines the Second Circuit’s analysis of copyright infringement claims brought by former Congressman George Santos against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC Entertainment. The appellate court affirmed the District Court's dismissal of Santos’s complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which requires a claim to be dismissed if it is lacking in legal theory. In reviewing the complaint, the court found that plausibility was established regarding a number of key issues. The central legal question was whether Kimmel’s use of Santos’s Cameo videos for his late-night show "Will Santos Say It?" constituted fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. The district court held that the videos were protected by fair use and that Santos’s state law claims were either preempted or failed to state a claim. This section focuses specifically on the copyright fair use analysis, detailing how the court applied the statutory factors to the facts and circumstances of the alleged infringement.

## Legal Standard: Section 107 Fair Use Factors

In a copyright action filed by Santos, Kimmel raised the affirmative defense of fair use, which excuses conduct that might otherwise be considered infringing. The court noted that fair use serves to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when such strictness would stifle creativity. When evaluating such claims, the court applies a multi-factor test set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use, (2) the nature of the copyrighted work, (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. These factors are non-exclusive and used on a sliding scale. The Second Circuit reviews the District Court’s dismissal of the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) *de novo*. This standard requires the court to consider "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference." If a fair use defense is "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal," it can be adjudicated at the pleading stage. Furthermore, the court considers copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry as secondary sources of information.

## Factor One Analysis: Purpose and Character of the Use

The first factor under § 107 evaluates the "purpose and character of the use," focusing chiefly on the degree to which the alleged use is transformative. In *Campbell* and various Second Circuit jurisprudence, a use is transformative if the new work adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, than the original creation. Conversely, if the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original, it is less likely to qualify as fair use. In *Santos*, the parties disagreed on the original purpose of the videos. Kimmel argued that the videos were restored to the public domain via parody and commentary, while Santos maintained that he was the intended creator of the original project. The court clarified that the purpose of the use must be assessed objectively, not based on the intent of the copyright holder.

## Transformative Use and Subjective Intent: The Reasonable Observer Test

A critical component of the transformative use analysis involves determining what a reasonable observer would think of the use, rather than the subjective intent of the user. Santos argued that the original videos conveyed feelings of "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." However, the court found that this secondary use, which he claimed was his purpose, did not transform the nature of the video for the audience. The court held that the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user does not determine the purpose of the use. Instead, as cited in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the subjective interpretation of the user does not fix the legal purpose. Therefore, the court ruled that a reasonable observer would view the use as a parody of a logical narrative, not a continuation of the original commercial intent, strongly supporting the fair use defense for the first factor.

## Bad Faith Defense: Why the Arguments Were Rejected

Santos argued that the defendants’ bad faith negated the fair use defense, relying on the Supreme Court’s earlier observation in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.* that "Fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." The Second Circuit addressed this claim, noting that while the Supreme Court stated this, it has since distanced itself from that proposition. Additionally, the court observed that the complaint painted a portrait of defendants motivated by linguistic criticism and commentary, rather than an intent to "supplant" Santos's "commercially valuable right." Under *Swatch Group Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, the evidence suggested that the defendants' purpose was sarcastic criticism, which is a protected element of the fair use analysis. The court concluded that the first factor strongly supported fair use because there was no evidence of bad faith predicated on commercial supplanting.

## Market Harm and Factor Four Analysis: Lack of Competition

The fourth fair use factor assesses the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The Second Circuit clarified that this factor asks whether the second work would usurp the market for the first by offering a "competing substitute." Citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the court reasoned that the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the concept of a potential licensing market. In *Santos*, the court noted that the copyright registrations established that the videos were published on the same day they were created. There was no evidence suggested that Kimmel’s use of the videos would devalue Santos’s original creation or open a competing marketplace for his work. Consequently, the court affirmed the District Court’s conclusion that the factor four analysis supported the dismissal of the copyright infringement claim.
