Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Market Harm in Parody: Applying the *Hachette* Test to Late-Night Commentary

## Why the Santos Kimmel Case Matters

While the cacophony surrounding George Santos has dominated the news cycle, the legal implications of the Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* provide a clear illustration of how copyright law protects expressive parody even in high-stakes entertainment disputes. This ruling clarifies a critical distinction in the philosophy of fair use that often gets lost in the noise of celebrity misuse. The core significance of *Santos* lies in its definition of "market harm" within the context of transformative commentary. By affirming the dismissal of copyright claims against a late-night host who mocked a first-person subject, the Second Circuit reinstates the Fourth Fair Use Factor as a gatekeeper against commercial substitutions rather than offensive criticism. It ensures that copyright holders cannot leverage the exclusivity of their work to suppress satire that merely comments on the content's creation, even if the commentary highlights the original creator's commercial success.

## The *Hachette* Test Limits Market Litigation

The panel explicitly applied the Fourth Factor, declaring that the test is not whether a work damages the market for the first, but whether it usurps that market by offering a "competing substitute." This reasoning invokes the Principles established in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, signaling that the court retains a firm commitment to the notion that critical reviews and lampoons are not "licensable" opportunities in the traditional sense. The Second Circuit reasoned that the strongest public value of the copyright is not the potential for profit, but the protection of expression. The court noted that it is clear on the face of the complaint that Santos has not suffered market harm within the meaning of the Fourth Factor. This distinction is vital for future litigation involving fictional works or real personalities used in satirical contexts. If the focus remains on the "purpose and character of the use," a court can dismiss a claim without needing to prove financial damages, provided the commentary is transformative enough to be a distinct, separate use.

## Rejecting the "Usurp" Argument

The insistence that the user's view is what matters, rather than the subjective intent of the user or the interpretation of a secondary user, further solidifies the transformative nature of Kimmel's segment. The opinion states that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was "to comment on the willingness of Santos ... to say absurd things for money," which a reasonable observer would view as a mockery of his conduct rather than a substitute for the commercial video itself. The court correctly rejected Santos's argument that the nature of the work was to be protected commercially, emphasizing that the secondary use does not negate the transformation of the original. This approach prevents copyright holders from claiming a monopoly on the "original" version of a meme or video, preserving the public's ability to engage with content for commentary, criticism, or entertainment. The court's finding was that the "potential market for or value of the copyrighted work" was not threatened because the use was clearly not a substitute but rather a distinct commentary on the creation of the original work.

## Downstream Effects on Entertainment and Contracting

Beyond the fair use doctrine, the decision also serves as a warning on contractual liability in entertainment platforms. The panel affirmed that state law claims failed to state a claim because Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service. This clarification on the limitations of third-party beneficiary clauses in TOS agreements reinforces the necessity of explicit contract language in entertainment and user-generated content platforms. While this aspect may be less relevant to the copyright fair use discussion, the combined ruling suggests that when a platform is breached by a third party in a non-consensual capacity, the legal framework for contract enforcement shifts to basic principles of standing and authorized parties. This reinforces the boundaries of contractual liability in digital networks.

## Final Takeaway on Transformative Usage

Ultimately, *Santos* affirms the precedent that parodies and critical works are immune from copyright claims if they are transformative and do not substitute the market for the original. The decision highlights that the Copyright Act is meant to promote creativity, not stifle it. By focusing on the Fourth Factor as a question of market substitution rather than mere devaluation, the Second Circuit ensures that the "will to say absurd things" remains a protected area of free expression. This precedent ensures that as the lines between user-generated content and licensed entertainment blur, the law continues to protect the right to critique and mock the very creators of the internet's media landscape.
