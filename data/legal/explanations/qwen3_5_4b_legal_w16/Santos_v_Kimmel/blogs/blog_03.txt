Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## Who Owns the Cameo Platform? Third-Party Liability and Contractual Preemption

While the copyright arguments in *Santos v. Kimmel* dominate the headlines due to the intersection of celebrity and artistic expression, the Second Circuit’s decision on the state law contract claims offers a more nuanced lesson for modern platform liability. Although the winning arguments for Jimmy Kimmel and ABC pivoted heavily on the transformative nature of the comedy used for fair use, the dismissal of Santos’s contract and fraud claims clarifies the rigidity of terms of service enforcement against third parties. This ruling reinforces a critical boundary in internet law: platform terms generally do not create contracts or obligations between users who never agreed to them or who are strictly third-party beneficiaries of a unimpaired deal. For the world of online networking, this decision cements the "strict third-party" doctrine, suggesting that creators can't hold content platforms' directives or users' platform obligations against them unless explicitly declared.

The core contractual contention arises not from a direct contract between George Santos and Jimmy Kimmel, but from attempts to enforce the Communications of Service (Cameo) terms Santos allegedly had with the site. Here, the Second Circuit emphasized the strict interstate boundaries of contract creation. Governing the Terms of Service is Illinois law, which applies a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court required an "express declaration" that the contract applied to bystanders like Kimmel. Finding none, the court concluded that no express declaration existed that prohibited non-signatories. Without such language, the presumption against third-party liability prevails. This ruling is a robust defense for platforms drafting terms that customers might not realize are binding to outsiders.

The New York court analysis, governing the implied contract claim, struck a similar blow to the contract formation narrative. Under New York law, the plaintiff must allege an implied-in-fact contract based on the "intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to show a "meeting of minds." The Second Circuit found Santos’s complaint failed to "allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract." This brings to light the pleading standard for implied contracts, which is notoriously difficult to meet. If a private party claims that the platform’s terms were breached effectively, the failure to state a claim for relief under state law makes preemption moot.

Furthermore, Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed over a technicality: under New York law, Sandy failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud. This requirement was a straightforward bar in the Ninth Circuit’s view, ensuring that contract and fraud theories could not be used to extort damages without a tangible financial injury. In this case, that injury simply did not exist. The Second Circuit’s decision to affirm the district court here means that Santos could not sue Kimmel for claiming the Kimmel’s actions violated Cameo rules or for inducing that action.

#  ## The Downstream Impact of Third-Party Bounded Contract Suits
The significance of this ruling extends beyond the specific allegations of George Santos versus Jimmy Kimmel. The Second Circuit’s decision added a layer of procedural and substantive difficulty for actors trying to sue based on digital contracts. By disentangling state law claims from copyright preemption, the court clarified that even when a contract claim is qualitatively different from an infringement claim, pleading requirements still operate as a gatekeeper. For platforms drafting terms of service, this is a clear signal: if you want a third party to be bound by your rules, you must explicitly declare that these rules apply to them. Ambiguity becomes a defense.

For user content creators, the ruling reinforces the boundaries of platform-generated intermediaries. While one can claim copyright or property rights, one cannot effectively turn a platform's user agreement into a bespoke contract between two users unless the platform itself forces that connection. There is also the lesser-known implication for state law contract claims: the burden of pleading "person-to-person" meeting of minds is high without a substantive relationship between the two parties. In the age of social media, where identity and reputation often reside in the platform rather than a distinct contract, this decision ensures that platform-generated rights do not supersede strict contractual realities.

Finally, while the court did not need to resolve the preemption issue regarding state law claims, the dismissal via failure to state a claim effectively underscores that copyright preemption is not a shield for copyright holders to evade plain contract formation issues when those parties are never signatories. The court guided us away from preemption for state law claims and back into practical contract law. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the platform’s rules do not create a contract between a non-party and a user, and one cannot enforce them without clear, express language.
