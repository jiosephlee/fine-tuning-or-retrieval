Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The Subjective Observer Standard: Reinterpreting 'Transformative Use' Post-*Warhol*

The copyright status of celebrity commentary remains one of the most contentious battlegrounds in modern IP law, particularly when digital intermediaries and late-night entertainment intersect. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit solidified the boundaries of this frontier by affirming a District Court dismissal of George Santos’s copyright and state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel and his network. The case arose from Kimmel’s use of videos purchased by former Congressman George Santos on the Cameo platform for a satirical segment on his late-night show. While the public implications of a former politician suing a comedian for parody are broad, the legal decision is specifically significant for its application of the “purpose and character” factor under the fair use doctrine. In a post-*Goldsmith* era where the Supreme Court has become more rigorous regarding subjective purpose in transformative analysis, this ruling offers a rare confirmation that the “reasonable observer” perspective can override a creator’s own intent.

The central legal victory rested on the court’s rejection of an internal subjective intent standard. George Santos argued that he, as the purchaser of the videos, was effectively the originating user of their purpose and therefore precluded the fair use defense against Kimmel’s parody. The Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that the subjective intent of Santos on the one hand and Kimmel on the other were irrelevant to the determination of fair use. The opinion explicitly stated that “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.” The court reasoned that the nature of the use—sarcasm about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things—would not be understood by the lay viewer as an endorsement of hope or positivity, as Santos claimed, but as criticism of inconsistency. This reasoning remains a cornerstone of the transformative use analysis, particularly for public figures whose commercial records can be co-opted by satirical commentators.

This decision illustrates how the Second Circuit continues to detoxify the *Goldsmith* decision’s guidance on the subjective intent factor. While the Supreme Court warned that fairness must be measured against the reality of the market and the work’s purpose, the court here emphasized that the "purpose of the use" is objective. By rejecting Santos’s assertion that he instigated the creation and thereby owned the secondary purpose, the court prevented an argument that turning a user’s product into a critique for the purpose of entertainment nullifies the fair use doctrine. The opinion noted that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was clearly to comment on the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money, a secondary purpose distinct from the primary intent of the visual likeness. This distinction suggests that celebrity parasitism or "relevance" does not transform a use into a non-marketable or non-commentary one simply because the subject matter was monetized in a previous transaction.

Beyond the transformative use analysis, the court addressed the state law claims of breach of contract and fraudulent inducement, which were also dismissed. The Second Circuit noted that under Illinois law, which governs Cameo’s Terms of Service, there is a strong presumption against benefitting noncontracting third parties. While this might seem technical to copyright watchers, it aligns with the principle of privity that limits personal liability in online platform litigation. The opinion also affirmed the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim due to a lack of out-of-pocket loss, a significant barrier for proving damages in civilian fraud by trade. The dismissal of these claims reinforces the specificity of the Second Circuit’s dismissal logic; it is difficult to claim strict liability when a photographically logged digital product is used in a context that does not claim the original creator’s endorsement in a way that creates economic reliance.

Nevertheless, the most profound doctrinal implication is the reaffirmation of the public satire shield against personal IP claims. There is a tension in the current IP landscape where creators like Podemos or Kimmel can argue the value of their content is diminished by unauthorized third-party use, especially when those videos were bought for future use by a secondary player. Here, the Second Circuit signals that this secondary player need not function as a gatekeeper of the creator’s brand if the use falls within critical commentary. If the Second Circuit has affirmed the District Court’s dismissal, copyright holders in similar cases should not expect to recover damages based on a user’s earlier transaction. Instead, they should look for evidence of market substitution, which a casual parody using a public figure generally will not constitute.

The reasoning is sound based on the facts presented, but the decision leaves an open question regarding how courts will handle cases involving impersonation or brand misrepresentation not captured by the Copyright Act. While the Copyright Act protects the digital medium here, the state law claims might still fall into a gap if the parody wasn’t transformative in a way that captures the "brand" value. However, given the Supreme Court’s cautious approach to IP expansion in recent terms, the Second Circuit’s resistance to the broad interpretation of the "designer" argument is likely to precedent-set. For lawyers and showrunners alike, this suggests that the line between a third-party creator and a public figure is the strongest defense of fair use, and that "inspired by" does not equal "original." This is a decisive victory for the public interest in satire as a means of accountability, as it confirms that a public figure cannot force a judge to ignore the objectives of a commercial parody simply because they monetized the original work themselves.



# Market Harm in Parody: Applying the *Hachette* Test to Late-Night Commentary

## Why the Santos Kimmel Case Matters

While the cacophony surrounding George Santos has dominated the news cycle, the legal implications of the Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* provide a clear illustration of how copyright law protects expressive parody even in high-stakes entertainment disputes. This ruling clarifies a critical distinction in the philosophy of fair use that often gets lost in the noise of celebrity misuse. The core significance of *Santos* lies in its definition of "market harm" within the context of transformative commentary. By affirming the dismissal of copyright claims against a late-night host who mocked a first-person subject, the Second Circuit reinstates the Fourth Fair Use Factor as a gatekeeper against commercial substitutions rather than offensive criticism. It ensures that copyright holders cannot leverage the exclusivity of their work to suppress satire that merely comments on the content's creation, even if the commentary highlights the original creator's commercial success.

## The *Hachette* Test Limits Market Litigation

The panel explicitly applied the Fourth Factor, declaring that the test is not whether a work damages the market for the first, but whether it usurps that market by offering a "competing substitute." This reasoning invokes the Principles established in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, signaling that the court retains a firm commitment to the notion that critical reviews and lampoons are not "licensable" opportunities in the traditional sense. The Second Circuit reasoned that the strongest public value of the copyright is not the potential for profit, but the protection of expression. The court noted that it is clear on the face of the complaint that Santos has not suffered market harm within the meaning of the Fourth Factor. This distinction is vital for future litigation involving fictional works or real personalities used in satirical contexts. If the focus remains on the "purpose and character of the use," a court can dismiss a claim without needing to prove financial damages, provided the commentary is transformative enough to be a distinct, separate use.

## Rejecting the "Usurp" Argument

The insistence that the user's view is what matters, rather than the subjective intent of the user or the interpretation of a secondary user, further solidifies the transformative nature of Kimmel's segment. The opinion states that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was "to comment on the willingness of Santos ... to say absurd things for money," which a reasonable observer would view as a mockery of his conduct rather than a substitute for the commercial video itself. The court correctly rejected Santos's argument that the nature of the work was to be protected commercially, emphasizing that the secondary use does not negate the transformation of the original. This approach prevents copyright holders from claiming a monopoly on the "original" version of a meme or video, preserving the public's ability to engage with content for commentary, criticism, or entertainment. The court's finding was that the "potential market for or value of the copyrighted work" was not threatened because the use was clearly not a substitute but rather a distinct commentary on the creation of the original work.

## Downstream Effects on Entertainment and Contracting

Beyond the fair use doctrine, the decision also serves as a warning on contractual liability in entertainment platforms. The panel affirmed that state law claims failed to state a claim because Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service. This clarification on the limitations of third-party beneficiary clauses in TOS agreements reinforces the necessity of explicit contract language in entertainment and user-generated content platforms. While this aspect may be less relevant to the copyright fair use discussion, the combined ruling suggests that when a platform is breached by a third party in a non-consensual capacity, the legal framework for contract enforcement shifts to basic principles of standing and authorized parties. This reinforces the boundaries of contractual liability in digital networks.

## Final Takeaway on Transformative Usage

Ultimately, *Santos* affirms the precedent that parodies and critical works are immune from copyright claims if they are transformative and do not substitute the market for the original. The decision highlights that the Copyright Act is meant to promote creativity, not stifle it. By focusing on the Fourth Factor as a question of market substitution rather than mere devaluation, the Second Circuit ensures that the "will to say absurd things" remains a protected area of free expression. This precedent ensures that as the lines between user-generated content and licensed entertainment blur, the law continues to protect the right to critique and mock the very creators of the internet's media landscape.



## Who Owns the Cameo Platform? Third-Party Liability and Contractual Preemption

While the copyright arguments in *Santos v. Kimmel* dominate the headlines due to the intersection of celebrity and artistic expression, the Second Circuit’s decision on the state law contract claims offers a more nuanced lesson for modern platform liability. Although the winning arguments for Jimmy Kimmel and ABC pivoted heavily on the transformative nature of the comedy used for fair use, the dismissal of Santos’s contract and fraud claims clarifies the rigidity of terms of service enforcement against third parties. This ruling reinforces a critical boundary in internet law: platform terms generally do not create contracts or obligations between users who never agreed to them or who are strictly third-party beneficiaries of a unimpaired deal. For the world of online networking, this decision cements the "strict third-party" doctrine, suggesting that creators can't hold content platforms' directives or users' platform obligations against them unless explicitly declared.

The core contractual contention arises not from a direct contract between George Santos and Jimmy Kimmel, but from attempts to enforce the Communications of Service (Cameo) terms Santos allegedly had with the site. Here, the Second Circuit emphasized the strict interstate boundaries of contract creation. Governing the Terms of Service is Illinois law, which applies a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court required an "express declaration" that the contract applied to bystanders like Kimmel. Finding none, the court concluded that no express declaration existed that prohibited non-signatories. Without such language, the presumption against third-party liability prevails. This ruling is a robust defense for platforms drafting terms that customers might not realize are binding to outsiders.

The New York court analysis, governing the implied contract claim, struck a similar blow to the contract formation narrative. Under New York law, the plaintiff must allege an implied-in-fact contract based on the "intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to show a "meeting of minds." The Second Circuit found Santos’s complaint failed to "allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract." This brings to light the pleading standard for implied contracts, which is notoriously difficult to meet. If a private party claims that the platform’s terms were breached effectively, the failure to state a claim for relief under state law makes preemption moot.

Furthermore, Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed over a technicality: under New York law, Sandy failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud. This requirement was a straightforward bar in the Ninth Circuit’s view, ensuring that contract and fraud theories could not be used to extort damages without a tangible financial injury. In this case, that injury simply did not exist. The Second Circuit’s decision to affirm the district court here means that Santos could not sue Kimmel for claiming the Kimmel’s actions violated Cameo rules or for inducing that action.

#  ## The Downstream Impact of Third-Party Bounded Contract Suits
The significance of this ruling extends beyond the specific allegations of George Santos versus Jimmy Kimmel. The Second Circuit’s decision added a layer of procedural and substantive difficulty for actors trying to sue based on digital contracts. By disentangling state law claims from copyright preemption, the court clarified that even when a contract claim is qualitatively different from an infringement claim, pleading requirements still operate as a gatekeeper. For platforms drafting terms of service, this is a clear signal: if you want a third party to be bound by your rules, you must explicitly declare that these rules apply to them. Ambiguity becomes a defense.

For user content creators, the ruling reinforces the boundaries of platform-generated intermediaries. While one can claim copyright or property rights, one cannot effectively turn a platform's user agreement into a bespoke contract between two users unless the platform itself forces that connection. There is also the lesser-known implication for state law contract claims: the burden of pleading "person-to-person" meeting of minds is high without a substantive relationship between the two parties. In the age of social media, where identity and reputation often reside in the platform rather than a distinct contract, this decision ensures that platform-generated rights do not supersede strict contractual realities.

Finally, while the court did not need to resolve the preemption issue regarding state law claims, the dismissal via failure to state a claim effectively underscores that copyright preemption is not a shield for copyright holders to evade plain contract formation issues when those parties are never signatories. The court guided us away from preemption for state law claims and back into practical contract law. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the platform’s rules do not create a contract between a non-party and a user, and one cannot enforce them without clear, express language.
