Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Procedural Foundations: Case Background and Standard of Review in *Santos v. Kimmel*

## Relatedness and Connection: The 'Santos v. Kimmel' Entertainment Dispute

The case *Santos v. Kimmel* arises from a conflict involving the repurposing of content created by former Congressman George Santos. After Kimmel produced or received requests for personalized videos through the Cameo platform, Santos fulfilled these requests, resulting in videos personalized to Vincenzo Kimmel (or vice versa, depending on usage). The transactions were then utilized by Kimmel on his late-night show *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of "Will Santos Say It?" segments. The appeal concerns the legal justification for using these videos.

The case was brought before the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, where Judge Cote dismissed Santos’s claims against Kimmel, ABC, and the Walt Disney Company for failure to state a claim under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). The appellate court in the Second Circuit reviewed this dismissal to determine if the lower court correctly assessed the sufficiency of the complaint. The dispute is not merely a business transaction but involves entertainment law, copyright law, and the interaction between political figures and media commentary on the future of the First Amendment and fair use principles.

## Procedural Posture: Appeal from Dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6)

A critical component of understanding this appeal is recognizing the procedural posture. The United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit is reviewing a final judgment of the District Court that dismissed the complaint. The dismissal was not based on a claim judge ruled, a trial, or discovery. Instead, it was executed under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which allows a court to dismiss a complaint if it fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

In this context, the District Court concluded that the copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine and the state law claims either were preempted or failed to state a factual basis for liability. The Second Circuit does not reweigh evidence or look for "smoking gun" facts not in evidence. Rather, it is limited to the "pleading stage," as this is the point where the legal sufficiency of the filing must be determined before fact discovery occurs. For the student, recognizing that the appeal comes from a 12(b)(6) dismissal informs the battle the Second Circuit must fight: interpreting the law on the point at the cost of a Pleading Documentators.

This means the Second Circuit cannot sit with "new evidence" from outside the complaint or exhibits, but rather looks to the document of the plaintiff's initial narrative of the world, its constraints, and the law applicable to that narrative.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Review of Pleading Sufficiency (Rule 12(b)(6))

When reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, the Second Circuit employs a de novo standard of review. This means the Second Circuit does not defer to the District Court’s legal rulings in deciding the strict threshold of the claim. Instead, the appellate court decides the case from the beginning using its own independent judgment on legal sufficiency.

The standard of review dictates how the court examines the facts; it is "the law that is binding and is the duty of the Court." The Second Circuit considers the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference. However, it does not look beyond these pleadings unless the record shows a different state of reality.

Furthermore, the court reviews whether the complaint provides sufficient information to show plausibility that the plaintiff is entitled to relief. If the facts alleged, if true, would suggest a claim under Rule 12(b)(6), the court must dismiss it. The Second Circuit often looks to *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.* and *Island Software* for the allowance of judicial notice of the Copyright Office's registry. If the complaint involves copyright, the court may take judicial notice of associated documents to determine the nature of the work and the authorship involved without needing a trial of the facts.

## Scope of Review: Consideration of Complaint Allegations and Judicial Notice of Copyright Registration

The Second Circuit's scope of review in this context is limited to assessing the "pleading stage" facts. The opinion states that the appellate court is not required to make new factual findings but must determine whether the alleged facts "support" the dismissal. In the case of Rule 12(b)(6), this involves evaluating the legal sufficiency of the claim based on the facts alleged. The court notes that if the fair use doctrine is "so clearly established by a complaint," the court may support dismissal at the pleading stage.

Additionally, the court's scope of review allows for judicial notice of copyright registration records. This is crucial in *Santos v. Kimmel*, as the District Court relied on the Copyright Office's registry to determine that works were published on the same day they were created, a key factual element for the fair use analysis. This judicial notice allows the court to rely on public records without needing witness testimony at the time of appeal.

The court's scope of review ends when it determines the fair use defense. The Second Circuit notes that the relevant copyright registration establishes the videos were published. This establishes the legal status of the work. The court further determines that the alleged harm from the second work does not equate to market harm under the fourth fair use factor. This scope of review defines the appellate court's role: to ensure that the plaintiff has not attempted to sue on a legal claim that is already substantively or procedurally extinguished, without needing to go through the motion of a witness trial or a fact-finding trial.

# II. Copyright Infringement Claims: Fair Use Analysis

This chapter examines the Second Circuit’s analysis of copyright infringement claims brought by former Congressman George Santos against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC Entertainment. The appellate court affirmed the District Court's dismissal of Santos’s complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which requires a claim to be dismissed if it is lacking in legal theory. In reviewing the complaint, the court found that plausibility was established regarding a number of key issues. The central legal question was whether Kimmel’s use of Santos’s Cameo videos for his late-night show "Will Santos Say It?" constituted fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. The district court held that the videos were protected by fair use and that Santos’s state law claims were either preempted or failed to state a claim. This section focuses specifically on the copyright fair use analysis, detailing how the court applied the statutory factors to the facts and circumstances of the alleged infringement.

## Legal Standard: Section 107 Fair Use Factors

In a copyright action filed by Santos, Kimmel raised the affirmative defense of fair use, which excuses conduct that might otherwise be considered infringing. The court noted that fair use serves to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when such strictness would stifle creativity. When evaluating such claims, the court applies a multi-factor test set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use, (2) the nature of the copyrighted work, (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. These factors are non-exclusive and used on a sliding scale. The Second Circuit reviews the District Court’s dismissal of the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) *de novo*. This standard requires the court to consider "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference." If a fair use defense is "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal," it can be adjudicated at the pleading stage. Furthermore, the court considers copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry as secondary sources of information.

## Factor One Analysis: Purpose and Character of the Use

The first factor under § 107 evaluates the "purpose and character of the use," focusing chiefly on the degree to which the alleged use is transformative. In *Campbell* and various Second Circuit jurisprudence, a use is transformative if the new work adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, than the original creation. Conversely, if the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original, it is less likely to qualify as fair use. In *Santos*, the parties disagreed on the original purpose of the videos. Kimmel argued that the videos were restored to the public domain via parody and commentary, while Santos maintained that he was the intended creator of the original project. The court clarified that the purpose of the use must be assessed objectively, not based on the intent of the copyright holder.

## Transformative Use and Subjective Intent: The Reasonable Observer Test

A critical component of the transformative use analysis involves determining what a reasonable observer would think of the use, rather than the subjective intent of the user. Santos argued that the original videos conveyed feelings of "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." However, the court found that this secondary use, which he claimed was his purpose, did not transform the nature of the video for the audience. The court held that the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user does not determine the purpose of the use. Instead, as cited in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the subjective interpretation of the user does not fix the legal purpose. Therefore, the court ruled that a reasonable observer would view the use as a parody of a logical narrative, not a continuation of the original commercial intent, strongly supporting the fair use defense for the first factor.

## Bad Faith Defense: Why the Arguments Were Rejected

Santos argued that the defendants’ bad faith negated the fair use defense, relying on the Supreme Court’s earlier observation in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.* that "Fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." The Second Circuit addressed this claim, noting that while the Supreme Court stated this, it has since distanced itself from that proposition. Additionally, the court observed that the complaint painted a portrait of defendants motivated by linguistic criticism and commentary, rather than an intent to "supplant" Santos's "commercially valuable right." Under *Swatch Group Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, the evidence suggested that the defendants' purpose was sarcastic criticism, which is a protected element of the fair use analysis. The court concluded that the first factor strongly supported fair use because there was no evidence of bad faith predicated on commercial supplanting.

## Market Harm and Factor Four Analysis: Lack of Competition

The fourth fair use factor assesses the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The Second Circuit clarified that this factor asks whether the second work would usurp the market for the first by offering a "competing substitute." Citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the court reasoned that the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the concept of a potential licensing market. In *Santos*, the court noted that the copyright registrations established that the videos were published on the same day they were created. There was no evidence suggested that Kimmel’s use of the videos would devalue Santos’s original creation or open a competing marketplace for his work. Consequently, the court affirmed the District Court’s conclusion that the factor four analysis supported the dismissal of the copyright infringement claim.

# III. State Law Contract Claims: Preemption and Breach of Contract

## Issue of Federal Preemption by the Copyright Act

In this appeal, Tosco (Santos) contends that the challenges to the defendants based on state contract law should not be dismissed. The District Court dismissed the Plaintiff's claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. Specifically regarding the contractual claims based on the Terms of Service (ToS), Santos argued that these claims were "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim and thus not preempted." 1 Santos cites *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.* to support their contention that state law claims challenging copyright material are not preempted by the federal Copyright Act. While Santos does raise this preemption argument, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit clarifies that it is not necessary to resolve the preemption question to affirms the dismissal of the contract claims. Because those specific contracts claims failed to state a claim for relief under state law, the appellate court applies Rule 12(b)(6) to find that the complaint does not even meet the threshold of pleading sufficient facts. The court notes that even though the District Court had dismissed based on federal preemption grounds first, the final agency was that of state law failure to state a claim. The court's decision to avoid resolving the preemption defense in a context where the case was decided entirely on state law pleading deficiencies reflects the principle that if a plaintiff fails to state a claim under the governing state law, there is no need to elevate the analysis to federal preemption. In practice, if a claim for breach of contract fails to state a valid contract, federal preemption becomes irrelevant because there is no actionable federal claim that could be preempted.

## Illinois Law: Third-Party Beneficiary and Non-Contracting Parties

Santos's primary contract claim is grounded in the Terms of Service that governed the Cameo platform. To submit requests for personalized videos, Kimmel and ABC needed to agree to the ToS provided on Cameo. However, Santos is not a party to the contract formed between Kimmel and Cameo. Under Illinois law, which governs the application of the Cameo Terms of Service, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." 2 The court cites *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016), to explain that this presumption means that a contract generally does not apply to parties who are not a signatory. There is a barrier to enforcing a term of a contract against a third party unless that term is plainly expressed. The implication that the contract applies to third parties must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

In the context of *Santos*, the court finds that Santos does not meet this burden to invoke the contract terms because he did not sign the agreement. The complaint identifies nothing close to an "express declaration" that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him or other non-contracting parties. The court observes that future provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third-party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly. Because the complaint failed to amplify on this issue in its traditional formulation, it cannot support the assertion that Santos was a third-party beneficiary of the contract.

## New York Law: Privity, Essential Terms, and Meeting of the Minds

For the remaining state law claim against Kimmel under New York law, the court addresses the Plaintiff's assertion of an implied-in-fact contract and fraudulent inducement. Under New York law, a court must consider "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine whether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms. *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271* (4th Dep’t 2002). Specifically regarding the implied contract claim, the complaint requires allegations of the specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated. *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147* (1st Dep’t 1994) cited the requirement to plead in nonconclusory language, the essential terms of the parties’ contract. The District Court found Santos's complaint failed to allege the essential terms of the putative implied contract that the Defendants allegedly breached, nor did Santos plausibly allege that there was "an indication of a meeting of minds" that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. Given the failure to allege the meeting of minds essential to an agreement, the claim fails. However, if one argues a construction of an existing agreement, the plaintiff must go to court and specify what terms of the contract they allege existed.

## Pleading Standards: Failure to State an Essential Contract Theory

The analysis of these contract claims is ultimately governed by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure as applied by the District Court under Rule 12(b)(6). The appellate court reviews the District Court's dismissal of the complaint de novo. The court holds in part that a complaint must allege facts sufficient to state a claim for relief. While Santos argues that the contract claims should stand based on the desire to enforce rights under an electronic agreement, the court emphasizes that the failure to state a claim is a straightforward pleading issue. The court notes that the complaint does not support the necessary elements required for a breach of contract claim. Specifically, for an implied contract, the complaint must show an agreement between the parties. To sustain a complaint for an implied contract, the complainant must plead the essential terms of the contract. The opinion does not specify whether the contract was oral or written but notes that the specific terms were not alleged. Furthermore, for the claim of fraudulent inducement, New York law requires a showing of actual out-of-pocket loss. The court clarifies that a plaintiff must allege that they suffered such loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.

The court's decision here illustrates the importance of pleading causation and privity. A party cannot rely on a third-party beneficiary status to enforce a contract they did not sign, nor can they rely on an implied agreement without specific factual allegations of a meeting of the minds. The absence of these facts precludes recovery of state law damages, regardless of whether federal preemption might support an infringement claim.

## Outcome: Affirmation of Dismissal on Contract Claims

By affirming the District Court's decision, the Court holds that the federal copyright claims are barred by the fair use doctrine, and the state law claims are dismissed for failure to state a claim. The court concludes that even though the contract claims were technically subject to preemption, the more fundamental issue was the failure to satisfy state law pleading requirements. The opinion affirms that even in the context of an arguably federalized claim, state law contract claims must be predicated on a plausible underlying contract or agreement. Since the complaint does not provide the necessary factual foundation for a contract between Kimmel and Santos, the court concludes that the Contract claims were properly dismissed and that the Appellant's case is without merit. This outcome reinforces that federal preemption is not an automatic shield against state claims; rather, it is an affirmative defense that requires a valid underlying state claim to be preempted. The decision ensures that state law plaintiffs are not distracted by federal defense, provided they meet the local state law requirements.

# IV. State Law Fraudulent Inducement Claims and Final Afformance

## Legal Elements: New York Fraudulent Inducement Requirements

In the Second Circuit’s review of the District Court’s dismissal of the state law claims under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), the court focused first on the specific substantive requirements for fraudulent inducement claims under New York law. While the Supreme Court established that the "objective intent of the parties" is often the supreme standard for contract formation, state law defines the specific elements necessary to sustain a fraud claim. Here, the court relied on *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), which mandated that a party alleging fraudulent inducement must plead specific elements to satisfy the strictness required by New York pleading standards. The appeal presented several fraud claims submitted by the appellant, including these were the most specific claim that the appellant failed to allege any damages. In this text, the court identified New York’s requirements for a state law claim to be made against a plaintiff. Specifically, the court noted that a plaintiff charged with fraudulent inducement must allege that the defendant knowingly made a misrepresentation of material fact, and that this misrepresentation was the inducement for the plaintiff to enter into the contract. However, the opinion distinguishes these claims from the federal copyright claims by examining the interplay with state law. In this section, the court explains that the rule 12(b)(6) dismissal was grounded in the failure to plead the elements necessary under state law, specifically the out-of-pocket loss requirement. The court noted that the District Court's analysis of the Fifth Circuit requirements applies to the Fraudulent Inducement claim, which is a state law cause of action.

## Application: Requirement of Actual Out-of-Pocket Loss

The district court's dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim turned heavily on the requirement of out-of-pocket loss. As the court reasoned, under New York law, this is a distinct requirement separate from the benefit obtained. The opinion states, "Finally, Santos challenges the District Court's dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law." The court held that Santos lacked standing or standing to sue alone on this theory because he could not prove the requisite definite loss. The court cited *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017), which establishes that a claim for fraud requires a plaintiff to allege a concrete loss of value, not just a loss of time or effort. The court reasoned that because Santos did not allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss, the claim failed as a matter of law. While the complaint alleged that Santos had suffered harm by paying for the videos, which were then mocked, the opinion clarifies that the court found no legal basis for the claim because the loss could not be quantified in the financial terms required for fraudulent inducement in this jurisdiction. The court emphasized that this element is distinct from the federal copyright claims, which did not involve these types of contractual damages.

## Pleading Deficiencies: Failure to Allegate Damages

In addition to the specific error regarding out-of-pocket loss, the court found that the complaint failed to allege damages with specificity sufficient to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The court referenced *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994), stating that a complaint must allege "[in nonconclusory language], . . . the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract." In the context of the fraudulent inducement claim, the court argued that the general allegation of fraud was insufficient without a specific description of the loss incurred. The court noted that where a complaint fails to plead these damages in non-authoritative detail, it cannot establish a prima facie case of injury. The opinion further suggests that this pleading deficiency is a standard procedural requirement that must be met before the court can consider the fairness of the defendant's conduct. The court concluded that Santos's complaint did not meet this standard, as it did not detail the nature and extent of the loss, thereby failing to state a valid claim for relief under state law as required by Rule 12(b)(6). The rejection of these claims did not depend on the merits of the fraud evidence, confirming that the complaint's factual allegations were insufficient to support the claim at this procedural stage.

## Duplication of Prior State Claims: Contract Law

Significantly, the court also addressed Santos's contract claims, including breach of contract and breach of implied contract, concluding that they failed to state a claim for relief under state law. The court noted that while Santos argued these claims were qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim and thus not preempted, the court determined it "need not resolve this issue." The reasoning was that the claims failed to state a claim for relief under state law regardless of preemption considerations. The court referenced *Lama Holding Co.* and *Connaughton* as the primary authorities for analyzing these contractual damages. In the case before them, the complaint failed to allege the essential terms of the parties' contract under state law, which is a requirement for a successful claim. Specifically, under Illinois law, the contract applies to third parties requires an express declaration, which was not present. Consequently, the cases were dismissed for failure to plead essential contract terms. The court did not require a separate analysis on the Federal Preemption issue because the state law elements themselves were not satisfied. The opinion highlights that when a plaintiff fails to plead the essential terms of a contract, the court can dismiss the claim without needing to analyze the statutory preemption hurdle. Thus, the contract claims were effectively ruled out for failing to state a claim.

## Final Disposition: Affirmation of the District Court's Judgment

Finally, the court concluded its review of the appeal by affirming the District Court's judgment in its entirety. Having reviewed the de novo analysis of the copyright fair use defense and the state law claims, the court found that the District Court had properly dismissed all claims brought by Santos. The opinion states, "We have considered Santos's remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit." The judgment of the District Court affirmed the dismissal of the state law claims. The court's disposition was an affirmation of the District Court's decision, which concluded that there were no claims for the appellant to maintain. The court's reasoning was consistent with the procedural standards set out in the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal analysis, where the complaint must reasonably raise the possibility of a claim before it can proceed to summary judgment or trial. In summary, the court affirmed that the District Court's decision was proper.
