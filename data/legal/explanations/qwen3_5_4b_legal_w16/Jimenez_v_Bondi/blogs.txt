# The Nexus of Violence: Why Cartels Targeting Political Activists Can Spoil Asylum Claims in Colombia

## The Stakes of Nexus in Asylum Claims

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit delivered a decision that resonates sharply with the stark contrasts between the ideological realities of Colombian politics and the rigid requirements of U.S. asylum law. This ruling matters because it reaffirms the strict linkage required between an applicant's protected status and the specific acts of persecution they face. The Petitioners, a Colombian family active in the Liberal Party and local governance, faced threats from a group linked to the office of Don Rafa and President Gustavo Petro, yet the court denied their asylum claims. The core issue was not the severity of the violence, but the *why* behind it. The court concluded that while the petitioners operated within a politicized environment, the violence itself stemmed from a specific property dispute rather than their political membership. This distinction is crucial for practitioners navigating asylum claims where threats occur at night in the employer's office or the home. The court's holding demonstrates that an asylum seeker's political sensitivity is not a shield for all violence if the violence occurs in a context (property rights, workplace disputes) unrelated to their protected status.

## The "Central Reason" Test and Its Application in Jimenez

A primary focus of this analysis is the court's interpretation of the "political opinion" nexus. Under current law, an applicant must show their suffering was not just associated with the government or a civil complaint, but that the political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the treatment. The Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez* applied this standard rigorously, rejecting the argument that the nexus was broken by a mix of motives. The District Court and Tenth Circuit found that while Petitioners were campaigning for a rival candidate, the actual harassment escalated only after Jimenez investigated a property dispute involving Don Rafa, a narco-trafficking leader related to the President. The court held that the "threats of death to snitches" and ensuing violence were not due to the political opinion but were fueled by the property stake. This reinforces the court's previous reasoning in cases like *Singh v. Bondi*, suggesting that circumstantial evidence of political affiliation, without a direct causal link from the persecutors to the protected status, is insufficient. The decision warns asylum adjudicators that local political campaigns are not enough to satisfy the nexus requirement if the violence is rooted in a subordinate reason, such as business entanglement with a drug syndicate.

## Government Protection and the Notice Formula

The court also addressed the "unable-or-unwilling" element of the harassment, a critical component if an applicant cannot prove persecution but fears future harassment due to state alignment. The court reiterated that the Colombian government must be responsible, or the applicant must show the government is unable/unwilling to control the persecutors. The opinion relies heavily on the "notice" factor, stating that without reporting to the government, it is impossible to judge the government's response. Petitioners testified that the police told them to avoid the problem and not seek trouble. The 10th Circuit found this an unhelpful reflection rather than evidence of systemic inability or unwillingness. This is an analytical point where doctrinal strictness can be dangerous; the court treated individual police isolationism as a lack of government protection rather than evidence of corruption. Furthermore, the court noted that law enforcement had attempted searches for the property in question, countering the argument that the government was wholly complicit or powerless. This strict adherence to the ability to report—in conjunction with the lack of broader evidence of systemic abandonment—creates a significant hurdle for applicants in environments where state authorities are known rackets but do not immediately respond to the specific threats presented to an asylum seeker.

## Conclusion and Potential Downstream Implications

The decision in *Jimenez* ends the asylum application on a technicality: the petitioners did not qualify for past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution under the specific nexus standards. The denial of the petition for review means the Board's decision stands firm. Doctrinally, this signals a potential tightening of the gateway for political activists residing in high-risk jurisdictions where violence is "freelancer" or "tax" based, rather than ribbing or mandated by the state. The court's insistence that the political opinion must be the "central reason" for harm effectively narrows the definition of what counts as "political" activity in nexus cases. While this consistency for adjudicating fairness is good, the practical effect is that activists in Colombia will find it harder to prove political persecution if their safety is threatened for economic reasons. For legal practitioners, this case raises the bar on proving the nexus requirement and highlights the necessity of demonstrating that the government's inability to protect is systemic and documented, rather than just anecdotal.



## Threats Are Not Persecution: The High Bar for Psychological Harm in Immigration Law

The decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, a recent holding from the Tenth Circuit, serves as a stark reminder of the extraordinary hurdles immigration applicants still face. While *Singh*, a higher-certiorari petition for review from the Supreme Court, it underscores the high bar. While *Singh*, the Tenth Circuit affirmed that a family fleeing violence in Colombia based solely on threats and psychological distress did not constitute the "suffering or harm" required for asylum. In denying their petitions, the court reinforced the requirement that asylum applicants must demonstrate tangible suffering rather than mere fear of persecution. As *Singh* highlights, the Tenth Circuit's reach remains tight, and this latest ruling reaffirms the necessity of showing that an applicant's harm was "offensive and surpassed the threshold of restrictions or threats to life and liberty."

In analyzing the case, one must look at the "past persecution" threshold.

The First circuit case, *Brandy*, cited. *Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App'x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).* The court found that "emotional or psychological harm alone rarely suffice[s]" when there is no physical injury or development of lasting harm. *Brandy* finds that the applicant's alleged harm, including a 10-hour arrest and detention and threats, did not rise to the level of persecution, even though they were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling."

## Nexus: Politics vs. Personal Vendetta

The court also addressed the critical nexus issue, which requires a showing that the persecution stems from one of the statutorily protected grounds. Here, the Tenth Circuit found the applicants' political opinion was tangential to their persecution, but "mere tangential and subordinate" to another reason for harm. Petitioners were first targeted and then had their threats escalate after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa, a leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas. Before the threat, the family continued their political activities without deterring. The Tenth Circuit determined that the main reason for the harm was the professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa, not their political opinion.

The "unable-or-unwilling" prong of the asylum claim was also the subject of the court's analysis. The court found that the Colombian government was aware of the property and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there. The court noted that a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government to show that a government is "unable or unwilling" to protect its people.

## Doctrinal Implications and the Future of Asylum

The implications of this decision are significant for applicants in the district. The court's strict interpretation of "past persecution" sets a high bar for future petitioners, particularly those relying on threats or psychological distress. The opinion emphasizes that an applicant's claim that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government is insufficient without evidence that the government would have protected them or that seeking protection would have been futile and dangerous.

This narrowing of the asylum standard follows a trend of tightening scrutiny in the Tenth Circuit. It reinforces the need for applicants to provide evidence of tangible suffering beyond fear of persecution. The decision also underscores the importance of nexus analysis, where even political activism can be overshadowed by a more significant, non-protected motive. Asylum seekers must now demonstrate that the harm is not only severe but singularly tied to a protected ground, and that there are no more viable options for safety.

In conclusion, *Jimenez v. Bondi* reinforces the conclusion that asylum is harder to obtain than many assume. The court's analysis highlights the rigorous standards required to shift from fear to relief. While the opinion does not establish a blanket prohibition on claims based on mental distress, it establishes a high bar for the court's discretion and the burden of proof for applicants. This means that future claims must be supported by a higher degree of tangible harm or a clear demonstration of government unresponsiveness to the extent that it fails to provide protection. For legal players, the takeaway is clear: asylum remains difficult, and applicants must provide irrefutable evidence of tangible harm to meet the threshold for protection.



# The Burden of Proof on State Protection: Why "Police Apathy" Isn't Enough for a Government Unable/Unwilling Argument

### The Burden of Proof on State Protection: Why "Police Apathy" Isn't Enough for a Government Unable/Unwilling Argument

The *Jimenez v. Bondi* decision by the Tenth Circuit serves as a stark reminder that the "unable or unwilling" clause in asylum law remains a formidable hurdle, even when petitioners face credible, life-threatening harassment linked to criminal networks. The court's analysis in rejecting the petitioners' claim underscores a critical evidentiary burden: an applicant must prove systemic governmental failure, not merely the cold indifference or generalized incapacity of isolated law enforcement officers. In the context of Cartagena-backed gang violence in Colombia—where alleged links between criminal elements and state officials abound—this holding ensures that asylum relief remains consistently tied to evidence of actual, recognizable state collapse rather than speculative fears of corruption.

For a petition to succeed on the "unable or unwilling" ground, the court looks to whether the government stopped the persecutors and whether it offered protection, such as triggering protective custody or investigating malicious plots. Jimenez failed to show that the Colombian government structure was unable to protect its citizens because of evidence pointing to a lack of police action that exceeded individual apathy. The Tenth Circuit noted that under the "able or unwilling" test, an applicant must establish that the persecutors are closely associated with the government and have fixed the inability of the state to protect. While this case arose in the shadow of the Colombian Liberal Party and government-aligned narcotrafficking gangs, the adjudicators rejected the petitioners' claim that the government was unresponsive.

## Systemic Intervention vs. Individual Indifference

The case explicitly draws a distinction between a single bad interaction with a police officer and a complete failure of state protection. Jimenez had worked with a policeman who explicitly told him to stay away from the issue and suggested they depart the country immediately. This turned a failure of law enforcement, but the court argued that the Colombian government had attempted to stop the criminal conduct. The court seems to be making a broad argument that a lack of police response is just a symptom of individual failure, not state collapse.

The court in section II E (Government Protection) states, "unless there is evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect its people." The exception for reporting is also unique to the context of the petitioners. While "failure to report" can undercut a claim, the court reasoned that there is no evidence that Jimenez's reporting was futile or dangerous because filing a police report would not have triggered a systemic response.

From an analytical perspective, this reasoning suggests that the probable fate of similar cases importing themes of "police corruption" is not automatically triggered by state failure. The court must look at the specific capacity of the state to protect its citizens and whether the protection failed as a result of a broader collapse of law enforcement capacity. The result is that the "unable or unwilling" standard becomes more rigid and evidence-heavy for petitioners who cannot document that state authorities were actively or passively working to displace their rights.

## Practical Implications for the Colombian Asylum Seekers

The practical implications of *Jimenez* will be felt with more admissibility standards on the other side of the court and potentially for all Colombian asylum seekers. For those navigating the asylum arena, the distinction between individual and systemic failures will remain a critical line to navigate. If a petitioner can effectively argue that police perform routine surveillance but respond disproportionately because of negative bias or direct connection to organized crime, this issue might raise its standards for the "government unable/unwilling" status.

However, the court's decision in this Tenth Circuit case suggests a clear path to denial for those relying solely on the general narrative that police are corrupt or that the government is incapable of protecting citizens from organized violence. The decision suggests that without evidence of systemic, widespread state action to protect its citizens, the danger cannot be established. This may lead to a tightening of the asylum process that is not just about individual fear but about visible state inaction. It raises the stakes of showing actual, concrete evidence of state failure across the country, rather than just anecdotal failure in a specific neighborhood.

In sum, *Jimenez v. Bondi* clarifies that for the government to successfully rebut a presumption of future persecution, it must demonstrate that an applicant has concrete, systemic evidence of state inaction that creates a clear inability or unwillingness to protect. Without that evidence, individual exposure to violence or apathetic police conduct will not constitute an "unable-or-unwilling" state, and thus will not satisfy the high bar of the INA for asylum relief.
