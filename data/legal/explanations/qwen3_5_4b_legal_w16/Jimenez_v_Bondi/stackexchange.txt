Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why are phone threats and sporadic intimidation rejected as constitutionally sufficient 'persecution' for asylum?
Question:
The opinion notes that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution under the INA. I understand that physical harm is not required, but can someone explain the specific test for 'offensive' or 'serious' harm that the 10th Circuit uses? Does the court require the harm to rise above mere stress, and how is 'more than restrictions' measured in a case with no physical injury?
Answer:
### The Persecution Threshold in the Tenth Circuit

**Short Answer:** No, phone threats and sporadic intimidation are rejected as sufficient for asylum not because the Constitution prohibits such claims, but because the **statutory definition of persecution** in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) requires suffering that goes beyond mere harassment or stress. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that while physical harm is not strictly necessary, the harm inflicted must be severe enough to be considered "offensive" and must entail **"more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."**

### Legal Standard and Relevant Case Law

The petitioner's arguments in *Jimenez* rest on the statutory requirement that an applicant proves past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. However, the Tenth Circuit relies on specific case law to define the scope of "persecution" not by what the court calls "constitutional," but by how the BIA and the courts interpret the INA.

To satisfy the **persecution prong**, the Tenth Circuit applies the standard set forth in *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004), as cited by the court in its own analysis:

> "A finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."

From this standard, the court extrapolated three critical failures in the Petitioners' case:

1.  **Threats Alone Are Insufficient:** The court explicitly states that while it considers threats in aggregate, "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." (*Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013)).
2.  **More Than Mere Restrictions:** Even if physical harm is absent, the harm must rise above the level of restrictions on liberty. (*Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014)).
3.  **Stress vs. Persecution:** The court distinguishes between persecution and psychological distress. It notes: "Although physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong... the suffered harm must be offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats."

### Analysis of the Petitioners' Harm

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm and only proposed psychological consequences (inability to sleep, panic attacks). The court applied the following logic to reject this:

*   **Absence of Physical Injunction:** Because the Petitioners "conceded at their merits hearing they were not physically harmed," the court looked to the severity of the psychological impact. They held that "fears stemming from their received threats are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution."
*   **Cumulative Aggregation:** While the court acknowledged the theory that multiple incidents can aggregate to meet the threshold (*Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011)), it applied this only where the incidents relied on were distinct enough to warrant a finding of "suffering or harm."
*   **Conclusion:** The court found that the "phone calls once or twice a week" and the "threatening brochure," even when combined with an in-person assault that ended without injury, did not amount to harm that was "regarded as offensive" in the *Wiransane* sense.

### Conclusion

To summarize the ruling: The **Tenth Circuit does not require physical injury** to find asylum, nor does it require a harm that must match the severity of torture or assault. However, it **does require that the harm be substantive and severe enough to rise above 'mere restrictions or sporadic threats.'**

In this specific case, the court found the Petitioners' injuries were insufficient because:
1.  There was no physical injury.
2.  The threats were sporadic.
3.  The mental anguish resulted from the threats themselves rather than independent cruelty.

Thus, under *Jimenez*, a claim relies on physical or severe psychological harm that manifests a "suffering or harm" beyond a general expectation of safety and liberty.

Citations: *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889 (10th Cir. 2004); *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792 (10th Cir. 2013); *Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App’x 744 (10th Cir. 2014); *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971 (10th Cir. 2011).

### How does the court apply the 'Central Reason' test when a Petitioner is harmed by multiple actors for multiple motives?
Question:
Petitioners alleged persecution for two reasons: political opinion (Liberal Party) and criminal activity (Narco-guerrilla). The court found the political opinion was merely 'tangential.' If a Perpetrator has multiple motives, does political opinion still qualify if it is just one of many factors, and what evidence places the other motive above it as the 'dominant' reason for the harm?
Answer:
### How does the court apply the 'Central Reason' test when a Petitioner is harmed by multiple actors for multiple motives?

The court in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 24 CC 9573 (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) provides specific guidance regarding the "Central Reason" test when an asylum applicant faces persecution stemming from multiple factors. Per the Tenth Circuit, a petitioner’s protected ground can qualify even if a persecutor has multiple motives, provided the protected ground acts as a **central reason** for the harm.

**1. The Legal Standard for Multiple Motives**
The court acknowledges that a persecutor may possess mixed motives. It relies on *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, 2025 WL 2447603 (10th Cir. 2025), stating:
> A persecutor can have multiple motives for targeting someone. Of course, in such mixed-motive cases, the persecutor must be motivated by at least one statutorily protected ground that is a central reason for the inflicted harm.

However, this protection does not lower the standard for political opinion. To establish a valid nexus, the petitioner must demonstrate that the protected ground "is not incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." Political opinion "cannot play a minor role" in the past treatment or fears of future mistreatment.

**2. Application to "Jimenez" Facts**
In this specific case, the court analyzed the timeline to determine which motive was "central" and which was "subordinate":

*   **Initial Political Motivation:** Petitioners had supported the Liberal Party since 2012 and received threats and even received threatening notices regarding their political involvement. However, they continued campaigning and were *not* physically harmed during this initial phase of political threats, despite ransom, kidnapping, or death of colleagues.
*   **Escalation via Criminal Activity:** Threats escalated only after Jimenez investigated a property dispute involving "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking leader.
*   **The "Central Reason" Determination:** The court found that the persecutors targeted Jimenez primarily because he interfered with Don Rafa’s property (implying a revenue/security threat) rather than his political affiliation. The specific accusations leveled against Jimenez were being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and responsible for searches at the property.
*   **Court Conclusion:** The court held:
    > "...Petitioners were primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security, not because of their political opinion. ... Based on these facts, the BIA did not err in finding that the predominant motive behind the threats was due to Jimenez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa rather than Petitioners' political opinion."

### Conclusion
To survive the "Central Reason" test in a multi-motive scenario, an applicant must prove the protected ground was a predominant or non-incidental driver of the harm, not merely a secondary context. In *Jimenez*, political opinion was deemed "tangential" because the actual escalated violence occurred in direct response to a criminal investigation (property seizure), where the accusers explicitly cited the applicant's role with law enforcement and the property dispute.

### Legal Takeaway for Petitioners
When facing multiple motives, a petitioner must prove:
1.  At least one motive was a "central reason" for the harm (*O.C.V.*).
2.  The protected ground was not subordinate or minor (*Dallakoti* nexus standard).
3.  The nexus exists cumulatively and not just incidentally (*Rivera-Barrientos* standard applied to *Jimenez*).

### Does a Petitioner's status as a government lawyer or National Prosecutor office employee affect the Government Unwilling/Unable analysis?
Question:
The opinion highlights that Petitioners worked for the government而在 the charge of reporting. The court notes that 'Without such reporting, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted.' If a Petitioner has a strong professional connection to the state, does that connection actually help them establish the government is 'unwilling' or 'unable' to help them protect the rest of the family?
Answer:
**Does a Petitioner's Government Affiliation Affect the "Unable/Unwilling" Standard?**

Based on *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025 (No. 24-9573), a Petitioner’s professional connection to the state (such as being a government lawyer or Prosecutor office employee) does **not** automatically help establish the government is "unable" or "unwilling" to provide protection. In fact, the court indicates that applicants with this status may be expected to have greater access through their government-employment channels, and their failure to utilize those channels can actually undercut their claim of a well-founded fear of future persecution.

### The Government's Duty and the Petitioner's Burden
The Tenth Circuit applies the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis to determine if an applicant qualifies for asylum or withholding of removal. As the opinion states, this inquiry requires "evidence about a government's response to an applicant's past persecution."

*Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025) holds the government's reaction and the availability of protection for the applicant are the central factors. Specifically, the court notes that the analysis hinges on whether the applicant "reported past persecution to the government." *Id.* (citing *Singh*). While the court acknowledges that the inability to report due to fear can satisfy the threshold, the core element remains: "Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." *Jimenez*, 24-9573, at 13 (quoting *Singh*).

This duty of notice places a particular onus on the Petitioner. They must show the government *could* have reacted before retreating or pleading the case impossible.

### The Impact of Professional Connection
In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners (specifically the father) were employees or interns for the government system in Colombia.

The opinion explicitly notes that because the Petitioners worked for the government, their failure to disclose wrongdoing is more relevant to the "notice" standard:
> "Petitioners' efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any. Jimenez and his father both worked or currently work for the government in Colombia. Yet, neither of them reported wrongdoing or corruption." *Jimenez*, 24-9573, at 13.

The court uses this fact to support the BIA's finding that the Petitioners had access to reporting mechanisms through their employment. When the Petitioners testified they were advised by a police officer that "nothing could be done" and that they "should leave," the court rejected the suggestion that this single interaction proved systemic inability.

*Jimenez* quoted *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th 738 (7th Cir. 2023) to emphasize:
> "Although police apathy can indicate a government's unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim."

### Conclusion on the Petitioners' Connection
The professional connection does not help the Petitioners establish that the state was unable to protect the family. The court reasoned:

1.  **Reporting Requirement:** The Petitioners failed to report threats or misconduct effectively until the last moments. Since reporting was the key trigger for the government's chance to act, the lack of reporting prevented the Petitioners from proving the state was "unable" to react.
2.  **Evidence of Capability:** The record showed the government had already attempted searches and seizures on the locations linked to the threat. The opinion states this "evidence counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government."
3.  **One-Off vs. Systemic:** An officer's advice to "stay away" does not automatically equate to a government-wide inability. "The evidence counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government." *Jimenez*, 24-9573, at 13.

Therefore, under the analysis in *Jimenez*, a Petitioner's government affiliation actually highlights the requirement for them to show they tried to report their claims. Without effective reporting, the connection does not translate to a state of "unable/unwilling" government protection.

### Is the testimony of unharmed family members in the home country sufficient to rebut a Presumption of Fear of Persecution?
Question:
The court finds internal relocation safe because Petitioners have family members left in Colombia. However, does the presence of family members generally preclude a finding of 'unable or unwilling' government protection or safe relocation? Why does the court distinguish between the safety of the family and the safety of the Petitioner, and how is 'presumption of Well-Founded Fear' affected by the presence of 'unharmed' relatives?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
No. According to *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the testimony of unharmed family members in the home country is not, in itself, sufficient to independently rebut the presumption of well-founded fear. Instead, the court treats the presence of unharmed relatives as *evidentiary support* for the feasibility of internal relocation. The court distinguishes this because the specific threats to the Petitioners arose from their political campaigning and neighborhood activities, which did not necessarily extend to the family's physical safety outside of those specific zones.

**Analysis of Legal Doctrine and Opinion**

**1. The Presumption of Well-Founded Fear and Safe Relocation**
Under the Immigration and Nationality Act and the Ninth Circuit'sprecedent (as cited in the opinion), a finding of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. The government can rebut this presumption, thereby negating the eligibility for asylum, if it demonstrates that: "(1) there has been a 'fundamental change' in circumstances; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country." (*Jimenez*, Section II.F).

**2. Family Presence as a Factor for Relocation Feasibility**
While the presence of relatives does not automatically extinguish the fear of persecution, it is a critical factual substrate in the internal relocation analysis. The Court notes that the government proved the Petitioners could mitigate future persecution because "Petitioners' family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed." This factual finding normally supports the argument that internal relocation is safe.

However, the opinion acknowledges that family presence is not a dispositive shield for the Petitioner. The Court explicitly notes that "the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety," citing the Petitioners' brief. This suggests that while family presence is positive evidence, the Court requires further proof that a specific *relocation* path is viable.

**3. Distinguishing Petitioner Safety from Family Safety**
The Court distinguishes between the family's general safety and the Petitioner's specific vulnerability. The Petitioners’ threats were tied to their specific involvement with the Liberal Party and their identity as "snitches" in the criminal justice system. The opinion states that the armed men told the Petitioners to "move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country."

This distinction supports the Court's finding that while the Petitioners faced targeted threat, the family remained as a safety net. Crucially, the Court grounds its finding of safe relocation on the Petitioners' ability to live with Jimenez's mother in the countryside, where they were physically unharmed for three weeks. This specific safe haven validates the internal relocation remedy, but it is not solely premised on the mere existence of relatives, but on their ability to live concurrently with the family outside the targeted political environment.

**4. Conclusion**
In *Jimenez*, the Court held that the record supported the finding that the applicants could reasonably relocate within Colombia because of the available safety of the countryside. However, the opinion confirms that the unfettered presence of unharmed relatives does not establish inherent safety. Instead, it requires the Court to verify that a specific relocation itinerary (e.g., living with the mother in the countryside) allows the Petitioner to avoid the specific conditions (such as neighborhood-level threats) that constitute the original fear. Consequently, the uncertainty of the Petitioner's status as the family allowed for the presumption of future persecution to be rebutted, but not merely by relying on family testimony.

### What is the difference between De Novo review and Substantial Evidence review for the Tenth Circuit when reviewing BIA asylum denials?
Question:
I see the court mentions they review the BIA's findings of fact under the 'substantial-evidence standard' and are 'conclusive' unless compelling evidence exists. How does this relate to the idea that 'Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear'? Does the substantial evidence standard prevent the court from deciding the case based on the Petitioner's own opinion of the evidence?
Answer:
**Substantial Evidence Standard in *Jimenez v. Bondi***

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Circuit (2025), the Court clarifies its opinion on BIA asylum denials primarily through the application of the **substantial-evidence standard**, specifically regarding the BIA’s **findings of fact**. The opinion explicitly holds that under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." *Jimenez* supports this explanation, stating: "We review the BIA's findings of fact under a substantial-evidence standard."

This distinction prevents the appellate court from substituting its own judgment for that of the BIA on factual matters, thereby limiting reliance solely on the petitioner’s subjective testimony if it is unsupported by the administrative record. The opinions discusses in *Jimenez* explicitly prohibit the court from reweighing evidence, stating: "It is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ's [or the BIA's] decision."

**Application to Petitioner's "Well-founded Fear" and Opinion**

The standard governs the petitioner's claim that they fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution. As the *Jimenez* opinion explains, the petitioner bears the burden of proving eligibility "by a preponderance of the evidence." 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Because the Court applies the substantial-evidence standard to the factual findings, the petitioner cannot simply assert their own opinion of the guilt or safety risks without corresponding support in the record.

In Section II.C, the Court found that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution" because, while physical harm is not necessary, the harm must be "offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats." The Court emphasized that "Petitioners' fail to establish a well-founded fear" was a factual question determined by whether the record contained substantial evidence for the BIA’s conclusion. The court did not grant relief based on the petitioner’s opinion of the evidence, but rather on whether the administrative record supported the BIA’s refusal to recognize the threats as persecution.

**Standard of Review and Limitations**

The opinion reinforces this by limiting the scope of review to the "administrative record on which the order of removal is based." *Jimenez* distinguishes between reviewing the BIA’s factual findings and applying legal standards. While the Court notes *Jimenez* does not use the phrase "de novo" in this context, the substantial-evidence requirement effectively ensures that the appellate court cannot independently evaluate the weight of evidence based on the petitioner's opinion. Instead, the Court asks if "substantial evidence supports the BIA" in denying the claim.

Consequently, the substantial evidence standard prevents the court from relying on the petitioner's own opinion to decide the case if that opinion lacks "substantial evidence in the record supporting" the BIA's decision. The Court denied relief because "there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA's findings that Petitioners do not qualify" and because petitioners could not show their political opinion was "at least one central reason for the infliction of harm."

**Conclusion**

In summary, *Jimenez* applies the substantial-evidence standard to the BIA's factual findings, holding them "conclusive" absent compelling evidence to the contrary. This prevents the appellate court from deciding the case based on the petitioner's subjective opinion of the evidence, requiring instead that the administrative record support the conclusion that the petitioner has failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution.

### Why does the failure to meet Asylum standards automatically result in a denial of Withholding of Removal in this case?
Question:
The opinion concludes that because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear under asylum standards, they necessarily fail the higher standard for withholding of removal. If Withholding holds a higher standard of protection, why isn't the court required to independently review the evidence for Withholding or simply deny for lack of evidence rather than relying on the Asylum failure?
Answer:
### Asylum and Withholding Have Identical Core Requirements
Because the legal standard for asylum claims and withholding of removal requires the same substantive factual findings regarding the existence of a "well-founded fear of persecution" and the requisite statutory nexus, the failure to meet the asylum standard necessarily precludes the grant of withholding.

Despite the grant of relief under withholding often being considered mandatory and the remedy under asylum being discretionary, the **Tenth Circuit** has relied on the logic that if a petitioner cannot satisfy the identical elements required for asylum, they cannot theoretically satisfy the "higher standard" for withholding. As the Court explicitly states in the Conclusion at **Section III**, "Because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal."

The Court relies on **Section C and Section D** of the opinion to justify this conclusion without conducting a separate, independent factual inquiry for withholding. 

1.  **Shared Factual Requirements:** Both asylum and withholding of removal require the petitioner to establish (a) an incident of persecution, (b) a nexus to a statutorily protected ground (e.g., political opinion), and (c) the government's inability or unwillingness to protect. The Court finds substantial evidence in the record that the Petitioners failed on these exact facts (e.g., **Section D** finding that the "political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm").
2.  **Logical Necessity:** If the evidence does not support a well-founded fear of persecution sufficient to meet the *lower* threshold of asylum (which involves a broader assessment of internal relocation and the government response), the same evidence cannot meet the *higher* threshold of withholding.
3.  **Judicial Efficiency:** By relying on the existing asylum findings, the Court provides a sound, evidence-based rationale for the denial. As the Court cites in **Section III**, this deference to the asylum findings is grounded in the principle that failure to meet the "well-founded fear" threshold for asylum is legally dispositive when the shared statutory elements are not fulfilled.

Therefore, the Court is not required to conduct a fresh, independent review of the withholding evidence because the sufficiency of the record regarding the "well-founded fear" and the "nexus to protected grounds" was already adjudicated as insufficient for asylum. Since "Withholding... [is a] higher standard," the failure to establish the elements for the lower standard means the elements for the higher standard are also fabricated or nonexistent.
