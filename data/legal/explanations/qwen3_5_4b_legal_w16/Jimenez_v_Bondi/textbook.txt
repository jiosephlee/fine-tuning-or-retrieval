Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Factual Background and Procedural History

## Petitioners' Profile: Surname, Origin, Immigration Status, and Cultural Context (Colombian Liberal Party Members)

To understand the strength or weakness of an asylum claim, the first step is to establish the petitioner's national affiliation and the statutory protection grounds implicated. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the petitioners are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, along with their minor sons. Although the opinion does not emphasize their specific socioeconomic class, the text identifies them as natives and citizens of Colombia who filed for asylum and withholding of removal protection under the Convention Against Torture. The most critical aspect of their profile for legal analysis is their political affiliation; collectively, the family served as members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012. This affiliation is not merely passive candidacy support but active campaign work, including door-to-door participation for a rival’s candidate in the 2022 Colombian presidential election. For immigration judges and appellate courts, this political involvement establishes the "nexus" requirement—a legal mandate that persecution be tied to one of the five statutorily protected grounds, such as race, religion, nationality, particular social group, or political opinion. If the harm suffered was merely due to gang violence unrelated to their personal beliefs, the eligibility for protection under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) would be significantly weakened. In this case, the court notes that the petitioners alleged persecution was grounded in their political opinion. However, the court later found this element weak. For a law student, the key takeaway from this section is that a petitioner's identity is the primary vehicle for satisfying the first element of an asylum eligibility inquiry: membership in a group protected by statute.

## Factual Foundation: Specific Incidents Constituting Alleged Persecution (Threats, Kidnapping, Property Dispute)

The factual pattern supporting the asylum claims relies heavily on the cumulative effect of events rather than isolated incidents, though the court notes the distinction between threats and physical persecution. The petitioners allege a timeline of intimidation beginning around May 2022, where Jimenez received threatening phone calls regarding his status as a Liberal Party supporter. While the petitioner did not claim to be kidnapped or assaulted by the government itself, the "colleague was kidnapped and murdered" incident served as a particularly severe leap into the asylum claim's "past persecution" prong. However, the most factually specific entry point for the court's analysis was not the political campaigning itself, but a subsequent property dispute. Jimenez, who held a provisional law license, became involved in a dispute regarding a property owned by an illegal flyer who was ousted after unlawful divorce settlements and later referenced in casework related to narcotrafficking and prostitution groups. He discovered the tenant, referred to as "Don Rafa" or Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, was considered a leader of a narco-trafficking group allied with President Gustavo Petro.

The court emphasizes that while political themes pervaded the threat narrative, the precipitating event for the escalated violence was Jimenez's investigation into the property. After Jimenez's initial visit to the property, where he observed seals caused by health authorities and found the leak prompt cause for searches and seizures attempts, he learned the ex-husband of the client was Don Rafa. This investigation led to threats such as the receipt of a brochure stating “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” followed by men holding Jimenez at gunpoint. Crucially, the petitioners stuck to the theory that the harm was political, but the court observed the timeline: victims were undeterred despite threats until Jimenez's specific investigation of the property compromised his enemies' security. This distinction is vital for the nexus analysis; the threat of "snitching" implies a professional retaliation more aligned with a criminal investigation than pure political dissent. The court noted that the threats were not merely collateral but contingent upon Jimenez's specific interaction with the property owner and his role in a potential search or seizure action. Therefore, the factual record needed to support the claim of persecution must demonstrate that the petitioner suffered harm that was not merely dangerous but evident to constitute the statutory definition of persecution, which requires suffering or harm beyond mere restrictions or threats to life.

## Procedural Posture: Jurisdiction, Compatibility of Claims (Asylum, Waiving Withholding), and Details of Administrative Orders (DJ, BIA, Court)

Understanding the procedural history is essential for understanding *Jimenez v. Bondi*'s jurisdictional rules regarding immigration appeals. The petitioners first arrived in the United States in December 2022, and three months later, they were served with Notices to Appear by Department of Homeland Security. The Immigration Judge (IJ), which operated under Title II of the INA, became the first administrative body to adjudicate the case, though the petitioners waived the constitutional claim regarding the right to counsel. When the IJ denied the asylum claim, the petitioners had to appeal this agency decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the second level of administrative adjudication. The BIA affirmed the IJ's decision and dismissed the appeal regarding the denial of protection under the Convention Against Torture. The dispute was then filed in the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit.

This procedural posture triggers specific judicial review standards that are defined by the INA and controlling precedent like *Singh v. Bondi*. Specifically, under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, the court exercises jurisdiction to review final orders of removal. However, this is not an appeal as of right; it is a discretionary grant of jurisdiction based on final orders of removal in the Administrative Tribunal. The court's review is limited to the administrative record, meaning the court cannot consider new evidence or new arguments that were not raised in the IJ or BIA proceedings. Furthermore, the court clarified it is reviewing the BIA's decision, but is not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of grounds, as reflected in *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*. This distinction matters for legal students as it indicates how much weight the appellate court gives to the lower court vs. the agency's factual findings, which the court directly controlled the judgment in this instance to conclude that the petitioners failed to demonstrate that they suffered persecution.

## Evidence of the Government's Response: Police Interaction and Lack of Assistance (The 'Unwilling or Unable' Threshold)

For asylum applicants, satisfying the "unable or unwilling" prong is often the distinction between protected status and removal. This standard requires the petitioner to show the state is incapable of, or unwilling to, provide protection. In *Jimenez*, the factual record of this threshold is central to the court's denial. The petitioner argued that the Colombian government was incapable of or unable to stop the threats because the persecutors were closely associated with the government. The court noted that this argument requires the petitioner to show the government either stopped the persecutors or placed the applicant in protective custody, which did not occur in this case. The pivotal evidence for the "unable or unwilling" standard lies in the interaction between the petitioner and state law enforcement. When Jimenez reported the incidents to a police patrol officer, the officer stated nothing could be done and told the family they should leave the country.

While the court acknowledged this suggests police apathy, legal precedent such as *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* indicates that one-off interactions with unhelpful officers do not necessarily prove the entire government is unable or unwilling to protect its citizens. The court emphasized that without evidence that the government attempted to stop the persecutors upon receiving notice, the claim of state inability relies on the petitioner's desire to react. The petitioners also claimed the police were contributing to the problem, but that alone failed to meet the statutory requirement. The court noted that the applicant may satisfy the government protection requirement by showing that reporting would have been futile, but they failed to demonstrate this beyond the lack of response. Thus, in the final analysis, this factual history supports the court's conclusion that there was no official attempt by the state to control the alleged persecutors, making the asylum claim legally insufficient despite the tension the family faced.

# Governing Law and Standard of Review

## Statutory Definition of a Refugee (INA ²07(a)(A)): Protected Grounds and Fear of Persecution

To understand the legal framework the Tenth Circuit applied in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the first step is identifying the statutory definition of a refugee. While the prompt outline references a citation that appears to contain a formatting artifact, the opinion text explicitly provides the controlling statutory authority found in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). Under this statutory definition, an applicant for asylum must be a person outside their country who is unable and unwilling to return to or avail themselves of the protection of their country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, this definition serves as the boilerplate for the court's review of the petitioners' claims. Petitioners, citizens of Colombia, are located within the United States and are currently nationals of Colombia. They argue that they cannot return to Colombia due to their membership in the Colombian Liberal Party (political opinion). The Tenth Circuit must determine whether this falls within the protected grounds listed in § 1101(a)(42)(A). As the *Jimenez* opinion explains, "Petitioners appeal the BIA's decision denying their applications for asylum and withholding of removal... arguing that the BIA erred by finding that they did not establish... a nexus to a statutorily protected ground." Thus, whether the petitioner is "unable or unwilling to return" because of one of these five statutory grounds is the threshold question for asylum eligibility.

## The Past Persecution Presumption (8 USC § 1158(b)(1)(B)) and its Rebuttable Nature

A core component of the legal framework in *Jimenez* is the presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution that arises when an applicant demonstrates past persecution. While past persecution is not a strict requirement for all asylum seekers, 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B) allows an applicant to obtain refugee status through a "showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution." In *Jimenez*, the petitioners failed to secure this presumption.

The Legal Framework section of the opinion outlines that to establish a showing of past persecution, the applicant must prove three elements: "(1) an incident, or incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control." This is not a standard of proof merely restating facts, but a structured inquiry where the court applies the code to the adjudicative record.

In *Jimenez*, the BIA and the Immigration Judge (IJ) found that while there were incidents of mistreatment (threats, surveillance), they did not meet the statutory requirements for past persecution. The opinion emphasizes that physical harm is not always necessary, but the harm inflicted must be "offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." Specifically, in *Jimenez*, the petitioners conceded they had no lasting physical harm. The court explained that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution" and that emotional or psychological harm stemming from received threats is typically insufficient to rise to the level of persecution unless supported by more extreme factual patterns than the requests.

## Standard of Review for Factual Findings (Substantial Evidence) under INA § 1252(b)(4)

Following the statutory definition, the appellate court must apply its standard of review to the BIA's decision to deny asylum. The opinion states that the court "reviews the BIA's findings of fact under a substantial-evidence standard." As defined in the text, under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive "unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This standard creates a high barrier for the appellate court to overturn the BIA. Furthermore, because a review of the asylum claim is governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4), the appellate court's scope is limited to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based."

This standard of review is particularly significant in *Jimenez* because it limits the court's ability to reweigh evidence. The opinion explicitly notes that "we have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone," resulting in the denial here. The court must decide if the petitioner has demonstrated that any reasonable adjudicator would reject the BIA's conclusion that past persecution was a necessary element. Therefore, the appellate court does not act as a fact finder but as a judicial functionary to determine if the record supports the BIA's findings.

## Scope of Judicial Review: Authority to Review BIA Decisions Despite BIA Member Composition

A unique procedural posture in *Jimenez v. Bondi* involves the composition of the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The procedural background indicates that the BIA rejected the petitioners' appeal, but the method of affirmation is relevant to the appellate court's jurisdiction. The court held that "Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, we review the BIA’s decision, but 'we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.'"

While the BIA was constituted to affirm an order of removal, the Tenth Circuit retains jurisdiction to review that decision because it affirming an order. This explains why the court could review the petition for review and apply the standards of review described in this chapter. The court exercised jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9), providing that the Administrative Review Board's order of removal is a final order subject to judicial review. This ensures that the appellate court can resolve whether the BIA's single member's affirmation was supported by theAdministrative Record.

The opinion references *Uanreroro v. Gonzales* to support the authority to review the BIA decision despite the unique composition of the board. This confirms that the appellate court must treat the final BIA decision as the subject of review, even if the adjudication timeline extended to "approximately three years." The court must decide if the BIA's decision to deny asylum was a reasonable application of the standard of review established in the previous sections.

# Analysis of Past Persecution and Nexus to Protected Grounds

## Threshold of Persecution: Requirement of Harm Beyond Threats Alone (Karki, Wiransane, Ritonga)
To succeed on an asylum claim, petitioners must establish a prima facie case of persecution. While the Immigration and Nationality Act (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)) defines a refugee, it leaves the specific elements of persecution undefined, leading the Fifth Circuit and other circuits developed particular tests that the Tenth Circuit applies. In *Wiransane v. Ashcroft* , 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004), the court held that a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in a protected ground in a way regarded as offensive. Crucially, this harm must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty. While threats are relevant in establishing the cumulative impact of mistreatment, the court has consistently ruled that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution. *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013) specifically notes that while an applicant may claim emotional distress or psychological harm resulting from threats, the harm must rise above mere restrictions or isolated threats to meet the statutory threshold. When evaluating these claims, the court considers the frequency and severity of the mistreatment rather than disparate incidents in isolation. *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) established that the defendant’s coach must consider incidents collectively, as the cumulative effects may indeed constitute persecution. However, this analysis does not transform every stressful or stressful event into persecution. In *Jimenez v. Bondi* , the petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed at their merits hearing, yet remained incarcerated. The court noted that even though physical harm is not strictly necessary to satisfy the persecution prong, the suffering must be offensive and far exceed sporadic threats. The court emphasized that unless the suffering or harm is offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats, the claim fails. *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)).

## Nature of Harm: Physical vs. Psychological Consequences and Cumulative Assessment
Discussions within the Tenth Circuit distinguish between physical harm and psychological consequences arising from those harms. While physical harm is not a necessary element of an asylum claim—even physical stress is not sufficient to rise to the level of persecution—the courts have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Petitioners argued that they endured unfortunate circumstances regarding threats, but the court found that such fears did not rise to the level of persecution. The court noted that although Duarte suffered from an inability to sleep and anxiety, and their son experienced panic attacks, the court held that these fears stemming from received threats were insufficient on their own. *Karki* also clarified that while physical harm is helpful, an applicant’s alleged harm must be more than merely stressful or psychologically unsettling. The court cited cases where threats were too contingent or vague to constitute persecution, noting that applicants who allege harm primarily resulting from emotional distress or psychological stress are often denied. *Chhetri v. Rosen*, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) illustrates this by rejecting a claim involving telephone threats and vague in-person threats where the harm was not severe enough to constitute truly spanning persecution. In *Jimenez*, the court found that the evidence supported the Board of Immigration Appeals’ finding that Petitioners’ harm, even in the aggregate, did not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA. *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) further supports that threats contingent on compliance with demands are insufficient. Thus, the Petitioners’ emotional trauma and threat exposure were in this specific view insufficient to satisfy the statutory requirement of persecution.

## The Nexus Requirement: Mainstream vs. Incidental Motive (Rivera-Barrientos, Dallakoti, Yuk)
Even when suffering meets the definition of persecution, petitioners must connect that harm to a statutorily protected ground, such as political opinion. Establishing a nexus requires the applicant to prove that the persecution was committed on account of a central reason that motivated the persecutor. According to *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder* , a nexus requires the applicant to establish that the political opinion was at least one central reason for the persecution. This means the applicant’s political opinion cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm. *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010) clarifies that the protected ground must play a minor role in the petitioner's past treatment or fear of future mistreatment. In cases where multiple motives exist, *O.C.V. v. Bondi* , No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025) holds that the persecutor must be motivated by at least one protected ground that is a central reason for the inflicted harm. However, the court in *Jimenez v. Bondi* noted that it is not the court’s prerogative to reweigh the evidence. *Yuk v. Ashcroft* , 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) reiterates that the court's role is sufficient to determine if substantial evidence supports the BIA's decision regarding the nexus. If the protected ground is secondary to a non-protected reason, such as property disputes, it does not meet the nexus requirement for asylum relief. *Rivera-Barrientos* emphasizes that the nexus is a distinct element of the asylum claim that must be proven through substantial evidence.

## Application to Petitioners: Why 'Don Rafa' and Property Disputes Override Political Campaign Activities
The Petitioners in *Jimenez* failed to meet the nexus requirement because the court found that the persecutors' primary motive was Jimenez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa rather than his shared political opinion. The Petitioners argued that they were persecuted because of their political opinion as Liberal Party members. However, the record indicates that Petitioners were undeterred by threatening phone calls regarding their political campaign, even after a colleague was kidnapped and murdered, and continued their campaign activities. *Rivera-Barrientos* requires showing that the political opinion was at least one central reason for the harm. In contrast, Petitioners began receiving escalated threats and harassment only after Jimenez became involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa, an aligned leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas. One of the attackers accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and being responsible for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. The court found that Jimenez’s identity as a politician was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm. Petitioners also failed to establish that Don Rafa would target their family’s political activities, noting that Don Rafa explicitly targeted them after finding their involvement in a property dispute. The court concluded that since the persecutors were targeted for professional reasons (investigating property and implicating them in vandalism) rather than political opinion, the nexus requirement was not satisfied. *Singh* homeless the requirement that the petitioner cannot fail to demonstrate that a protected ground was a central reason for the harm. Since the primary motive was the protection of Jimenez’s own family from financial and physical harm due to a property dispute, the political amateur status of the family did not trigger the political opinion protection.

# Government Protection, Safe Relocation, and Final Disposition

## Chapter Description

This chapter concludes the opinion by analyzing the final prongs necessary for asylum: the Colombian government's actual willingness and ability to protect Petitioners, and the feasibility of safe internal relocation. It culminates in the court's holding and denial of relief.

## Government Protection Standard: 'Unable or Unwilling' Analysis (Systemic vs. Individual Evidence)

To qualify for asylum, Petitioners must demonstrate a well-founded fear that foreign nationals in their country of prosecution are unable or unwilling to protect them from persecution. In this case, the court analyzes this standard under Section II.E, emphasizing that the government cannot stop the government from responding to known events or failing to stop them without proof of systemic inability. The court distinguishes between individual failures and systemic failures when evaluating a government's reaction. While a single unhelpful interaction with law enforcement might suggest apathy, it is not sufficient to prove that the government is unable or unwilling to protect a victim unless there is systemic evidence.

The court looks at whether the government stopped the persecutors or offered protection, such as through protective custody, Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859-60 (10th Cir. 2025). A crucial part of this inquiry is whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond. The record shows that Petitioners reported the threats to a police officer. However, the officer told them that police were contributing to the problem and urged them to leave, but nothing else is on record. The court cautions that a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government is unable or unwilling to control the persecutors, but this is not necessarily fatal if the applicant demonstrates that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.

The court rejects the Petitioners' argument that municipal aid could have been sought on the grounds that the government was unaware of the property's involvement. The government was aware of the property and had attempted several searches and seizures, meaning the evidence counters the argument that the private persecutors were aligned with the government to the extent that the government could not act. The court notes that a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that the government is "unable or unwilling" to protect its people. While Petitioners claimed that government contacts were associated with the persecutors, the court found that in such cases, the applicant must show that filing a police report would have been futile, which the Petitioners did not sufficiently demonstrate.

## Internal Relocation Availability: Assessment of Threat Scope (Neighborhood vs. Country) and Family Ties

Even if Petitioners had failed to establish the requirements for asylum, the court addressed the feasibility of internal relocation as a potential rebuttal to the well-founded fear of persecution. The government may rebut an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and prevent his asylum application if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that there has been a fundamental change and the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant's country. The standard for this is distinct from the burden of proof required for the initial claim.

The court finds that the record supports the finding that Petitioners could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia. Petitioners' family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed, and Petitioners' appointee Jimenez's father still works for the criminal justice system there. Notably, Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks following an incident involving threats, where they decided not to leave until after that time. The court emphasizes that the threat scope was primarily neighborhood-specific; the men who threatened the Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood but did not threaten the family to move out of the country. The threat to the body of the family members was specific to the local area, not the entire nation.

The court holds Petitioners cannot mitigate potential future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia because the threats were contained to their original neighborhood. The court found that the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety as the relatives live, but the Petitioners did not need to show this because the initial claim was already failed based on subjective reasons. However, even if the applicants claimed they were safe elsewhere, the court found relocation feasible because the threats were not country-wide.

## Symbolic vs. Realistic Threats: The Insufficiency of Hypothetical Hardship

Petitioners attempted to broaden their asylum claim by arguing that relocation would be unworkable due to hypothetical hardships, such as administrative burdens, economic costs, and the fact that their persecutors had ties to the president of Colombia. The court treats these arguments as symbolic rather than realistic. While the Petitioners' proposal of hypothetical hardships is noted, the court cannot grant asylum based on such hypothetical hardships. The court explains that these arguments do not pass muster because the claimants rely on the absence of evidence rather than a direct link to the actual risk of harm.

The Petitioners assert that family in the home country are safe, but argue that their relatives may also have political opinions against the government. However, the court rejects the reliance on such hypotheses, noting that they only triggered a broad but inconclusive explanation. The court states that when relocation is questioned, the Petitioners must show actual barriers, not just potential administrative hurdles. The court notes that the administrative and economic burdens of the country or the need for judicial infrastructure are not sufficient grounds to deny the relocation presumption if the primary threat has already been deemed internal and not systemic.

By finding no legitimate evidence of future persecution and no valid evidence that relocation is infeasible, the court concludes that the Petitioners' arguments regarding relocation are insufficient to establish a credible fear. The court re-emphasizes that the evidence must show that the current fear is based on the reality that the persecutors will stop the applicant from returning to the country, which is not supported here. Therefore, the court views the relocation arguments as not supporting a well-founded fear of persecution.

## Holding: Conclusion on Denial of Petition for Review and No Error Found

In sum, the court concludes that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA's findings that the Petitioners do not qualify for asylum. The court reiterates that because the Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under the applicable standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. The court found that none of the Petitioners' evidence, regarding the nature of the threats or the feasibility of relocation, overcome the initial findings of no persecution.

For the foregoing reasons, the court denies the petition for review. The court held that there was no error found by the Board of Immigration Appeals in denying the Petitioners relief based on the unavailability of internal relocation and the lack of credible fear of persecution despite the threats. The court ultimately dismisses all claims by the Petitioners, affirming the BIA's decision that the evidence is insufficient to change their statutory protections for asylum.
