Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What is the difference between De Novo review and Substantial Evidence review for the Tenth Circuit when reviewing BIA asylum denials?
Question:
I see the court mentions they review the BIA's findings of fact under the 'substantial-evidence standard' and are 'conclusive' unless compelling evidence exists. How does this relate to the idea that 'Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear'? Does the substantial evidence standard prevent the court from deciding the case based on the Petitioner's own opinion of the evidence?
Answer:
**Substantial Evidence Standard in *Jimenez v. Bondi***

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Circuit (2025), the Court clarifies its opinion on BIA asylum denials primarily through the application of the **substantial-evidence standard**, specifically regarding the BIA’s **findings of fact**. The opinion explicitly holds that under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." *Jimenez* supports this explanation, stating: "We review the BIA's findings of fact under a substantial-evidence standard."

This distinction prevents the appellate court from substituting its own judgment for that of the BIA on factual matters, thereby limiting reliance solely on the petitioner’s subjective testimony if it is unsupported by the administrative record. The opinions discusses in *Jimenez* explicitly prohibit the court from reweighing evidence, stating: "It is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ's [or the BIA's] decision."

**Application to Petitioner's "Well-founded Fear" and Opinion**

The standard governs the petitioner's claim that they fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution. As the *Jimenez* opinion explains, the petitioner bears the burden of proving eligibility "by a preponderance of the evidence." 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Because the Court applies the substantial-evidence standard to the factual findings, the petitioner cannot simply assert their own opinion of the guilt or safety risks without corresponding support in the record.

In Section II.C, the Court found that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution" because, while physical harm is not necessary, the harm must be "offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats." The Court emphasized that "Petitioners' fail to establish a well-founded fear" was a factual question determined by whether the record contained substantial evidence for the BIA’s conclusion. The court did not grant relief based on the petitioner’s opinion of the evidence, but rather on whether the administrative record supported the BIA’s refusal to recognize the threats as persecution.

**Standard of Review and Limitations**

The opinion reinforces this by limiting the scope of review to the "administrative record on which the order of removal is based." *Jimenez* distinguishes between reviewing the BIA’s factual findings and applying legal standards. While the Court notes *Jimenez* does not use the phrase "de novo" in this context, the substantial-evidence requirement effectively ensures that the appellate court cannot independently evaluate the weight of evidence based on the petitioner's opinion. Instead, the Court asks if "substantial evidence supports the BIA" in denying the claim.

Consequently, the substantial evidence standard prevents the court from relying on the petitioner's own opinion to decide the case if that opinion lacks "substantial evidence in the record supporting" the BIA's decision. The Court denied relief because "there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA's findings that Petitioners do not qualify" and because petitioners could not show their political opinion was "at least one central reason for the infliction of harm."

**Conclusion**

In summary, *Jimenez* applies the substantial-evidence standard to the BIA's factual findings, holding them "conclusive" absent compelling evidence to the contrary. This prevents the appellate court from deciding the case based on the petitioner's subjective opinion of the evidence, requiring instead that the administrative record support the conclusion that the petitioner has failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution.
