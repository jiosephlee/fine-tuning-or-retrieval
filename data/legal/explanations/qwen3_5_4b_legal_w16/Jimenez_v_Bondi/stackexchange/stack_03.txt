Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Does a Petitioner's status as a government lawyer or National Prosecutor office employee affect the Government Unwilling/Unable analysis?
Question:
The opinion highlights that Petitioners worked for the government而在 the charge of reporting. The court notes that 'Without such reporting, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted.' If a Petitioner has a strong professional connection to the state, does that connection actually help them establish the government is 'unwilling' or 'unable' to help them protect the rest of the family?
Answer:
**Does a Petitioner's Government Affiliation Affect the "Unable/Unwilling" Standard?**

Based on *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025 (No. 24-9573), a Petitioner’s professional connection to the state (such as being a government lawyer or Prosecutor office employee) does **not** automatically help establish the government is "unable" or "unwilling" to provide protection. In fact, the court indicates that applicants with this status may be expected to have greater access through their government-employment channels, and their failure to utilize those channels can actually undercut their claim of a well-founded fear of future persecution.

### The Government's Duty and the Petitioner's Burden
The Tenth Circuit applies the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis to determine if an applicant qualifies for asylum or withholding of removal. As the opinion states, this inquiry requires "evidence about a government's response to an applicant's past persecution."

*Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025) holds the government's reaction and the availability of protection for the applicant are the central factors. Specifically, the court notes that the analysis hinges on whether the applicant "reported past persecution to the government." *Id.* (citing *Singh*). While the court acknowledges that the inability to report due to fear can satisfy the threshold, the core element remains: "Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." *Jimenez*, 24-9573, at 13 (quoting *Singh*).

This duty of notice places a particular onus on the Petitioner. They must show the government *could* have reacted before retreating or pleading the case impossible.

### The Impact of Professional Connection
In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners (specifically the father) were employees or interns for the government system in Colombia.

The opinion explicitly notes that because the Petitioners worked for the government, their failure to disclose wrongdoing is more relevant to the "notice" standard:
> "Petitioners' efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any. Jimenez and his father both worked or currently work for the government in Colombia. Yet, neither of them reported wrongdoing or corruption." *Jimenez*, 24-9573, at 13.

The court uses this fact to support the BIA's finding that the Petitioners had access to reporting mechanisms through their employment. When the Petitioners testified they were advised by a police officer that "nothing could be done" and that they "should leave," the court rejected the suggestion that this single interaction proved systemic inability.

*Jimenez* quoted *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th 738 (7th Cir. 2023) to emphasize:
> "Although police apathy can indicate a government's unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim."

### Conclusion on the Petitioners' Connection
The professional connection does not help the Petitioners establish that the state was unable to protect the family. The court reasoned:

1.  **Reporting Requirement:** The Petitioners failed to report threats or misconduct effectively until the last moments. Since reporting was the key trigger for the government's chance to act, the lack of reporting prevented the Petitioners from proving the state was "unable" to react.
2.  **Evidence of Capability:** The record showed the government had already attempted searches and seizures on the locations linked to the threat. The opinion states this "evidence counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government."
3.  **One-Off vs. Systemic:** An officer's advice to "stay away" does not automatically equate to a government-wide inability. "The evidence counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government." *Jimenez*, 24-9573, at 13.

Therefore, under the analysis in *Jimenez*, a Petitioner's government affiliation actually highlights the requirement for them to show they tried to report their claims. Without effective reporting, the connection does not translate to a state of "unable/unwilling" government protection.
