Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 3: # Analytical Application to Consent and Scope

## Determination of Voluntariness: Assessing 'Need' Language and Tone (United States v. Hicks)

When analyzing a motion to suppress based on consent, the appellate court's primary task is to determine whether the consent was "freely and voluntarily" given. While the Seventh Circuit recognizes that consent does not need to follow a formal legal warning, the voluntariness of consent is always judged by the totality of the circumstances. In this opinion, the challenge focuses not on the existence of consent, but on its quality under the specific language used during the exchange at the threshold. Specifically, Defendant raises the issue of Officer Kizer using the phrase “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside.” Some might argue that the use of “need” implies an assertion of power that negates voluntariness. However, the court analyzes this within the framework of the factors established in *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566 (7th Cir. 2008). The court emphasizes that courts must evaluate whether the suggestion of necessity or urgency coarsely infringes upon a suspect's right to refuse. In *Coleman*, the court acknowledges this is a "plenty fair argument" for Coleman, but rests the determination on the broader context. The court Reasoning follows that while the defendant’s reaction to the word “need” must be considered, it is insufficient to render consent involuntary if the other circumstances do not support a finding of coercion. This highlights the importance of reading the consent challenge as a holistic inquiry rather than a linguistic one, requiring students to look beyond fragmented phrases to determine if the “need” was perceived as an ultimatum or a statement of urgent concern.

## Assessment of Police Demeanor and Absence of Coercion

The appellate court’s reasoning relies heavily on the visual evidence of the interaction found in the body camera footage. The standard for voluntariness is objective: it asks whether a reasonable person would feel coerced by the circumstances. In *Coleman*, the district court found the police officer’s tone was one of concern rather than control. The preliminary facts show that the officer paused for a specific period of time, indicating the nature of the consent was willing rather than compelled. A key element of this analysis is establishing whether the defendant was in police custody when consent was given. While Officer Kizer asked questions and asked for permission, the court notes that Lisa was never in custody. When evaluating the absence of coercion, the appellate court highlights that even repeated requests for permission do not violate voluntariness if they remain unaccompanied by overt threats or intimidation. Here, the officer asked twice; he withdrew a request implied by stating "need to come inside." The court reasons that while *Coleman* is entitled to rely on the video record, which he saw on the record, the video itself confirmed that Lisa's willingness to answer subsequent questions did not indicate she was suffocated under the state. The action of her consenting, even if pressured by societal duty to assist a report, remains valid if she retains the ability to say no. The analysis here teaches that the court must look for the absence of the "paddy wagon" effect—where submission becomes automatic under custody.

## Scope Analysis: Legitimate Purpose of Safety Checks and Locating Suspects

Even if consent is voluntary, the Fourth Amendment limits the government to a reasonable scope of that permission. The standard of review is based on an "objective reasonableness" inquiry: what would a typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent (*Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991)). In *Coleman*, the triggering question was whether the officers exceeded the scope of the consent given during the initial safety entry. The officers' purpose was to check on the welfare of the children and determine if Coleman posed a danger. The court reasoned that scanning the home, locating everyone inside, and asking basic security questions were intrinsic to the ability to fulfill an initial safety check. Because the specific area and items searched—living areas where the children and father were located—were the locus of the consent, the search was within the scope of consent. This section distinguishes between the initial entry scope and subsequent actions. While the officers eventually obtained a warrant for a full search, the initial closure of the entries (the firearms) occurred without warrant because they fell within the scope of the consent to check the safety of the children. The court explicitly notes that it does not need to consider exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery because the consent holding resolves the suppression challenge in favor of the Government. This illustrates to the student how the scope of consent is defined not by the specific items requested, but by the reasonable inference of the entry's purpose from the individual's perspective.
