Chapter 2: # Governing Legal Standards for Fourth Amendment Consent

## Consent as a Fourth Amendment Exception to Warrant Requirements

The Fourth Amendment generally requires law enforcement to obtain a warrant before violating a citizen's right to be free from unlawful search and seizure. However, the Seventh Circuit recognizes that the Fourth Amendment is not the only path to entry. *United States v. Banks*, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023). This court acknowledges that consent is a recognized exception that permits officers to enter a home without a warrant. This is consistent with landmark precedent and case law agreed upon by both sides in *Coleman*. As noted in *United States v. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973), a search is valid if conducted under the consent of a person authorized to consent. In *Coleman*, the defense argued that the Grande consent given by Lisa was invalid, specifically that officer communication was coercive, but the court applied the established legal principles that consent serves as the primary exception to the warrant requirement when entering a home to check on the welfare of family members or residents.

## Authority for Third-Party Consent (United States v. Matlock)

A critical legal threshold in consent cases is the source of the consent required to enter a property or home. In *Coleman*, the defense asserted that the wife, Lisa, was not a defendant and therefore her consent should not bind the federal defendant for Fourth Amendment purposes, though the opinion clarifies otherwise. Consent need not come from the defendant, but from a third party who has lawful access to the property. This is based on the holding in *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974). The Seventh Circuit further explains this in *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing *Matlock*), stating that a third party may give consent to search a place in which both the third party and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy. In this case, Lisa Coleman had the authority to consent to the police entry within the scope of the consent, even though Coleman was the one charged with the firearm possession in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). This distinction is significant for entry, as the police sought to secure that authority before entering the home, and the district court ultimately found the consent valid.

## The 'Totality of Circumstances' Test for Voluntariness (Bumper)

Once consent is sought, the court must determine whether that consent was voluntary. *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, 24-3051 (7th Cir. 2025)*. The governing standard under the Fourth Amendment is that consent must be freely and voluntarily given. *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). To determine voluntariness, the Seventh Circuit requires an evaluation of the totality of the circumstances. *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022). This standard involves looking at all relevant aspects of the situation rather than relying on any single factor, with courts assessing whether coercion was present.

In applying this standard, courts often consider specific factors as outlined in *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing *United States v. Santiago*, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). These factors include the person's age, intelligence, and education; whether the individual was advised of his constitutional rights; how long the individual was detained before consent was given; whether the consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; whether any physical coercion was used; and whether the individual was in police custody when consent was given. In *Coleman*, the court noted that Officer Kizer did not ask for permission in a coercive manner, but that the specific tone and demeanor of the officer was that of someone concerned for safety rather than someone demanding entry. Furthermore, because Lisa had the ability to deny consent by refusing the initial request and became in a state of concern, her consent remained voluntary despite Officer Kizer asking repeatedly.

## Objective Reasonableness Standard for Scope of Consent (Florida v. Jimeno)

Beyond the voluntariness of consent, a law enforcement entry requires consideration of the scope of consent to determine if the subsequent search was authorized. Under *United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012), once consent is established, the search must respect the scope of that consent. The proper inquiry is not whether the goal was the same, but whether the scope exceeded the boundaries of permission given. *United States v. Jackson*, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010). The standard applied is one of objective reasonableness, asking what a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent. *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991).

In *Coleman*, the district court did not find that the officers exceeded the scope of Lisa's permission. The officers' stated purpose was to check the welfare of her children after receiving information that the dweller enjoyed being спокойный. Finding Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. Doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions. In this instance, the scope of the consent was not violated because locating Coleman required locating all occupants to determine if they were compromised during a family violence situation. The opinion concludes that these conclusions eliminate any need to consider the government's alternative contention that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the police's entry into and initial sweep of the Coleman residence. The court reviews for clear error, finding that the district court did not commit an error in finding that Lisa consented to the police entry and that the officers conducted the search within the scope of that authority.
