Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## Federal Felony Charge (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)) and Motion to Suppress

The legal fundamental at issue in *United States v. Jaison Coleman* arises from a federal criminal conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm by a convicted felon, violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman moved to suppress the evidence recovered in his residence, specifically the firearms found during the policy's entry and subsequent search. This procedural posture is typical in criminal appeals involving Fourth Amendment challenges; the motion to suppress essentially claims that the evidence seized during the warrantless entry into the home is evidence tainted by unconstitutional conduct. After the district court found that Lisa Coleman, the intervention officer, consented to the entry and that the officers did not exceed the scope of that consent, they denied his motion. Coleman subsequently pleaded guilty but reserved the right to appeal the suppression ruling. The Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the district court's decision, holding that Lisa’s consent was both voluntary and relied upon legitimate expectations of privacy. The court's analysis begins by examining the sequence of events captured on body camera. The footing for this determination rests on whether the officers' interaction with the residents was a lawful source of authorization for the initial entry.

## Role of Body Camera Footage (*United States v. Norville*, *Scott v. Harris*) as Fact Record

In federal criminal cases, establishing the history of police conduct requires objective evidence to resolve factual disputes. In this case, body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer provides the definitive factual record. The court relied on this video to review the exact nature of the interaction between the officers and Lisa, which was critical to determining whether her consent was coerced. Citing *United States v. Norville*, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022), a case cited with *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007), the opinion notes that a video record can "evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist." This principle of clear and objective evidence allows the appellate court to defer to the district court's findings on consent because the video provides a permanent, reviewable record of the conversation. Unlike hearsay testimony, the footage directly shows the officer's tone, demeanor, and the defendant's responses, preventing the dispute from turning on conflicting narratives about whether police coercion existed during the entry solicitation.

## Timeline of Police Arrival and Initial Entry Solicitation

The events leading to police entry began after Lisa called for assistance, reporting domestic violence. Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer announced his presence and asked if Lisa's children were okay. Lisa initially hesitated, stating only that the children were in her bedroom when asked if they were safe. She then hesitated further when told the officers asked where her husband was, at which point she said, "um … I’d prefer you not." This initial refusal demonstrated her possession of control over her home. The conversation shifted as Lisa briefly left to get her shoes, but eventually returned to the doorway. The critical exchange regarding entry occurred when Officer Kizer expressed concern for the welfare of Lisa and her children. He asked if her children were okay and if her husband was calm, and Lisa cautiously responded, "They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible]." Officer Kizer responded, "Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay]." Lisa responded with, "Okay." Following this, Lisa led the officers inside and identified the locations of the children. This timeline demonstrates a process where hostility and uncertainty were mitigated by reassurance and a final request for entry.

## Specific Circumstances of the Domestic Violence Incident

The context of the domestic violence incident informs the scope of the police investigation and the urgency of the entry. Lisa had identified Colem as the husband. She later confirmed the police were aware of a specific threat: Coleman had pointed a gun at his mother and threatened to shoot her and light the house on fire. The police officers learned this information from the two children, who confirmed they had seen the gun and witnessed the threats. While this information provided the specific reason for the officers' concern regarding the safety of the residents inside, the court ultimately determined that the legality of the entry rested primarily on consent. The entry occurred to locate Coleman and confirm the safety of the children and the husband, specifically the purpose of checking the welfare of the family. The information obtained regarding the gun was found during the subsequent search and led to the government's liquidation of Coleman's possession of firearms, which was the basis for the charge.

# Governing Legal Standards for Fourth Amendment Consent

## Consent as a Fourth Amendment Exception to Warrant Requirements

The Fourth Amendment generally requires law enforcement to obtain a warrant before violating a citizen's right to be free from unlawful search and seizure. However, the Seventh Circuit recognizes that the Fourth Amendment is not the only path to entry. *United States v. Banks*, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023). This court acknowledges that consent is a recognized exception that permits officers to enter a home without a warrant. This is consistent with landmark precedent and case law agreed upon by both sides in *Coleman*. As noted in *United States v. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973), a search is valid if conducted under the consent of a person authorized to consent. In *Coleman*, the defense argued that the Grande consent given by Lisa was invalid, specifically that officer communication was coercive, but the court applied the established legal principles that consent serves as the primary exception to the warrant requirement when entering a home to check on the welfare of family members or residents.

## Authority for Third-Party Consent (United States v. Matlock)

A critical legal threshold in consent cases is the source of the consent required to enter a property or home. In *Coleman*, the defense asserted that the wife, Lisa, was not a defendant and therefore her consent should not bind the federal defendant for Fourth Amendment purposes, though the opinion clarifies otherwise. Consent need not come from the defendant, but from a third party who has lawful access to the property. This is based on the holding in *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974). The Seventh Circuit further explains this in *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing *Matlock*), stating that a third party may give consent to search a place in which both the third party and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy. In this case, Lisa Coleman had the authority to consent to the police entry within the scope of the consent, even though Coleman was the one charged with the firearm possession in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). This distinction is significant for entry, as the police sought to secure that authority before entering the home, and the district court ultimately found the consent valid.

## The 'Totality of Circumstances' Test for Voluntariness (Bumper)

Once consent is sought, the court must determine whether that consent was voluntary. *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, 24-3051 (7th Cir. 2025)*. The governing standard under the Fourth Amendment is that consent must be freely and voluntarily given. *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). To determine voluntariness, the Seventh Circuit requires an evaluation of the totality of the circumstances. *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022). This standard involves looking at all relevant aspects of the situation rather than relying on any single factor, with courts assessing whether coercion was present.

In applying this standard, courts often consider specific factors as outlined in *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing *United States v. Santiago*, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). These factors include the person's age, intelligence, and education; whether the individual was advised of his constitutional rights; how long the individual was detained before consent was given; whether the consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; whether any physical coercion was used; and whether the individual was in police custody when consent was given. In *Coleman*, the court noted that Officer Kizer did not ask for permission in a coercive manner, but that the specific tone and demeanor of the officer was that of someone concerned for safety rather than someone demanding entry. Furthermore, because Lisa had the ability to deny consent by refusing the initial request and became in a state of concern, her consent remained voluntary despite Officer Kizer asking repeatedly.

## Objective Reasonableness Standard for Scope of Consent (Florida v. Jimeno)

Beyond the voluntariness of consent, a law enforcement entry requires consideration of the scope of consent to determine if the subsequent search was authorized. Under *United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012), once consent is established, the search must respect the scope of that consent. The proper inquiry is not whether the goal was the same, but whether the scope exceeded the boundaries of permission given. *United States v. Jackson*, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010). The standard applied is one of objective reasonableness, asking what a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent. *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991).

In *Coleman*, the district court did not find that the officers exceeded the scope of Lisa's permission. The officers' stated purpose was to check the welfare of her children after receiving information that the dweller enjoyed being спокойный. Finding Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. Doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions. In this instance, the scope of the consent was not violated because locating Coleman required locating all occupants to determine if they were compromised during a family violence situation. The opinion concludes that these conclusions eliminate any need to consider the government's alternative contention that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the police's entry into and initial sweep of the Coleman residence. The court reviews for clear error, finding that the district court did not commit an error in finding that Lisa consented to the police entry and that the officers conducted the search within the scope of that authority.

# Analytical Application to Consent and Scope

## Determination of Voluntariness: Assessing 'Need' Language and Tone (United States v. Hicks)

When analyzing a motion to suppress based on consent, the appellate court's primary task is to determine whether the consent was "freely and voluntarily" given. While the Seventh Circuit recognizes that consent does not need to follow a formal legal warning, the voluntariness of consent is always judged by the totality of the circumstances. In this opinion, the challenge focuses not on the existence of consent, but on its quality under the specific language used during the exchange at the threshold. Specifically, Defendant raises the issue of Officer Kizer using the phrase “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside.” Some might argue that the use of “need” implies an assertion of power that negates voluntariness. However, the court analyzes this within the framework of the factors established in *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566 (7th Cir. 2008). The court emphasizes that courts must evaluate whether the suggestion of necessity or urgency coarsely infringes upon a suspect's right to refuse. In *Coleman*, the court acknowledges this is a "plenty fair argument" for Coleman, but rests the determination on the broader context. The court Reasoning follows that while the defendant’s reaction to the word “need” must be considered, it is insufficient to render consent involuntary if the other circumstances do not support a finding of coercion. This highlights the importance of reading the consent challenge as a holistic inquiry rather than a linguistic one, requiring students to look beyond fragmented phrases to determine if the “need” was perceived as an ultimatum or a statement of urgent concern.

## Assessment of Police Demeanor and Absence of Coercion

The appellate court’s reasoning relies heavily on the visual evidence of the interaction found in the body camera footage. The standard for voluntariness is objective: it asks whether a reasonable person would feel coerced by the circumstances. In *Coleman*, the district court found the police officer’s tone was one of concern rather than control. The preliminary facts show that the officer paused for a specific period of time, indicating the nature of the consent was willing rather than compelled. A key element of this analysis is establishing whether the defendant was in police custody when consent was given. While Officer Kizer asked questions and asked for permission, the court notes that Lisa was never in custody. When evaluating the absence of coercion, the appellate court highlights that even repeated requests for permission do not violate voluntariness if they remain unaccompanied by overt threats or intimidation. Here, the officer asked twice; he withdrew a request implied by stating "need to come inside." The court reasons that while *Coleman* is entitled to rely on the video record, which he saw on the record, the video itself confirmed that Lisa's willingness to answer subsequent questions did not indicate she was suffocated under the state. The action of her consenting, even if pressured by societal duty to assist a report, remains valid if she retains the ability to say no. The analysis here teaches that the court must look for the absence of the "paddy wagon" effect—where submission becomes automatic under custody.

## Scope Analysis: Legitimate Purpose of Safety Checks and Locating Suspects

Even if consent is voluntary, the Fourth Amendment limits the government to a reasonable scope of that permission. The standard of review is based on an "objective reasonableness" inquiry: what would a typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent (*Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991)). In *Coleman*, the triggering question was whether the officers exceeded the scope of the consent given during the initial safety entry. The officers' purpose was to check on the welfare of the children and determine if Coleman posed a danger. The court reasoned that scanning the home, locating everyone inside, and asking basic security questions were intrinsic to the ability to fulfill an initial safety check. Because the specific area and items searched—living areas where the children and father were located—were the locus of the consent, the search was within the scope of consent. This section distinguishes between the initial entry scope and subsequent actions. While the officers eventually obtained a warrant for a full search, the initial closure of the entries (the firearms) occurred without warrant because they fell within the scope of the consent to check the safety of the children. The court explicitly notes that it does not need to consider exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery because the consent holding resolves the suppression challenge in favor of the Government. This illustrates to the student how the scope of consent is defined not by the specific items requested, but by the reasonable inference of the entry's purpose from the individual's perspective.

# Disposition, Standard of Review, and Significance

## Standard of Review: Clear Error Assessment for Consent Findings

The appellate court in *United States v. Jaison Coleman* applied a deferential standard of review when evaluating the magistrate judge's factual findings regarding the validity of consent to enter the home. Specifically, the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals reviews a district court's determination of whether consent was voluntary on summary for clear error. This definition requires the appellate court to conclude that no error had occurred unless the potential probative findings at the appellate court were so clearly erroneous that they were arbitrary, unreasonable, and beyond the reliance of the appellate court. The governing legal principle for this level of review is that the district court's determination of "voluntary consent" and challenges to the "scope of consent" must pass a clear error test.

The opinion explains that while consent is an exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement, a search is still subject to the discretion of the court. The court notes that a court of review, which includes the appellate court, has a "more limited" role when reviewing the district court's finding of voluntary consent. This deference stems from the recognition that small details regarding tone, timing, and interaction are often better assessed by the fact-finder who reviewed the body camera footage and evidence directly. In this case, the district court adopted a magistrate judge's report finding that Lisa did consent to the entry, and the appellate court determined no clear error in that finding. This emphasizes that factual determinations regarding the "totality of the circumstances" surrounding the consent—such as the officers' demeanor or Lisa's hesitation—are binding unless arbitrary.

## Final Disposition: Affirmation of Conviction

The district court side finished its operation of the suppression hearing by finding that Lisa consented to the entry of the officers into the Coleman home and that the officer's activity did not exceed the scope of that consent. Following this, the federal indictment charging Coleman with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), was sustained. Coleman's challenge to the suppression was exhausted within the lower court proceedings, and he chose to plead guilty while reserving the right to challenge the suppression ruling on appeal. The appeal before the Seventh Circuit is now before the court, and it is resolved with the final disposition: the appeal is dismissed, and the conviction is affirmed. The court states explicitly, "In the end, then, we AFFIRM."

This disposition solidifies the admissibility of the firearms recovered from the home as evidence in the criminal prosecution. Because the district court did not suppress the firearms, and there is no clear error in the lower court's finding regarding the scope of consent or the entry itself, the legal basis for the conviction remains intact. The court's affirmation indicates that while Lisa's initial hesitation or the officers' use of the word "need" were factors, they did not rise to the level of coercion that would invalidate the entry. Consequently, the government's chain of custody for the evidence seized following the entry holds, and the conviction for unlawful possession under § 922(g)(1) stands.

## Contextual Significance for Police Decision Making in Domestic Calls

While the primary focus of the case is the Fourth Amendment analysis of consent and scope, the court includes a significant observation regarding police decision-making in the closing remarks of the opinion. The court notes that Officer Kizer and his colleagues exhibited "sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." This observation is crucial for law enforcement training in domestic violence situations where safety perceptions and consent dynamics are often blurred. The opinion underscores that while police officers are reacting to an emergency, their perception of the situation must not amount to coercion that invalidates a consent entry.

This context leads to a practical takeaway for future police encounters involving domestic disputes: officers must act with care and balance. The court highlights that the officers did not seek to intimidate the parties but were acting out of concern for the safety of children. The decision to enter was grounded in the belief that Lisa had provided the necessary consent to ensure her children were safe from potential danger. This suggests that in similar domestic situations, officers are afforded some latitude to scan a home for the alleged offenders to ensure public safety, provided the officers' actions remain reasonable and do not coerce the homeowner's permission when a genuine offer of consent is present.
