Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 4: # Court Holding and Disposition

## Disposition of Conviction Appeal: Affirmation of Guilty Verdict
The Eighth Circuit affirmed Justin Cutbank’s conviction on all counts, finding that the district court’s evidentiary admissions and the jury’s verdict were supported by substantial evidence. In this textbook example, the appellate court notes that the conviction relies on two distinct legal theories: the physical possession of a sawed-off firearm and the predicate crimes establishing the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). In *Cutbank* the 8th Cir clarifies that a jury does not need to specifically separate facts regarding the gun from facts regarding the priors when the evidence overwhelmingly supports the elements of the charged offense. The district court did not commit reversible error because the circumstantial evidence of possession was consistent with the aggressive conduct of the prior incidents. This highlights for students that on a review of a jury trial, the appellate court defers to the jury’s role as the sole arbiter of facts, provided the testimony and exhibits were legally admissible.

## Disposition of Sentencing Appeal: Affirmation of 292-Month Term
The Court affirmed the district court's imposition of a 292-month prison term, holding that the sentence was both procedurally and substantively reasonable. Cutbank argued that the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was incorrectly applied, claiming a minimum statutory sentence conflict with the purposes of § 3553(a). The appellate court reasoned that the district court properly calculated the Guidelines range based on the PSR (Presentence Investigation Report) findings, which were accepted without objection. The court emphasized that when a defendant is sentenced within the applicable Guidelines range, the appellate court presumes that sentence was reasonable. To challenge the reasonsableness of a sentence, the Court explained that one must first identify a "significant procedural error," such as failing to calculate the range correctly or ignoring mandatory factors. Since the range was calculated correctly and the defendant was sentenced within that range (though at the lower end), the appeal was dismissed on procedural grounds alone, as the defendant was required to demonstrate that the Guidelines calculation itself was flawed.

## Summary of Court Reasoning on Evidence Admissibility
The opinion details five primary evidentiary rulings that supported the defense's arguments. First, the Court affirmed the admission of prior possession testimony as Rule 404(b) non-projection evidence (*indeci*) relevant to proving ownership of the specific gun, citing *United States v. Battle*. Second, the Court upheld the district court's decision regarding hearsay limiting instructions and Confrontation Clause claims, stating that a limiting instruction can be provided at close of trial rather than during immediate cross-examination. Third, the Court ruled that testimony regarding the victims' prior hiring of the gun was admissible under Rule 403, as the probative value on possession outweighed the slight unfair prejudice of the victims' family history. Fourth, the Court dismissed the defense's attempt to read police reports due to the lack of proper foundation/authenticity. Finally, the opinion emphasizes the "harmless error" doctrine, noting that any potential evidentiary error must be shown to have substantially swayed the jury or not been cumulative with the overwhelming government evidence. This standard prevents the reversal of a trial verdict based on minor or cured procedural mishaps.

## Summary of Guidelines Calculation and Reasonableness
Regarding the Clerk calculation, the Ninth Circuit rejected Cutbank's claim that the enhanced sentence conflicted with § 3553(a). The PSR established a base offense level of 35 by combining the firearm's nature and the defendant's criminal history and risk factors. The Court noted that USSG § 4B1.4(b) dictates that the greater offense level applies, which places the sentence within the ACCA-enhanced advisory range. The Court also clarified the "clear error" standard for appellate review of sentencing guidelines, distinguishing between the "significant procedural error" standard for the initial calculation and the "substantive reasonableness" standard for the final sentence. The opinion concluded that because the district court reviewed the § 3553(a) factors and chose a 292-month sentence within the range, there was no abuse of discretion. This reinforces the principle that appellate courts should not second-guess a district court's calculated Guidelines range unless there is a clear mathematical error or a misapplication of the enhancement criteria.

## Educational Takeaway: Standard Threats vs. Substantive Review
This chapter illustrates the critical distinction between the *Standard of Review* for evidentiary rulings and the *Standard of Review* for substantive sentencing findings. While the opinion uses the adversarial term "standard threats" (likely referring to the threshold of the threshold claims), the legal core is the difference between deferential review and substantive review. On evidentiary issues, the appellate court reviews for "abuse of discretion," meaning the court will not disturb a decision unless the trial judge made a clearly erroneous or arbitrary judgment. In contrast, sentencing appeals often require a clearer showing of "significant procedural error" to trigger a "substantial reasonableness" review if the sentence was not within the Guidelines range. Students must understand that while evidentiary claims can be harmless even if erroneous, sentencing claims require a demonstration that the procedural error fundamentally altered the outcome, such as inserting a FYT (Factual) error or a calculation error. This distinction mandates that defendants carefully preserve errors by citing case law and analysis on appeal, asCutbank failed to do, leading to reversal of the evidentiary challenge.
