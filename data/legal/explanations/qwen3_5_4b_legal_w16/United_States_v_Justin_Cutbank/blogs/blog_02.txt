Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Beyond the ACCA Cap: Why Specific Firearms Enhancements Survive the Guideline Calculation

## Why This Case Matters

The intersection between the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and specific sentencing enhancements often marks the line where statutory penalties meet judicial discretion, and *United States v. Cutbank* is prime evidence of the tension. The Eighth Circuit’s decision serves as a reminder that granting a defendant the benefit of an ACCA finding—fixed and severe for prior violent felonies—does not act as a blanket immunization against granular guideline adjustments that acknowledge the specific, immediate dangers presented in a particular instance of gun violence. Historically, the ACCA has functioned as a "backstop" to dealer the maximum length of prison for career felons, but this holding clarifies that the ACCA does not override the Sentencing Guidelines' federal policy to assess the unique nature of the weapon at issue, even after finding the defendant eligible for heightened penalties based on past conduct.

For defense attorneys and appellate judges alike, the decision stabilizes an expectation that no amount of aggressive statutory interpretation of the ACCA can entirely erase the specific harm of the current offense.

## Navigating the Procedural Minefield

Before diving into the sentencing equilibrium, it is worth noting that Cutbank successfully garnered support in the Eighth Circuit on multiple procedural fronts. The appeal turned largely on an array of evidentiary rulings that were challenged only after the case had proceeded to a conviction. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's decision regarding testimony from previous possessors of the firearm. The court reasoned that prior possession was directly relevant to establishing continuous control of the weapon, a stance consistent with the Eighth Circuit's reasoning in *Battle*. This evidentiary affirmative serves as a robust reminder of how admissible testimony must support the existence of the possession itself, tying the weapon directly to the defendant more than a simple hearsay exemption allows.

However, the more consequential component of the decision is the dismissal of the defendant's arguments regarding the sentencing enhancement itself. Cutbank appealed the mandatory application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), arguing that applying a four-level enhancement for firearm possession after triggering ACCA penalties violated the due process and specific purposes of § 3553(a).

## The Sentencing Equilibrium

At the heart of the Eighth Circuit's reasoning lies the conclusion that the ACCA finding, while setting a baseline for the defendant’s criminal history, does not exceed the need for sentencing to reflect the specific circumstances of the current offense. The district court had adopted a sentencing guideline recommendation based on a sawed-off rifle with an altered serial number, which resulted in a total offense level of 35. While the ACCA findings alone would have produced an offense level of 34, the specific harm of the firearm operating in the PSR outweighed the relatively minor difference in offense level.

The court invoked USSG § 4B1.4(b), noting that "the greater offense level applies." The Eighth Circuit declined to grant Cutbank relief that would invoke a variance from the guidelines range solely based on the overlap between ACCA and the specific firearm enhancement. The reasoning was clear: the applicant for the firearm enhancement is simply identifying that the gun used was different in nature (e.g., altered serial number, sawed-off barrel), which justifies a higher offense level on top of the ACCA baseline. The appeal court viewed the specific harm of the gun as necessary to account for the unique specifics of the use of that firearm, even in the context of a career violent felon.

## Implications for Sentencing Discretion

The importance of this decision lies in the guidance it offers for future sentencing proceedings. It reinforces the principle that the Guidelines act as a structured mechanism for assessing the specific nature of the crime, even when the underlying culpability for prior felonies pushes the defendant into a higher statutory tier. This means that defense counsel may look elsewhere for arguments challenging excessive sentences under § 3553(a), but they should accept the baseline of the enhancement provisions for conduct that the Guidelines explicitly design for categorizing specific firearms and their serial numbers.

Furthermore, the decision ensures that the ACCA remains a determinant of prior violence eligibility, but does not suggest that the specificity of the current offense need to be dampened to fit a legislative grid. This preservation of specific sentencing enhancements is important for ensuring that the severity of sentencing remains proportional to the immediate threat presented by the weapon at issue, regardless of how far back the criminal litigation record recedes.
