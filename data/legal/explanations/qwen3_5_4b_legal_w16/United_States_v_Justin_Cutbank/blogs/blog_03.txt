Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# When the Court Says 'Irrelevant': Hearsay, Limiting Instructions, and the No-Instruction Precedent

The complexity of federal criminal trials often turns on technical procedural scaffolding much more than the edifice of substantive law. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed a holding that functions as a significant check on prosecutorial strategy and defense counsel’s burden: when a declarant actually appears for trial, a limiting instruction on hearsay is often unnecessary. While prosecutors often rely on live testimony to establish the context of evidence admissible beforehand, the lower court in this instance found that requiring a jury deliberation on what was irrelevant might actually expose the party to unnecessary prejudice. The question of when to give a limiting instruction is narrow, yet its ripple effects regarding the Confrontation Clause and evidentiary discretion are substantial. As the Eighth Circuit navigated a charged domestic violence and firearms case, it used this procedural conduit to affirm the district court’s broad evidentiary discretion, reinforcing a line of precedent that prioritizes clarity in jury deliberations over rigid warnings to jurors.

Procedually, the trial presented a stark choice: permit cross-examination on prior possession or forbid it as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. In Section II.C of the opinion, Loken Circuit Judge explicitly states that prior possession is intrinsically relevant, but the defense argued that probing such factors without direct confrontation violated the Confrontation Clause. The key here was that the declarants, specifically Detainant D.F. and witness C.F., did not simply testify to the events in court; they also testified that they had previously observed the weapon. The Eighth Circuit rejected Cutbank’s argument that a limiting instruction was necessary to separate the hearsay. Relying on *Brown* and *Charboneau*, the panel noted that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when declarants appear and testify in person. The reasoning is that if the witness is there to tell the truth, the jury can do the work of weighing credibility without needing a mechanical instruction on what they can and cannot rely on. This is a powerful tool in the prosecutor’s toolkit, as it allows them to effectively question witnesses about events they can’t directly testify to, without triggering a constitutional panic that might taint the jury’s view of the live testimony.

The court’s application of this standard in *Cutbank* is significant because it underscores the reliance on the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause to validate the weight of hearsay in the absence of a direct instruction. As the opinion notes, prior possession helps establish ownership and control, which is crucial in possession cases. The court omitted the need for a limiting instruction because the witnesses were present to testify personally, effectively clearing the path for question-and-answer regarding witness observation of prior acts. This reflects the Eighth Circuit’s body of law that emphasizes the judge’s discretion in instructing juries, consistent with the hub that obscuring relevant evidence is a rare violation that warrants review. By adopting the position that the absence of a limiting instruction does not constitute an error, the court signals that the constitutional mandate to be present is the primary bar rather than the potential usability of information for impeachment. This is a win for procedural efficiency, as it reduces the risk of inflaming a jury by explaining that certain statements are merely character evidence.

However, there is a tension in this approach, as noted by the appellate court itself. If the use of limiting instructions were consistently blocked by a blanket reliance on the Confrontation Clause, it could encourage unethical practices where defense counsel might inadvertently mislead the jury on the nature of extrinsic evidence. The court clarified this by emphasizing that limiting instructions are not always required if the witness is testifying. But as the court acknowledged, there is a standard that can trigger an instruction, specifically when an item of evidence is sensitive and not proper to be considered by the jury in the manner in question. The *Battle* and *Charboneau* citations show that this is not a new rule but a reaffirmation of established standards. As a practical matter, this means defense attorneys who seek to object to the admissibility of hearsay must be prepared to argue the direct confrontation, as the mere absence of an instruction may be assumed unless there is clear prejudice. This burden shifts the focus of litigation from the mechanics of the instruction to the actual confrontation of the witness.

Looking downward, the implications of this ruling suggest that the right to confront counsel is not the only right to be exercised in a criminal trial; the jury’s right to a clear verdict may also require a more flexible approach to procedural direction. If the district court’s discretion to waive the instruction stands, the Eighth Circuit signals that judges will likely maintain the same level of deference in evidentiary rulings as long as the core constitutional rights were met through direct testimony. This case, which ultimately affirmed both conviction and a 292-month sentence grounded in ACCA enhancements, demonstrates that these procedural victories rarely come at the expense of substantive outcomes in the Eighth Circuit. The court suggests that where the offensive evidence is strong and the evidence is properly authenticated under Fed. R. Evidence 403, the defense does not need to win the signal battle over instructions. Ultimately, *Cutbank* offers a rebuke to counsel who argues that minor procedural irritations should dictate the outcome, encouraging a focus on the actual confrontation rather than the absence of an instruction.
