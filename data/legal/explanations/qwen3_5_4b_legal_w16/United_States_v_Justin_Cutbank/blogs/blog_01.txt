Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# The Intrinsic Requirement: When Prior Possession Is Evidence of Ownership

The interplay between evidentiary rules and the mechanics of proving possession forms a critical intersection in federal criminal law. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit provided a clear, rigorous reaffirmation that prior possession of a specific firearm is not inherently inadmissible "propensity evidence" under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). While Propositions they apply, the Court clarified that when firearms are present in the scene, testing prior possession establishes the ownership or control necessary to prove the current possession charge. This ruling reinforces the necessity of analyzing the intrinsic nature of evidence when possession is contested.

## The Intrinsic Requirement for Ownership Proof

A prolonged appellate history has developed around Rule 404(b) and the probative value of prior statements when evidence is offered. Defense presentations often argue for the suppression of prior encounter evidence, citing character evidence or instructions regarding the limitations of use within the jury's mind. The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument in the case of Cutbank, who faced charges for felon in possession of a firearm after an incident involving domestic violence and confinement. The court determined that the testimony was not merely relevant to character, but was intrinsic to proving the base elements of the crime.

The key to this distinction lies in the nature of the prosecution's evidentiary goal. The Court noted that "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." By framing the defense's argument solely as a crime of character, the appellate court highlighted that Dismissal orders aimed at 404(b) material often fail where the evidence links the defendant to the specific object. The court's reasoning emphasized that without proof of direct ownership, the possession charge collapses. This clarifies that the "intrinsic evidence" exception is not a loophole for character attacks on the witness, but a procedural safeguard for establishing the necessary control required for possession.

## Confrontation and Notification of Rights

The evidentiary ruling in *Cutbank* was bolstered by the district court's handling of hearsay and Confrontation Clause challenges during the trial. The defense challenged the admissibility of statements made to police that were not testifying firsthand, but the Court affirmed that where a declarant testifies in person, the clause requirement is satisfied. This alignment of testimony and evidentiary admissibility meant that the district court's discretion to admit hearsay as context or foundation did not constitute reversible error. Furthermore, the Eighth Circuit noted that the district court did not need to instruct the jury immediately on each item unless required, as long as limiting instructions could follow the close of trial. While this undermines strict proceduralist arguments regarding trial management, it reduces the administrative burden on the bench without violating constitutional guarantees of fair notice.

## Impact on Sentencing Guidelines and 3553(a)

Beyond the evidentiary issue, *Cutbank* provided an interesting look at the sentencing calculus involving the Armed Career Criminal Act and the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. Cutbank argued that applying the four-level enhancement for possession in connection with a felony offense resulted in a sentence that conflicted with the underlying sentencing principles of § 3553(a). Specifically, there was a conflict regarding whether the acceleration of the guideline range or the advisory base level would govern the final sentence. The Court affirmed the sentence within the guidelines range, noting that a district court must calculate the range correctly before considering substantive reasonableness. This reinforces the rule that the guidelines dictate the structure of the sentence, even if the 3553(a) factors offer a broader range of discretion.

Ultimately, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder of the standard pressure test that guides lower courts in federal prosecutions: can the defense show that specific evidence benefits a purpose outside the elements of the offense, or is the prior possession itself the foundation of guilt. As the opinion notes, the court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. In a case as factually heavy as this one, the weight of the evidence outweighed the procedural tendency to suppress. The ruling stands as a beacon of efficiency in possession cases while noting that defendants must consider whether their own factual construction of the evidence meets the standard of prejudice.

## Conclusion

The Eighth Circuit's decision in *Cutbank* does not simply affirm a conviction; it solidifies the mechanism by which prior possession evidence is integrated into the proof of ownership. By distinguishing this evidence from outlier propensity evidence, the Court ensures that jurors are presented with the relevant context necessary to adjudicate gun possession crimes. For defense counsel, this implies that motion practice should focus harder on validating the connection to the current charge rather than simply labeling the evidence inadmissible. For practitioners, it highlights the importance of understanding how prior possession is defined and measured against the current charge in government prosecutions. The reasoning here provides a stable framework for evidentiary rulings, balancing the right to a fair trial with the utilitarian need for accurate fact-finding.
