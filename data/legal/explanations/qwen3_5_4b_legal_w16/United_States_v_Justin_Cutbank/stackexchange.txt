Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why does the court apply Rule 404(b) to evidence of prior possession as 'intrinsic' evidence rather than character evidence?
Question:
The opinion cites *United States v. Battle* regarding prior possession of a firearm. I understand that Rule 404(b) allows for reputation evidence regarding prior acts, but here the district court called it 'intrinsic evidence.' In substantive criminal law, 'character' evidence shows why someone behaved a certain way, while 'intrinsic' evidence usually shows motive or intent. Can someone explain specifically why having seen the gun once before the indictment counts as 'prior possession directly relevant' to the possession charge itself under the 8th Circuit's interpretation, rather than a propensity rule violation?
Answer:
### Answer

The Eighth Circuit in *United States v. Cutbank* distinguishes between character evidence and "intrinsic" evidence based on whether the prior act proves a fact essential to the conviction or merely the defendant's disposition to commit crimes.

To understand the court's ruling, you must look at the specific distinction the court draws in Section II.A regarding the evidence of prior possession. The defense argued that witnesses' testimony showed a propensity to possess firearms, which would violate Rule 404(a) (and thus be barred under Rule 404(b)'s restrictions on character evidence). However, the court rejected this, ruling the evidence was "intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident."

The legal foundation for this distinction is not merely about "motives" or "character" in the abstract, but specifically about what element of the crime is being proven. In *Cutbank*, the court explicitly relied on *United States v. Battle*, stating:

> “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish **ownership or control of the weapon.**”

Because "ownership or control" is the specific legal element required to sustain a conviction for felon-in-possession, evidence of prior possession is considered intrinsic to the crime itself. This contrasts with character evidence, which would show the defendant generally behaved in a certain way regarding firearms (e.g., "he is a dangerous person who habitually carries a gun") without necessarily linking that general behavior to the specific facts of the current charge.

By characterizing the evidence as intrinsic to ownership, the court concluded the evidence was admissible under *Battle* rather than prohibited as propensity evidence. Therefore, the evidence was not being used to show Cutbank "did the act because it is in his character" but was used to prove he had "ownership or control" of the specific weapon charged, which is a necessary factual predicate for his conviction.

### How does the cumulative error test apply when reviewing multiple evidentiary rulings for harmless error?
Question:
In Section II.E, the court discusses the standard for harmless error and cumulative effect. I am confused by the distinction between the *DeMarce* citation (requiring substantial sway) and the *Keys* citation (cumulative effect). If there were multiple evidentiary errors—like the Rule 404(b) admission, the hearsay instruction refusal, and the authentication issue—does a 'substantial' prejudice require a specific amount of evidence to be excluded, or does the mere presence of multiple rulings change the standard of review for the appellate court?
Answer:
### Harmless Error and Cumulative Prejudice: A Standard of Substantial Prejudice

The short answer to your question is that the presence of multiple evidentiary rulings does not alter the standard of review itself. Under the Eighth Circuit, the mere accumulation of errors does not mandate reversal; rather, the appellate court retains a sole focus on whether those multiple rulings, taken together, caused **"substantial prejudice"** to the defendant.

Based on the Eighth Circuit’s reasoning in *United States v. Cutbank*, 24-1225 (Oct. 7, 2025), the distinction between the *DeMarce* and *Keys* citations lies in the scope of analysis, but both apply to the same substantive threshold.

### The Legal Standard: DeMarce and Keys

In Section II.E of the opinion, the court delineates two distinct but related prerequisites for reversal under the harmless error doctrine:

1.  **Individual Error Standard (*DeMarce*):** The court cites *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), establishing the baseline test for any single evidentiary ruling. The standard is low-the appellate court must determine that the error did not "substantially sway" the jury. If a ruling was "harmless," the conviction stands. As the *Cutbank* opinion states: *"this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless... in other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence."*

2.  **Cumulative Error Standard (*Keys*):** The court then addresses the scenario where there are *multiple* errors, citing *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019). This citation does not lower the bar for prejudice; rather, it sets a strict rule for when that cumulative analysis affects the verdict. The opinion clarifies that even review of cumulative effects is gated by the same requirement: *"We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant."*

Therefore, the "mere presence of multiple rulings" does not create a separate standard of review that triggers reversal. If the cumulative effect is alleged, the appellate court applies the *Cutbank* standard: the defendant must prove the cumulative errors substantially swayed the jury’s verdict.

### Application in *Cutbank*

In *Cutbank*, the court applied this dual standard to dismiss cuts of the evidentiary record. While Cutbank argued that multiple rulings—specifically the admission of prior possessions, refusal to give a hearsay instruction, and authentication of reports—should collectively prejudice his conviction, the appellate court found he failed to meet the substantive burden.

The court noted that the government's evidence was substantial and corroborated by both testimonial and physical evidence (*Federal R. Evidence 403* analysis). Because the government established a strong foundation for the charge, the court concluded that the cumulative influence of the errors remained "only a slight influence on the verdict." As explicitly stated in Section II.E: *"The government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial. Thus, the district court’s evidentiary rulings 'did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.'"*

### Conclusion

To answer your specific question: A 'substantial' prejudice requires showing that the error (even if cumulative) swung the jury's decision. The multiple evidentiary rulings in *Cutbank* did not create a "specific amount of evidence to be excluded"; rather, the defense failed to demonstrate that the **cumulative prejudice** was substantial enough to overcome the weight of the admitted evidence.

In short, the standard of review for both individual and cumulative errors remains tethered to the requirement of **"substantial prejudice"** to the defendant. Multiple rulings do not change the standard of review; they simply accumulate, yet the final threshold for reversality is still the high bar set by *DeMarce* and confirmed by *Keys* in *Cutbank*.

### What is the procedural standard for Confrontation Clause limitations when a district court refuses initial jury instructions on hearsay?
Question:
The court addressed the absence of a limiting instruction for D.F.'s statements in Section II.B, citing *United States v. Charboneau*. It seems the instruction was given at the 'close of trial' or implied. Is there a specific 8th Circuit rule that allows a controlling instruction at the end of the trial to cure a lack of instruction during trial, and how does this strictly limit the Confrontation Clause violations under the right to cross-examine existing evidence?
Answer:
### Answer

The Eighth Circuit provides specific procedural flexibility regarding jury instructions on hearsay and the Confrontation Clause, governed primarily by an **abuse of discretion** standard.

**1. Procedural Standard for Refusal of Initial Instructions**
When reviewing a district court's refusal to provide an initial limiting instruction on hearsay, the Eighth Circuit applies a standard of **abuse of discretion** (*United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), quoted in *Cutbank*). Pursuant to the rule articulated in *Cutbank* (Section II.B), a district court is **not required** to instruct the jury on hearsay limitations at the moment evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the **close of trial**.

**2. Rule on Closing Instruction**
The Eighth Circuit has established that the absence of a controlling instruction during testimony does not constitute a reversible error if an appropriate instruction is provided at the end of the trial. In *United States v. Cutbank* (Section II.B), the court stated:
> "The court 'need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.' *Id.* Here, the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial. In addition, D.F. testified."

**3. Limiting Confrontation Clause Violations**
Regarding the Confrontation Clause, the Eighth Circuit determinacy is satisfied under *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). The clause is satisfied "when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person." Because D.F. testified in person at trial (*United States v. Cutbank*, Section II.B), the specific procedural issue of whether the instruction was given initially is rendered moot. The court found that "The Confrontation Clause is satisfied 'when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.'" (Id.). Consequently, a control instruction at the close of trial effectively cures any limitation concerns, as the presence and testimony of the declarant itself satisfies the Clause requirements.

### How does USSG § 4B1.4(b) interact with the ACCA to determine the 'greater offense level' in a felon-in-possession case?
Question:
In the sentencing section, the court discusses USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) as a 4-level enhancement for 'possession of a firearm in connection with another felony.' The text notes that the Guidelines provide that the 'greater offense level applies' under § 4B1.4(b). Does this mean that if the base offense level (26) and the enhancement (4 levels) result in a higher total than the ACCA level calculation, the court must select the higher number, regardless of whether the enhancement technically conflicted with ACCA sentencing?
Answer:
**Answer: Application of USSG § 4B1.4(b) and ACCA in Felon-in-Possession Cases**

Under USSG § 4B1.4(b), when both the ACCA and standard sentencing guidelines apply to a felon-in-possession charge, the court must select the "greater offense level" between the two calculations. This principle ensures that the advisory guidelines range reflects the most severe applicable enhancement while maintaining statutory compliance.

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit held that the Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies under § 4B1.4(b). The district court adopted a PSR-recommended offense level of 35, derived from the base offense level of 26, plus: (1) a 3-level increase for an altered/obliterated serial number, (2) a 2-level increase for reckless creation of risk of death or serious bodily injury when fleeing police, and (3) a 4-level increase for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense. In contrast, the ACCA calculation alone produced an offense level of 34. Because 35 exceeds 34, § 4B1.4(b) mandated the higher offense level despite the ACCA's independent role in the Criminal History Category determination.

The key consideration is that the court must not apply any enhancement compels a higher THC than the ACCA calculation. While the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement technically conflicts with ACCA's premise—since ACCA already accounts for violent felonies—the Guidelines themselves require selecting whichever calculation produces the larger total offense level. This approach upholds the § 3553(a) mandate that the sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary," ensuring the court does not result in double-counting the ACCA contribution.

Therefore, in this case, the district court correctly imposed a within-range sentence of 292 months, as it was consistent with the advisory guidelines range derived from the highest applicable offense level under § 4B1.4(b). The Eighth Circuit affirmed the sentence, confirming that the greater offense level rule applies and that the court's discretion was consistent with the ACCA and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) standards simultaneously.

### Why is the district court's discretion under § 3553(a) factors upheld even if they conflict with the calculated Guidelines range?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes that a district court must first calculate the Guidelines range before considering the substantive reasonableness of the sentence (§ 3553(a)). However, Cutbank argued that using the firearm enhancement to enhance the sentence conflicted with the 'serve for § 3553(a)' principle of not over-sentencing. Why is the appellate court's presumption that the district court considered the § 3553(a) factors enough to sustain the sentence, given that sentencing must be 'sufficient, but not greater than necessary'?
Answer:
In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s sentence despite Cutbank’s argument that the application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) demonstrated a conflict between the Guidelines range and the § 3553(a) “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” standard. The court upheld the sentence primarily because it was imposed within the calculated advisory Guidelines range, which triggers a strong presumption of reasonableness that elevates the district court’s substantive discretion.

The appellate court’s reasoning rests on two key procedural and substantive requirements derived from the Text of the Opinion:

**1. The Premise of Sentencing: Calculating the Guidelines First**
Under *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 585 U.S. 129 (2018), a district court must first correctly calculate the applicable Guidelines range before considering substantive reasonableness (§ 3553(a)). In *Cutbank*, the district court explicitly calculated the range, which included the four-level enhancement for possession in connection with another felony. Because the sentence (292 months) fell within this calculated range (292 to 365 months), the district court did not utilize a § 3553(a) variance that would have required a detailed purpose analysis outside the Guidelines. Instead, the sentence was determined *within* the range mandated by USSG § 4B1.4(b).

**2. The Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Range Sentences**
Once a sentence is within the Guidelines, the appellate court applies a significant standard of review. As stated in *Cutbank*, citing *United States v. Goodhouse*, the court “imposes a sentence within the applicable guidelines range, which we presume is reasonable on appeal.” Consequently, the district court’s determination that the sentence was sufficient and not unreasonably high is presumed correct. To overturn this presumption, the appellate court does not need to independently second-guess the mathematics of the Guidelines; it only needs to ensure no significant procedural error occurred (such as an improper calculation).

**3. Procedural Sufficiency for § 3553(a) Consideration**
While the § 3553(a) factors must be considered to ensure the sentence meets the “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” standard, the district court is not required to provide a mechanical, written explanation for every factor weighed if the record supports the consideration. As the opinion notes, citing *United States v. Masood*, the district court need only provide a clear record that it reviewed § 3553(a) factors. The district court in *Cutbank* reviewed the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and sentencing memoranda, noting it understood Cutbank’s arguments regarding the enhancements.

Therefore, the appellate court held that the district court “considered and rejected them” when the record demonstrated awareness of the arguments. The conflict asserted by Cutbank—that the enhancement violated § 3553(a)’s “not greater than necessary” principle—does not justify reversal for a within-range sentence because the district court is presumed to have weighed that principle within the discretion of choosing a point within the provided range. The court affirmed only because the sentence was substantively reasonable, defined here as residing within the protected advisory range.

**Conclusion**
The discretion was upheld because the sentence was within the statutory Guidelines range, triggering a presumption of reasonableness that limits appellate interference. The district court satisfied its obligation to consider § 3553(a) factors through the review of the PSR and memoranda, satisfying the "substantive reasonableness" requirement without needing to recalculate the Guidelines or impose a variance.

### What is the procedural standard for admitting policing reports as extrinsic evidence in defense cross-examination?
Question:
Section II.E mentions that a motion to strike C.F.'s testimony regarding prior statements was denied because the police report was not authenticated. The court cites *Cumbie* regarding the requirement for proper authentication of extrinsic evidence for impeachment. Can someone clarify the specific 8th Circuit standard here—is the report treated as hearsay requiring the original author's signature or testimony, and when does an unauthenticated report become inadmissible as a matter of law in a collateral factual finding?
Answer:
### The Procedural Standard for Admitting Extrinsic Evidence in Impeachment

Based on the Eighth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the procedural standard for admitting policing reports as extrinsic evidence during defense cross-examination rests on the requirement of **authentication**, reviewed under an **abuse of discretion** standard.

**1. The Authentication Requirement**
The court explicitly applies the standard from *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), which stipulates that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be properly authenticated before it can be admitted. The Eighth Circuit held the dough court did not abuse its discretion to reject defense counsel's attempt to read portions of a police report into the record "without proper foundation because the report was not authored by C.F." Therefore, the report is treated as extrinsic evidence for the purpose of *Cumbie* which requires authentication.

**2. Implications for Hearsay and Confrontation**
The opinion notes that the government provided a limiting instruction that hearsay statements from a witness cannot be used substantively until the witness testifies. While the defense questioned the authenticity of the report itself (which were not authenticated), the court affirmed that such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated to be admissible. The failure to authenticate prevents the defense from using police report excerpts to cross-examine a witness on prior inconsistent statements.

**3. Standard of Review and Harmless Error**
Regarding the procedural outcome of an improper evidentiary ruling, the court standard applies the rule of **abuse of discretion**. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. Specifically, "this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence" (*DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997).

**Conclusion**
In summary, under *United States v. Cutbank*, a policing report cannot be read as part of a defense cross-examination to impeach a witness on a prior inconsistent statement unless the defense provides a foundation demonstrating its authentication (generally via authorship or testimony). The appellate court reviews this evidentiary ruling with abuse of discretion and will only reverse if the improper exclusion is not cured by the harmless error doctrine.
