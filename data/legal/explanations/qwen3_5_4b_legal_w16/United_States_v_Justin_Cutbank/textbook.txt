Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## Overview of the Eighth Circuit Appeal Process

This appellate opinion arises from the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, an intermediate appellate court possessing jurisdiction over criminal appeals within its geographic jurisdiction. The opinion, authored by Circuit Judge Loken, details the appellate court's review of the district court's rulings regarding the defendant's conviction and sentence. In federal criminal procedure, the Court of Appeals generally reviews the district court's decision for abuse of discretion or reversible error. This stage provides the appellate court an opportunity to correct procedural errors made during the trial, such as misconduct by the court or improper instructions to the jury, or to overturn a sentence that falls outside the applicable guidelines. The opinion opens by establishing the procedural posture, noting the defendant's conviction and the specific arguments brought against it. The appeal was docketed as No. 24-1225, and the appellate court's disposition is a summary affirmation of the lower court's judgments.

## Statutory Elements: 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (Felon in Possession) and 924(e) (Armed Career Criminal)

To understand the trial court's proceedings and the scope of the appeal, one must examine the statutory elements at issue under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). The first statute, § 922(g)(1), establishes the crime of being a "felon in possession of a firearm." This charge requires proof that the defendant is a prior felon, who is currently in possession of a controlled firearm. The appeal in this case challenged whether the jury was required to demonstrate prior possession or if the evidence supported the finding of possession under this statute. The second statute, § 924(e), embodies the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), which mandates a statutory sentencing enhancement for defendants with multiple prior violent felony convictions. Combined, these statutes form the basis of the indictment and the district court's sentencing guidelines application. The appellate court specifically noted that the enhancement under § 924(e) applied because the jury found Cutbank had been convicted of at least three prior violent felonies, triggering the enhanced penalty provisions for the Armed Career Criminal Act.

## Factual Narrative: The November 2020 Incident at D.F.'s Residence

The factual underpinning of the case centers on domestic violence and firearm use on November 19-20, 2020. The defendant, Justin Cutbank, was intoxicated and under the influence of methamphetamine when he confronted his girlfriend, D.F. At the incident location, Cutbank pointed a firearm at D.F.'s face and ordered her into a closet. He held her at gunpoint for an extended period, making paranoid accusations regarding listening devices and threatening her life. Cutbank later commanded D.F. to rouse others sleeping in the house, including two teenage sons and a housemate. Cutbank eventually fled the premises with D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm. Officers arrived in response to calls, and there were multiple points of contact with law enforcement, including a report from a separate house half a mile away regarding Cutbank's presence. The narrative establishes the criminal conduct that led to the trial proceedings, specifically highlighting the use of a firearm in a domestic setting under aggravated circumstances.

## Evidence Collection: Police Logistics, Wire Logs, and DNA Match

The evidence collected at the scene and after the initial arrest formed the basis of the government's case. Police interveners found a barrel sawed-off pistol and a stock had been retrieved from the path Cutbank likely used between the victims' residence and the garage. In addition to the physical evidence, officers recovered a Brittle-style knife, a Prince cell phone, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. The opinion notes that DNA evidence from the gun matched Cutbank. Testimonial evidence included confessions made by D.F. and C.F., a housemate. While the subtopic title references wire logs, the opinion relies primarily on witness testimony for the prior possession of the weapon and police observations of the defendant. The search methods and the likelihood of using the destroyed firearm were confirmed through physical evidence found near the escape path. The defense argued that some prior use of the weapon was being admitted as propensity evidence which did not require witness testimony, relying solely on physical evidence and witness testimony.

## Scope of Appeal: Challenges to Conviction Versus Sentence

Cutbank appealed both his conviction and sentence, raising distinct arguments regarding the trial process and the sentencing calculation. Regarding conviction, he argued that the district court engaged in multiple evidentiary and procedural errors that deprived him of a fair trial and did not apply the guidelines correctly. Specific arguments included objections to the admission of the prior possession evidence, the jury instruction regarding hearsay, and the Cross-Examination of witnesses. Regarding the sentence, Cutbank argued that the four-level sentencing enhancement for firearm use connected to another felony offense affected the sentence because the enhancement is eliminated under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's ruling on the evidentiary issues, stating that the error was harmless and the rationality of applying the ACCA guidelines was supported by the record.

# Analysis of Evidentiary and Procedural Objections

## Rule 404(b) and Intrinsic Evidence regarding Gun Possession Continuity

On appeal, Justin Cutbank argued that a ruling allowing his co-wife, D.F., to testify about prior observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin rifle violated Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence, which generally prohibits evidence of a crime or act other than the current one from proving the defendant's inherent disposition or propensity to commit a crime. In a straightforward correction of this claim, the Eighth Circuit clarified the distinction between evidence offered for improper purpose and evidence offered for intrinsic context. The court explained that the evidentiary ruling in question concerned the ownership and control of the weapon, rather than general character.

The appellate court cited *United States v. Battle*, noting that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." In the background facts, the jury found Cutbank to be a felon in possession of the gun. However, the government also needed to prove continuity of that possession at the moment of the incident to support the enhancement regarding the specific nature of the firearm. Without corroborating testimony that Cutbank possessed the specific Marlin *before* the shooting, it becomes difficult to prove possession later if he was seeking to access it for the first time after the violation. By allowing D.F. to testify that the gun was "in his possession" at the time of the incident, the district court was not invoking Rule 404(b) propensity evidence rules but was validating the continuous nature of the offense's ingredients. The ruling stands because the evidence was intrinsic to the possession charge itself, proving that the gun in question was the one he had access to at the time the indictment was framed.

## Confrontation Clause and Hearsay Limitations on Jury Instructions

A procedural issue raised by Cutbank concerned jury instructions regarding hearsay and the Confrontation Clause. Specifically, before trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed that they could not consider statements made by D.F. to police officers as substantive evidence unless D.F. herself testified to the events independently. The district court declined this request, arguing that an instruction at the close of the trial would suffice. The appellate court reviewed this under the standard for abuse of discretion, holding that the district court need not provide limiting instructions for every item of evidence if an appropriate instruction is given at the end of a proceeding.

The core legal issue turned on the satisfaction of the Confrontation Clause under the Sixth Amendment, which generally requires that the accused face the witnesses against them. However, the court applied an exception articulated in *United States v. Charboneau*: the Clause is satisfied when hearsay declarants "actually appear in court and testify in person." Because D.F. testified at trial regarding the prior observations and the incident, the court found that the Confrontation Clause was met. Since the court provided no limiting instruction during the trial but did note the potential need for one, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion. This highlights that while instructing a jury on when evidence may or may not be used is standard, the Confrontation Clause does not mandate a continuous instruction against hearsay if the declarant is present through direct testimony. The court affirmed that the district court's choice to defer the instruction to the close of trial was within its discretion.

## Standard of Review: Abuse of Discretion and Remand Considerations

When the Eighth Circuit reviews the district court's evidentiary rulings, it cannot micromanage the trial judge on the presentation of the facts and rules. Instead, the standard of review is deferential, often characterized as a "manifest authority" or "abuse of discretion" analysis. The briefs consistently refer to the wide scope of the district court's duty over evidentiary matters, allowing the appellate court to affirm where the lower court's decision "was not clearly erroneous." In this case, the court emphasized that the district court did not need to authenticate every piece of evidence at the moment of admission as long as it provided a cohesive record for the jury.

Although there is no explicit remand order in this opinion because the court affirmed the convictions, the principles generally apply in lower-filer scenarios. If a lower court denied an evidentiary ruling and the appellate court finds it wrong, it must determine if that was clearly erroneous. In the *Cutbank* opinion, the text assumes that the district court acted within its "broad evidentiary discretion" in *United States v. Sprint/United Management Co v. Mendelsohn* referenced the terms of the discretion. The standard implies that if the lower court acted within the bounds of its discretion and there was no clear error, the court of appeals must make no call of remand. This means the appellate court must ensure that the evidence presented in *Cutbank* met the legal standards of admissibility, including relevance and proper foundation, before overturning the trial prosecutor's decision.

## Harmless Error Doctrine: Required Impact on the Verdict

Even if the appellate court finds that the trial court made an error regarding evidence, they are not required to reverse the conviction if that error was harmless. The Harmless Error Doctrine, as cited in *United States v. DeMarce*, states that this court will reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling only if "the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." In other words, the appellate court weighs the impact of the prohibited evidence against the totality of the evidence presented.

In the *Cutbank* opinion, the court explicitly applied this standard. It noted that Cutback could not clear the hurdle of proving that the trial court's evidentiary rulings, even if they may have been technically flawed in isolation, actually influenced the verdict. The government presented substantial testimonial and physical evidence, creating a challenge that the defense objections were isolated. The court also cited *United States v. Keys* to reinforce the point that they will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless that cumulative effect resulted in substantial prejudice to the defendant. Since the record showed that the government's case was robust, the court found that any errors were harmless. This review ensures that vindicating the peremptory discretion of the lower court does not jeopardize the rights of the accused unless the errors truly compromised the trial's integrity.

## Application to Specific Testimony: Witness Impeachment and Character Evidence

The final evidentiary issue examined tests the duty of the trial court to preserve character evidence and ensure the proper witness authentication required for impeachment. When D.F. testified about her past observations of the gun, Cutbank cross-examined her regarding her sister's death, claiming that this suggestion could reopen questions about her mental state or fairness. The district court daunted the objection, ruling that the testimony was relevant to why D.F. initially testified differently about the gun. The appellate court found that the district court acted within its discretion because no prejudice was apparent.

In *Cutbank*, this focus on specific testimony extends to witness authentication. Specifically, the defense sought to cross-examine C.F. regarding a police report from her statement to an investigator. Cumbie*, the court explained that while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach witnesses by prior inconsistent statement, "such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." The appellate court noted the defense did not preserve this specific ruling on appeal but addressed it to demonstrate the court's procedural adherence. The court also cited *United States v. Sprint/United Management Co. v. Mendelsohn* when reviewing the inclusion of sensitive testimony regarding the victims' death to determine if there was an abuse of discretion. By requiring authentication and weighing presumptive evidence against legal standards of prejudice, the court ensured that the specific testimony was meaningful, relevant, and admissible without overwhelming the determinations of the jury.

## Conclusion

Through these analyses, the Eighth Circuit affirmed that the district court did not abuse its discretion in the evidentiary rulings, proceedings, and sentencing applications. The court overturned the challenge based on the lack of successful evidence burden and valid admissibility rulings, upholding the conviction and sentence.

# Sentencing Guidelines Calculation and Reasonableness Analysis

## PSR Calculations: Base Offense Level, Criminal History Category, and Guidelines Range

When reading a sentencing opinion, a law student must first locate the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and understand the mathematical foundation of the advisory guidelines range before analyzing substantive reasonableness. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirms the district court’s calculation process, emphasizing the necessity for the initial mathematical determination of the applicability of the Guidelines range. The court notes that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions without objection, establishing a procedural foundation for the sentencing range selection. 

The court calculates the offense level by aggregating specific statutory enhancements. In this case, the base offense level was 26, imposed because the charged offense involved a sawed-off firearm described in 21 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank had two prior convictions for crimes of violence. Importantly, the court identified specific three-level increases due to an altered serial number and a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing. Cutbank also received a four-level increase because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. This totaled an offense level of 35. Additionally, Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms, resulting in a Criminal History Category of VI. Combining the base offense level with the criminal history category establishes the specific advisory guidelines sentencing range. In this instance, the court assigned a range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment, as determined by the district court. This calculation serves as the first step in the appellate court’s review; a sentence is presumptively reasonable if it falls within this range, provided the range was calculated correctly according to the Guidelines.

## USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement: Connection to Other Felonies

The core of Cutbank’s appellate concern focused on the proper application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which adds four criminal history points for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony. Cutbank argued that applying this enhancement created a conflict with the core sentencing principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence should not be greater than necessary. While Cutbank argued that the enhancement resulted in a sentence within-range but that the ACCA penalty system alone would have produced a lower total offense level, the court analyzed the text of 18 U.S.C. § 2K2.1, comment line 14(C).

The objective analysis of the opinion clarifies that this enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." In Cutbank’s case, the district court did not err in finding that the facts in the PSR established the gun was used in connection with another felony. The court treated the prior convictions as relevant to the enhancement under the Guidelines. The appellate court reaffirms that when a defendant’s case involves complex sentencing enhancements based on past conduct not adjudicated in the current case, the district court must review the PSR factual statements against the application notes. The record here was sufficient to support the enhancement because the firearm was involved in a specific incident (the arrest in the garage) that served as part of the alleged conduct.

## Resolving USSG Conflicts: USSG § 4B1.4(b) and Enhancements

A law student must understand how the Sentencing Guidelines resolve conflicts when multiple enhancements push the offense level higher than calculated by other sentencing statutes. In *Cutbank*, Cutbank contended that applying the four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement resulted in a within-range sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)." However, the court utilized USSG § 4B1.4(b) to resolve the tension between the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) penalties and the Guideline enhancements. The opinion explicitly states that "the Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies." 

This provision mandates that when calculating the offense level for sentencing purposes, the court must use the higher guideline level available. If the ACCA sentencing tier (resulting in a total offense level of 34 without the firearm enhancement) and the Guideline enhancement (resulting in 35) produce different levels, the Guidelines command the higher offense level for the purpose of determining the sentencing range. The court clarified that while the jury did not separately find the firearm was used in connection with other felonies, the district court was permitted to adopt the PSR’s factual findings at sentencing. Thus, the district court did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony. The appellate court affirmed that the stricter, higher guideline level governs the advisory range.

## 3553(a) Factors and the Requirement for Substantive Sentencing Reasonableness

Before a court of appeals can consider whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error regarding the Guidelines calculation, as established in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*. The opinion explicitly grounds its review in this procedural requirement: "Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range." Once the range is verified, the court shifts to § 3553(a) factors to determine if a departure or variance is warranted from the guidelines.

In *Cutbank*, the court noted that it did not require a mechanical recitation of the factors the district court weighed, but rather required that it is clear from the record that the court considered them. The opinion cites *United States v. Masood* and *United States v. Maluoth* to support the standard that simply knowing the factors are on the record is sufficient if the court acknowledges considering them. The court noted that Cutbank had acknowledged the § 3553(a) factors and the district court was aware of his arguments. The sentencing outcome itself—292 months imprisonment—was within the applicable guidelines range, which "we presume is reasonable on appeal." The opinion explains that a within-range sentence is presumed reasonable, and the district court’s review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda suggests the factors were weighed properly. Therefore, the appellate court refrains from second-guessing the substantive reasonableness unless the record indicates otherwise.

## District Court Discretion: Review Standards and Considerations

The appellate court applies a deferential standard of review to the district court’s discretion on sentencing discretion. The opinion states that "the district court did not abuse its substantial sentencing discretion and impose a substantively unreasonable sentence when it sentenced Cutbank to a within-range 292-month term of imprisonment." This deference is significant because sentencing judges possess broad judgment and capacity to weigh the § 3553(a) factors. Even when the appellate court finds that the sentence was within the statutory Guidelines range, it cannot overturn the decision unless the sentence is manifestly unreasonable or was based on a clear calculation error.

The court also addresses the preservation of the error. In discussing evidentiary rulings, the opinion notes the general principle that "this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless." While the current chapter focuses on sentencing, the reasoning applies broadly: "We will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." Similarly, regarding the Sentence of Reasonableness, the court notes that "the district court was aware of Cutbank’s arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them." This standard protects the district court’s broad discretion unless the court of appeals finds that the exercise of that discretion violates the reasons within the Guidelines or statutory scheme. Ultimately, the judgment of the district court is affirmed because the sentence was calculated correctly, the enhancements were properly applied, and the discretion was within the bounds of reasonableness.

# Court Holding and Disposition

## Disposition of Conviction Appeal: Affirmation of Guilty Verdict
The Eighth Circuit affirmed Justin Cutbank’s conviction on all counts, finding that the district court’s evidentiary admissions and the jury’s verdict were supported by substantial evidence. In this textbook example, the appellate court notes that the conviction relies on two distinct legal theories: the physical possession of a sawed-off firearm and the predicate crimes establishing the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). In *Cutbank* the 8th Cir clarifies that a jury does not need to specifically separate facts regarding the gun from facts regarding the priors when the evidence overwhelmingly supports the elements of the charged offense. The district court did not commit reversible error because the circumstantial evidence of possession was consistent with the aggressive conduct of the prior incidents. This highlights for students that on a review of a jury trial, the appellate court defers to the jury’s role as the sole arbiter of facts, provided the testimony and exhibits were legally admissible.

## Disposition of Sentencing Appeal: Affirmation of 292-Month Term
The Court affirmed the district court's imposition of a 292-month prison term, holding that the sentence was both procedurally and substantively reasonable. Cutbank argued that the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was incorrectly applied, claiming a minimum statutory sentence conflict with the purposes of § 3553(a). The appellate court reasoned that the district court properly calculated the Guidelines range based on the PSR (Presentence Investigation Report) findings, which were accepted without objection. The court emphasized that when a defendant is sentenced within the applicable Guidelines range, the appellate court presumes that sentence was reasonable. To challenge the reasonsableness of a sentence, the Court explained that one must first identify a "significant procedural error," such as failing to calculate the range correctly or ignoring mandatory factors. Since the range was calculated correctly and the defendant was sentenced within that range (though at the lower end), the appeal was dismissed on procedural grounds alone, as the defendant was required to demonstrate that the Guidelines calculation itself was flawed.

## Summary of Court Reasoning on Evidence Admissibility
The opinion details five primary evidentiary rulings that supported the defense's arguments. First, the Court affirmed the admission of prior possession testimony as Rule 404(b) non-projection evidence (*indeci*) relevant to proving ownership of the specific gun, citing *United States v. Battle*. Second, the Court upheld the district court's decision regarding hearsay limiting instructions and Confrontation Clause claims, stating that a limiting instruction can be provided at close of trial rather than during immediate cross-examination. Third, the Court ruled that testimony regarding the victims' prior hiring of the gun was admissible under Rule 403, as the probative value on possession outweighed the slight unfair prejudice of the victims' family history. Fourth, the Court dismissed the defense's attempt to read police reports due to the lack of proper foundation/authenticity. Finally, the opinion emphasizes the "harmless error" doctrine, noting that any potential evidentiary error must be shown to have substantially swayed the jury or not been cumulative with the overwhelming government evidence. This standard prevents the reversal of a trial verdict based on minor or cured procedural mishaps.

## Summary of Guidelines Calculation and Reasonableness
Regarding the Clerk calculation, the Ninth Circuit rejected Cutbank's claim that the enhanced sentence conflicted with § 3553(a). The PSR established a base offense level of 35 by combining the firearm's nature and the defendant's criminal history and risk factors. The Court noted that USSG § 4B1.4(b) dictates that the greater offense level applies, which places the sentence within the ACCA-enhanced advisory range. The Court also clarified the "clear error" standard for appellate review of sentencing guidelines, distinguishing between the "significant procedural error" standard for the initial calculation and the "substantive reasonableness" standard for the final sentence. The opinion concluded that because the district court reviewed the § 3553(a) factors and chose a 292-month sentence within the range, there was no abuse of discretion. This reinforces the principle that appellate courts should not second-guess a district court's calculated Guidelines range unless there is a clear mathematical error or a misapplication of the enhancement criteria.

## Educational Takeaway: Standard Threats vs. Substantive Review
This chapter illustrates the critical distinction between the *Standard of Review* for evidentiary rulings and the *Standard of Review* for substantive sentencing findings. While the opinion uses the adversarial term "standard threats" (likely referring to the threshold of the threshold claims), the legal core is the difference between deferential review and substantive review. On evidentiary issues, the appellate court reviews for "abuse of discretion," meaning the court will not disturb a decision unless the trial judge made a clearly erroneous or arbitrary judgment. In contrast, sentencing appeals often require a clearer showing of "significant procedural error" to trigger a "substantial reasonableness" review if the sentence was not within the Guidelines range. Students must understand that while evidentiary claims can be harmless even if erroneous, sentencing claims require a demonstration that the procedural error fundamentally altered the outcome, such as inserting a FYT (Factual) error or a calculation error. This distinction mandates that defendants carefully preserve errors by citing case law and analysis on appeal, asCutbank failed to do, leading to reversal of the evidentiary challenge.
