Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What is the procedural standard for admitting policing reports as extrinsic evidence in defense cross-examination?
Question:
Section II.E mentions that a motion to strike C.F.'s testimony regarding prior statements was denied because the police report was not authenticated. The court cites *Cumbie* regarding the requirement for proper authentication of extrinsic evidence for impeachment. Can someone clarify the specific 8th Circuit standard here—is the report treated as hearsay requiring the original author's signature or testimony, and when does an unauthenticated report become inadmissible as a matter of law in a collateral factual finding?
Answer:
### The Procedural Standard for Admitting Extrinsic Evidence in Impeachment

Based on the Eighth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the procedural standard for admitting policing reports as extrinsic evidence during defense cross-examination rests on the requirement of **authentication**, reviewed under an **abuse of discretion** standard.

**1. The Authentication Requirement**
The court explicitly applies the standard from *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), which stipulates that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be properly authenticated before it can be admitted. The Eighth Circuit held the dough court did not abuse its discretion to reject defense counsel's attempt to read portions of a police report into the record "without proper foundation because the report was not authored by C.F." Therefore, the report is treated as extrinsic evidence for the purpose of *Cumbie* which requires authentication.

**2. Implications for Hearsay and Confrontation**
The opinion notes that the government provided a limiting instruction that hearsay statements from a witness cannot be used substantively until the witness testifies. While the defense questioned the authenticity of the report itself (which were not authenticated), the court affirmed that such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated to be admissible. The failure to authenticate prevents the defense from using police report excerpts to cross-examine a witness on prior inconsistent statements.

**3. Standard of Review and Harmless Error**
Regarding the procedural outcome of an improper evidentiary ruling, the court standard applies the rule of **abuse of discretion**. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. Specifically, "this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence" (*DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997).

**Conclusion**
In summary, under *United States v. Cutbank*, a policing report cannot be read as part of a defense cross-examination to impeach a witness on a prior inconsistent statement unless the defense provides a foundation demonstrating its authentication (generally via authorship or testimony). The appellate court reviews this evidentiary ruling with abuse of discretion and will only reverse if the improper exclusion is not cured by the harmless error doctrine.
