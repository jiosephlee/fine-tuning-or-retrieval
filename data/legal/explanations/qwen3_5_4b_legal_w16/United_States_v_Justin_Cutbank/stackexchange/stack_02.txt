Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the cumulative error test apply when reviewing multiple evidentiary rulings for harmless error?
Question:
In Section II.E, the court discusses the standard for harmless error and cumulative effect. I am confused by the distinction between the *DeMarce* citation (requiring substantial sway) and the *Keys* citation (cumulative effect). If there were multiple evidentiary errors—like the Rule 404(b) admission, the hearsay instruction refusal, and the authentication issue—does a 'substantial' prejudice require a specific amount of evidence to be excluded, or does the mere presence of multiple rulings change the standard of review for the appellate court?
Answer:
### Harmless Error and Cumulative Prejudice: A Standard of Substantial Prejudice

The short answer to your question is that the presence of multiple evidentiary rulings does not alter the standard of review itself. Under the Eighth Circuit, the mere accumulation of errors does not mandate reversal; rather, the appellate court retains a sole focus on whether those multiple rulings, taken together, caused **"substantial prejudice"** to the defendant.

Based on the Eighth Circuit’s reasoning in *United States v. Cutbank*, 24-1225 (Oct. 7, 2025), the distinction between the *DeMarce* and *Keys* citations lies in the scope of analysis, but both apply to the same substantive threshold.

### The Legal Standard: DeMarce and Keys

In Section II.E of the opinion, the court delineates two distinct but related prerequisites for reversal under the harmless error doctrine:

1.  **Individual Error Standard (*DeMarce*):** The court cites *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), establishing the baseline test for any single evidentiary ruling. The standard is low-the appellate court must determine that the error did not "substantially sway" the jury. If a ruling was "harmless," the conviction stands. As the *Cutbank* opinion states: *"this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless... in other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence."*

2.  **Cumulative Error Standard (*Keys*):** The court then addresses the scenario where there are *multiple* errors, citing *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019). This citation does not lower the bar for prejudice; rather, it sets a strict rule for when that cumulative analysis affects the verdict. The opinion clarifies that even review of cumulative effects is gated by the same requirement: *"We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant."*

Therefore, the "mere presence of multiple rulings" does not create a separate standard of review that triggers reversal. If the cumulative effect is alleged, the appellate court applies the *Cutbank* standard: the defendant must prove the cumulative errors substantially swayed the jury’s verdict.

### Application in *Cutbank*

In *Cutbank*, the court applied this dual standard to dismiss cuts of the evidentiary record. While Cutbank argued that multiple rulings—specifically the admission of prior possessions, refusal to give a hearsay instruction, and authentication of reports—should collectively prejudice his conviction, the appellate court found he failed to meet the substantive burden.

The court noted that the government's evidence was substantial and corroborated by both testimonial and physical evidence (*Federal R. Evidence 403* analysis). Because the government established a strong foundation for the charge, the court concluded that the cumulative influence of the errors remained "only a slight influence on the verdict." As explicitly stated in Section II.E: *"The government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial. Thus, the district court’s evidentiary rulings 'did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.'"*

### Conclusion

To answer your specific question: A 'substantial' prejudice requires showing that the error (even if cumulative) swung the jury's decision. The multiple evidentiary rulings in *Cutbank* did not create a "specific amount of evidence to be excluded"; rather, the defense failed to demonstrate that the **cumulative prejudice** was substantial enough to overcome the weight of the admitted evidence.

In short, the standard of review for both individual and cumulative errors remains tethered to the requirement of **"substantial prejudice"** to the defendant. Multiple rulings do not change the standard of review; they simply accumulate, yet the final threshold for reversality is still the high bar set by *DeMarce* and confirmed by *Keys* in *Cutbank*.
