Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# The Intrinsic Requirement: When Prior Possession Is Evidence of Ownership

The interplay between evidentiary rules and the mechanics of proving possession forms a critical intersection in federal criminal law. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit provided a clear, rigorous reaffirmation that prior possession of a specific firearm is not inherently inadmissible "propensity evidence" under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). While Propositions they apply, the Court clarified that when firearms are present in the scene, testing prior possession establishes the ownership or control necessary to prove the current possession charge. This ruling reinforces the necessity of analyzing the intrinsic nature of evidence when possession is contested.

## The Intrinsic Requirement for Ownership Proof

A prolonged appellate history has developed around Rule 404(b) and the probative value of prior statements when evidence is offered. Defense presentations often argue for the suppression of prior encounter evidence, citing character evidence or instructions regarding the limitations of use within the jury's mind. The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument in the case of Cutbank, who faced charges for felon in possession of a firearm after an incident involving domestic violence and confinement. The court determined that the testimony was not merely relevant to character, but was intrinsic to proving the base elements of the crime.

The key to this distinction lies in the nature of the prosecution's evidentiary goal. The Court noted that "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." By framing the defense's argument solely as a crime of character, the appellate court highlighted that Dismissal orders aimed at 404(b) material often fail where the evidence links the defendant to the specific object. The court's reasoning emphasized that without proof of direct ownership, the possession charge collapses. This clarifies that the "intrinsic evidence" exception is not a loophole for character attacks on the witness, but a procedural safeguard for establishing the necessary control required for possession.

## Confrontation and Notification of Rights

The evidentiary ruling in *Cutbank* was bolstered by the district court's handling of hearsay and Confrontation Clause challenges during the trial. The defense challenged the admissibility of statements made to police that were not testifying firsthand, but the Court affirmed that where a declarant testifies in person, the clause requirement is satisfied. This alignment of testimony and evidentiary admissibility meant that the district court's discretion to admit hearsay as context or foundation did not constitute reversible error. Furthermore, the Eighth Circuit noted that the district court did not need to instruct the jury immediately on each item unless required, as long as limiting instructions could follow the close of trial. While this undermines strict proceduralist arguments regarding trial management, it reduces the administrative burden on the bench without violating constitutional guarantees of fair notice.

## Impact on Sentencing Guidelines and 3553(a)

Beyond the evidentiary issue, *Cutbank* provided an interesting look at the sentencing calculus involving the Armed Career Criminal Act and the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. Cutbank argued that applying the four-level enhancement for possession in connection with a felony offense resulted in a sentence that conflicted with the underlying sentencing principles of § 3553(a). Specifically, there was a conflict regarding whether the acceleration of the guideline range or the advisory base level would govern the final sentence. The Court affirmed the sentence within the guidelines range, noting that a district court must calculate the range correctly before considering substantive reasonableness. This reinforces the rule that the guidelines dictate the structure of the sentence, even if the 3553(a) factors offer a broader range of discretion.

Ultimately, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder of the standard pressure test that guides lower courts in federal prosecutions: can the defense show that specific evidence benefits a purpose outside the elements of the offense, or is the prior possession itself the foundation of guilt. As the opinion notes, the court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. In a case as factually heavy as this one, the weight of the evidence outweighed the procedural tendency to suppress. The ruling stands as a beacon of efficiency in possession cases while noting that defendants must consider whether their own factual construction of the evidence meets the standard of prejudice.

## Conclusion

The Eighth Circuit's decision in *Cutbank* does not simply affirm a conviction; it solidifies the mechanism by which prior possession evidence is integrated into the proof of ownership. By distinguishing this evidence from outlier propensity evidence, the Court ensures that jurors are presented with the relevant context necessary to adjudicate gun possession crimes. For defense counsel, this implies that motion practice should focus harder on validating the connection to the current charge rather than simply labeling the evidence inadmissible. For practitioners, it highlights the importance of understanding how prior possession is defined and measured against the current charge in government prosecutions. The reasoning here provides a stable framework for evidentiary rulings, balancing the right to a fair trial with the utilitarian need for accurate fact-finding.



# Beyond the ACCA Cap: Why Specific Firearms Enhancements Survive the Guideline Calculation

## Why This Case Matters

The intersection between the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and specific sentencing enhancements often marks the line where statutory penalties meet judicial discretion, and *United States v. Cutbank* is prime evidence of the tension. The Eighth Circuit’s decision serves as a reminder that granting a defendant the benefit of an ACCA finding—fixed and severe for prior violent felonies—does not act as a blanket immunization against granular guideline adjustments that acknowledge the specific, immediate dangers presented in a particular instance of gun violence. Historically, the ACCA has functioned as a "backstop" to dealer the maximum length of prison for career felons, but this holding clarifies that the ACCA does not override the Sentencing Guidelines' federal policy to assess the unique nature of the weapon at issue, even after finding the defendant eligible for heightened penalties based on past conduct.

For defense attorneys and appellate judges alike, the decision stabilizes an expectation that no amount of aggressive statutory interpretation of the ACCA can entirely erase the specific harm of the current offense.

## Navigating the Procedural Minefield

Before diving into the sentencing equilibrium, it is worth noting that Cutbank successfully garnered support in the Eighth Circuit on multiple procedural fronts. The appeal turned largely on an array of evidentiary rulings that were challenged only after the case had proceeded to a conviction. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's decision regarding testimony from previous possessors of the firearm. The court reasoned that prior possession was directly relevant to establishing continuous control of the weapon, a stance consistent with the Eighth Circuit's reasoning in *Battle*. This evidentiary affirmative serves as a robust reminder of how admissible testimony must support the existence of the possession itself, tying the weapon directly to the defendant more than a simple hearsay exemption allows.

However, the more consequential component of the decision is the dismissal of the defendant's arguments regarding the sentencing enhancement itself. Cutbank appealed the mandatory application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), arguing that applying a four-level enhancement for firearm possession after triggering ACCA penalties violated the due process and specific purposes of § 3553(a).

## The Sentencing Equilibrium

At the heart of the Eighth Circuit's reasoning lies the conclusion that the ACCA finding, while setting a baseline for the defendant’s criminal history, does not exceed the need for sentencing to reflect the specific circumstances of the current offense. The district court had adopted a sentencing guideline recommendation based on a sawed-off rifle with an altered serial number, which resulted in a total offense level of 35. While the ACCA findings alone would have produced an offense level of 34, the specific harm of the firearm operating in the PSR outweighed the relatively minor difference in offense level.

The court invoked USSG § 4B1.4(b), noting that "the greater offense level applies." The Eighth Circuit declined to grant Cutbank relief that would invoke a variance from the guidelines range solely based on the overlap between ACCA and the specific firearm enhancement. The reasoning was clear: the applicant for the firearm enhancement is simply identifying that the gun used was different in nature (e.g., altered serial number, sawed-off barrel), which justifies a higher offense level on top of the ACCA baseline. The appeal court viewed the specific harm of the gun as necessary to account for the unique specifics of the use of that firearm, even in the context of a career violent felon.

## Implications for Sentencing Discretion

The importance of this decision lies in the guidance it offers for future sentencing proceedings. It reinforces the principle that the Guidelines act as a structured mechanism for assessing the specific nature of the crime, even when the underlying culpability for prior felonies pushes the defendant into a higher statutory tier. This means that defense counsel may look elsewhere for arguments challenging excessive sentences under § 3553(a), but they should accept the baseline of the enhancement provisions for conduct that the Guidelines explicitly design for categorizing specific firearms and their serial numbers.

Furthermore, the decision ensures that the ACCA remains a determinant of prior violence eligibility, but does not suggest that the specificity of the current offense need to be dampened to fit a legislative grid. This preservation of specific sentencing enhancements is important for ensuring that the severity of sentencing remains proportional to the immediate threat presented by the weapon at issue, regardless of how far back the criminal litigation record recedes.



# When the Court Says 'Irrelevant': Hearsay, Limiting Instructions, and the No-Instruction Precedent

The complexity of federal criminal trials often turns on technical procedural scaffolding much more than the edifice of substantive law. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed a holding that functions as a significant check on prosecutorial strategy and defense counsel’s burden: when a declarant actually appears for trial, a limiting instruction on hearsay is often unnecessary. While prosecutors often rely on live testimony to establish the context of evidence admissible beforehand, the lower court in this instance found that requiring a jury deliberation on what was irrelevant might actually expose the party to unnecessary prejudice. The question of when to give a limiting instruction is narrow, yet its ripple effects regarding the Confrontation Clause and evidentiary discretion are substantial. As the Eighth Circuit navigated a charged domestic violence and firearms case, it used this procedural conduit to affirm the district court’s broad evidentiary discretion, reinforcing a line of precedent that prioritizes clarity in jury deliberations over rigid warnings to jurors.

Procedually, the trial presented a stark choice: permit cross-examination on prior possession or forbid it as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. In Section II.C of the opinion, Loken Circuit Judge explicitly states that prior possession is intrinsically relevant, but the defense argued that probing such factors without direct confrontation violated the Confrontation Clause. The key here was that the declarants, specifically Detainant D.F. and witness C.F., did not simply testify to the events in court; they also testified that they had previously observed the weapon. The Eighth Circuit rejected Cutbank’s argument that a limiting instruction was necessary to separate the hearsay. Relying on *Brown* and *Charboneau*, the panel noted that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when declarants appear and testify in person. The reasoning is that if the witness is there to tell the truth, the jury can do the work of weighing credibility without needing a mechanical instruction on what they can and cannot rely on. This is a powerful tool in the prosecutor’s toolkit, as it allows them to effectively question witnesses about events they can’t directly testify to, without triggering a constitutional panic that might taint the jury’s view of the live testimony.

The court’s application of this standard in *Cutbank* is significant because it underscores the reliance on the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause to validate the weight of hearsay in the absence of a direct instruction. As the opinion notes, prior possession helps establish ownership and control, which is crucial in possession cases. The court omitted the need for a limiting instruction because the witnesses were present to testify personally, effectively clearing the path for question-and-answer regarding witness observation of prior acts. This reflects the Eighth Circuit’s body of law that emphasizes the judge’s discretion in instructing juries, consistent with the hub that obscuring relevant evidence is a rare violation that warrants review. By adopting the position that the absence of a limiting instruction does not constitute an error, the court signals that the constitutional mandate to be present is the primary bar rather than the potential usability of information for impeachment. This is a win for procedural efficiency, as it reduces the risk of inflaming a jury by explaining that certain statements are merely character evidence.

However, there is a tension in this approach, as noted by the appellate court itself. If the use of limiting instructions were consistently blocked by a blanket reliance on the Confrontation Clause, it could encourage unethical practices where defense counsel might inadvertently mislead the jury on the nature of extrinsic evidence. The court clarified this by emphasizing that limiting instructions are not always required if the witness is testifying. But as the court acknowledged, there is a standard that can trigger an instruction, specifically when an item of evidence is sensitive and not proper to be considered by the jury in the manner in question. The *Battle* and *Charboneau* citations show that this is not a new rule but a reaffirmation of established standards. As a practical matter, this means defense attorneys who seek to object to the admissibility of hearsay must be prepared to argue the direct confrontation, as the mere absence of an instruction may be assumed unless there is clear prejudice. This burden shifts the focus of litigation from the mechanics of the instruction to the actual confrontation of the witness.

Looking downward, the implications of this ruling suggest that the right to confront counsel is not the only right to be exercised in a criminal trial; the jury’s right to a clear verdict may also require a more flexible approach to procedural direction. If the district court’s discretion to waive the instruction stands, the Eighth Circuit signals that judges will likely maintain the same level of deference in evidentiary rulings as long as the core constitutional rights were met through direct testimony. This case, which ultimately affirmed both conviction and a 292-month sentence grounded in ACCA enhancements, demonstrates that these procedural victories rarely come at the expense of substantive outcomes in the Eighth Circuit. The court suggests that where the offensive evidence is strong and the evidence is properly authenticated under Fed. R. Evidence 403, the defense does not need to win the signal battle over instructions. Ultimately, *Cutbank* offers a rebuke to counsel who argues that minor procedural irritations should dictate the outcome, encouraging a focus on the actual confrontation rather than the absence of an instruction.
