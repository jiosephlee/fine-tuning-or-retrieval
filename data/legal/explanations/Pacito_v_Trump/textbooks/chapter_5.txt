Chapter 5: # The USRAP Ecosystem: Who Does What and How

The United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) is not a single agency or program; it is an interlocking ecosystem of federal offices, statutory duties, cooperative and contractual relationships with nongovernmental partners, and annual appropriations. The recent Ninth Circuit expedited proceedings in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) provide a useful lens for understanding how this ecosystem operates in practice and how disputes about roles, instruments, and funding turn into litigation. This chapter explains the operational division of labor among the Department of State (and its Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration, PRM), the Department of Health and Human Services (via the Office of Refugee Resettlement, ORR), and the private nonprofit resettlement organizations (the “VOLAGs” and their local affiliates). It also walks through the legal instruments—cooperative agreements, grants, and contracts—used to deliver services, the distinct legal consequences of those instruments for administrative-law review, and how funding flows and appropriation constraints shape what actually can be done on the ground.

## State Department (PRM) and ‘Reception and Placement’ (R&P) cooperative agreements

At the overseas and initial U.S.-entry level, responsibility largely lies with the State Department’s Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration (PRM). PRM coordinates overseas processing of refugee claims and manages key cooperative agreements with international and domestic nongovernmental organizations to facilitate both overseas processing and the immediate reception of refugees once admitted. The Ninth Circuit’s opinion in Pacito frames this role: the State Department had entered into cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations to provide reception and placement (R&P) services and then terminated those agreements in February 2025, precipitating the litigation.

Reception and placement (R&P) services refer to the immediate, short-term services that newly admitted refugees receive—transportation from port of entry, initial reception at airport or arrival point, short-term housing, an initial package of cash support, health checks/referrals, case management, and rapid employment services or referrals. These services exist to bridge the initial gap after admission and to carry out the statutory purpose of facilitating resettlement and self-sufficiency.

Legally, PRM most often uses “cooperative agreements” with public or private nonprofit organizations (the VOLAGs and their local affiliates) to deliver R&P services. Cooperative agreements are one of the statutory mechanisms contemplated by Congress in the Refugee Act and related statutory schemes for funding and partnering with NGOs; they permit PRM to be more programmatically involved than a simple grant relationship. The Ninth Circuit’s order recognized the historic use of cooperative agreements for these functions and tied their termination to the government’s responsibility under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. In practice, PRM’s cooperative agreements typically contain programmatic obligations, reporting requirements, and conditions about performance, but they remain assistance instruments that depend on appropriations.

Because PRM manages the overseas flow and the handoff to domestic resettlement partners, decisions at PRM about terminating cooperative agreements have immediate, operational consequences: if PRM ends cooperative agreements without a viable alternative, there may be no organized mechanism to receive and place admitted refugees in U.S. communities. That factual reality informed the Ninth Circuit’s interim remedy directing reinstatement of necessary cooperative agreements pending fuller adjudication.

## HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) grants for longer-term integration services under § 1522

On the domestic side, longer‑term services and integration efforts—such as cash and medical assistance for up to thirty-six months, employment training and placement, English language services, and special protections for unaccompanied refugee children—are statutorily tied to the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) within HHS, principally under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Section 1522 and implementing regulations (e.g., 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.) create the statutory framework for the federal government’s provision of resettlement assistance and authorize (and, as litigated, arguably constrain) the types of programs ORR should support.

ORR typically distributes funds through grants and cooperative agreements to state and local agencies and to nonprofit resettlement organizations. These instruments fund the medium-term services that enable economic self-sufficiency: employment services, English-learning programs, case management, and targeted cash/medical supports. ORR also exercises a statutory responsibility with respect to unaccompanied children, including assuming certain responsibilities “during any interim period while such a child is in the United States” (8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B)), which can entail immediate financial and legal responsibility in limited circumstances.

The Ninth Circuit’s order emphasized § 1522’s statutory regime when evaluating whether the termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of funding could violate the Administrative Procedure Act. The court distinguished the President’s broad immigration entry authority (8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)) from statutory obligations to provide services to refugees already admitted. In short: PRM and ORR have different but complementary roles—PRM handles overseas processing and arrival logistics; ORR funds and oversees domestic integration services—but both operate within statutory and appropriations constraints.

## Public and private nonprofit partners: the role of voluntary agencies (VOLAGs) and affiliates

Private nonprofit partners—often called “VOLAGs” (voluntary agencies)—are the operational backbone of USRAP inside the United States, and many also provide overseas liaison or processing support. Historically prominent VOLAGs include Church World Service, HIAS, Lutheran Immigration and Refugee Service, U.S. Committee for Refugees and Immigrants, Episcopal Migration Ministries, and others. These national VOLAGs manage national intake, coordinate local affiliate networks, and receive federal funding (via grants, cooperative agreements, or contracts) to deliver R&P and longer-term integration services. Local affiliates and subcontractors carry out on-the-ground tasks—housing placements, initial casework, employment services, and local community integration.

The legal relationship between VOLAGs and the federal government is shaped by the instrument used (cooperative agreement vs grant vs contract), by the terms of individual agreements (performance metrics, reporting, and termination clauses), and by applicable federal assistance rules (e.g., 2 C.F.R. Part 200 and agency-specific regulations). VOLAGs act as both implementers of public policy and as private parties that may have statutory or contractual expectations. As Pacito demonstrates, when the government abruptly terminates cooperative agreements, VOLAGs may seek judicial relief—either on statutory or APA grounds—arguing that the termination deprives refugees of congressionally required services or rests on arbitrary or unlawful agency action.

VOLAGs’ enforcement options vary. Where an agreement creates enforceable rights or is a “final agency action” for APA purposes, a recipient may challenge its termination in federal court under the APA. If a termination implicates breach-of-contract theories, sovereign immunity can limit remedies unless an express waiver exists in the contract. The structure and wording of funding instruments therefore matter greatly to downstream legal remedies.

## Cooperative agreements vs. grants vs. contracts: legal distinctions and implications for APA review

Practically and legally, it matters whether the federal government uses a cooperative agreement, a grant, or a procurement contract. Each instrument carries different expectations about federal involvement, statutory oversight, procurement law, and the potential for judicial review.

- Cooperative agreements: These are assistance instruments used when the federal agency expects substantial programmatic involvement with the recipient. The government plays an ongoing role in program design, monitoring, and technical collaboration. Cooperative agreements are governed by the agency’s grant regulations and by the Uniform Guidance (2 C.F.R. Part 200) for federal awards. Their collaborative nature does not automatically make them federal procurement contracts, but they do reflect a close operational relationship. When terminated, cooperative agreements often present claims under the APA (if the termination is an “agency action”), and under the statutory framework applicable to federal assistance. Courts may treat termination of cooperative agreements as reviewable final agency action where the termination has legal consequences (cf. Bennett v. Spear) or where statutes create mandatory duties that funding decisions frustrate.

- Grants: Grants are assistance instruments where the federal government generally provides funds with more limited programmatic involvement. Grants remain subject to uniform grant rules and typically contemplate greater recipient discretion in program execution. Like cooperative agreements, grants depend on congressional appropriations and are governed by grant regulations. Challenges to grant decisions—award, denial, or termination—are sometimes treated as nonreviewable if they amount to policy choices or resource allocation, but they can be reviewable where the agency’s action is final, has legal consequences, or violates statutory duties.

- Contracts: Procurement contracts are governed by procurement law and the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR). They are exchange agreements in which the government procures specific goods or services for its own use. Contract law claims typically proceed in the Court of Federal Claims or under procurement dispute mechanisms; sovereign immunity is often waived under the Contract Disputes Act for bilateral remediation, but different remedies and doctrines apply than for grants/cooperative agreements.

For APA review, the central question is whether the agency action being challenged is “final” and not committed to agency discretion by law. Bennett v. Spear supplies the two-part test for final agency action: (1) the action must mark the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process (i.e., not be tentative or interlocutory); and (2) it must be one by which rights or obligations have been determined or from which legal consequences will flow. The Ninth Circuit’s order in Pacito found that termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of USRAP funding could produce such legal consequences—implicating statutory duties under § 1522—and thus may be reviewable under the APA. This contrasts with pure programmatic, resource-allocation decisions that the Supreme Court has often treated as nonreviewable (Heckler v. Chaney) unless statutory duties produce a different calculus.

One practical implication: agencies that want to preserve discretion should draft assistance instruments and termination decisions carefully to emphasize discretionary language and to avoid creating enforceable legal entitlements—though statutory frameworks (like § 1522) may limit that effort.

## Funding flows and appropriation constraints; rescissions and apportionment

The legal reality of USRAP is shaped by the concentric rings of statutory authorization, congressional appropriation, and executive apportionment. § 1522 and related statutory provisions authorize programs and specify statutory expectations. But authorization alone does not create an appropriation: Congress must appropriate funds in annual or supplemental appropriation acts for agencies to obligate and expend federal money. The Pacito opinion underscores this duality: the panel recited § 1522’s duties while also acknowledging that many of those duties are qualified “to the extent of available appropriations.” Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent focused on this point—reading § 1522 as an authorization framework that contemplates but does not independently create funding obligations in the absence of appropriations.

Appropriations law also encompasses rescissions and apportionment. Congress may enact rescissions that reduce previously appropriated sums (the Pacito opinion cited the FY2025 appropriations figure of $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance and a subsequent $800 million rescission). Once Congress rescinds or reduces appropriations, agencies must operate within those reduced limits and OMB must apportion the remaining funds during the fiscal year. Antideficiency Act constraints prohibit agencies from obligating funds beyond the appropriated and apportioned amounts, so executive decisions—like suspending program funding—must be grounded in lawful appropriation management.

The Ninth Circuit observed these fiscal facts when balancing the stay factors: the court recognized statutory duties but also the reality that funding depends on congressional appropriations and that the executive exercises discretion when apportioning or suspending obligations. Where the agency claims it cannot fulfill statutory functions because funds have been rescinded or not apportioned, courts will weigh the administration’s representation against evidentiary showings. In Pacito, the government told the court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency” yet could not identify any progress; the court treated that uncertainty as part of the justification for directing interim reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to meet statutory obligations.

The practical lesson is that statutory “shall” language must be read in context of appropriations clauses and the separation of powers. Courts will confront tensions between congressional mandates and appropriations cuts and may order relief where the record shows the executive cannot lawfully or practically fulfill statutory duties without reinstating existing arrangements.

## Practical consequences and doctrinal takeaways

Pacito highlights several recurring doctrinal themes a practitioner or student should internalize:

- Allocation of authority matters. The President’s broad statutory authority under § 1182(f) to suspend admission of classes of aliens is distinct from statutory duties to provide services to aliens already admitted. Courts will afford deference on entry controls but scrutinize executive actions that frustrate specific statutory programs.

- The choice of instrument matters. Cooperative agreements create programmatic entanglement and often produce concrete legal consequences when terminated—consequences that may be reviewable under the APA. Grants provide more recipient discretion but are still tied to appropriations and regulatory strings. Contracts live in procurement law’s separate world.

- Statutory language matters. “Shall” and “authorized” create different expectations. “To the extent of available appropriations” is a limiting clause that frequently controls outcome, but it does not automatically nullify statutory expectations; courts will examine whether appropriations cuts or executive rescissions render the agency incapable of meeting mandatory duties.

- Funding is political and legal. Appropriations and rescissions are congressional choices that constrain agencies; OMB apportionment and the Antideficiency Act constrain administrative action. An agency that terminates cooperative agreements because of budgetary choices must show a lawful and reasoned basis that accords with statutes and regulations.

- Remedies will be tailored to operational realities. Where termination of agreements produces immediate harm and the government cannot show an alternative to achieve statutory aims, courts may order reinstatement of agreements or other equitable relief pending fuller review—what the Ninth Circuit directed here.

For students, litigators, and policymakers, Pacito is a reminder that USRAP is a layered system of statutory directives, appropriations, federal implementing instruments, and private partners. Understanding who does what—PRM overseas and for R&P cooperative agreements; ORR for longer-term integration under § 1522; and VOLAGs as the implementers—is necessary to predict legal exposure and to design instruments and litigation strategies. When disputes arise, the legal classification of an instrument, the statutory text, and the fiscal record will often be dispositive both for reviewability and for the form of judicial relief.
