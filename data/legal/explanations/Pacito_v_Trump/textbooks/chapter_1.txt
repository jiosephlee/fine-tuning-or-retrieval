Chapter 1: # Presidential Power, Refugee Protection, and Judicial Review: An Overview

This chapter examines how the President’s authority over the admission of noncitizens, the statutory architecture of the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), and the courts’ reviewing posture interact when the executive takes emergency or broad policy action affecting refugees. The recent Ninth Circuit interim order in Pacito v. Trump, Nos. 25-1313, 25-1939 (9th Cir. Sept. 12, 2025) (stay pending appeal), provides a compact case study: the court accepted that the President may suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) yet identified a distinct avenue of judicial enforcement for statutorily mandated resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. From that starting point we trace four strands law students must master: (1) the plenary-power doctrine and its evolution; (2) the difference between inherent executive power and powers delegated by statute; (3) the statutory design and operation of USRAP; and (4) the institutional rules and standards courts use to review executive decisions in immigration and foreign affairs.

## The plenary power doctrine and its evolution in immigration law

The plenary power doctrine—often summarized as the political branches’ broad authority to control entry and removal of noncitizens—rests on a long line of decisions dating to the late nineteenth century. The Supreme Court’s early pronouncements in cases such as Chae Chan Ping v. United States (the Chinese Exclusion Case) and Fong Yue Ting v. United States established that control of the border and immigration is “a political question” largely entrusted to the political branches. Those early rulings recognized that border control implicates national sovereignty and foreign relations, and thus courts should exercise restraint.

Over time, the Court refined the practical consequences of plenary power: it does not mean that the Constitution and statutes are irrelevant, but it does mean that courts typically defer to the political branches when they exercise authority over admission and exclusion. This deference has several dimensions. First, when the President acts under statute to regulate entry, courts often interpret the statute in light of a strong presumption of executive latitude, especially where national security or foreign affairs are invoked. Second, where the President acts pursuant to what he asserts are inherent powers (those tied to the constitutional role in foreign affairs and national security), courts treat the action as presumptively within executive competence, subject to only a limited judicial inquiry.

A modern articulation of this deference appears in Trump v. Hawaii (2018). The Court there upheld presidential travel restrictions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and reasoning that review should not involve “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications” when the statutory grant confers broad authority in a field touching national security and foreign relations. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito relied on that language, describing § 1182(f)’s text as one that “exudes deference” and concluding the President had “undoubtedly” made the required finding that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Pacito thus exemplifies the contemporary rule: when the President acts under § 1182(f) to suspend entry of a class of aliens, courts will generally treat the President’s factual finding and policy judgment as within the core of executive discretion.

But plenary power is not absolute. The courts retain the power to interpret Congress’s statutes and to enforce statutory rights where the political branches’ actions collide with clear statutory mandates. Plenary power reduces—but does not obliterate—judicial review. The boundary between judicial modesty in immigration and the obligation to enforce statutory and constitutional limits is a central doctrinal tension illustrated in Pacito, where the panel granted a stay as to admissions but declined to stay the district court’s order directed to resettlement services payable to refugees already admitted.

## Delegated statutory authority vs. inherent executive power

Distinguishing between authority that is inherent to the President and authority delegated by Congress is crucial. Inherent executive power derives from the Constitution’s allocation of foreign affairs and commander-in-chief functions to the President. Delegated authority arises when Congress enacts a statute that assigns certain powers or procedures to the executive branch.

8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is a paradigmatic example of a statutory delegation that is interpreted against the backdrop of the executive’s foreign-affairs responsibilities. It provides that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may … suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens … or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.” The statute’s language—particularly the “may” and the requirement of a presidential “find[ing]”—has been interpreted to grant the President very broad discretion to restrict entry. Courts have historically treated such delegations as entitling the President to substantial deference, especially where national security and foreign relations are implicated.

But delegated authority has limits: Congress can impose substantive obligations and procedural constraints on how the executive acts. Where a statute uses mandatory language (“shall”), establishes substantive duties, conditions relief, or ties obligations to appropriations and reporting, courts will consider whether the executive action is consistent with the statute’s mandatory terms. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit applied this bifurcated approach. It concluded that the President’s use of § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions fit comfortably within the broad delegation of power recognized in Trump v. Hawaii. By contrast, the court treated certain provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1522—statutory provisions addressing reception and placement services—as potentially imposing enforceable duties on the executive that could be violated by unilateral defunding and termination of cooperative agreements.

Two doctrinal tools frame this analysis. First, statutory interpretation: when Congress has clearly required the executive to take specific actions (for example, “shall” combined with unambiguous directives and mandatory reporting requirements), courts are likelier to find non-discretionary duties. Second, the Administrative Procedure Act and related nondelegation/reviewability doctrines govern whether the courts can review agency or executive action that allegedly violates statutory commands. The APA provides a general cause of action—except where agency action is “committed to agency discretion by law,” 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)—and authorizes review for actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law,” 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The key question becomes whether the statute either committed the matter to executive discretion or conversely provided sufficient legal standards for the courts to apply.

This divide is precisely the locus of disagreement in Pacito. The majority read § 1522 as creating enforceable obligations to provide specified reception and placement services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and therefore subject to judicial enforcement under the APA. Judge Lee’s partial dissent argued § 1522’s structure, headings, and frequent use of the verb “authorize” support a permissive reading: authorization to act, not a judicially enforceable command to operate programs. The dispute illustrates how textual cues—titles, verbs, cross-references, and appropriations language—shape courts’ conclusions about whether a statutory provision constitutes an enforceable mandate or a grant of discretionary authority.

## The U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP): purposes and architecture

The Refugee Act of 1980 substantially reorganized federal refugee policy and created the contemporary architecture of USRAP. Negusie v. Holder (2009) described the Refugee Act as establishing “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” USRAP is a multi‑agency enterprise with distinct roles:

- The Department of Homeland Security (through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, USCIS) adjudicates refugee claims and determines eligibility for resettlement.
- The Department of State manages overseas processing and works with international partners and non‑governmental organizations to identify and refer cases to the United States.
- The Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), primarily through the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), is statutorily charged with reception and placement—or “resettlement”—services for refugees after arrival.

Congress authorized and funded refugee programs through provisions now codified at, among other places, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and § 1523. Section 1522 contemplates a suite of services: employment training and placement; English language instruction; cash and medical assistance projects; and particular protections and responsibilities regarding unaccompanied refugee children. The statute authorizes the Secretary (and the Director of ORR) to provide these services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and authorizes the use of contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies to deliver services.

In practice, resettlement in the United States has been delivered through cooperative agreements and grants with a network of national and local non‑profits. These organizations perform intake, provide initial housing and cash assistance, enroll refugees in social services, place refugees with employment and language programs, and coordinate with local communities. Because of the logistical complexity and the importance of local knowledge, the federal government has long relied on cooperative agreements with nonprofits for reception and placement.

Pacito puts the practical operation into relief. There, an executive order suspending admissions was accompanied by a separate suspension or termination of funding for USRAP cooperative agreements. The district court enjoined significant parts of the executive action; on appeal the Ninth Circuit recognized that the President’s statutory authority to suspend entry under § 1182(f) likely supported suspension of admissions, but it also confronted the statutory text of § 1522 and the machine of resettlement. The court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted, based on the conclusion that the government might be unable to show it could meet § 1522’s mandates. Thus, USRAP’s multi-agency structure and reliance on cooperative agreements create discrete legal claims: one about the executive’s authority to stop future admissions, and another about statutory duties to care for those already admitted.

## Courts’ institutional role and standards of deference in immigration and foreign affairs

When courts review executive action affecting immigration and foreign affairs, they balance three institutional considerations: the need to enforce legal limits imposed by Congress and the Constitution, the need for judicially manageable standards, and respect for the political branches’ prerogatives in national security and external affairs. The results are a series of doctrinally distinct—but overlapping—standards.

1. Deference under statutory delegations in the national‑security/foreign‑affairs context. As the Supreme Court explained in Trump v. Hawaii, courts should accord substantial deference where Congress has delegated broad authority and where action touches foreign affairs or national security. Under this approach judicial review is limited: courts accept the President’s “finding” unless Congress has provided clear text to the contrary, and they decline to conduct “searching” scrutiny of the President’s policy justifications.

2. Consular nonreviewability and the Kleindienst framework. When the consular officer or the political branches deny a visa, Kleindienst v. Mandel instructs that federal courts will not substitute their own judgment where the executive provides a “facially legitimate and bona fide” reason for denial. That doctrine narrows judicial inquiry into the motives or merits of exclusion or refusal of entry in many individual visa cases, but it is not an absolute bar to all review—especially when procedural or statutory requirements have been violated.

3. The APA and the scope of judicial review. Where executive action is taken pursuant to statute, the APA supplies the baseline framework for judicial review—if the action is reviewable. Courts ask whether statutes leave room for judicial review (5 U.S.C. § 701) and whether the action was “arbitrary or capricious” in light of the statute’s commands (5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A)). In Pacito the Ninth Circuit applied APA principles to § 1522 claims, concluding that the government might not satisfy the statutory duties and that the APA therefore provided a vehicle for judicial enforcement. The court’s order directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements exemplifies how courts can fashion relief to vindicate statutory rights even while staying other injunctions that affect foreign affairs.

4. Political‑question, state‑secrets, and national‑security limits. Some executive acts raise institutional concerns that lead courts to find matters nonjusticiable or to apply highly constrained review. Political‑question doctrine or tailpiece doctrines like state‑secrets privilege may foreclose judicial relief where no judicially manageable standard exists or where litigation would compromise national security.

5. Equitable remedies and the stay standard. When injunctions or stays are sought pending appeal, courts use the Nken v. Holder four‑factor framework (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable injury absent a stay; injury to other parties; and the public interest), with the first two factors “most critical.” Pacito applied Nken: finding strong deference to the President under § 1182(f) on admissions but concluding plaintiffs were likely to prevail on at least some APA claims tied to resettlement services, and crafting relief accordingly.

Practical consequences follow from these standards. Courts will ordinarily resist detailed second‑guessing of executive judgments about whom to admit when the action rests on broad statutory authority linked to foreign affairs. But courts are more willing to enforce clear statutory mandates governing domestic programs and benefits—even when the beneficiaries are noncitizens already lawfully admitted—because those statutory duties fit readily within judicial competence and the APA’s remedial framework.

Conclusion: applying the lessons

Pacito encapsulates the core lesson: immigration law divides into domains where judicial deference is heavy and domains where ordinary statutory enforcement applies. Students must be able to identify which regime governs a given dispute. Ask these questions when you analyze a new fact pattern: (1) Is the executive action premised on an expression of inherent power or on a statutory delegation? (2) If statutory, does the statute grant broad, discretionary authority or impose clear, mandatory duties? (3) Is the action intertwined with foreign affairs or national security such that the judiciary should exercise heightened restraint? (4) Is there a manageable legal standard (statutory text, APA) that courts can apply without intruding on politically charged judgments?

Mastering those questions will enable you to predict when courts will enforce statutory protections for refugees and when courts will defer to presidential decisions about who may enter the United States. Pacito shows both outcomes can occur in the same dispute: suspension of admissions found likely within presidential power; statutory resettlement duties found judicially enforceable.
