Chapter 8: # Statutory Duties to Admitted Refugees: Is § 1522 Mandatory or Merely Authorizing?

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes a recurring statutory‑interpretation problem: when Congress uses a mixture of permissive language (“is authorized”), mandatory verbs (“shall”), and limiting conditions (“to the extent of available appropriations”), has it created a judicially enforceable duty or merely a discretionary power for the executive to exercise when convenient? This chapter explains how to analyze that question under ordinary tools of statutory interpretation, illustrates the analysis with the specific provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 at issue in Pacito, weighs the competing readings adopted by the panel majority and by Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent, and provides a practical framework a court (or an advocate) should follow when confronted with similar “authorization vs. command” problems.

At the heart of Pacito is not the President’s power to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) — the panel accepts that the President enjoys broad authority there — but whether § 1522 imposes a non‑discretionary obligation on the federal government to furnish reception and placement services to refugees admitted to the United States. The majority concluded that, as applied to refugees already admitted and where appropriations exist, the government is not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the executive unlawfully stopped statutorily mandated services; it therefore ordered reinstatement of necessary cooperative agreements for reception and placement. Judge Lee, by contrast, read § 1522 as largely authorizing the executive branch to create refugee programs at its discretion, and thought the district court improperly compelled implementation of what is essentially discretionary policy. The interpretive tensions that produce this divide are familiar: what weight to give the word “shall” when embedded in a section whose title and many subsections use “is authorized,” and how to read contingent phrases such as “in providing assistance” and “to the extent of available appropriations.”

## Textual analysis: ‘Authorization for programs’ vs. operative ‘shall’ clauses in § 1522(a)

The starting point for any statutory‑interpretation exercise is the text. Section 1522 is headed “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees,” and many of its subsections use the phrase “is authorized” or similar permissive formulations. A naïve reading of the heading together with the recurring “is authorized” language supports Judge Lee’s view that Congress granted the executive authority to run refugee resettlement programs but stopped short of imposing a positive command to run them at all times.

Yet the solid intuition generated by the heading and “is authorized” locutions collides with other text in § 1522 that uses mandatory verbs. Section 1522(a)—the provision labeled “Conditions and consideration”—states that “In providing assistance under this section . . . the Director shall, to the extent of available appropriations, make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement . . .” The word “shall” is commonly treated as indicating a mandatory duty, and many courts begin from a presumption that “shall” imposes an obligation unless other textual indicators suggest otherwise. When a statute requires an official to do something “to the extent of available appropriations,” courts typically interpret that limitation as making the duty contingent on funding (i.e., the requirement is operational if appropriations exist), rather than converting a mandatory command into a mere suggestion.

Resolving whether § 1522 is commanding therefore requires harmonizing the surface conflict: does the fact that the program provisions are described as “authorizations” and many operative provisions use “is authorized” rebut the ordinary force of a “shall” command in subsection (a)? A textualist approach examines the immediate statutory context. Subsection (a) explicitly ties its “shall” obligations to “providing assistance under this section.” If assistance is being “provided” (for example, because Congress has appropriated funds for refugee assistance or because the executive has chosen to run programs), subsection (a) prescribes how that assistance must be structured. Put bluntly, the textual grammar supports either of two readings: (1) a contingent mandatory duty—when assistance is provided and funds are available, the executive must provide it in the manner prescribed; or (2) a set of mandatory constraints that are triggered only if the executive chooses to use the permissive authorities in the remainder of the section. The majority in Pacito favored the first reading when appropriations and admissions exist; Judge Lee favored the second, emphasizing the permissive title and the recurring “is authorized” language. Close textual reading alone does not conclusively settle the matter; one must integrate other interpretive aids.

## Qualifiers and triggers: ‘In providing assistance,’ ‘to the extent of available appropriations,’ and ‘as needed’

Three recurring qualifiers in § 1522 are dispositive in many disputes over whether the statute creates enforceable duties: (1) the triggering phrase “In providing assistance under this section”; (2) the funding contingency “to the extent of available appropriations”; and (3) need‑based language such as “as needed.” Each operates in different ways and must be read together.

“In providing assistance under this section” is a textual trigger: it links the detailed requirements in subsection (a) to the core grant of authority in the rest of § 1522. If a court reads that phrase as a conditional — i.e., these requirements only apply if and when the executive elects to “provide assistance” under the programs authorized by the section — then the substantive mandates in subsection (a) are not absolute. The alternative reading, embraced by the Pacito majority for the circumstances before it, treats “in providing assistance” less as an elective trigger and more as a description of the circumstances in which Congress expects federal resettlement efforts to operate: where the government admits and resettles refugees, it must provide certain core reception and placement services. Under this view the presence of actual admitted refugees, combined with appropriations directed to refugee assistance by Congress, satisfies the “providing assistance” condition and thus activates the “shall” commands.

“To the extent of available appropriations” is commonly seen as a limiting clause that prevents courts from imposing obligations that would require spending beyond what Congress has appropriated. It does not, however, nullify statutory obligations when appropriations exist. The critical interpretive question is temporal and scope‑based: is Congress telling the executive branch only that any obligations in § 1522 are limited by the amount Congress chooses to appropriate (a ceiling on performance), or is Congress merely acknowledging that performance is contingent on appropriations but otherwise mandatory if funds are available? Most courts read “to the extent of available appropriations” as creating a funding contingency: the agency need not spend beyond existing appropriations, but when the Congress in fact appropriates funds for refugee programs the statutory duties are operative. This reasoning anchors the majority’s order in Pacito: the panel cited enacted appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 and treated those appropriations as sufficient to trigger statutory duties.

“As needed” and similar phrases (e.g., “if necessary”) appear in provisions dealing with unaccompanied minors and with certain alternative projects. These phrases invite a factual inquiry: whether a particular form of assistance is “needed” or “necessary” is a question of circumstance. They are not, by themselves, grant clauses that convert permissive language into an unconditional duty; rather, they define the scope of an otherwise mandatory requirement by tying the duty to an objective or factually determinable condition. Thus subsection (d)(2)(B)’s requirement that the Director “shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary,” is best read as a conditional obligation: if circumstances render such assumption necessary to provide for the child’s immediate care, the Director has a duty to do so.

A careful analysis therefore takes these qualifiers as operational triggers and limits: they narrow the scope of what is mandatory and define when and how a duty must be performed. The legislative choice of qualifiers supports neither a categorical presumption of permissiveness nor an assumption of unconditional mandated spending; rather, their function is to make statutory duties contingent on circumstances that can be evaluated by courts and agencies.

## Specific mandates: unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and cash/medical support projects (§ 1522(e)(7)(A))

Two provisions generated particular dispute in Pacito: subsection (d)(2)(B), which concerns unaccompanied refugee children, and subsection (e)(7)(A), which addresses alternative projects for cash and medical assistance to recently resettled refugees.

Subsection (d)(2)(B) directs: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Read narrowly, the phrase “if necessary” plainly conveys that the duty to assume responsibility is not automatic but is triggered when necessary to care for the child. The government can therefore argue that the condition precedent—necessity—does not exist in a particular fact pattern, while plaintiffs can point to concrete facts (e.g., an unaccompanied child without sponsors or other caretakers) to demonstrate that necessity exists and thus that the Secretary’s duty is triggered. Judge Lee emphasized that “if necessary” undercuts any broader reading of subsection (d)(2)(B) as creating a sweeping, unconditional duty; the panel majority recognized the conditionality but still thought the undisputed presence of admitted refugees with needs made at least some duties enforceable.

Subsection (e)(7)(A) commands that the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” Two interpretive points matter here. First, the use of “shall” suggests a requirement to develop and implement projects—i.e., programmatic commitments. Second, the modifier “as needed” applies to the content of those projects (interim support and medical services) rather than to the existence of the projects themselves; it therefore can be read as instructing the Secretary to design programs that supply services that refugees need, rather than telling the Secretary that the programs themselves are optional. Judge Lee stressed that “is authorized” elsewhere in the section makes even the “shall develop and implement” phrasing permissive in operation, while the majority read the clause as a genuine programmatic mandate whose operation is contingent on appropriations and demonstrable need.

One lesson from these two specific provisions is that qualifying phrases require a fact‑sensitive inquiry. “If necessary” and “as needed” are not mere tokens of discretion; they are standards that can be assessed by evidence. Where an agency terminates programs wholesale, leaving refugees without any services, a court can examine whether statutory triggers (admitted refugees present, appropriations available, demonstrable need such as unaccompanied minors lacking guardians) are satisfied and, if they are, whether the agency’s action violates an enforceable duty.

## Titles and headings as interpretive aids and their limits

Statutory headings and titles are routinely invoked as interpretive aids. They offer useful signals about how Congress conceived a statutory scheme, and a title such as “Authorization for programs” naturally suggests a permissive framework. Judge Lee anchored much of her analysis in the title of § 1522 and in the frequent use of “is authorized” across subsections. That approach has intuitive appeal: headings and statutory labels help courts discern whether language should be read as enabling the agency or constraining it.

Nevertheless, headings are subordinate to the statutory text and cannot override clear operative language. Where a provision contains explicit mandatory verbs, courts will not let an isolated heading defeat an otherwise straightforward command. In Pacito the majority pointed to the “shall” verbs in § 1522(a) and the appropriations history as a textual anchor; it treated the heading as informative but not dispositive. The general rule for interpreters, then, is to treat headings as supportive, not controlling. They can tip the balance where the textual indicators are ambiguous, but they do not carry the force of operative legislative text. This principle explains why Judge Lee’s insistence on the title was persuasive in part but insufficient to carry the day for the panel majority.

## Judge Lee’s view: pervasive authorization signals; the panel’s contrary view: enforceable duties once refugees are admitted and funds are available

The disagreement within the Pacito panel is a useful case study in how judges weigh textual nuance against statutory purpose and real‑world conditions. Judge Lee’s partial dissent repeatedly emphasized the structural and textual signals of permissiveness: the section heading, numerous “is authorized” clauses, and the structure that links the mandatory language of subsection (a) to “providing assistance under this section.” Her analysis treats Congress’s choice of authorization language as decisive evidence that it intended to create an enabling statute, not a suite of command obligations that would bind the executive irrespective of policy judgments about refugee admissions and resettlement capacity. On this view, courts are ill‑suited to micromanage resettlement programs; the political branches must retain discretion to determine when and how to implement resettlement services.

The majority, by contrast, took a more pragmatic and functionally textual approach. It read subsection (a)’s “shall . . . to the extent of available appropriations” as creating enforceable obligations where the triggering predicates are present: refugees have been admitted, and Congress has appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance. The majority relied on statutory specificity (the listing of particular services and the authorization of cooperative agreements with non‑profit agencies), historical practice (longstanding cooperative agreements and customary reception and placement services), and congressional accountability provisions (such as § 1523’s reporting requirement) to infer that Congress meant to ensure minimal core services for admitted refugees. Under this approach, “authorization” language in the section title and in particular subsections does not negate specific “shall” duties; instead, the sections operate together such that Congress authorizes programs and, where funds and admissions exist, prescribes certain standards and services that must be provided.

The difference between these approaches is not merely academic. If § 1522 is understood as largely authorizing, then the executive has wide latitude to suspend funding and cooperative agreements; judicial review is limited. If § 1522 contains enforceable duties, then wholesale termination of reception and placement contracts when refugees are already present is subject to meaningful judicial oversight under the APA: an agency cannot simply terminate statutorily required services without reasoned explanation and, where appropriations make obligations operational, without complying with statutory commands.

## How courts should decide and how to apply the analysis to new fact patterns

The Pacito dispute supplies a practical roadmap for litigants and courts confronting similar statutes that contain both permissive and mandatory language.

First, identify the operative verbs and their grammatical scope. Is the mandatory language (e.g., “shall”) addressed to a specified official and does it apply to clearly defined circumstances? If so, the default presumption favors a mandatory meaning unless countervailing textual or structural signals exist.

Second, locate trigger phrases. Determine whether clauses like “in providing assistance under this section” function as conditions precedent (i.e., the duty only arises if the executive elects to provide assistance under the statutory scheme) or as descriptive qualifiers tying the duty to factual circumstances (admission of refugees and availability of funds). Ask whether the predicate condition is present in the case at hand.

Third, analyze funding contingencies. “To the extent of available appropriations” ordinarily means that a duty is limited by funding; it does not mean the duty ceases to exist whenever the executive decides not to spend money. If Congress has appropriated funds directed to the activity, the statute’s substantive requirements are likely operative; the executive may not nullify statutory duties by reallocating appropriations without a reasonable statutory basis.

Fourth, evaluate qualifying standards like “if necessary” and “as needed.” These phrases generally create evidence‑based triggers: they require factfinding. A court reviewing an agency’s termination of programs must examine whether the agency reasonably determined that the condition (necessity or need) was or was not satisfied. Wholesale, unexplained termination is especially vulnerable to judicial invalidation when a statutory standard is susceptible to objective proof.

Fifth, assess structural and purposive indicators. Reporting requirements, delegation to specific officers, detailed lists of services, and historical practice can all be used to infer congressional intent to create minimum programmatic obligations rather than mere permission. Conversely, broad program titles and repeated “is authorized” language that pervades the statutory section weigh in favor of permissiveness, especially when the statute is otherwise spare of specific, concrete directives.

Finally, apply basic canons cautiously. Headings and legislative history can inform ambiguous text but cannot override clear statutory commands. When text is genuinely ambiguous, consider the statute’s purpose and the practical consequences of each interpretation (for instance, whether a permissive reading would allow the executive to frustrate congressional policy by refusing to implement any programs despite appropriations and admissions).

Two brief hypotheticals illustrate application of this roadmap. First, suppose Congress appropriates funds for refugee assistance, the executive admits refugees, and the agency terminates cooperative agreements without explanation. Under the majority’s reasoning in Pacito, a court should examine whether the appropriations trigger § 1522’s “shall” obligations and then whether the agency’s termination is consistent with the statute and reasoned decisionmaking; absent a satisfactory justification, the court may enjoin the termination. Second, suppose no appropriations exist for refugee resettlement in the relevant fiscal year. Under the funding‑contingent reading, § 1522’s substantive duties are not operational, and a plaintiffs’ challenge asserting a mandatory statutory right to services is unlikely to succeed.

## Conclusion: practical consequences and a checklist for litigants

Pacito underscores that the question whether a statutory provision is mandatory or authorizing often turns on close textual parsing combined with practical context — notably whether Congress has appropriated funds and whether the administrative facts satisfy conditionals like “if necessary.” For advocates, the path is clear: marshal textual evidence of mandatory commands (plain “shall” clauses directed at officials), show the presence of statutory triggers (admissions and appropriations), and develop the factual record to prove that qualifying standards (necessity, need) are met. For agencies, the lesson is that wholesale program terminations that leave beneficiaries without statutorily described services invite judicial scrutiny, particularly when Congress has allocated funds and the statute contains specific programmatic prescriptions.

In sum, the interpretive framework for § 1522 is not binary. Congress layered authorization and mandatory elements into the statutory scheme, and a court’s job is to tease apart when the contingency predicates are satisfied so that the “shall” provisions become operational. Pacito’s majority and Judge Lee illustrate the two plausible poles of interpretation: one that views § 1522 as enabling with conditions and another that reads it as imposing enforceable obligations where those conditions are present. Future disputes will turn on the same careful analysis of operative words, triggers, funding, qualifying standards, and the statutory structure as a whole.
