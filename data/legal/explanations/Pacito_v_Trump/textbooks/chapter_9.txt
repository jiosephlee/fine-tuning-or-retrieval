Chapter 9: # APA Challenges to Funding Suspensions and Terminations of Cooperative Agreements

This chapter uses Pacito v. Trump (9th Cir. interim order, Sept. 12, 2025) to teach how courts analyze agency cessations of programs and terminations of cooperative agreements when statutes may impose duties to provide services. Pacito presented a recurring set of questions that arise whenever an administration abruptly suspends a program or cancels cooperative agreements that third‑party organizations rely on to deliver services to vulnerable populations: (1) is the termination “agency action” subject to the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)? (2) if so, what is the appropriate standard of review and what counts as reasoned decision‑making in the funding context? (3) when do foreign‑policy and national‑security considerations remove action from ordinary administrative review? (4) when does a statute create judicially manageable standards (a “law to apply”) as opposed to committing decisions entirely to agency discretion? and (5) what remedial tools can courts employ — including reinstatement of cooperative agreements — to cure likely statutory violations? Pacito is particularly useful because the panel split the question set: it deferred to executive authority over refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) but concluded that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on their APA claim that the government violated statutory duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 by defunding reception and placement services and terminating cooperative agreements. The opinion thus illustrates the limits of deference in foreign‑affairs/immigration contexts and the continuing power of the APA to police statutory compliance over domestic program delivery.

Below we treat each of the subtopics that logically arise from Pacito. The goal is practical: give students the doctrinal tools to analyze whether a funding suspension or termination of cooperative agreements is reviewable and, if so, how courts evaluate the substance of the agency decision and fashion relief.

## Is terminating cooperative agreements “agency action” subject to APA review?

The APA’s definition of “agency action” is capacious. 5 U.S.C. § 551(13) defines “agency action” to include “the whole or a part of an agency rule, order, license, sanction, relief, or the equivalent or denial thereof, or failure to act.” That definition, together with the APA’s cause of action (5 U.S.C. § 706), ordinarily makes a broad range of agency decisions — including grants, contracts, and cooperative agreements — susceptible to judicial review. But the basic inquiry has two parts: standing aside, courts must first decide whether the challenged government conduct is “agency action” and, if it is, whether it is final and thus reviewable under the APA.

Two connected tests control reviewability. First, the decision must constitute “final agency action” under Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154 (1997). Bennett asks whether the action (a) marks the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process and (b) is one by which rights or obligations have been determined or from which legal consequences flow. An affirmative, published termination of cooperative agreements that cuts off funding and tells grantees the government will no longer pay plainly meets that test: termination is a consummated decision (a choice has been made) and it produces legal and practical consequences for beneficiaries and contractors. Pacito treated the February 2025 terminations as final and reviewable agency conduct for the purposes of plaintiffs’ APA claims.

Second, even when an action is final, the challenge is barred from review if it is among the narrow classes that the APA excludes. There are two relevant statutory exceptions. Section 701(a)(1) excludes “other statutes” that preclude judicial review and so would deprive courts of jurisdiction. More commonly litigated is § 701(a)(2), which exempts actions “committed to agency discretion by law.” The Supreme Court has emphasized that § 701(a)(2) is a narrow exception: courts should find action “committed to agency discretion” only where “statute is drawn so that a court would have no meaningful standard against which to judge the agency’s exercise of discretion.” Lincoln v. Vigil, 508 U.S. 182, 191 (1993) (quotation cleaned up); see also Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985) (agency refusal to take enforcement action traditionally unreviewable because of lack of judicially manageable standards). The upshot is that not every discretionary funding decision is unreviewable: when Congress sets out substantive duties, clear statutory terms, or criteria Congress expects to be applied, courts can and will review.

Applying those rules to funding terminations and cooperative‑agreement cancellations: when a department issues a definitive termination decision that ends funding and imposes consequences for beneficiaries, the action is final for APA purposes. Whether it is reviewable depends on the statute under which the program operates. If that statute provides standards, duties, or other law to apply, judicial review is available; if the statute leaves the matter to unconstrained discretion, the agency is much more likely to prevail on the § 701(a)(2) exception. Pacito illustrates both possibilities: the panel accepted executive deference to the President’s suspension of admissions under § 1182(f) (a statutory grant of broad national‑security/entry authority), while concluding that § 1522 contains “law to apply” with respect to reception and placement obligations and therefore that the decision to terminate funding for those statutorily described services is reviewable.

A final point: the APA’s definition includes “failure to act.” When agencies terminate funding but also stop performing duties the statute requires (e.g., providing reception and placement services), a plaintiff may challenge the agency’s refusal or failure to perform. Such inaction is reviewable if the statute imposes a non‑discretionary duty; Heckler v. Chaney is often invoked when the claim is an agency refusal to enforce, but it is not an automatic bar. Thus, funding termination can present two cognizable legal claims: (a) a challenge to a discrete, final affirmative act (termination) and (b) a claim that the agency had a statutory duty to take or continue action and unlawfully failed to do so.

## Arbitrary and capricious review: reasoned decision‑making, reliance interests, and program continuity

Once a funding termination or cancellation is subject to review, the governing standard for substantive review of the merits is usually the “arbitrary and capricious” standard of 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The leading modern articulation is Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29 (1983): an agency must examine the relevant data, consider important aspects of the problem, and articulate a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made. An agency changing course must supply a reasoned explanation for its change; it must address “serious reliance interests” and show that either those interests were considered or that countervailing considerations justify disruption. See FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc., 556 U.S. 502 (2009); Encino Motorcars, LLC v. Navarro, 579 U.S. 211 (2016).

In the funding/termination context there are a few recurrent themes courts focus upon:

- Reasoned factual record. Termination decisions that rest on record evidence — e.g., a program is not delivering intended results, fraud has been found, or severe mismanagement is established — are far more defensible than blunt edicts that do not articulate a factual predicate. Courts will look for contemporaneous analysis, not post hoc rationalizations. Pacito emphasized that the government told the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” for new resettlement providers but admitted later that it had no idea whether arrangements were in place — a factual lacuna that undermined a claim of reasoned planning.

- Consideration of statutory duties. If the statute prescribes services or responsibilities, the agency must address how termination squares with those duties. State Farm requires the agency to grapple with obvious statutory constraints. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit concluded plaintiffs were likely to succeed on their APA claim because § 1522 contains specific provisions outlining reception and placement responsibilities; the government had not explained how terminating cooperative agreements complied with those statutory obligations.

- Reliance and disruption. Courts frequently require agencies to evaluate reliance interests — the investments, staffing, and client expectations that organizations built on prior policy. Abrupt termination that fails to consider reliance can be arbitrary and capricious. The Supreme Court’s recent turn in Encino (and Fox) made clear that agencies must account for significant reliance interests when changing settled policies. In funding contexts, reliance often includes the statutory beneficiaries’ reliance on planned services (e.g., refugees’ expectation of reception and placement assistance) and third‑party grantees’ contractual and staffing commitments. Pacito highlighted these issues: organizations had historically provided reception and placement services pursuant to cooperative agreements; an abrupt termination demolished the infrastructure needed to serve refugees and raised acute reliance and continuity problems.

- Program continuity and transition planning. When an agency cannot lawfully or practically maintain funding at previous levels, it must still consider continuity, transitional arrangements, and less‑disruptive alternatives. Courts will look for consideration of whether notice, phased reductions, or targeted protections for vulnerable populations were possible. A failure to adopt any transition plan or to assess alternatives is a frequent basis for finding an action arbitrary. Pacito’s directive that the government reinstate cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted to the United States reflects this principle: when statutory duties to admitted refugees exist, the agency cannot simply terminate programs without an adequate plan to ensure delivery of required services.

In sum, arbitrary and capricious review in the funding‑termination context focuses on whether the agency conducted a reasoned analysis that engages statutory duties, relies on evidence, addresses reliance interests, and contemplates less disruptive alternatives and transition measures. An agency whose record resembles an off‑hand policy pronouncement is vulnerable.

## The foreign affairs exception to notice‑and‑comment rulemaking and why many funding terminations remain reviewable

Two different, but sometimes conflated, questions arise about notice‑and‑comment and foreign affairs. First, does APA notice‑and‑comment rulemaking apply to funding decisions and cooperative‑agreement terminations? Second, do foreign‑affairs or national‑security concerns remove otherwise reviewable agency action from judicial scrutiny entirely?

On the procedural side, 5 U.S.C. § 553 governs notice, comment, and publication. Section 553(a) expressly exempts certain categories from the notice‑and‑comment requirement: it exempts “matters relating to military or foreign affairs functions” (subsection (a)(1)) and “matters relating to agency management or personnel or to public property, loans, grants, benefits, or contracts” (subsection (a)(2)). These statutory text exemptions reflect Congress’s judgment that notice‑and‑comment procedures are often impracticable or unnecessary for actions that are fundamentally contractual or implicate foreign‑policy sensitivities.

But the exemptions to notice‑and‑comment do not equate to wholesale unreviewability. APA § 553’s procedural exemptions are narrow in application: they permit agencies to forego notice and comment when the statute so contemplates or when the subject matter fits within the listed categories. Courts will parse both the nature of the action (is it a rule of general applicability?) and the statutory context. Termination of specific cooperative agreements that are essentially contractual and individually targeted is less likely to be treated as rulemaking; such terminations are often governed by separate administrative or contractual procedures and, in any event, are frequently subject to APA review on the merits. Pacito demonstrates this point: even though refugee admissions implicate foreign affairs, the court reviewed the domestic statutory obligations in § 1522 that concern services provided to refugees once they are admitted to the United States — an essentially domestic statutory scheme that does not fall within an absolute foreign‑affairs shield.

On the substantive non‑reviewability side, the foreign affairs and national security context sometimes limits the court’s inquiry into the merits of executive action. The Supreme Court in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) described an exceptionally deferential posture toward presidential judgments on entry and national security under § 1182(f), cautioning against a searching inquiry into the President’s stated national‑security reasons. Courts have often treated core foreign‑affairs powers as presumptively nonjusticiable or deserving of substantial deference. Yet this deference is not limitless: when Congress has enacted domestic statutes that constrain executive action — for example, by imposing specific programmatic duties or domestic protections — courts may review whether the Executive complied with those statutory obligations, even if the wider policy concern involves foreign affairs. Pacito illustrates this boundary: the panel accepted robust deference to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), but it also held that the government’s termination of resettlement funding pursuant to § 1522 is subject to traditional APA review because § 1522 contains statutory duties applicable to domestic service delivery.

The practical rule is therefore contextual. Actions that are core immigration‑admission decisions or quintessential foreign‑policy judgments (e.g., expelling diplomats, deciding war strategy) may be insulated from meaningful judicial review. But many funding and program‑management decisions — especially those governing domestic service delivery and statutory obligations — are closer to routine administrative functions and remain amenable to ordinary APA review even when they touch on international programs. Exemptions to notice‑and‑comment or heightened deference do not automatically remove all judicial oversight.

## ‘Committed to agency discretion’ vs. ‘law to apply’ when a statute prescribes service levels or responsibilities

A central doctrinal battleground in cases like Pacito is whether the statute at issue leaves agencies with unconstrained discretion or instead creates judicially manageable standards that permit review and relief. This distinction is addressed by two related but different doctrines.

First, the § 701(a)(2) doctrine: the APA does not apply where “agency action is committed to agency discretion by law.” The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that this exception is narrow — it applies only when “no law” limits the agency or when the statute vests the agency with “unfettered discretion.” See Lincoln v. Vigil (distribution of discretionary grants) and Heckler v. Chaney. Lincoln held that the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ allocation of limited grant funds was essentially unreviewable because the statute placed discretion with the Secretary and left no judicially manageable standards for judicial review. Courts ask: does the statutory text, structure, or context give meaningful criteria or standards by which a court could judge an action? If not, the action may be unreviewable for lack of "law to apply."

Second, the “law to apply” inquiry operates across both Bennett finality and § 706 review, because arbitrary‑and‑capricious review presupposes that there is law for the court to apply. The question is whether Congress prescribed substantive duties, benchmarks, timelines, or other directives. Statutory language matters: “shall” and explicit enumerated duties strongly indicate that Congress intended enforceable obligations; broad authorizations phrased as “may” or “notwithstanding” often indicate discretionary grants.

Pacito hinged on this statutory parsing. Section 1522 is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” and includes language that at first glance appears discretionary — e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations.” That language is often used by agencies to argue that program funding is subject to political appropriations and therefore discretionary. But a careful textual and functional analysis can produce a different conclusion: § 1522(a) and other subsections set out specific programmatic responsibilities — language requiring the Director to make available resources for employment training and placement, to provide English language training opportunities, to ensure cash assistance is structured so as not to discourage self‑sufficiency, and to take responsibility for unaccompanied minors during interim periods. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito concluded that these statutory provisions supplied “law to apply” — there are specific substantive duties and programmatic requirements the court can enforce — so the government’s termination of funding and cooperative agreements could be reviewed under the APA.

Comparative statutory examples help illustrate the difference. Where Congress writes: “The Secretary may provide grants as appropriate,” with no further content, courts are more likely to treat the matter as committed to agency discretion. Where Congress writes: “The Secretary shall ensure that X program provides Y services to persons meeting Z criteria” or “the Director shall make available sufficient resources for employment training…,” the statute provides a substantive benchmark courts can enforce.

Two important caveats follow. First, the presence of qualifying language like “to the extent of available appropriations” does not necessarily render an obligation discretionary. That clause often means Congress recognizes that the program depends on appropriations, not that Congress intended complete deference on how to satisfy statutory duties within available funds. When statutory duties describe substantive obligations, courts will ordinarily assume Congress intended those duties to guide agency action even when tied to appropriations. Second, statutory duties may coexist with agency discretion over implementation; the question is not whether the agency has any discretion at all, but whether there are judicially manageable standards constraining that discretion. Where such standards exist, courts can require agencies to comply with them and to explain how their funding decisions comport with statutory duties.

## Remedial authority: ordering reinstatement of agreements to cure likely statutory violations

If a court finds that an agency likely violated the APA or a statute, what relief can the court grant? Funding cases often create particularly urgent remedial issues because beneficiaries and third‑party providers are immediately harmed when programs are terminated. Courts have two main remedial tools: (1) declaratory and injunctive relief restoring the status quo (which can include ordering the agency to reinstate funding, reinstitute cooperative agreements, or continue payments until the agency complies with law) and (2) vacatur or remand of agency action. In the preliminary injunction context, the standard is equitable: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm absent relief, balance of equities, and public interest (Winter v. NRDC; though some circuits use a sliding scale; Nken supplies stay factors). On the merits, courts may also order relief to remedy statutory violations under federal‑court powers to enforce federal statutes.

Pacito exemplifies the remedial approach courts will adopt when statutory duties are at stake and the government lacks a plausible immediate alternative. The Ninth Circuit directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That remedial order—narrowly tailored to those already admitted and aimed at ensuring statutory obligations are fulfilled—reflects several remedial principles:

- Narrow tailoring and temporal limits. Courts should and do tailor relief to address the specific statutory violation and avoid unnecessary interference with legitimate executive or foreign‑policy prerogatives. Pacito’s remedy was limited to refugees already admitted and to reinstating agreements to provide statutorily required services; it did not order admissions to proceed nor did it substitute the courts’ judgment for the President’s immigration policy.

- Preservation of statutory rights and avoidance of irreparable harm. When the statutory duty protects vulnerable populations (e.g., refugees already admitted, unaccompanied minors), courts are more willing to order interim relief to prevent irreparable harm. Reinstatement can be an appropriate way to ensure statutory obligations are actually performed while the underlying legal issues are decided.

- Use of equitable discretion rather than micromanagement. Courts typically avoid micromanaging agency programs. Rather than dictating program design, courts often order agencies to comply with statutory duties and to take necessary steps consistent with the statute and subject to further judicial monitoring if necessary. Pacito’s order to reinstate cooperative agreements is an example of restoring the pre‑existing contractual infrastructure rather than prescribing the content of every service.

- Consideration of separation‑of‑powers limits. Courts must respect executive branch responsibilities in areas where the Constitution vests discretion. That is why remedies are often narrow and grounded in statutory enforcement rather than broader policy commands. Pacito demonstrates this balance: the court rejected wholesale interference with admissions policy (deferring to the President under § 1182(f)) but enforced domestic statutory obligations that Congress had imposed.

A final remedial point concerns the form of relief when the government has already terminated cooperative agreements and reallocated funds. Courts can order the agency to reinstate termination, to continue funding, to award contracts, or to remand with instructions to reconsider under the correct legal standard. The particular remedy depends on the record, on whether plaintiff requests specific performance, on any equitable defenses, and on the feasibility of court‑ordered implementation. Pacito favored the pragmatic approach: reinstate agreements to preserve services pending resolution, rather than trying to design new programs from the bench.

## Practical guidance and litigation strategies

For students and practitioners preparing or defending APA challenges to funding suspensions and cooperative‑agreement terminations, the Pacito framework suggests several practical strategies:

- Plaintiffs should frame their claims in statutory terms: show that the statute creates specific duties or programmatic obligations (use of “shall,” enumerated duties, mandated reports, statutory timelines) so that courts will find “law to apply” and reject § 701(a)(2) defenses. Emphasize concrete statutory obligations relating to specific beneficiaries (e.g., reception and placement services for admitted refugees).

- Build evidence of reliance and disruption: collect affidavits and contemporaneous documents showing that organizations and beneficiaries relied on funding, invested resources, and that abrupt termination would and did cause significant harm. Demonstrate a lack of transition planning.

- Document the administrative record: show absence of a reasoned explanation, post hoc rationalizations, or gaps in planning. Agencies are stronger when they produce evidence of deliberation, alternatives considered, and transition measures.

- Anticipate foreign‑affairs and national‑security responses: be ready to distinguish core immigration‑admissions or national‑security actions from domestic program responsibilities. If a challenged action is primarily domestic (service delivery to persons present in the United States, statutory domestic reporting and oversight), stress the domestic statutory framework and the limits of foreign‑affairs deference.

- Seek tailored relief: craft requests for preliminary relief that are narrowly tailored to statutory beneficiaries and that avoid intruding on core executive prerogatives. Courts are more likely to provide narrowly crafted relief (reinstatement for admitted beneficiaries, temporary funding for certain programs) than broad injunctions affecting foreign policy or admissions.

Defendants (agencies or the Executive) should:

- Create and preserve a contemporaneous record for major funding decisions. Explain the factual basis for termination, the alternatives considered, reliance or equity considerations, and transition planning. Where foreign affairs or national security are implicated, articulate the nexus between the decision and those concerns without relying on conclusory assertions.

- Emphasize statutory language granting discretion and, when appropriate, earlier case law (Lincoln v. Vigil, Heckler v. Chaney) showing the court lacks judicially manageable standards. If the statute is discretionary, frame the decision as an exercise of political judgment.

- If relief is likely, propose practical, narrowly tailored interim measures to preserve statutory beneficiaries’ rights (e.g., limited bridge funding, alternative providers) and avoid an adverse injunction forcing reinstitution of complex contractual arrangements without time to implement.

Conclusion

Pacito illustrates the doctrinal line courts must draw between deference in the foreign‑affairs and admission contexts and judicial enforcement of congressional commands about domestic service delivery. Termination of cooperative agreements is usually “agency action” and final when it produces concrete consequences; whether reviewable depends on whether the statute leaves “no law to apply.” Where statutes impose specific duties, agencies must demonstrate reasoned decisionmaking, account for reliance interests, and provide transition plans or face arbitrary and capricious review. When courts find likely statutory violations, they have equitable tools — including narrow orders to reinstate cooperative agreements for protected beneficiaries — to preserve statutory rights without supplanting executive prerogatives in matters of foreign affairs. The lesson for both litigants and agencies is clear: design sound statutory analyses, build a contemporaneous record, consider reliance and continuity, and calibrate remedies to statutory duties and separation‑of‑powers limits.
