Chapter 4: # Doctrinal Baselines from Supreme Court Precedent

This chapter surveys the Supreme Court authorities that anchor the modern legal landscape of presidential authority over immigration, the Court’s willingness to defer to the political branches on entry and foreign‑affairs decisions, and the limits on that deference when statutory text or the Refugee Act rights are implicated. Those precedents form the doctrinal backdrop to the Ninth Circuit’s Pacito decision: on one hand, a strong current of judicial deference to Presidential judgments about “entry” and national security; on the other hand, statutory obligations and administrative‑law doctrines that can check executive action where Congress has commanded or conditioned a program. We examine five doctrinal loci: the mid‑20th century entry‑control cases (Knauff and Mezei), the extraterritorial/refugee context of Sale v. Haitian Centers Council, the modern statement of deference in Trump v. Hawaii, the Refugee Act framework and its application in Negusie v. Holder, and the post‑Loper Bright environment for agency statutory interpretation. Each section explains the holding, the reasoning, the applicable standards of review or interpretive tests, and how the precedent matters when courts evaluate executive action that intersects immigration and statutory duties.

## Knauff and Mezei: historical roots of plenary power and entry control

Knauff v. Shaughnessy (1950) and Landon v. Plasencia/United States ex rel. Mezei (1953) are the classical authorities that established and exemplified the doctrine that the political branches possess plenary and predominantly unreviewable authority to exclude noncitizens at the border or to control their entry. Those cases are the starting point for any inquiry into judicial review of Presidential or executive decisions categorically suspending or limiting admission.

Knauff arose out of an administrative decision to exclude a noncitizen spouse of a United States citizen during an era of heightened national‑security concern. The Supreme Court upheld the exclusion even though the excluded alien had been married to a U.S. citizen and despite procedural limitations on the opportunity to contest the ground for exclusion. The Court famously stated that “the power to exclude or expel aliens” is “a fundamental sovereign attribute exercised by the Government’s political departments largely immune from judicial inquiry or control.” The opinion justified narrow procedural protections for arriving aliens but refused to substitute the courts’ judgment for the political branches’ national‑security determinations.

Mezei extended those principles to an even starker factual pattern. Mezei had been a lawful long‑term resident who left the United States and later sought readmission; the Attorney General denied entry and the alien was detained on Ellis Island for years. The Court held that the exclusion of a noncitizen who had not obtained a right of reentry could be indefinite and did not create the kind of “presence” that would trigger full constitutional protections. The key doctrinal points from Mezei are (1) a sharp distinction between “entry” (the point at which the sovereign may control who comes into its territory) and mere presence or residency; and (2) a very limited role for the judiciary in second‑guessing policy judgments about exclusion. In Mezei the Court accepted absence of judicial inquiry into the merits of the admission decision when the political branches say exclusion is required.

What these cases teach about standards of review is essential. Knauff and Mezei do not articulate a multi‑factor test the way later cases will; instead they establish a doctrine of very great deference to the executive and legislative judgments on entry. Judicial review—when permitted at all—looked to process and to whether the exclusion was authorized by competent authority rather than to the substantive reasonableness of the exclusion decision. Those decisions therefore underpin modern holdings that courts will often refrain from “searching” review when a national‑security or foreign‑policy justification for excluding classes of noncitizens is proffered.

Limitations and subsequent developments must be noted. Knauff and Mezei were decided in a different era, and later cases have given more procedural protections where presence or residency is at stake, or where deprivation of fundamental rights follows from prolonged detention or the withdrawal of a long‑standing status. Nonetheless, for present purposes their enduring doctrinal contribution is clear: when the question is admission or exclusion at the border, the political branches possess dominant authority and courts defer—often intensely—to those branches’ judgments.

## Sale v. Haitian Centers Council: extraterritorial application and statutory interpretation in the refugee context

Sale v. Haitian Centers Council, 509 U.S. 155 (1993), addresses a distinct but related question: how statutory protections for refugees operate when actions take place outside the sovereign territory of the United States. The case involved U.S. interdiction of vessels on the high seas to prevent Haitian migrants from reaching the United States; the migrants challenged that practice on statutory and treaty grounds, notably under the Refugee Act and the Convention Against Torture (CAT).

The core holding of Sale was that the protections of the United States’ refugee statutes (as interpreted in light of the Refugee Act) do not automatically reach aliens intercepted and turned away on the high seas. The Court parsed Congress’s statutory language and applied the presumption against extraterritoriality: because the Refugee Act and related provisions spoke to rights and processes “in the United States,” the Court declined to read an extraterritorial application into the statute absent an unmistakably clear statement from Congress. The Court also emphasized that when immigration controls are executed as part of foreign‑affairs and interdiction practices, the political branches’ policy judgments deserve deference.

Sale is instructive for two doctrinal reasons. First, it reminds students that statutory text matters when determining whether statutory constraints limit executive power: the presence or absence of an explicit territorial application can be dispositive. Courts will typically invoke the presumption against extraterritorial application and will not assume that Congress intended to apply domestic statutory regimes to conduct abroad unless it clearly said so. Second, Sale exemplifies a repeated theme: where border control policy intersects international relations, the judiciary is reluctant to substitute its own judgment for the political branches’ assessments of how best to pursue those policies.

Sale also shows the limits of international law arguments in domestic statutory interpretation. The Court acknowledged CAT and other international obligations but refused to import those norms into domestic law absent clear Congressional instruction. For students, Sale is a cautionary lesson on how statutory methodology (text, structure, presumption against extraterritoriality) and institutional comity to the political branches combine to constrain a court’s willingness to impose domestic remedies that would regulate executive action outside national territory.

## Trump v. Hawaii: breadth of § 1182(f), deference to presidential findings, and limits on searching inquiry

Trump v. Hawaii, 585 U.S. ___ (2018), is the modern anchor for judicial deference to Presidential proclamations made under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The case involved a Presidential proclamation restricting entry by nationals of several countries; plaintiffs brought constitutional and statutory challenges. The Supreme Court upheld the proclamation and articulated a mode of review that is both deferential and doctrinally specific to this statutory context.

Three principles from Trump v. Hawaii are most significant. First, the Court reaffirmed that § 1182(f) grants the President broad authority to suspend the entry of any “class of aliens” whenever the President “finds” their entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The statute’s plain language and historical pedigree, the Court said, justify substantial deference to Presidential findings that underpin an entry restriction.

Second, the Court described the permissible scope of judicial inquiry into those findings. Importing the language of rational‑basis review in an immigration context, the Court declared that courts should not conduct “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justification.” Put differently, as long as the proclamation is “facially legitimate and bona fide,” and the President has set forth some stated reason that could plausibly support the determination that entry should be restricted, courts must be circumspect. The Court reasoned that the combination of the statutory grant to the President and the foreign‑policy character of the decision counsels against intensive evidentiary review by the judiciary.

Third, the Court carefully delineated limits: § 1182(f) is not unbounded. The President's proclamation must fit within the statutory text (there must be a finding that entry would be detrimental), and constitutional constraints remain operative. The plurality also intimated that overtly discriminatory motives that are evident on the face of a proclamation could produce different results; the Court’s approach emphasized a focus on the facial validity and bona fides of the Presidential declaration.

How should students apply Trump v. Hawaii? When an executive action suspending or restricting entry is challenged, a court assessing a claim about § 1182(f) ought first to determine whether the President has complied with the textual predicate of the statute (i.e., has “found” detriment) and whether the measure is facially legitimate and bona fide. If so, the judicial role is narrow: courts will not substitute their own appraisal of the wisdom of the policy and will accept reasonable inferences from the executive’s stated justification, subject to the usual protections for constitutional rights. Trump thus strengthens the executive’s hand in defending categorical suspensions of entry while leaving some space for judicial protection against categorical races to violate obvious textual or constitutional limits.

Pacito’s Ninth Circuit relied directly on Trump to conclude that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the claim that Executive Order No. 14163 suspending refugee admissions falls within the President’s § 1182(f) authority. The Ninth Circuit quoted Trump’s instruction that courts should not undertake a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness” of the President’s stated reasons and that § 1182(f)’s “sole prerequisite” is the President’s “finding.” Students should understand that Trump reduces the inquiry a court will perform when an admission suspension is framed as a Presidential finding under § 1182(f), but it does not make the statute a blank check.

## Negusie v. Holder and the Refugee Act as a general framework for refugee treatment

The Refugee Act of 1980 incorporated the U.N. Refugee Convention’s definition of “refugee” into U.S. law and created statutory mechanisms for asylum and for refugee resettlement and assistance. Understanding that statutory framework is essential when statutory claims — for example, under the Administrative Procedure Act — are brought to constrain executive action affecting refugees.

Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511 (2009), illustrates an application of the Refugee Act’s substantive statutes to a delicate fact pattern concerning the persecutor bar. The question was whether an alien who had participated in persecutory acts while acting under duress (compelled by government actors) nevertheless could be held to have “assisted” persecutors, thus triggering the bar to asylum or withholding of removal. The Supreme Court held that where the administrative decision implicitly relied on an incorrect legal standard — for instance, by failing to consider whether assistance was compelled rather than voluntary — review and remand were required. The case highlights two points.

First, Negusie characterizes the Refugee Act as providing a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” The Act defines who qualifies for refugee status, the processes for adjudicating claims, and important protections that apply to refugees and certain classes of applicants — rules that operate independently from, and sometimes in tension with, the political branches’ authority over admissions. In other words, even as the political branches control entry, Congress has established substantive and procedural rights that apply when persons are found to be refugees or when refugees are in the United States.

Second, Negusie emphasizes that administrative adjudications under the Refugee Act remain subject to ordinary judicial review for legal error. When an agency or board misstates the legal standard — as the Board of Immigration Appeals did by failing properly to account for duress — courts must ensure that statutory standards are correctly applied. The case is therefore a reminder that an individual asylum or withholding claim is not beyond judicial correction where legal mistakes affect the outcome.

This doctrinal balance — broad executive authority over admission, but statutory rights and agency adjudication subject to judicial correction — explains why a court like the Ninth Circuit in Pacito could simultaneously find strong deference to the President under § 1182(f) and yet conclude that statutory obligations created by Congress (for example, the reception and placement duties of § 1522) may be enforceable against the executive under the APA. Negusie helps students see that the Refugee Act supplies substantive expectations about treatment of refugees that do not evaporate simply because the executive announces a change in admissions policy.

## Post–Loper Bright environment: agency interpretations after Chevron’s demise

For over thirty years, Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984), shaped judicial review of agency statutory interpretations. Under the Chevron two‑step, courts asked (1) whether Congress’s statutory text was unambiguous on the particular question; if ambiguous, then (2) whether the agency’s interpretation was reasonable. Auer deference extended similar deference to agencies’ good‑faith interpretations of their own regulations. Those doctrines gave agencies significant interpretive primacy.

Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) changed that landscape. The Supreme Court in Loper Bright curtailed the Chevron framework, returning to a model in which courts apply traditional tools of statutory interpretation and give agency interpretations weight only to the extent persuasive under Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134 (1944), or under whatever mode of analysis the Court prescribed. The result is twofold.

First, courts must now resolve statutory ambiguities without the presumption that agency interpretations are entitled to controlling deference merely because Congress left a gap. Courts instead conduct an independent textual and contextual analysis and may adopt the agency’s view if it is persuasive under ordinary canons and if it coheres with the statute’s structure and purpose. Second, agencies cannot rely on a presumption of deference to immunize their statutory constructions from searching judicial analysis; they must persuade courts that their reading is correct.

What are the implications for immigration law and for cases like Pacito? Two points matter.

- Agency statutory interpretations that previously might have survived scrutiny by invoking Chevron must now survive a more searching review under ordinary interpretive tools. Thus, an agency that claims that § 1522 authorizes or mandates particular resettlement services will no longer be able to depend on Chevron to shield its interpretation from a court’s independent textual analysis. In Pacito, this dynamic helps explain why the Ninth Circuit felt authorized to scrutinize the government’s statutory obligations under § 1522 and to conclude that reinstating cooperative agreements might be required.

- Chevron’s reduction does not erase other doctrines of judicial deference that are rooted in the Constitution’s allocation of powers to the political branches, especially in foreign affairs and national security. Trump v. Hawaii’s instruction that courts should not conduct a “searching inquiry” into Presidential findings under § 1182(f) rests less on agency‑interpretation deference and more on separation‑of‑powers principles and international‑relations comity. Loper Bright does not automatically displace that mode of deference; it changes how courts treat agencies’ claims about what Congress meant when Congress alone wrote the statute.

Students should therefore appreciate the post‑Loper Bright legal environment as one in which statutory disputes with agencies are resolved more by independent judicial interpretation of the statutory text, but where the judiciary still may give substantial, functionally different deference to Presidential or executive determinations when Congress has conferred special, textually grounded authorities (as with § 1182(f)) and when foreign affairs or national‑security considerations are implicated. In short, Loper Bright narrows the shield Congress historically gave agencies under Chevron, but it does not eliminate the separate, often robust, reason for judicial deference to political‑branch judgments on immigration admissions and foreign‑affairs policy.

## Bringing the precedents together: how the doctrinal baselines govern cases like Pacito

Taken together, these precedents establish two complementary lessons that inform Pacito and future litigation about refugee suspensions, resettlement funding, and APA challenges.

First, when the executive claims authority to suspend or limit entry under a statute like § 1182(f), courts will typically be highly deferential to the President’s finding that entry would be “detrimental,” and they will refrain from conducting a searching inquiry into the merits of the national‑security or foreign‑policy rationale. Knauff and Mezei supply the historical foundation for that deference; Trump v. Hawaii furnishes the modern formulation: judicial review is narrow when the action is framed as an exercise of the President’s admission authority.

Second, statutory duties that Congress imposes on the executive — including obligations to provide reception and placement services for refugees under the Refugee Act (e.g., 8 U.S.C. § 1522) — remain judicially enforceable. Sale emphasizes the territorial boundaries of statutory protection and the role of text in determining coverage; Negusie emphasizes that the Refugee Act frames substantive rights and fair adjudicatory practices to which courts may hold the executive. After Loper Bright, courts will resolve statutory ambiguities without automatic deference to agencies and will more readily subject agency interpretations to independent textual analysis. Thus, if Congress’s statutory commands are clear, the executive cannot evade them merely by invoking policy judgments about admission; and even where ambiguity exists, the agency must persuasively justify its reading to a court that now performs a more searching textual inquiry.

Applying these lessons in practice requires careful sequencing. A court faced with a case like Pacito must first identify the legal question and the source of authority or obligation. If the question concerns the President’s authority to suspend entry under § 1182(f), Trump v. Hawaii guides the analysis and calls for deference to the Presidential finding provided that the proclamation is facially legitimate and bona fide. If the question concerns whether the executive has complied with statutory obligations (for example, duties to provide reception and placement services to persons already admitted), the court will examine the statutory text, structure, and purposes, and—post‑Loper Bright—will give the agency’s interpretation no special presumption of correctness. Where Congress has spoken clearly, the executive must comply; where Congress has not, the agency must persuade the court that its interpretation is the correct reading.

That bifurcation explains the Ninth Circuit’s result in Pacito: a likelihood of success for the government’s claim under § 1182(f) (drawing on Trump and the plenary power tradition) and, simultaneously, a ruling that the government may have violated the statutory resettlement obligations in § 1522 (where independent statutory interpretation and enforcement via the APA remain appropriate). Students should therefore be alert to the differing doctrinal pathways courts use—one deferring to Presidential factual and policy judgments at the border; the other enforcing statutory duties that Congress imposed on the executive even as the outer contours of executive authority over admissions remain broad.
