Chapter 10: # Stays Pending Appeal and Tailored Interim Relief

In expedited, high‑stakes litigation—especially where national policy, immigration, and executive power intersect—courts must frequently decide whether to preserve the status quo while an appeal proceeds. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump provides a compact but instructive example of how appellate courts apply the Supreme Court’s stay framework, balance deference to the President in immigration matters against statutory obligations owed to particular classes of persons, and craft narrowly tailored interim remedies. This chapter uses Pacito to explain (1) how the Nken factors operate (with emphasis on the “most critical” factors); (2) how courts weigh equities and public interest when national security and immigration judgments are at issue; (3) how and why courts carve out limited categories from broad injunctions; (4) how enforcement and supervision mechanisms can make provisional relief workable; and (5) practical lessons about appellate case management—administrative stays, vacatur of prior orders, and iterative clarifications.

## Likelihood of success and irreparable harm as ‘most critical’ factors

The governing framework for a stay pending appeal is Nken v. Holder, which directs courts to consider four factors: (1) the stay applicant’s likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable harm absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) the public interest. Nken, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The Supreme Court described the first two—likelihood of success and irreparable harm—as “the most critical.” Applying Nken requires that courts identify what showing must be made for each factor and then test the movant’s evidence against that standard.

Pacito illustrates both how deferential substantive doctrines can affect the “likelihood of success” inquiry and how the nature of the asserted harms shapes the irreparable‑harm analysis. On likelihood of success, the Ninth Circuit concluded the government was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The panel relied on Trump v. Hawaii’s commanding language that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and imposes only a single textual prerequisite—a presidential “finding” that the entry of aliens or any class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” 585 U.S. 667, 685–86 (2018). Where a statutory text expressly gives the President authority and the Supreme Court has pronounced substantial deference, an appellate court assessing a stay should treat the government’s likelihood of prevailing as stronger than it would be in contexts where de novo review applies. In practical terms, counsel seeking a stay in such a context must confront precedent that will compress the range of plausible merits outcomes.

By contrast, the Ninth Circuit also concluded that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on a separate theory—an APA claim based on 8 U.S.C. § 1522’s provision of reception and placement services for refugees. The court thereby split the merits inquiry: it credited the government’s strong showing on the presidential‑authority issue under § 1182(f) while finding a plausible legal basis for plaintiffs’ statutory claim. This serves as an important lesson: “likelihood of success” is claim‑specific. A single case may present claims of different legal character, and the court must analyze the probability of prevailing on each claim the stay implicates.

Irreparable harm requires concrete, non‑remediable injury if interim relief is denied. In immigration and administrative contexts, irreparable harm often takes forms that are statutory, practical, and human—loss of legal status, separation from family, disruptions in health or care, or the removal of contracted services that cannot be replicated later. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit tied irreparable‑harm concerns to the government’s own admissions of uncertainty about its ability to provide statutorily‑mandated reception and placement services: absent interim relief, refugees already admitted would be left without services that Congress contemplated. The court thus found the government had not shown it would suffer irreparable harm from a limited injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees; the harms to plaintiffs (lost services, disruption) were real and immediate.

For litigants and courts, the practical implications are clear. When arguing irreparable harm, parties should (1) explain why monetary relief will not cure the injury; (2) provide specific factual evidence of immediate need or deprivation (e.g., termination notices, government admissions of non‑readiness, testimonies about children or critical services); and (3) connect the injury to legal protections or statutory duties where relevant. Conversely, the party seeking a stay must show that absent a stay it will suffer significant and non‑remediable injury—often by pointing to national security, foreign relations, or other sovereign interests that cannot be compensated later. But where statutory obligations to particular persons exist, courts will scrutinize asserted sovereign harms against the immediacy of individual harms.

## Balancing equities and the public interest in immigration/national security contexts

The third and fourth Nken factors require the court to weigh harms to the parties and the public interest. In national‑security or immigration matters, courts historically grant wide deference to executive policy judgments, and that deference affects how the public interest is evaluated. Pacito demonstrates how courts operationalize that deference while still protecting statutory or constitutional limits.

The Ninth Circuit recognized the breadth of executive authority over entry restrictions, reiterating that § 1182(f) vests the President with “ample power” to impose restrictions and that the text requires only a presidential finding. That recognition informed the court’s judgment that the public interest and the balance of harms favored a stay of the district court’s injunction to the extent it prohibited the President’s suspension of refugee admissions. In other words, when the substantive legal standard is deferential and implicates national security or foreign affairs, the “public interest” factor will often point toward allowing the executive action to proceed—unless the opposing party demonstrates a compelling countervailing interest.

But this deference is not absolute. The Ninth Circuit refused to stay the district court’s order in full because, on the record before it, plaintiffs demonstrated that the government might be violating statutory duties to refugees already admitted. The court balanced sovereign interests in controlling admissions against the public and private interests in ensuring newly admitted refugees receive statutorily mandated services. That balancing led to a tailored result: a stay of the injunctions in their entirety except as to reception and placement obligations for refugees already admitted (and the court directed that cooperative agreements necessary to provide such services be reinstated).

Key takeaways for practitioners and judges: (1) characterize the public interest according to the governing substantive standard—if the operative statute or precedent commands deference, the public‑interest factor will likely favor the executive; (2) present concrete evidence of public‑interest harms (e.g., specific risks to national security, credible foreign policy disruptions) rather than abstract assertions; and (3) when arguing against deference, emphasize statutory text, oversight obligations, or human consequences that the executive cannot evade without legal consequence.

## Structuring limited carveouts (e.g., conditionally approved refugees) and avoiding overbreadth

Carveouts are a common judicial tool for reconciling competing equities in interim relief. Pacito’s sequence of orders shows two important principles in crafting carveouts: precision in defining the covered class and principled predicates for the exception.

The Ninth Circuit’s motions‑panel orders gradually narrowed the district court’s injunction, ultimately preserving a “limited carveout” for individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) before January 20, 2025. The panel’s early stay denial “to the extent” the injunction applied to those conditionally approved reflected several considerations: (1) a recognition that the equities and reliance interests of applicants who had already secured conditional approval are stronger than those of hypothetical future applicants; (2) a desire to avoid disrupting individual reliance expectations; and (3) the need to avoid blocking the executive’s larger policy decision pending appeal.

Designing such carveouts requires careful attention to both fairness and manageability. The court must define the putative class in administrable terms—dates of approval, specific statutory status, or discrete administrative actions—so that enforcement is feasible and does not require the court to re‑weigh merits on remand. Overbroad injunctions that purport to govern future executive decisionmaking, or that lack clear eligibility criteria, invite appellate reversal or partial stays because they impinge on policy choices. Conversely, excessively narrow carveouts that fail to protect reasonable reliance interests risk leaving vulnerable individuals without recourse.

In Pacito, the appellate court engaged in iterative clarification to avoid overbreadth: it issued a limited denial of a stay with explicit temporal and administrative contours, then later vacated earlier administrative stays and issued a more comprehensive stay subject to reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees. Practitioners should therefore anticipate and litigate over precise definitions: who exactly is in the protected class, what administrative actions count as qualifying, and what documentation will establish membership. Where practicable, litigants should seek (or oppose) definitions that can be implemented from existing administrative records, thereby preserving enforceability and minimizing further litigation over boundaries.

## Supervision mechanisms: enforcement frameworks and magistrate oversight

When courts order tailored interim relief—especially relief that requires administrative action (reinstating contracts, reassigning staff, or providing services)—they often must also build enforcement and supervision mechanisms into their orders. The district court in Pacito did so: following the preliminary injunction, it issued a July 14 order establishing an enforcement framework and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee implementation. The Ninth Circuit’s emergency orders interacted with that framework, temporarily staying the enforcement order and eventually directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted.

Three practical lessons emerge about supervision:

1. Specify the required obligations and the scope of required performance. Orders that direct agencies to “provide reception and placement services” should also identify the ordinary and customary services intended, the population covered (e.g., refugees admitted prior to the suspension), and any temporal limits. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito expressly required the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and clarified that such services “shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” This specificity reduces dispute about what the order requires.

2. Appoint a neutral supervisor where ongoing administrative activity must be monitored. The district court’s appointment of a magistrate judge to oversee the enforcement framework illustrates a common device: a magistrate or special master can manage implementation (deadlines, reporting, coordination) without burdening the trial judge or the appellate court with minutiae. However, courts must be careful not to impermissibly delegate adjudicative functions; supervision should be tethered to the court’s equitable power to enforce its orders.

3. Build in procedures for compliance monitoring and dispute resolution. Orders should require periodic reports, delineate the evidence necessary to show compliance, and specify prompt procedures for resolving implementation disputes—e.g., expedited motions practice, emergency hearings, or appointment of an independent monitor. These mechanisms increase compliance and make later appellate review more focused.

Judges and counsel should also anticipate and litigate the practicalities of reinstating terminated agreements: what it means to “reinstate” (reactiveffectuate the same terms? reenter negotiations?), whether funds are available, and what interim funding or staffing arrangements are necessary. In Pacito, these practical questions underlay the appellate court’s directive because the government itself disclosed uncertainty about its ability to secure new providers.

## Appellate management: administrative stays, vacatur, and iterative clarification orders

High‑profile, fast‑moving litigation often generates multiple, successive interim orders as courts respond to emergency filings, clarifications, or shifting facts. Pacito’s procedural history—motions panels, administrative stays, partial stays, vacatur of prior stays, and final temporary orders—offers a roadmap for appellate management.

First, motions panels frequently issue temporary relief with limited reasoning to maintain the status quo while fuller panel consideration is prepared. In Pacito, a motions panel initially granted only a partial stay and left a limited carveout intact. Such temporary orders serve triage functions but can create confusion if not swiftly followed by full‑panel disposition or clarifying orders. Practitioners should be prepared to move quickly to seek clarification, to request full‑court consideration, or to press for expedited briefing when interim orders leave important operative questions unresolved.

Second, courts may use administrative stays to pause enforcement of district court orders while the appellate court resolves procedural or managerial disputes. Administrative stays are often shorter and more flexible; they can be vacated or replaced by more reasoned stay orders. Pacito’s sequence—administrative stay followed by an interim order that vacated prior stays and issued a new stay pending resolution of the appeal—illustrates this iterative approach. Parties should track each temporary order closely and anticipate that prior stays may be withdrawn or modified as the appellate court gathers more information.

Third, the appellate court must reconcile competing interlocutory claims and ensure its orders are internally coherent. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit balanced deference on entry suspensions with statutory obligations on resettlement services and accordingly crafted an order staying the district court’s injunctions in their entirety “except as noted below,” then specified reinstatement obligations for admitted refugees. That pattern—stay in large measure, but preserve narrow enforcement where statutory duties are implicated—can be a model for appellate courts seeking to respect lower‑court rulings while protecting serious national policy prerogatives.

Finally, appellate counsel must be strategic about emergency motions. The government in Pacito filed multiple emergency motions and was denied some as moot after iterative orders. Emergency motions should focus on the narrowest obtainable relief, present a clear record-based case for urgency, and anticipate counterarguments about statutory obligations and reliance. Likewise, litigants opposing stays should use the appellate process to foreground concrete harms and narrow remedies that preserve both individual rights and the executive’s prerogatives.

## Practical drafting advice for interim remedies and appellate advocacy

From Pacito flow several pragmatic recommendations for litigants and judges involved in stay and preliminary‑injunction litigation in the national‑policy arena:

- Be claim‑specific in your merits showing. Where different claims have different legal standards, explain likelihood of success on each claim separately. If deference will limit review of one claim, emphasize other claims where review is less deferential.

- Quantify and document irreparable harms. Use affidavits, administrative records, and governmental admissions to show immediacy and non‑remediability.

- Propose or request administrable definitions for any protected class. Dates of approval, specific kinds of agency actions, and documentary thresholds make carveouts practicable.

- Offer practicable enforcement mechanisms. Suggest use of magistrate judges, reporting schedules, and specific metrics of compliance to make orders feasible.

- Anticipate sovereign defenses creatively but concretely. When the government alleges national security or foreign policy harms, require specific evidence of imminent harm and frame relief that minimizes interference with core executive functions.

- Draft relief with an eye to minimal intrusion. Narrow, tailored orders are more defensible on appeal than broad injunctions that second‑guess policy judgments.

- Manage appellate timing: seek expedited briefing early where possible, and be prepared to litigate in multiple interim stages (motions panel, full panel, emergency filings). Keep appellate arguments tightly focused—appellate courts’ resources for provisional orders are limited, and clarity aids quick resolution.

## The role of statutory interpretation and dissenting views

Pacito’s partial concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee highlights the interpretive dispute that often shapes interim outcomes: whether language like “is authorized” and section titles that say “Authorization” indicate permissive congressional intent or whether other statutory provisions containing mandatory language create enforceable duties. Judge Lee read § 1522 as authorizing, not mandating, reception and placement programs; the majority read the statutory scheme differently and ordered reinstatement for admitted refugees.

When seeking or opposing interim relief premised on statutory duties, litigants should frame textual arguments clearly: analyze section headings, verbs (“shall” vs “may” vs “is authorized”), statutory context, and the relevant appropriations language (“to the extent of available appropriations”). Also consider reporting or oversight provisions (e.g., § 1523’s reporting requirement to Congress) as indicators of congressional expectations. Appellate courts—especially in stay contexts—must decide whether the statutory ambiguity tips in favor of the executive’s discretion or requires preservation of statutory protections for a defined group.

In sum, Pacito is a paradigmatic decision about how appellate courts navigate the tension between deference in immigration and national security decisions and judicial protection of statutory or individual rights. It shows that stays pending appeal are not all‑or‑nothing: courts can sustain executive prerogatives while carving out narrowly focused interim relief where statutory duties or concrete harms demand it. The case also underscores the importance of precise, administrable remedies and robust supervision mechanisms so that interim orders are both effective and legally sustainable.
