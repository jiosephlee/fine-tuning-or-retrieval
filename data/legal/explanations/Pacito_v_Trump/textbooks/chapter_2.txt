Chapter 2: # The Statutory Framework: INA § 1182(f), § 1185(a), and the Refugee Act of 1980

This chapter explains the statutory architecture that governs presidential power to restrict entry, the statutory regime that governs refugee admissions and post‑arrival resettlement, and the ways appropriations language conditions executive obligations. Pacito v. Trump (9th Cir. 2025) provides the immediate doctrinal hook: the majority emphasized the breadth of the President’s § 1182(f) authority to suspend entry while simultaneously concluding that statutory duties created by the Refugee Act—chiefly 8 U.S.C. § 1522—may impose judicially enforceable obligations to provide reception and placement services once refugees are admitted. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent in Pacito illustrates the contrasting interpretive paths courts take when statutory text uses both “shall” and permissive language such as “authorized” and “to the extent of available appropriations.” The remainder of this chapter unpacks the statutory text, history, and interpretive tools that a student must use to analyze actions at the intersection of immigration, refugee law, and appropriations law.

## INA § 1182(f): text, history, and reach of the President’s suspension authority

Text and mechanism. 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (often cited historically as § 212(f) of the INA) provides in its operative form that whenever the President “finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” he “may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.” Two features of the text deserve emphasis: (1) the presidential finding is the statutory trigger, and (2) Congress used permissive verbs—“may,” “suspend,” and “impose … restrictions”—conveying broad discretion.

Historical context. § 1182(f) was enacted as part of the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952. Its historic purpose was to provide an executive mechanism to respond rapidly to risks presented by foreign nationals—broadly conceived as threats to national interests—without requiring new legislation. Over time courts came to treat § 1182(f) as one of the key textual bases for the executive’s plenary control of the border and admission decisions. That posture matured in a series of modern decisions that emphasize deference to the President’s foreign‑policy and national‑security judgments when he acts pursuant to § 1182(f).

Judicial reach and limits. The principal line of Supreme Court authority that recent courts cite for extreme deference is Trump v. Hawaii (585 U.S. ___ (2018)), where the Court accepted that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and supplies “ample power” to impose entry restrictions. From that premise courts often conclude that a court’s review of a § 1182(f) proclamation must be restrained: the statute requires only that the President “find” detriment to U.S. interests, and courts should not conduct a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Yet that deference is not limitless. Two kinds of constraints remain relevant.

First, the statute is a creature of text; it does not allow actions that plainly exceed textual limits or that violate other statutory commands. If a separate statutory provision unambiguously prohibits the President’s chosen measure, a court will not be constrained by § 1182(f). Second, constitutional limits still bind—equal protection principles, the Establishment Clause, Due Process, and the First Amendment are not erased by § 1182(f). Courts therefore frame their review as constrained: examinable doxastic or rational basis inquiries into whether the President made the requisite “finding,” while generally declining to substitute their assessment of foreign‑policy judgment for that of the political branches.

Operational consequences. When a President invokes § 1182(f), the analysis begins with whether the invocation satisfies the textual trigger: did the President make the requisite finding and issue the proclamation? If so, courts typically treat the action as presumptively within the President’s authority under § 1182(f) unless another statute or the Constitution provides a clear limit. As Pacito illustrates, a court may nevertheless find other statutory duties (for example, duties to provide services after admission) that the executive cannot disclaim simply by suspending future admissions.

## INA § 1185(a): regulation of entry and departure; interaction with § 1182(f)

Text and function. 8 U.S.C. § 1185(a) grants the executive power to regulate the entry and departure of aliens by promulgating “such rules and regulations” and taking such steps “as the President may prescribe” for the inspection and control of arrivals. Historically, § 1185(a) has been read as a regulatory complement to § 1182(f): where § 1182(f) authorizes suspension or restrictions on entry by proclamation, § 1185(a) authorizes rulemaking and administrative management of entry, exit, and ports of arrival.

Complementary scope. The two provisions operate at different levels. § 1182(f) is a blunt instrument that authorizes suspension or restriction of classes of entry as a matter of declarative presidential policy. § 1185(a) is a delegated rulemaking provision enabling the executive to set procedural and regulatory requirements for inspection, boarding, departure, and conditions at ports. In practice, a President may rely on § 1182(f) to effect a broad policy pause in admissions and on § 1185(a) to craft implementing rules that give operational form to that pause—procedures at airports, visa rescissions, and parole rules, for example.

Interaction in judicial review. Courts often cite both provisions when evaluating executive action that affects entry. When a broad prohibition looks like a policy decision grounded in national‑security judgment, judges point to § 1182(f) as the operative authority and invoke the high level of deference recognized in cases like Trump v. Hawaii. When litigants challenge the method of enforcement or the administrative rules the executive uses to implement an admission restriction, courts review the § 1185(a) rulemaking authority to determine whether the regulatory action stays within statutory bounds and administrative procedure norms. The distinction matters practically: § 1182(f) signals the President’s policy choice and carries deference; § 1185(a) often demands that implementing rules be within the bounds of delegated authority and, where they are “rulemaking” in the administrative‑law sense, that they conform to statutory or constitutional procedural requirements.

Boundaries and congressional intent. Neither § 1182(f) nor § 1185(a) is absolute. If Congress has elsewhere enacted a statute that creates a particular right or process, such as the Refugee Act’s requirements for consultation and admissions ceilings (discussed below), courts must harmonize the provisions. That harmonization often requires attention to statutory timing and operative words: does congressional language evince intent to limit executive discretion, or merely to authorize particular programs? The necessary textual work is a standard task in statutory construction.

## The Refugee Act of 1980: §§ 1157 (admissions ceilings, consultation) and 1522 (domestic resettlement and assistance)

Structure and purpose. The Refugee Act of 1980 reformed U.S. refugee law by embedding a statutory process for refugee admissions, establishing an annual admission ceiling subject to presidential determination and congressional consultation, and creating mechanisms to deliver post‑arrival services and funding. The Act reflects a dual design: to preserve executive flexibility in setting admission numbers and vetting individuals while establishing statutory responsibilities for resettlement so that refugees can integrate effectively after admission.

8 U.S.C. § 1157 — admissions ceilings and consultation. Section 1157 sets the framework for annual refugee admissions. It empowers the President to set—the “Presidential determination”—a worldwide ceiling on refugee admissions for a fiscal year and to consult with Congress on that determination. The statute thus places the decision over the annual ceiling squarely in the political branches: the President proposes and consults; Congress participates through appropriations and oversight. The Refugee Act’s procedures are not an absolute constraint on the President’s § 1182(f) power, but they do supply a regime that governs planned admissions. When a President exercises § 1182(f) to suspend a program that would otherwise admit refugees, courts must read that suspension against the background statutory framework Congress established in § 1157.

8 U.S.C. § 1522 — domestic resettlement and assistance. Section 1522 addresses what happens to refugees after they enter the United States. Its provisions include authorizations for a range of services: employment training and placement, English language instruction, cash and medical assistance for newly arrived refugees, and particular protections and responsibilities for unaccompanied minors. The statute authorizes the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public and private nonprofit agencies to provide reception and placement services; it provides reporting and oversight requirements; and it includes language—“to the extent of available appropriations”—that links the substantive duties to the availability of funding.

Interpretive tensions illustrated by Pacito. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit majority concluded that the government’s termination of cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services likely contravened § 1522: even if the President may suspend future admissions under § 1182(f), the government may not simply withdraw statutory reception and placement services from refugees already admitted. The majority therefore directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The opinion grounds that remedy in the statutory language that imposes obligations—frequently phrased with “shall”—on the Director and on the Secretary to see that services are provided “in such a manner” as to promote self‑sufficiency, to assume legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors when necessary, and to “develop and implement” certain projects for medical and cash assistance.

Judge Lee’s concurrence and dissent in Pacito demonstrates the countervailing interpretive path: § 1522’s title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated use of the word “authorized” suggest to her that Congress intended § 1522 primarily as permissive funding authority rather than a mandate to operate programs. Her view foregrounds the statute’s structure and the reality that many of § 1522’s operative provisions are expressly conditioned on appropriations. That interpretive divide—mandatory duty versus permissive authorization—governs plaintiffs’ ability to obtain judicial relief. If § 1522 creates a nondiscretionary duty, then courts can compel the executive to fulfill it (or at least to proceed consistent with the statute); if § 1522 is permissive, courts lack a basis to command the executive to run programs.

Practical doctrinal takeaway. When confronting conflicts between a presidential suspension of admissions and statutory resettlement obligations, a court must (1) determine the text’s operative force—whether Congress intended to create a mandatory duty or merely an authorization; (2) evaluate whether Congress has appropriated funds to satisfy the duty; and (3) reconcile the resulting obligation with the President’s national‑security or foreign‑policy prerogatives under § 1182(f). Pacito shows that these steps can lead a court to require the executive to provide statutorily mandated services to those already admitted while allowing the President great deference with respect to future admissions.

## Appropriations riders and qualifiers: “to the extent of available appropriations” and their legal effect

What the phrase does. “To the extent of available appropriations” is a frequently used statutory qualifier that conditions a statutory duty on Congress’s provision of funds. It has two primary effects. First, it recognizes the constitutional fact that Congress controls the purse: an executive agency cannot lawfully spend funds that Congress has not appropriated. Second, it signals that the specified statutory program or obligation is subject to funding limits and may not be enforceable if Congress declines to provide money for it.

Interpretive consequences. The presence of the qualifier creates a threshold question for courts asked to enforce a statutory duty: have appropriations been made and are they still available? If Congress has appropriated funds for the program and has not expressly rescinded them, then the agency must decide whether to expend money consistent with statutory conditions. If the statute otherwise creates a nondiscretionary duty (for example, by using “shall” in ways that impose an obligation), then a court may have authority to compel the agency to act within the appropriation. But if Congress has not appropriated funds—or has appropriated and then rescinded or conditioned them—the government can plausibly contend that the duty cannot be executed because the financial means are absent.

Limits on judicial compulsion. Courts are careful in using equitable power to compel executive spending because of separation‑of‑powers concerns. When an agency alleges that no appropriations are available, a court should demand clear proof before enjoining or ordering spending. If funds are available but the executive claims policy reasons for withholding them, courts will scrutinize whether the statute imposes a non‑discretionary duty and whether judicial relief would unconstitutionally commandeer executive functions. Thus, the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” creates a practical barrier to judicial enforcement in many cases, but it is not an absolute bar: appropriation has both legal and evidentiary dimensions.

Appropriations riders and policy. In the Pacito record Congress had appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 but had enacted later rescissions that reduced the totals. The Ninth Circuit pointed to those appropriations and to statutory reporting and accountability duties to explain why it could order reinstatement of cooperative agreements for those already admitted, reasoning that the government had not demonstrated its ability to provide the statutorily mandated services without the cooperative agreements. That approach highlights how courts often treat appropriations questions as fact‑intensive: availability must be shown; mere assertions of funding shortfall are insufficient to prevent enforcement of statutory duties when funds are in play.

A student applying these principles should proceed in two steps: (1) identify whether the statutory duty is expressly conditioned on appropriations, and (2) inquire—on the record—whether appropriations exist or are available. If appropriations exist and the statute imposes a non‑discretionary duty, judicial relief may be appropriate; if appropriations do not exist, the duty cannot be performed and courts generally will not (and often cannot) force the executive to expend funds it says are not available.

## Entry vs. admission post‑IIRIRA and how courts reconcile § 1182(f)’s “entry” language

Definitional distinction. The INA draws a formal distinction between “entry” and “admission.” “Admission” is defined in the statute (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)) as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” “Entry” is not as precisely defined in the INA’s text, and in common usage “entry” can mean either the physical act of crossing a border or the legal status that follows. Courts therefore have had to interpret what “entry” means in different statutory contexts.

Post‑IIRIRA landscape. The Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA) revised many removal and entry provisions and affected the conceptual architecture around “entry” and “admission.” In particular, IIRIRA sharpened the legal consequences of being “admitted” and broadened removal consequences for those without proper admission or those reentering unlawfully. The post‑IIRIRA statutory environment therefore encourages careful attention to whether a person is an “admitted” alien (i.e., had lawful entry) versus someone who is merely physically present or in transit and who never secured admission.

Application to refugees and suspensions. How do courts reconcile § 1182(f)’s power to suspend “entry” with the Refugee Act’s protections for those already admitted? The general answer courts adopt is pragmatic: § 1182(f) targets future inbound movement—suspension of the ability to enter or be admitted—while statutory protections that attach after admission remain operative for persons already inside the United States. Thus, a refugee who has completed the admission process and is physically within the United States is not typically within the class that § 1182(f) is designed to control; § 1182(f) suspends the President’s willingness to allow new entries or admissions, not to strip legal status from those here already.

The Ninth Circuit’s handling of “conditionally approved” refugees in Pacito illustrates the doctrinal complications. The court carved out from the stay individuals who were “conditionally approved for refugee status by USCIS before Jan 20, 2025,” implicitly recognizing that certain pre‑entry approvals carry reliance‑based or statutory expectations that make a categorical suspension harder to apply. That approach reflects two points. First, pre‑admission administrative steps (such as USCIS approval) may place an alien in a liminal position that invites narrower judicial protection than someone already present but still gives greater protection than a simple visa applicant abroad. Second, courts will often focus on whether an individual has a vested right or a completed statutory status that the executive cannot extinguish by fiat.

Consequential distinctions for relief. For students analyzing fact patterns, the key doctrinal choice is whether the person at issue has been “admitted” (and so enjoys statutory and constitutional protections that the executive cannot abrogate wholesale) or is still abroad and seeking “entry.” If the person is abroad and has not obtained admission, the executive’s § 1182(f) suspension will generally be given much greater deference; if the person has been admitted, statutory duties like § 1522’s reception and placement obligations and constitutional protections likely govern the dispute.

Synthesis and doctrinal checklist

When a legal problem involves a presidential suspension of refugee admissions, statutes governing immigration entry, and post‑arrival resettlement responsibilities, apply the following worksheet:

- Identify the authoritative statutory texts. Ask whether the President invoked § 1182(f) and whether § 1185(a) or any implementing regulations are implicated. Determine which Refugee Act provisions (especially §§ 1157 and 1522) are potentially inconsistent with or limiting of the presidential action.

- Resolve the temporal status of the persons at issue. Are they already admitted to the United States? Are they conditionally approved for admission? Courts treat these categories differently; the former typically invokes statutory and constitutional protections that cannot be undone by an order suspending future entries.

- Read the statute for mandatory versus permissive language. Look for “shall” in combination with other textual signals: titles (“Authorization”), repeated use of “authorized,” and appropriations qualifiers. If a duty is framed as mandatory and funds are available, it may be judicially enforceable; if it is discretionary or expressly conditioned on appropriations, the executive has more latitude.

- Investigate appropriations. Determine whether Congress has appropriated funds for the program and whether the funds remain available. The presence or absence of appropriations will often decide whether a statutory duty can realistically be performed or judicially compelled.

- Account for constitutional constraints. Even when the text seems to give the President broad authority, ask whether constitutional protections (equal protection, due process, Establishment Clause) place limits on the executive action.

- Consider practical remedies and separation‑of‑powers risks. Courts are more willing to enjoin withholding of statutory duties when funds are appropriated and the duty is nondiscretionary; they are more reluctant to mandate specific budgetary allocations or micro‑manage executive decisionmaking in areas where the statute leaves substantial discretion.

Conclusion

The intersection of § 1182(f), § 1185(a), and the Refugee Act of 1980 is a site where statutory text, historical practice, and constitutional values collide. Pacito displays how a court can simultaneously give broad deference to a presidential finding that entry should be suspended while recognizing that Congress enunciated post‑arrival statutory duties that persist for those already admitted. Students should approach such cases with a layered statutory‑textual analysis that carefully distinguishes entry from admission, reads mandatory words against appropriations qualifiers, and recognizes that judicial relief turns on both legal interpretation and factual showings about appropriations and administrative capability.
