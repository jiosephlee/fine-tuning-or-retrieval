Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

Chapter 7: # Presidential Entry Suspensions and Judicial Deference

This chapter examines how the Ninth Circuit applied the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) to an executive suspension of refugee admissions in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) and what Pacito means for the limits of judicial review over presidential findings under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Pacito arose from an executive order that suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) and simultaneous defunding of programs that provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The Ninth Circuit, in a stay opinion issued while the case was expedited, applied the deferential framework of Hawaii to sustain the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) but identified a separate, non-§1182(f) route by which plaintiffs could likely prevail—an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge to the termination of statutory resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The panel thus stayed the district court injunctions enjoining the moratorium on admissions but ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already in the United States. The opinion — and Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent — give students a useful vantage for understanding what courts will and will not scrutinize when the President invokes § 1182(f).

The discussion that follows unpacks Pacito’s application of Hawaii’s deference doctrine, analyzes what counts as a “finding” of detriment, describes the permissible scope and duration of entry suspensions under § 1182(f), explains how § 1182(f) interacts with refugee-specific statutory regimes (notably the admissions ceiling and the Refugee Act’s resettlement obligations), and draws out practical implications for litigants who seek to challenge or defend executive entry suspensions in the post-Hawaii landscape.

## Application of Hawaii’s deferential framework to § 1182(f) suspensions

Trump v. Hawaii is the starting point for modern judicial treatment of presidential entry restrictions. In Hawaii, the Supreme Court held that when the President acts pursuant to § 1182(f), courts should give substantial deference to the executive’s findings and national-security judgments. The Court characterized § 1182(f) as a textual grant of broad power to the President to “suspend the entry” of classes of aliens “for such period as he shall deem necessary,” and it rejected searching judicial inquiry into whether the President’s national-security rationales were persuasive. The Hawaii opinion instructs courts to treat the President’s “finding” as presumptively legitimate, leaving review narrow in scope.

Pacito follows that path. Faced with an executive order suspending refugee admissions, the Ninth Circuit applied the stay factors articulated in Nken v. Holder but treated the likelihood-of-success-on-the-merits inquiry through the lens of Hawaii’s deference. The panel concluded the government had “made a strong showing that it is likely to prevail” on the question whether the President lawfully suspended refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That conclusion rested on two propositions taken from Hawaii: (1) § 1182(f) requires only that the President “find” that admissions “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and (2) courts may not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications when that statutory predicate is met.

What does this mean doctrinally? First, courts will treat presidential proclamations and executive orders that express a finding of detriment as satisfying the statutory prerequisite unless the proclamation is a legal nullity or clearly outside § 1182(f)’s textual scope. Second, the standard of inquiry is not normal administrative review (e.g., substantial evidence or arbitrary-and-capricious review applied to agency factfinding); rather, courts decide whether the President made a formal “finding” and whether the suspension falls within § 1182(f)’s textual grant. If so, the President’s policy judgment is insulated from in-depth factual attack.

Pacito illustrates how this framework operates on a preliminary-relief posture. Because Nken places heavy weight on the merits and irreparable-injury factors, the Ninth Circuit found that Hawaii’s deference made it difficult for plaintiffs to show they were likely to prevail on their facial statutory challenge to the moratorium’s validity. Thus, where a challenge is fundamentally an attack on the President’s § 1182(f) finding and the scope of authority under that section, courts will be especially reluctant to enjoin the President at the preliminary stage.

Yet Pacito also demonstrates the limits of Hawaii’s protection. The panel did not allow the executive’s invocation of § 1182(f) to immunize related government actions that are statutory in origin and distinct in nature—here, the termination of cooperative agreements to provide services to refugees already admitted. The court distinguished a suspension of the “entry” of aliens from agency decisions affecting duties owed to people already within the United States under other statutory schemes. Where statutory duties exist apart from the entry power, courts retain the capacity to review agency compliance with those statutes under the APA. This is why Pacito sustained the stay as to the admissions moratorium but ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements to preserve resettlement services: judicial deference under § 1182(f) does not automatically shield unrelated agency actions from statutory review.

## What counts as a ‘finding’ of detriment and how specific it must be

A central, recurring question under § 1182(f) is what the statute requires for the President to “find” that entry of certain aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The threshold is low. Hawaii makes clear that the statutory predicate is satisfied when the President articulates a formal finding in a proclamation or executive order; courts need not adjudicate the empirical merits of that finding. Pacito follows Hawaii’s reasoning and states flatly that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” In short, explicit language in an executive order stating that continued refugee admissions would be “detrimental” suffices as a statutory finding.

Does the finding need to be concrete, detailed, or supported by a particularized factual record made public? Pacito and Hawaii suggest the answer is no: a formal yes/no finding formally expressed by the President will meet the statutory requirement. The Court in Hawaii declined to require the President to assemble a public evidentiary record or meet a judicially crafted standard of specificity for national-security findings. The Ninth Circuit echoed that approach in Pacito, refusing to subject the President’s judgment to “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The operative lesson is that the “finding” is principally a political-branch judgment, not a judicially enforceable factual adjudication.

That said, litigants and courts should not assume the finding is entirely immune to any form of judicial consideration. There are limited scenarios in which the “finding” might be treated as insufficient. For example: if the document labeled a finding actually contains only conclusory or inconsistent language that has no plausible claim to be a presidential finding; if the alleged “finding” plainly concerns persons or conduct that the text of § 1182(f) does not reach; or if the finding were a pretext for action that violates other explicit statutory limits or constitutional protections (e.g., if the recommendation rested on race-based animus that implicates constitutional equal-protection principles). None of these borderline categories was present in Pacito; the court accepted the executive order’s formal finding as satisfying § 1182(f).

Practical consequence: challengers who want to attack a § 1182(f) suspension should not, as a first step, attempt to litigate the factual sufficiency of the President’s finding. That route is unlikely to succeed post-Hawaii. Instead, they should pursue arguments that the presidential action falls outside § 1182(f)’s textual scope or that other statutes grant concrete, mandatory duties that cannot be overridden by an § 1182(f) proclamation. Alternatively, constitutional challenges that do not rest on reweighing foreign-relations judgments may also remain viable where they do not require courts to second-guess national-security findings.

## Scope of permissible entry restrictions and duration under § 1182(f)

The text of § 1182(f) is capacious. It authorizes the President, “whenever [he] finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens” and to impose “any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate,” “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” The statutory grammar conveys three principal features: (1) coverage of any class of aliens (broad reach); (2) discretion to limit entry in several ways (broad mode-of-action); and (3) authority to set duration as the President thinks fit (broad temporal reach).

Pacito, reading Hawaii, gave full effect to that breadth in the entry context: the court indicated that a moratorium on refugee admissions is within the core textual grant of § 1182(f) and that courts should not substitute their judgment about whether the duration is necessary. The Ninth Circuit’s stay reasoning therefore treats duration and scope — including a complete moratorium on a program admitting refugees — as political judgments that fall within Executive Branch competence absent some other statutory or constitutional constraint.

But breadth in the statute does not mean unlimited power in practice. A few important textual and structural limits remain salient:

- The statute speaks about “aliens” and “entry.” It is not a license to regulate post-entry conduct of persons lawfully within the United States apart from matters plainly tied to the entry decision. Pacito stressed this distinction: the government could suspend future refugee admissions, but that did not free it to ignore statutory duties to refugees already admitted.

- The President’s exercise of § 1182(f) must be a genuine exercise of the power conferred by the statute, not a pretext for overriding other specific statutory mandates. If a specific provision elsewhere in the INA or another statute sets forth mandatory duties or limits that conflict with the President’s action, courts will need to reconcile those texts. In Pacito, the court found that statutory duties under § 1522 could give plaintiffs a likely APA claim even as the moratorium itself was protected by § 1182(f).

- Constitutional constraints remain in the background. Hawaii reserved to the judiciary the role of enforcing the Constitution; the Court emphasized deference, not abdication. There may yet be constitutional limits to an executive suspension depending on the particular facts — for example, if executive action were motivated by hostile discrimination against a protected class and the legal challenge could be resolved without second-guessing national-security judgments. Those issues did not carry the day in Pacito, but students should not read Hawaii or Pacito as extinguishing all constitutional review of entry restrictions.

In sum, § 1182(f) grants broad authority to suspend entry, including the duration of that suspension, but scope is not limitless: statutory text, other statutory duties, and the Constitution can constrain the exercise of that power.

## Interaction with § 1157 admissions ceilings and consultation duties

The Refugee Act and implementing statutes create both planning and reporting mechanisms to manage refugee admissions. Section 1157, among other provisions, governs admissions of refugees and provides mechanisms for setting admissions ceilings for annual refugee admissions. The administrative apparatus created by the Refugee Act anticipates a continuing program of admissions accompanied by consultation, appropriation, and reporting duties. How does the President’s invocation of § 1182(f) interact with these other statutory elements?

Pacito did not fully litigate every statutory cross-reference between § 1182(f) and the refugee-admissions framework, but its reasoning points to the general approach courts will take. If the President lawfully suspends entry under § 1182(f), that suspension can operate to override the practical implementation of any admissions ceiling for the period of suspension: a suspension saying “no entries” will prevent the filling of the ceiling. The statutory ceiling is a maximum number the government may admit in a fiscal year; it is not an individual right that would necessarily bind the President’s § 1182(f) power. Pacito, following Hawaii’s deference, treated the President’s moratorium as within § 1182(f) even though § 1157 contemplates admissions ceilings.

That said, the interaction is not entirely free from statutory constraints. Congress controls appropriations and the substantive obligations connected with the refugee program. The Refugee Act imposes affirmative duties on agencies (e.g., resettlement services in § 1522 and reporting duties in § 1523) that cannot simply be nullified by an executive proclamation if the statute requires action for persons already admitted or if Congress has appropriated funds conditioned on certain uses. Pacito’s holding about reinstating cooperative agreements grew out of precisely this tension: while suspending future admissions falls squarely within § 1182(f), cutting funding and aborting the statutory resettlement infrastructure required by the statute for refugees already admitted could violate other statutory provisions and thus be reviewable under the APA.

Moreover, some statutory provisions require consultation or reporting to Congress. If the statute conditions the President’s ability to take certain actions on consultation or notice requirements, an executive action in disregard of those conditions might be vulnerable. In practice, however, courts will be reluctant to find such conditions decisive when the action is a textually authorized suspension under § 1182(f), unless Congress’s text unmistakably constrains the President’s suspension authority. The lesson is that the most plausible statutory challenges will target collateral actions—funding decisions, programmatic terminations, or procedural failings—in the refugee regime rather than a direct attack on the decision to suspend admissions under § 1182(f).

## Implications for litigants: evidentiary showings and litigation posture post-Hawaii

Pacito shows that after Hawaii, litigants face a changed landscape when attacking presidential entry suspensions. The litigation strategy that once focused on compiling factual proof of the President’s error or lack of evidence is no longer promising. Courts will treat the President’s formal finding under § 1182(f) as a political judgment and will not entertain a searching factual inquiry into its wisdom. The consequence is twofold: plaintiffs must pick their targets, and defendants should shape the administrative record to the extent possible.

For plaintiffs seeking injunctive relief, the Nken stay framework remains controlling at the preliminary-relief stage. Because courts weigh likelihood of success and irreparable harm most heavily, a plaintiff who bases the merits entirely on contesting the President’s finding will likely fail to show a likelihood of success in light of Hawaii-style deference. Pacito illustrates this point: the Ninth Circuit found the government likely to succeed on the merits of the § 1182(f) suspension and therefore granted a stay of the district court’s injunctions as to the moratorium.

What should plaintiffs do instead? Consider these practical approaches illuminated by Pacito:

- Focus on statutory obligations that operate independently of the entry power. Pacito’s recognition that the Department’s statutory duties under § 1522 may not be displaced by an § 1182(f) proclamation shows that APA challenges to ancillary agency actions (e.g., termination of cooperative agreements that deliver statutorily mandated services) can succeed where the government cannot show that the statutory obligation has been lawfully altered.

- Emphasize concrete, imminent harms to protected statutory beneficiaries that are not contingent on future admissions. Injunctive relief is much more plausible when plaintiffs can show direct harm flowing from agency noncompliance with statutory obligations (for example, loss of benefits, services, or statutory entitlements already enjoyed by admitted refugees).

- Preserve record evidence of pretext where possible without relying solely on the merits of the President’s foreign-relations judgment. If plaintiffs can show that an agency’s stated reason for terminating services bears no relationship to the agency’s established statutory mission or that agency decisionmaking was procedurally defective (e.g., arbitrary-and-capricious action without reasoned explanation), those claims are adjudicable under the APA and do not require second-guessing the President’s national-security findings.

For defendants defending a § 1182(f) suspension, Pacito suggests several defensive steps:

- Ensure the formal “finding” is articulated and documented. While courts do not require an evidentiary record in every case, a clearly expressed formal finding in the President’s proclamation or supporting documentation strengthens the government’s position under Hawaii and Pacito.

- Separate entry-related actions from unrelated statutory obligations. If policy requires suspending admissions, preserve statutory programs that serve persons already in the United States unless there is a clear statutory basis to pause those programs. Pacito’s decision to require reinstatement of cooperative agreements reflects judicial willingness to enforce statutes directed at in-country beneficiaries even while deferring on entry restrictions.

- Anticipate collateral litigation under the APA and spend administrative effort to show reasoned decisionmaking for related agency actions (e.g., termination of contracts or cooperative agreements). A thorough administrative record may avoid an APA reversal even if the decision follows from a presidential suspension.

Finally, for courts and litigants alike, Pacito underscores the normative balance courts are trying to strike: respect for the President’s foreign-affairs and national-security decisionmaking combined with enforcement of statutory and constitutional constraints that protect rights and entitlements for persons already within the United States. The practical upshot is that successful constitutional or statutory challenges in this domain are more likely to be routed through non-§1182(f) avenues—procedural-regularity claims, statutory-mandate claims, funding and appropriations law, contractual obligations, and distinct statutory duties to persons already admitted—rather than direct factual attack on the President’s finding under § 1182(f).

## Conclusion — doctrinal synthesis and future contours

Pacito applies Hawaii’s deferential framework to a refugee moratorium, reasserting the principle that courts should not engage in searching fact-finding about presidential judgments made under § 1182(f). The Ninth Circuit’s stay opinion thus confirms that the formal “finding” requirement in § 1182(f) is a low hurdle and that courts will generally defer to presidential determinations about the detriment of admitting classes of aliens.

Yet Pacito also clarifies an important limitation: § 1182(f) is not a universal voucher that permits the Executive to extinguish other statutory obligations. Where Congress has conferred mandatory duties with respect to persons already in the United States (as in the reception-and-placement obligations of § 1522), courts retain the power to require agency compliance under the APA. Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito illuminates the textual debate over whether § 1522 imposes mandatory duties or an authorization; how lower courts resolve that interpretive question will have significant practical consequences for future litigation about the scope of executive power during suspensions of entry.

For students and practitioners, Pacito teaches three operating maxims. First, when the President invokes § 1182(f), courts will usually accord great deference to the formal presidential finding; litigants should not expect to prevail by litigating the factual merits of that finding. Second, statutory and procedural constraints that operate independently of entry (including statutory duties to persons already admitted) remain enforceable, and plaintiffs should press those claims energetically. Third, because the most defendable litigation strategies in this area increasingly turn on statutory interpretation and administrative-law doctrines (not reweighing foreign-affairs judgments), both sides should build robust records focused on statutory text, appropriations and obligations, procedural regularity, and concrete harms rather than primarily on contesting the President’s national-security rationales.
