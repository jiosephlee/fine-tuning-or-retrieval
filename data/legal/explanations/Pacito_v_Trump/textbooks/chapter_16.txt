Chapter 16: # Problems and Hypotheticals

This chapter uses the Ninth Circuit’s interim Pacito opinion to frame practical problems that test your ability to bring together statutory interpretation, Administrative Procedure Act reviewability and standards, and remedial design. Each hypothetical presents a realistic factual variation that implicates the same core doctrinal tensions the Pacito court confronted: the breadth of the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f); the content and enforceability of Congress’s statutory directions for refugee resettlement in 8 U.S.C. § 1522; and the scope of judicial supervision of executive action that terminates cooperative agreements and grants. For each fact pattern below, work through the legal issues the court must address, identify the relevant rules and tests, sketch how the parties will argue, and describe plausible judicial dispositions and remedial contours consistent with Pacito.

## Hypo 1 — Agency pauses all § 1522 grants citing “as needed” — is there law to apply?

Fact pattern. The Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) issues a one‑paragraph memo: “Effective today HHS will pause all discretionary grants and cooperative agreements made under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The Department interprets the statutory phrases ‘as needed’ and ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ to permit a complete, temporary pause.” The pause halts cash assistance, English language training, and other reception‑and‑placement services to thousands of refugees already admitted to the United States. Several resettlement organizations and recently admitted refugees sue under the APA and § 706(1) and (2), seeking a declaration that the memo is unlawful and an injunction requiring the agency to resume grant payments.

Step 1 — Identify the statutory question: mandate or authorization? The first and most important move is statutory interpretation. Pacito turned on competing readings of § 1522: the majority treated § 1522 as imposing binding duties that the executive branch must satisfy for refugees already admitted; Judge Lee read § 1522 principally as an authorization—i.e., permission to establish programs when appropriations are available. In this hypo the tension is the same. Your analysis should begin with the text of § 1522: notice the repeated use of “shall” in § 1522(a) together with qualifying language “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the use of “authorized” in subparts that describe specific programs. Context matters: compare the section title (“Authorization for programs…”) and the operative provisions. Can the “shall” in § 1522(a) be read as a condition on discretionary programs (mandatory instructions about how to run programs if they are funded), or does it create independent, enforceable duties regardless of whether Congress or the executive has chosen to fund and run those programs?

Step 2 — Consider the Canon of Construction and Contextual Signals. Courts will apply ordinary tools of interpretation: canons concerning titles and section headings (which are helpful but not dispositive), the significance of “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the statutory scheme as a whole. Pacito’s majority found that certain obligations in § 1522—for example, care for unaccompanied minors—create legally enforceable duties. In your memo you should explain why a court might treat some subsections as mandatory (where the text is precise and the underlying program addresses vulnerable persons already on U.S. soil) and others as discretionary (program design and outreach overseas).

Step 3 — Is there final agency action subject to APA review? If HHS simply issues a general policy memo pausing grants, plaintiffs must show that this memo constitutes “final agency action” under Bennett v. Spear: the action must mark the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking and determine rights or obligations or create legal consequences. A categorical pause that cuts off funding and is immediately operative likely will be treated as final—it causes concrete harm (lost services) and is not merely tentative. Pacito treated termination of cooperative agreements as a decisive step with legal consequences and ordered reinstatement in part because of the statutory provisions.

Step 4 — APA Standards. If the agency action is final, the court will review for compliance with the APA. Two principal paths: (a) the agency failed to follow required procedures (notice-and-comment, if the pause is a substantive rule), and (b) the policy is arbitrary and capricious—i.e., the agency failed to consider important aspects of the problem, offered no rational connection between facts and decision, or ignored statutory obligations. In Pacito the court found that the government had not demonstrated it could meet statutory duties and required reinstatement at least for already‑admitted refugees; analogously, plaintiffs here will argue the pause ignores statutory commands and is arbitrary because HHS did not evaluate how statutory duties to admitted refugees would be satisfied.

How the arguments will be framed
- Plaintiffs’ arguments. Textual reading of § 1522 that gives content to mandatory language; Bennett finality; arbitrary and capricious review because the agency failed to consider the consequences for refugees already admitted and has not explained how statutory duties will be met; irreparable injury and emergency relief for vulnerable populations; the agency cannot use the “to the extent of appropriations” clause to escape obligations where funds exist or were appropriated for the fiscal year.
- Government’s arguments. § 1522 is permissive; the memo is an exercise of discretion; the agency acted within the statutory authorization and to preserve resources; the decision is committed to agency discretion by law (5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)) or is policy-making not subject to judicial second-guessing; lack of finality if the memo is a preliminary internal statement.

Plausible outcomes consistent with Pacito
- Court finds the memo to be final agency action and reviews it. If the statutory text and circumstances show statutory duties to refugees already admitted, the court could enjoin the pause as arbitrary and capricious with limited scope—requiring resumption of services for refugees already in the United States while allowing the agency discretion regarding new awards. This mirrors Pacito’s direction to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted.
- Alternatively, a court persuaded by the government’s reading may treat § 1522 as discretionary, find the memo within agency discretion, and deny relief, leaving plaintiffs to pursue political channels or seek injunctive relief on non‑statutory grounds (e.g., constitutional claims if any).

Key practical lessons for litigants
- Plaintiffs should develop a record showing the concrete reliance and reliance interests of refugees, existing appropriations earmarked or previously allocated, and the operational consequences of a pause.
- The government should document decisionmaking, explain budgetary constraints, and identify any competing statutory obligations or appropriations limitations that make continuation impossible without violating the Antideficiency Act.

## Hypo 2 — President narrows admissions by nationality under § 1182(f) with sparse findings — apply Hawaii analysis

Fact pattern. The President issues an order suspending admission of refugees from Nation X for 90 days, citing § 1182(f). The order recites a single sentence: “I find that entry of refugees from Nation X would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” No supporting factual findings, reporting, or assessment accompanies the order. Plaintiffs challenge under the APA and the Constitution, arguing the order fails even the modest judicial check announced in Trump v. Hawaii and described in Pacito.

Legal framework — what Pacito tells us about § 1182(f)
Pacito applied the Hawaii framework: the statute requires only that the President “find” entry would be detrimental; courts must accord substantial deference. The Ninth Circuit emphasized that “the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’” and that the language “exudes deference,” quoting Trump v. Hawaii. But deferential review is not no review: the President’s action must fall within the statutory language (suspension of “entry”) and must not require courts to probe the merits of national‑security determinations beyond the statutory prerequisite. Moreover, there are constitutional limits—if the action is motivated by invidious discrimination (e.g., religious animus), constitutional scrutiny can apply (Trump travel‑ban litigation is instructive).

How to litigate sparse findings
- Plaintiffs’ approach. Plaintiffs should argue that while courts do not demand a full classified record, there must be some minimal factual showing or administrative record tying the suspension to identifiable risk—evidence that Nation X poses specific security risks or that existing vetting is inadequate relative to demonstrated risks. They can rely on precedent that asks for a “facially legitimate and bona fide” justification (Kleindienst v. Mandel) for exclusion decisions, and emphasize that a one‑sentence conclusion is conclusory and may be arbitrary and capricious if reviewable under the APA (assuming agency delegation or that the order is not nonreviewable presidential action).
- Government’s approach. The government will emphasize executive primacy in national security and immigration, contend that the President's single‑sentence “finding” satisfies § 1182(f)’s sole statutory prerequisite, and stress that courts should not probe the sufficiency of the President’s factual determinations. It may submit classified declarations or ex parte material to the district court for in camera review to buttress the finding without revealing sensitive sources.

Standards of review and possible outcomes
- Deference under Trump v. Hawaii is powerful but not absolute. If the government provides at least a minimal record—classified or unclassified—showing particularized risk or explanation, courts will likely uphold the suspension. Pacito’s interim order suggested courts should not engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Thus an order with sparse but plausible support or a classified submission will likely survive review.
- Conversely, if the record is literally the single sentence and the suspension impacts persons with no plausible connection to national security risk (e.g., refugees vetted and approved, or a country with no history of terrorism), a court could find the action arbitrary or procedurally deficient if reviewable. For constitutional claims, evidence of discriminatory intent (statements, pattern of targeting) could trigger heightened scrutiny and defeat the suspension.

Practical considerations: use of classified evidence and remedial scope
- The government’s practical strategy is to supply classified affidavits in camera to show the factual basis without public exposure; courts accepting such submissions have often afforded deference.
- Remedies should be tailored. Pacito’s interim ruling allows that the presidential authority may be broad but does not absolve the executive of its statutory duties to individuals already admitted or conditionally approved. A court could enjoin the suspension insofar as it affects refugees who have already received approvals and have reliance, while deferring to the government for future admissions decisions.

## Hypo 3 — Partial rescission mid‑year; must ORR continue existing case management?

Fact pattern. Congress enacts a mid‑fiscal‑year rescission that reduces funds for refugee resettlement by $600 million and attaches a rider forbidding expenditure of those rescinded sums. The Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) announces it will not pay for ongoing case management for refugees beyond the current month. Refugee service providers and refugees sue, asking the court to order ORR to continue case management for refugees admitted earlier in the fiscal year.

Statutory and appropriations law conflicts
- “To the extent of available appropriations.” A key phrase in § 1522 is that programs are to occur “to the extent of available appropriations.” The interplay of a statutory “shall” and appropriations restrictions forces courts to address whether a statutory duty survives a lawful rescission. Pacito acknowledged appropriations numbers and a rescission but nevertheless directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees who have been admitted, suggesting that statutory responsibilities to those already admitted carry weight even amid funding reductions.
- The Antideficiency Act and constitutional separation of powers. A court cannot order an agency to spend money that Congress has lawfully rescinded. Courts remedy unlawful executive action with injunctions reinstating prior agency behavior only when funding legally permits. If Congress has lawfully rescinded the funds with proper enactment, ORR may lack funds to continue case management. But the timing and legality of the rescission matter: if the rescission unlawfully targeted an appropriated obligation or violated statutory requirements (e.g., if the rescission was improperly enacted or limited to retroactive amounts beyond statutory authority), plaintiffs could seek relief.

Analytical steps for the court
1. Determine whether the rescission is in force and legally effective. A mid‑year rescission is presumptively valid if it is a product of regular order and properly enacted. If the rescission was accompanied by a clause suspending the obligation to spend previously obligated funds, the agency may be unable to comply with a court order that requires new expenditures.
2. Determine whether expenditures for case management were already obligated under binding cooperative agreements. Contract obligations or cooperative agreements signed before the rescission might create payment obligations that are not extinguished by later rescission of funds for future obligations. If the government had binding obligations, the court may order performance or payment unless the rescission was lawful and retroactively eliminated those obligations.
3. Reconcile statutory duty and appropriations. If § 1522 imposes mandatory duties for those already admitted (Pacito majority’s view), then a court may find that the executive was required to plan and obligate funds for ongoing services and that a rescission that frustrates those statutory duties may be unlawful if Congress intended otherwise.

Possible judicial outcomes
- If the rescission is valid and there are no preexisting contractual obligations that bind the government to continue discrete payments, a court will likely be unable to order ORR to spend money Congress has rescinded. The court may, however, fashion alternative equitable relief—e.g., ordering the agency to prioritize available funds for the most vulnerable or to provide nonmonetary assistance that does not require new appropriations.
- If the plaintiffs show that the agency had binding commitments or that the rescission is unlawful or misapplied, a court could order continuation of services for already admitted refugees, limited in time and scope, and leave prospective obligations to Congress.

Practical remedial design
- Where appropriations constraints bind, courts should avoid ordering the executive to create new spending. Instead, a practical remedy is an injunction requiring the agency to allocate remaining lawful funds in a manner that gives priority to statutorily protected categories (e.g., unaccompanied minors) and to provide transitional assistance for a reasonable period to avoid immediate harm.
- Courts can also retain jurisdiction to monitor compliance and to adjust remedies if Congress acts or if additional funds become available.

## Hypo 4 — Termination‑for‑convenience clauses in cooperative agreements and APA reviewability

Fact pattern. The State Department has cooperative agreements with ResettleCo that include a standard termination‑for‑convenience clause allowing the government to terminate with 30 days’ notice. The Department invokes that clause and terminates the cooperative agreement immediately with no explanation. ResettleCo sues, asserting the termination is arbitrary and capricious under the APA and that the Department violated its statutory obligations under § 1522 by failing to ensure reception and placement services for admitted refugees.

Is termination of cooperative agreements reviewable?
- Final agency action and the contract context. Not every contractual decision by the government is an agency action reviewable under the APA; but if the termination is made by an agency and has concrete legal consequences—termination of services, loss of funds, and downstream statutory impacts—Bennett’s finality test is likely satisfied. Pacito treated the termination of cooperative agreements as an action with legal consequences and directed reinstatement for a certain category of refugees.
- Contract remedies versus APA remedies. The government emphasizes the termination‑for‑convenience clause as private law that allocates risk; typically, a private contractor’s remedies would be contractual (Breach, reprocurement costs, bid protests, claim before the contracting officer). But the presence of a statutorily mandated obligation (if § 1522 is read as mandatory) gives rise to overlap: a business plaintiff may have primarily contract law remedies, while third parties (refugees) injured by the termination may seek APA review focused on statutory compliance.

Arbitrary and capricious review in the termination context
- An agency that terminates for convenience is not free to act capriciously. The APA’s arbitrary and capricious standard requires agencies to consider relevant factors and avoid unexplained flip‑flops. If the agency terminates because of policy reasons tied to legitimate statutory management considerations, it may be within its discretion. But if the termination is pretextual—intended solely to frustrate statutory protections for refugees—or made without considering obligations under § 1522 for currently admitted refugees, the termination can be set aside.
- Procedural obligations attached to cooperative agreements matter. Some cooperative agreements require notice, hearings, or mitigation steps before termination. The agency’s failure to comply with its own procedures can be a basis for APA relief.

How litigants should frame claims
- Plaintiffs (contractor and refugees) should build a record: timing of termination, whether termination was used to cut off services to already‑admitted refugees, internal communications showing pretext, and whether termination disregarded statutory duties. For refugees, the key claim is that the agency’s termination violated § 1522 duties and was arbitrary because the agency made no plan to replace services.
- Government’s defenses: the clause is a contractual right, contracting officers must be given deference, the decision is committed to agency discretion, and the agency acted to serve a broader policy objective (budgetary reallocation or reorganization).

Outcome scenarios
- Court fashions split relief: it may find the termination is final agency action reviewable under the APA and set aside the termination to the extent it prejudices statutory duties for refugees already admitted, while leaving the remainder of the termination intact and preserving the government’s contractual discretion for future planning. This is closely aligned with Pacito’s remedy ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted.
- Alternatively, if the court treats the termination as an ordinary contractual exercise or finds no statutory duty to the refugees, the relief may be limited to contract damages for the contractor and no broader remedy for refugee plaintiffs.

Practical tips
- Contractors should preserve claims under the Contract Disputes Act and also plead APA claims if termination implicates statutory obligations and public‑interest harm.
- Third‑party plaintiffs should emphasize statutory language and reliance interests and seek preliminary relief that restores services for currently admitted refugees pending full review.

## Hypo 5 — Unaccompanied minors in transit during an entry suspension — scope of § 1522(d)(2)(B)

Fact pattern. The President suspends refugee admissions generally for 60 days. At the time the suspension takes effect, several unaccompanied minors are in transit to the United States—on flights with approved resettlement visas but not yet physically at border control—and their overseas processing agencies ask whether ORR must assume legal and financial responsibility under § 1522(d)(2)(B): “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.”

Statutory interpretation and the meaning of “in transit”
- Plain meaning and temporal coverage. Pacito emphasized that § 1522(d)(2)(B) explicitly covers unaccompanied minors “in transit to the United States.” The statute thus anticipates situations where children are en route but not yet placed in ORR custody. A court inclined to enforce statutory text will read “in transit” broadly to include children physically en route on aircraft or other conveyances bound for U.S. points, and who are reasonably expected to enter. This reading is consonant with protective, child‑welfare‑oriented statutory purposes.
- The “if necessary” qualification. The statutory duty is conditional—“shall assume legal responsibility…if necessary.” The “if necessary” clause gives the Director some discretion to intervene only when other mechanisms are inadequate. But “if necessary” is not empty; it requires agency assessment and explanation. For example, if commercial carriers or resettlement partners can provide continuous care, the Director might conclude ORR assumption is unnecessary; if not, ORR must act.

Operational complications with a suspension of entry
- Does a suspension of entry extinguish the category of “in transit”? A suspension that bars “entry” does not necessarily erase the child’s status as “in transit.” Congress drafted § 1522 mindful of contingencies, and Pacito noted the statute’s explicit text. If the suspension stops flights or border processing, the effect on ORR obligations depends on the nature of the suspension: if entry is halted by presidential proclamation under § 1182(f), the government may assert that the children cannot legally enter and therefore need not be taken into custody. Plaintiffs will counter that statute requires action to protect vulnerable minors and that the agency must assume responsibility until the children are either admitted, returned safely, or otherwise placed.
- Burdens of proof and emergency relief. For minors physically in transit, a court is likely to treat the issue as an emergency because of the heightened vulnerability of children. Plaintiffs should seek immediate declaratory relief that ORR must evaluate individual cases and assume responsibility where necessary.

Litigation strategies and likely judicial approaches
- Plaintiffs should seek individualized determinations: judges are more comfortable ordering agency processes (a narrow order to assess each child’s needs and decide whether ORR must assume responsibility) than broad programmatic mandates. Pacito’s majority imposed a narrow reinstatement obligation for certain refugees; similarly, courts may order ORR to exercise its statutory judgment in a reasoned manner for each child in transit.
- The government should document operational constraints and may propose a contingency plan: for children in transit, the agency could provide interim funding to carriers or resettlement partners to secure care without formally assuming legal responsibility—if statute permits. But ORR must show whether the plan satisfies the “if necessary” criterion or explain why formal assumption of legal responsibility is unnecessary.

Possible judicial outcomes
- The court orders ORR to make individualized determinations and to assume legal and financial responsibility where the agency cannot ensure the minors’ safety or welfare through other means—consistent with § 1522(d)(2)(B)’s protective purpose and Pacito’s deference to statutory obligations for admitted refugees.
- If the government can show safe alternative arrangements without ORR taking on legal responsibility, the court may allow such arrangements but retain jurisdiction for oversight and require periodic reporting.

Policy and practical implications
- The “in transit” language functions as a safeguard: courts will treat unaccompanied minors as a high‑priority group. Agencies should adopt clear procedures to evaluate “necessity” and document decisions to avoid arbitrary outcomes and litigation.
- Prosecutorial and immigration authorities must coordinate with ORR to prevent gaps where children are left without responsibility simply because of a suspension order.

***

Concluding guidance for exam essays and litigation memos

1. Start from the text, but do not stop there. Pacito demonstrates that textual cues (e.g., “shall,” “authorized,” “to the extent of appropriations,” and “in transit”) are determinative in framing the plausibility of statutory duties. Yet context, purpose, and statutory scheme are critical to refining the textual interpretation.

2. Distinguish categories of claim. Claims that challenge the President’s suspension of entry under § 1182(f) raise separation‑of‑powers and national security deference questions; claims that challenge agency suspensions or terminations of cooperative agreements under § 1522 raise APA and statutory‑compliance questions. Treat each claim with the distinct standard and remedies that the relevant doctrine supplies.

3. Seek tailored remedies. Pacito’s remedial approach is instructive: the court enforced statutory duties for a defined class (refugees already admitted or conditionally approved) without ordering the President’s policy reversed wholesale. In practice, the most sustainable relief is narrowly tailored—reinstatement for those already admitted, individualized determinations for unaccompanied minors, and monitoring of implementation—rather than broad programmatic injunctions that run directly into appropriations constraints and separation‑of‑powers concerns.

4. Build the administrative record. Plaintiffs must assemble evidence of reliance, concrete harms, and the agency’s failure to consider statutory duties. The government should prepare reasoned explanations and, where national security is invoked, appropriate classified justifications or declarations showing a rational predicate.

These hypotheticals are designed to force you to deploy the statutory‑interpretation tools and the APA’s procedural and substantive standards in a messy intersection of immigration policy, appropriations law, contract law, and child‑welfare concerns—precisely the mix the Pacito court balanced in its interim decision.
