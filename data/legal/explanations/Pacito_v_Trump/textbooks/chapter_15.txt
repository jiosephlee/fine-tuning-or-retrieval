Chapter 15: # Pacito’s Place in the Doctrinal Landscape and Future Directions

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes a key doctrinal posture that the federal courts are likely to take in the next phase of litigation over presidential immigration policymaking: robust deference to a President’s statutory authority to suspend entries under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), coupled with a willingness to enforce statutory obligations that Congress has placed on the executive for those already admitted to the United States. Pacito thus functions as a synthesis: it reaffirms the high degree of judicial deference described in Trump v. Hawaii when the President finds entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States,” but it also draws a line at the executive’s ability to defeat otherwise mandatory statutory duties—here, the reception-and-placement obligations in 8 U.S.C. § 1522—by terminating cooperative agreements and cutting off services for refugees already admitted. This chapter unpacks Pacito’s doctrinal holdings, the tests the court applied, and the practical and doctrinal consequences for future litigation, executive action, and legislation.

## Reaffirmation of Hawaii’s deference on § 1182(f) entry suspensions

Pacito begins where modern § 1182(f) jurisprudence now resides: with Trump v. Hawaii’s articulation of a heavily deferential standard. In Trump, the Supreme Court explained that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President, furnishing “ample power” to suspend entries in addition to the otherwise enumerated INA provisions. Pacito’s motions panel invoked the same language and applied the same logical constraint: the “sole prerequisite” under § 1182(f) is a presidential finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Once the President makes that finding in an executive order, Pacito treats the court’s role as circumscribed; it stated that courts cannot undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” That language signals continued judicial reluctance to substitute judicial assessments for presidential judgments about national security, foreign affairs, and related immigration concerns.

Two points about the nature of that deference are worth emphasizing for doctrinal application. First, the deferential posture is statutory—grounded in the text and structure of § 1182(f)—not an abdication of judicial review. Courts will still confirm the existence of a formal presidential “finding” and whether the authority invoked is textually available. But the inquiry does not typically extend to searching review of the empirical or policy premises underlying the finding. Second, deference is contextual: it is strongest where the President purports to act pursuant to a clear, textually granted statutory power to regulate entry, and where national security and foreign-relations considerations are implicated. By contrast, Pacito shows that deference will not permit the executive to fold otherwise applicable domestic statutory obligations into the presidential decision and thereby nullify them.

In practice, therefore, future challenges to presidential entry suspensions under § 1182(f) face a steep uphill path. Litigants should anticipate that courts will limit judicial review to whether the President invoked a statutory mechanism correctly, whether the measure is reasonably within the scope of that mechanism’s textual grant, and whether any nonjusticiable political-question barriers apply. Proof that the President’s paper finding was merely a pretext, or that the order was adopted without any factual tether at all, will be the difficult but necessary route to more searching review. Conversely, litigation that focuses on collateral executive actions (funding cuts, agency terminations, programmatic changes) rather than the core admission determination is more likely to survive the deference wall.

## Boundary-setting on executive defunding when statutes impose service obligations

Pacito’s most significant doctrinal contribution is the boundary it draws between the President’s power to suspend entries and the executive’s statutory obligations toward refugees already in the United States. The panel recognized that Congress, through 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions, created a statutory regime for “reception and placement” services: employment and language training, cash and medical assistance, and special care duties for unaccompanied children. The court concluded—at least for the preliminary posture and for purposes of a stay pending appeal—that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the executive’s termination of cooperative agreements unlawfully frustrated those statutory duties. Accordingly, the court ordered the reinstatement of those agreements necessary to provide services to refugees who had been admitted.

Two doctrinal threads explain why the court reached this result, and both have wider application.

First, statutory interpretation. Where Congress uses mandatory language—“shall”—and pairs it with programmatic detail (what services must be provided, by whom, and in what form), courts are predisposed to read the text as imposing substantive duties on the executive. Pacito emphasized the specific obligations in § 1522 (employment training and placement, English language instruction, cash assistance administered to promote self-sufficiency, parity for women, obligations toward unaccompanied minors, and projects for cash and medical assistance). Although § 1522 contains phrases like “to the extent of available appropriations,” the majority reasoned that those qualifications modulate the intensity of obligations but do not automatically convert substantive mandates into mere authorizations. The practical consequence is important: statutes that prescribe programs, beneficiaries, and administrative responsibilities can create judicially enforceable duties even when funding is subject to appropriations.

Second, the court treated the executive’s programmatic contractions as reviewable agency action under the Administrative Procedure Act. The termination of cooperative agreements was not dismissed as a purely political decision insulated from review; instead, it was treated as a concrete change in the executive’s administration of a statutorily authorized program that could frustrate statutory obligations. That approach brings into play the spectrum of APA doctrines—failure to follow required procedures, arbitrary-and-capricious review of abrupt policy shifts, and enforcement of mandatory statutory duties—and permits courts to enjoin agency action that undermines clear statutory aims, especially where vulnerable populations are involved.

For lawyers and judges, Pacito therefore sets out a dividing line: pre-entry policy (suspension of admissions) will normally be judged under the Arabia of deference owed to presidential findings under § 1182(f); post-entry program administration that implicates specific statutory obligations will be judged under ordinary statutory-interpretation and administrative-law principles. That syntactic divide is consequential when counsel frames claims: pleading and proof strategies that highlight concrete statutory commands and their frustrated implementation (rather than abstract policy disagreements about who may enter) will be more successful at enlisting judicial review.

## Strategic implications for plaintiffs and the government in crafting records and remedies

Pacito is a roadmap for litigation strategy on both sides. Plaintiffs who hope to enjoin executive actions should differentiate pre-entry claims from post-entry operational claims and structure their records accordingly.

For plaintiffs:
- Focus on statutory duties and the administrative record. Pacito shows that a detailed record demonstrating how agency actions (terminations of cooperative agreements, defunding, cancellation of support services) interfere with statutory mandates is powerful. Collect and present documentary evidence of terminated agreements, communications showing abrupt termination without transition, evidence that alternative providers are not ready, and affidavits showing concrete harms to admitted refugees.
- Emphasize discrete, enforceable statutory commands. Claims that the executive has violated specific provisions that use mandatory language (“shall”) and identify duties or beneficiaries are more judicially cognizable than broad policy complaints. Seek to prove both the textual basis and the practical consequences of noncompliance.
- Seek narrow, program-specific equitable relief. Pacito’s remedy—to direct reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services for admitted refugees—illustrates the power of carefully tailored injunctive relief that respects the broader policy prerogatives of the political branches while protecting statutory rights of those already within the United States.
- Preserve exigent evidence of irreparable harm. Even where courts give substantial weight to presidential findings on entry, they still weigh irreparable injury in stay and preliminary-injunction analyses. For admitted refugees, personal affidavits describing lost services, delay in medical care, or loss of benefits will help establish the kind of irreparable injury that many courts find persuasive.

For the government:
- Build a robust administrative record if invoking § 1182(f). Although Pacito reiterates deference, it also underscores that courts will treat a presidential finding as formalistic if the record is empty. Documentation of the predicate facts, internal interagency assessments, and reasoned explanation for decisions will make it harder for courts to find the President’s finding a mere pretext.
- Avoid abrupt terminations that leave statutory duties unfulfilled. The court in Pacito reacted to the government’s sudden termination of cooperative agreements and expressed concern that no ready replacement existed. Even where the President may lawfully suspend admissions, the executive should preserve continuity of services for admitted persons unless a reasoned plan exists. Maintaining or phasing out agreements with notice and transition plans reduces the risk of judicial intervention on APA grounds.
- Use clear appropriation and policy-based explanations where funding changes. Where the government relies on the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations,” it should document budgetary constraints, identify what services can be funded, and delineate the statutory consequences of any funding shortfalls. An opaque or ad-hoc claim that funds are unavailable will be vulnerable in court.
- Consider narrowly tailored remedies and settlements. Where judicial intervention is likely, agencies can negotiate court-supervised transition plans for service provision that meet statutory minima. Such negotiated remedies reduce litigation risk and permit the executive to pursue its broader policy goals while meeting statutory obligations.

Remedially, Pacito shows courts will not hesitate to craft targeted equitable orders—reinstatement of cooperative agreements, specific program directives—so long as the remedies are tailored to statutory duties and do not attempt to micromanage policy decisions that fall squarely within presidential authority.

## Potential Supreme Court review and circuit splits to watch

Pacito is poised to invite further appellate and possibly Supreme Court attention because its core holdings push against two longstanding tensions: executive flexibility in immigration policymaking and judicial enforcement of statutory duties toward noncitizens already present in the United States. Several doctrinal issues are likely to generate circuit splits and ultimately to attract certiorari.

Key questions likely to generate division:

1. The scope of judicial review of § 1182(f) determinations. Some judges and circuits may read Trump v. Hawaii as effectively insulating § 1182(f) presidential findings from any meaningful review; others may insist on a minimal but substantive record of factual basis for such findings. Pacito falls in the latter camp on procedural sufficiency (requiring an adequate administrative record in practice) while endorsing deference to the presidential finding’s substance. Circuits that reject any meaningful review could conflict with Pacito.

2. Whether § 1522 creates enforceable, mandatory duties. Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito takes the opposite view: § 1522 and its programmatic provisions are authorizations, not compulsions. That interpretive split—mandatory statute versus permissive authorization—presents a pure statutory-interpretation conflict that different circuits could resolve differently, especially given the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification. A split over the mandatory/authorization point would be a natural vehicle for Supreme Court review because it implicates separation of powers (what duties Congress can impose on the executive) and the role of courts in enforcing statutory programs.

3. The reach of APA review when the executive changes programmatic operations tied to immigration policy. Some circuits may hold that core immigration decisions are nonreviewable political questions; others may treat programmatic changes that affect individuals inside the United States as ordinary agency actions subject to APA review. A circuit split over justiciability and the proper scope of APA review in immigration-adjacent program changes would be significant.

4. The role of appropriations language in converting statutory commands into discretionary duties. If circuits disagree about how to interpret “to the extent of available appropriations,” the Supreme Court may be asked whether that language unambiguously makes statutory duties contingent on appropriations or whether it simply modulates the extent of performance but leaves the duty intact.

If the Supreme Court takes up any of these questions, expect briefing and argument to turn on text-and-structure arguments, the nature of agency discretion, and the special role of the political branches in national-security and foreign-affairs domains. The Court may be especially interested in carving out the “admission vs. post-admission” distinction: even if the executive has great latitude in deciding who the United States admits, does that latitude extend to refusing to perform statutory duties for those already present? Pacito suggests the answer is “no,” a proposition that could produce doctrinal clarification at the highest level.

## Legislative options: clarifying § 1522’s mandatory scope and funding mechanisms

Pacito also highlights the levers that Congress possesses if it wishes to reduce litigation and provide clearer guidance to the executive. Two broad legislative approaches are available: clarification of substance (what services must be provided and to whom) and clarification of funding (how those services are to be financed and when the executive may stop paying).

Substantive clarifications:
- Make statutory duties explicit and categorical where Congress wishes them enforced. Rewriting key provisions in § 1522 to say “The Director shall provide [specified services] to refugees admitted to the United States” without caveats would reduce ambiguity and strengthen judicial enforcement.
- Define the trigger events and covered populations. Congress can specify whether obligations run to refugees conditionally approved abroad, refugees physically present in the United States, unaccompanied minors in transit, or other defined classes. Clear definitions will preclude disputes over standing and temporal scope.
- Require minimum continuity plans for changes. Mandating that, before program terminations, agencies must publish a transition plan that preserves services for a set period or until substitute providers are in place would prevent abrupt service cessations like those that prompted Pacito.

Funding and appropriations clarity:
- Create mandatory appropriation lines or trust funds. Instead of authorizing programs “to the extent of available appropriations,” Congress can appropriate dedicated funds for reception-and-placement services, which would reduce the executive’s ability to avoid program obligations through budgetary maneuvers. Such mandatory funding raises constitutional and political considerations (Congressional control of the purse), but it is the most direct means to ensure program continuity.
- Enact emergency contingency funds. A standby appropriation could be used to meet statutory obligations during short periods when other funds are withdrawn for policy reasons, thereby preventing immediate service collapses.
- Require notice to appropriations committees. If the executive wants to reallocate or rescind funding for refugee programs, it must provide advance notice and a justification to the Committees on Appropriations, together with a plan to avoid interruption of statutorily mandated services. This would enhance congressional oversight and create administrative friction to deter abrupt cuts.

Congress may also consider more surgical solutions: procedural requirements (notice-and-comment before terminating cooperative agreements), reporting obligations with specific metrics, and criminal or civil penalties for agencies that violate statutorily defined duties (the last is politically charged and constitutionally fraught, but it is a legislative lever).

By clarifying both the substantive duties and the funding mechanisms, Congress can reduce the kinds of statutory-interpretation disputes Pacito exposed and provide agencies with clearer guidance on how to reconcile competing policy objectives.

## Practical litigation and doctrinal developments to watch

Looking forward, several doctrinal ripples from Pacito merit close attention.

1. The admission/post-admission divide will be litigated repeatedly. Future plaintiffs will press APA and statutory claims that focus on concrete harms to persons already admitted. Defendants will continue to assert broad § 1182(f) authority. How courts define the line between admission policy and program administration will shape outcomes.

2. The role of “to the extent of available appropriations” will generate doctrinal work. Courts will have to develop standards for evaluating executive invocation of funding constraints—what proof is necessary to sustain a claim that funds are genuinely unavailable, and how should courts assess agency efforts to reallocate monies?

3. Procedural prerequisites will be litigated. Where programmatic changes are effected through administrative mechanisms (terminations of cooperative agreements, cancellation of grants, or revisions of agency guidance), courts will examine whether notice-and-comment or other procedures were required and whether the agency’s action was arbitrary and capricious given reliance interests and abruptness.

4. Standing, mootness, and scope-of-relief litigation will follow. Pacito’s carveouts (protecting certain classes of refugees and services) suggest that careful record-development about who is affected and how services have been interrupted will be decisive in shaping both relief and appellate outcomes.

5. The intersection with constitutional doctrine remains alive but secondary. Pacito resolved disputes primarily through statutory and administrative law tools, but litigants may continue to press Due Process or other constitutional claims (especially where the termination of services results in particularly severe harms). The courts’ willingness to entertain such claims may vary across circuits.

## Conclusion

Pacito is best read as a pragmatic reconciliation of two familiar but competing imperatives. On the one hand, it reaffirms the deference long accorded to presidential determinations about entry under § 1182(f). On the other hand, it asserts that the executive cannot use that deferential cloak to nullify detailed statutory obligations Congress placed on the government to protect and assist refugees already present in the United States. For practitioners, Pacito clarifies that litigation should separate the entry decision (where judicial relief will be difficult) from programmatic implementation (where courts will enforce clear statutory duties). For policymakers, Pacito points toward legislative fixes that can reduce future litigation by clarifying statutory commands and funding structures. And for the judiciary, Pacito invites a finer-grained approach to immigration-adjacent disputes: give space for the political branches on who may enter, but protect statutory rights and administrative accountability once people are within the United States’ borders.
