Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

Chapter 6: # Pacito v. Trump: Facts, Procedure, and the Interim Stay Order

This chapter uses the Ninth Circuit’s expedited interim order in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) as a vehicle to study three intertwined themes that commonly arise in litigation over executive immigration action: (1) the scope of presidential authority to exclude classes of aliens under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and related immigration statutes; (2) the limits imposed by federal statutes that create programmatic duties or authorize resettlement services for refugees; and (3) the procedure and standard for stays pending appeal of preliminary injunctions (the Nken factors), including how courts reconcile deference to the President on immigration policy with statutory duties that fall on other agencies. The opinion is unusual because the panel simultaneously concluded that the President likely had authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) while also concluding the government was unlikely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge tied to statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee provides a contrasting interpretive account of § 1522 that sharpens the doctrinal issue: is § 1522 a source of mandatory statutory duties that the courts may enforce, or merely an authorization that leaves the matter to executive discretion?

Below we set out the relevant executive actions and statutes, then walk through the district court’s injunctions, the Ninth Circuit’s stay analysis, and Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent. At each step we explain the doctrinal rules and how to apply them to new fact patterns.

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Entry under § 1182(f) and § 1185(a)

Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program,” directed a suspension of refugee admissions to the United States. The Order invoked two statutory authorities: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and § 1185(a). Section 1182(f) provides that “whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may by proclamation . . . suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens.” Section 1185(a) gives the President power to “make rules and regulations for the conduct of [immigration-related] functions” and to protect border security and the administration of immigration laws. Together, these provisions have been repeatedly cited by Presidents as the source of authority to impose entry restrictions on foreign nationals.

Legal doctrine. Pacito shows the continuing force of two related principles. First, courts traditionally afford substantial deference to the President in the immigration and national security contexts, particularly when Congress has granted broad, textually explicit authority. The Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) is the key precedent: it characterized § 1182(f) as a grant of broad, plenary authority that “exudes deference” to the President and limited courts’ inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. Under that line of cases, when the President “finds” that entry would be detrimental, courts generally treat that finding as the prerequisite that Congress required, and judicial review of the substantive policy decision is highly constrained.

Second, however, deference under § 1182(f) is not absolute. The statute’s plain text, as interpreted in the context of statutory structure, limits — at least in theory — the President’s authority to what Congress authorized. If another statute imposes duties or protects particular classes of people, the interplay between those statutory directives and § 1182(f) may be a matter for judicial resolution. The Pacito panel recognized this tension: it emphasized the high barrier to overturning the President’s suspension of admissions, while preserving the possibility that other statutory obligations (for example, duties to provide reception and placement services) could constrain the executive’s implementation of a suspension.

Applying the doctrine. Where an executive action cites § 1182(f), a court considering a challenge must ask (1) whether the President has complied with § 1182(f)’s textual prerequisite (i.e., that he “finds” entry of a class would be detrimental), and (2) whether the action exceeds textual or structural limits imposed by statute or constitutional constraints. Under Trump v. Hawaii, the first question will usually be resolved in the President’s favor; the second question requires careful attention to whether other statutes create mandatory rights or duties that the President’s action would nullify.

Practice tip. When litigating or analyzing a § 1182(f) action, separate the core question of authority under § 1182(f) (where deference is strong) from other statutory or constitutional claims (e.g., claims that the action violates a separate statutory duty). Demonstrating that § 1182(f) language is present is necessary but often not sufficient to win on every related claim.

## Executive Order 14169 and the Suspension of State Department Funding for USRAP

Shortly after EO 14163, the State Department suspended all funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), citing a separate executive order concerning foreign aid (EO 14169). The funding suspension was broad: it included overseas processing support and domestic resettlement (“reception and placement”) funding for refugees already admitted to the United States.

Statutory context. The Refugee Act of 1980 and the Immigration and Nationality Act create both authorization and appropriations pathways for refugee assistance and resettlement services. Relevant for Pacito is 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” Section 1522 contains several subsections: (a) “Conditions and consideration” (with some mandatory phrasing), (b) and (c) through (e) describing programmatic authorities (often phrased as “is authorized”), (d) addressing unaccompanied refugee children, and (e) addressing cash and medical assistance (including § 1522(e)(7)(A) requiring the Secretary to “develop and implement” projects “under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed”). Section 1523 requires reporting and accountability to Congress. All of these provisions operate in the shadow of the appropriations power: some duties appear “to the extent of available appropriations” and many programmatic provisions presuppose congressional funding.

Legal doctrine. Two separate, but related, statutory doctrines are in play. One is the classic distinction between authorization statutes (which permit or authorize spending or program creation) and appropriations statutes (which provide the money). Courts insist that an authorization standing alone does not obligate an agency to spend; only an appropriation obliges money to be spent. The second doctrinal axis is statutory interpretation: whether language in § 1522 is mandatory (creating a judicially enforceable duty) or permissive (a delegation of discretion to the executive). The presence of words like “shall” weighs toward a mandatory reading; words like “is authorized” point towards discretion. Context and structure matter: provisions that state conditions “in providing assistance under this section” may mean that the “shall” language creates conditions that apply only if the government chooses to provide assistance.

Applying the doctrine. In Pacito the government used EO 14169 as the means to cut off the financial infrastructure that supported refugee resettlement. Whether that cutoff was lawful depends on whether § 1522 imposes enforceable duties that continue even when the President issues an order suspending admissions or when the State Department cuts funding. If § 1522 creates a mandatory statutory duty to provide certain reception and placement services for refugees already admitted, then suspending funding for those services may violate statute and the APA; if § 1522 is merely an authorization, the executive’s funding decisions remain within the political branches’ discretion.

Practice tip. When arguing whether a statute is mandatory or permissive, examine (a) the statutory text (presence of “shall,” “is authorized,” or “to the extent of available appropriations”), (b) the structure of the statutory scheme (does the “shall” qualify an authorization, or is it freestanding?), (c) legislative history sparingly where necessary, and (d) the practical possibility of enforcing the provision (e.g., whether an injunction ordering an agency to spend money is consistent with appropriations law).

## The District Court’s Preliminary Injunctions and Their Scope

After the two executive orders and the State Department’s terminations of cooperative agreements, a group of plaintiffs — refugees already admitted, refugees approved for resettlement but outside the country, and three resettlement organizations — sued in the Western District of Washington. The complaint asserted that EO 14163’s suspension violated the Refugee Act and that the defunding of USRAP violated the APA and other statutory duties. The district court issued preliminary injunctive relief in two stages.

Scope of the injunctions. On February 25, 2025, the district court orally enjoined the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of EO 14163 — i.e., the operative provisions suspending refugee admissions and setting terms for the program. The court supplemented that order in a written February 28 order that explicitly prohibited suspension of USRAP funding. When the State Department terminated cooperative agreements in late February, the district court, on March 24, issued a second preliminary injunction ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling. In short, the district court enjoined (1) the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, (2) the State Department’s suspension of funding for USRAP, and (3) the terminations of cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services.

Legal doctrine. Preliminary injunctions are extraordinary equitable remedies. To obtain preliminary relief, a plaintiff ordinarily must show (1) a likelihood of success on the merits (or at least serious questions going to the merits and a balance of hardships that tips sharply in the plaintiff’s favor), (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief, (3) that the balance of equities favors the plaintiff, and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest. Here the district court concluded that plaintiffs had satisfied those requirements as to the suspensions and terminations.

Two features of this particular set of injunctions are important for doctrine and strategy. First, injunctive relief against executive action that implicates national security or foreign policy normally triggers a heightened concern for judicial restraint; courts will not lightly order executive agencies to conduct particular policy choices in those realms. Second, where the relief ordered requires agencies to spend money — for example, to reinstate cooperative agreements or resume funding for services — courts must take care not to exceed their authority under the Appropriations Clause or to issue orders that effectively compel the Executive to spend money without an appropriation. The district court in Pacito remedied what it viewed as statutory violations affecting refugees already admitted and the organizations that provide reception and placement services; the tension with executive authority and appropriations law is precisely why the case was immediately appealed and moved rapidly through the Ninth Circuit.

Practice tip. When seeking preliminary relief against a federal agency, frame the relief to avoid requiring the court to order spending in the absence of appropriations, or be prepared to justify such relief by reference to statutory mandatory duties (i.e., show a clear statutory command that controls agency discretion).

## The Ninth Circuit’s Stay Analysis under Nken: Carveouts, Vacatur of Prior Stays, and Current Directives

Procedure and the stay question. The government sought a stay of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions pending appeal. The Ninth Circuit applied the Supreme Court’s four-factor test for a stay pending appeal articulated in Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418 (2009): (1) whether the stay applicant is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken directs courts to give particularly close attention to the first two factors.

How the panel applied Nken. The Ninth Circuit, sitting in an expedited motions panel and then en banc-ishly as a merits panel issuing an interim order, concluded that the government was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of EO 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. The court relied on Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and the President’s “finding” that entry would be detrimental is the sole textual prerequisite the statute requires. Because the President had made that finding, the court reasoned, the Executive Order was within the President’s statutory authority, and courts should not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s policy justifications in that context. This finding supported a grant of a stay as to the district court’s injunctions that paused the suspension of admissions.

But the panel did not stop there. It also considered the plaintiffs’ challenges that were not directed at the President’s authority under § 1182(f) but instead argued that other statutory duties — specifically, the reception-and-placement obligations in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 — created enforceable duties that the government had failed to meet when it terminated cooperative agreements and suspended funding. The Ninth Circuit concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of these APA-based challenges. In particular, the court concluded that § 1522 imposes statutory obligations, and that the government’s actions created uncertainty about the availability of reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted. For that reason the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. At the same time, the court stayed the district court’s orders in their entirety except for this narrow directive.

Carveouts, vacatur, and the interim posture. The procedural posture of Pacito is messy but instructive. Earlier motions-panel orders had partially granted stays and partially denied them — the panel had carved out from a stay individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status prior to January 20, 2025. The court subsequently vacated prior administrative stays and other stay orders and issued the present interim stay pending final resolution on appeal, but it carved out the continued enforcement of statutory reception-and-placement duties for refugees already admitted. The practical result: the government could proceed, for the time being, with the suspension of refugee admissions and with some funding suspensions, but the Ninth Circuit required the government to reinstate cooperative agreements and services for persons who were already inside the United States and who depended on those services.

Legal doctrine. Pacito demonstrates how Nken plays out where national security or immigration deference is pitted against statutory duties that implicate non-immigration agencies. The Nken factors can pull in different directions because the courts must weigh (a) deference to executive findings under § 1182(f), which makes “likelihood of success” on a challenge to the suspension of admissions difficult to show, against (b) the likelihood of success on statutory claims under § 1522 that may impose programmatic duties on agencies such as HHS and the State Department. When a court thinks that the government is likely to prevail on the core immigration authority claim but not on a separate statutory duty, the court may issue a limited stay: enjoin enforcement of the district court’s injunction generally but leave intact the district court’s orders that protect a narrow class of persons covered by a non-delegable statutory duty.

Applying the doctrine. When applying Nken to an appeal from a preliminary injunction that targets executive immigration action, separate the challenged action’s sources: is the challenge primarily to the President’s statutory authority under § 1182(f) (where deference is strongest), or is it to the executive branch’s compliance with other statutory obligations that may impose ministerial or mandatory duties? If the former, a stay pending appeal is more likely to be granted; if the latter, plaintiffs may be able to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits and the court may preserve the injunction with limited carveouts.

Practice tip. In seeking a stay, the government should (a) emphasize Supreme Court precedents that constrain judicial review of the President’s finding under § 1182(f) and (b) separate any action that may be challenged as violating other statutory duties. Plaintiffs seeking to resist a stay should frame discrete classes of persons for whom statutory duties create a heightened need for injunctive protection (e.g., refugees already admitted who will suffer immediate and irreparable harm if reception-and-placement services disappear).

## Judge Lee’s Partial Concurrence and Partial Dissent: Authorization versus Mandate under § 1522

Judge Lee’s opinion, concurring in part and dissenting in part, crystallizes the statutory-interpretation question at the heart of Pacito: does § 1522 create mandatory statutory duties that a court may enforce, or is § 1522 a set of authorizations that leaves the funding and programmatic decisions to executive discretion?

Judge Lee’s position. Judge Lee agreed with the majority that the President has broad authority under § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions and therefore would have stayed the district court’s injunctions as to that suspension. He parted company, however, with the majority’s conclusion that § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations requiring the government to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted. His textual argument runs along familiar lines:

- Section 1522’s title is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” The use of the word “authorization” signals permissiveness rather than compulsion.
- Many subsections of § 1522 use the phrase “is authorized” or otherwise grant programmatic authority to the Director or Secretary; such phrasing indicates discretion.
- Where § 1522 uses “shall,” that language is best read as setting conditions “in providing assistance under this section” — that is, mandatory conditions that apply only if the Executive chooses to exercise the discretionary authority and provide such assistance.
- Several provisions that might be read as more commanding (for example, subsection (d)(2)(B) on unaccompanied refugee children and subsection (e)(7)(A) on developing interim projects) are qualified by words such as “if necessary” or “as needed,” further suggesting a discretionary, contingent duty rather than an unconditional statutory command.

From these premises Judge Lee concluded that the district court’s order requiring the Executive to reinstate cooperative agreements is an overreach: courts are not in a position to compel the Executive to operate programs that Congress has only authorized but not mandated or funded.

Doctrinal analysis. Judge Lee’s opinion presents a classic statutory-interpretation contest between a textualist reading that privileges labels and bandwidth words like “authorized,” and a more functional reading that examines the interplay of “shall” and appropriations language together with the statutory purpose. The majority read § 1522 differently: it treated at least some of § 1522’s duties as mandatory or at least judicially enforceable in the face of abrupt executive terminations that left refugees and resettlement organizations in immediate peril. The majority’s approach rests on the practical and textual observation that some parts of § 1522, read together, suggest that when Congress contemplates the temporary care of refugees (including unaccompanied minors and those within their first thirty-six months in the U.S.), the statute places affirmative responsibilities on the relevant agencies that cannot be lightly evaded through an across-the-board funding suspension.

Strengths and weaknesses. Judge Lee’s approach has virtues: it respects the text’s explicit use of “authorization,” hews closely to the ordinary meaning of “is authorized” vs. “shall,” and avoids judicial ordering of inter-branch budgetary choices. But it also has weaknesses. If a statute’s “authorization” is only ornamental and Congress clearly intended to establish a baseline program of reception-and-placement services essential to the functioning of the refugee resettlement framework, then a court may find that certain statutory duties are intended to be substantive and not easily suspended. The majority was concerned with precisely that problem: the governmental terminations left refugees already inside the United States unable to access statutorily contemplated supports, and the Ninth Circuit concluded that the district court’s narrow remedy — reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for admitted refugees — was a proportionate response.

Applying the doctrine. Students should see Judge Lee’s opinion as a case study in statutory interpretation methods. When deciding whether a statutory provision is mandatory, ask:

- What does the title and structure of the statute suggest (authorization vs. requirement)?
- Are there clear “shall” commands, and if so, do they apply unconditionally or only “in providing assistance”?
- Does the statutory scheme presuppose appropriations, and if so, does Congress elsewhere provide funding or require reporting and oversight that implies an expectation of program continuity?
- Are there practical consequences if the provision is read as permissive rather than mandatory (for example, immediate harms to vulnerable populations)?

Practice tip. If you represent plaintiffs seeking to enforce a statutory program against the Executive, emphasize textual elements that indicate mandatory treatment (unqualified “shall,” ministerial duties, reporting requirements) and practical consequences of non-enforcement. If you represent the government, emphasize the appropriations context and the permissive language (e.g., “is authorized,” “to the extent of available appropriations”), and argue that courts should avoid intruding into budgeting and programmatic discretion absent a clear congressional directive.

## Synthesis: How Pacito Teaches to Divide Issues and Predict Outcomes

Pacito is useful for learning how to break complex litigation down into analytically separable claims that attract different standards and degrees of deference. The case shows that:

- Claims that target the President’s statutory authority to exclude aliens under § 1182(f) face a steep hill: courts applying Trump v. Hawaii will usually treat the President’s finding as satisfying § 1182(f)’s textual prerequisite and will not subject the decision to searching substantive review.
- Claims that do not directly challenge the President’s authority to suspend admissions but instead assert that other statutes impose ministerial or mandatory duties on agencies are more likely to survive under normal APA analysis, because deference to presidential immigration authority does not automatically displace other statutes’ programmatic commands.
- In the stay context (Nken), these separate tracks can produce mixed results: a court may stay a district court injunction as to the President’s suspension of admissions while refusing to stay relief that protects persons who are within the U.S. and covered by separate statutory obligations.

For law students, Pacito provides a roadmap for litigating complex cross-cutting disputes: identify each statutory or constitutional source of authority or duty, determine the standard of review applicable to each (e.g., de novo statutory interpretation, arbitrary-and-capricious review under the APA, or heightened deference for national-security/immigration decisions), and then map how each claim should fare under the Nken factors where a stay is requested.

Concluding practice pointers

- When drafting complaints or motions in this area, organize claims so that challenges to presidential authority under § 1182(f) are separated from claims that seek enforcement of statutory obligations on other agencies. That separation maximizes the chance that at least some relief will survive if the court is inclined to defer to the President on admissions.
- When opposing a stay, focus on irreparable harms to individuals within the United States and on statutory language that creates immediate responsibilities (e.g., care of unaccompanied minors, time-limited cash and medical supports). Emphasize the practical immediacy of harm to tip the Nken balance.
- On appeal, remember that the first Nken factor — likelihood of success on the merits — is a compound issue. A single appeal may present multiple merits questions governed by different legal rules and standards; show how each one should be evaluated on the stay record.

Pacito v. Trump therefore stands as an instructive example of how federal courts handle high-stakes, multi-dimensional challenges to executive immigration policy: strong deference to presidential authority on exclusions, concurrent enforceability concerns under separate statutory programs, and the use of the Nken stay framework to craft provisional remedies that reflect the court’s best assessment of competing statutory and equitable claims.
