Chapter 3: # Judicial Review and Remedies: APA, Deference, and Emergency Relief

This chapter examines how courts review executive immigration actions and agency funding decisions, the current posture of judicial deference in statutory interpretation (post–Loper Bright), and the standards for obtaining emergency relief: preliminary injunctions and stays pending appeal. The Pacito v. Trump opinion (Ninth Circuit, Sept. 12, 2025) offers a compact vehicle for illustrating how these doctrines intersect in practice. Pacito presents three interlaced issues: (1) the President’s use of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions; (2) challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) to the termination of cooperative agreements and defunding of resettlement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522; and (3) interlocutory relief — district-court preliminary injunctions and an appellate stay pending appeal. The court’s treatment of these issues, and Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent, illuminate the limits of deference, the boundary between nonreviewable foreign-affairs power and reviewable statutory duties, and the practical contours of equitable relief.

## APA reviewability: § 701(a)(2), final agency action, and the “law to apply” inquiry

A litigant’s starting point is whether the APA permits judicial review at all. Two doctrinal thresholds control: (1) the APA’s exclusion of review for agency action “committed to agency discretion by law” (5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)), and (2) whether the challenged conduct constitutes final agency action subject to review (5 U.S.C. § 704). Pacito is useful because the court treated these thresholds differently for two separate problems.

Section 701(a)(2) and “law to apply.” Section 701(a)(2) denies review when “agency action is committed to agency discretion by law,” a phrase courts have interpreted to mean there is no judicially manageable standard — no “law to apply” — that would allow meaningful review. The Supreme Court’s immigration precedents (most notably Trump v. Hawaii) show how § 701(a)(2) can operate in the foreign-affairs and entry context: when Congress confers extraordinarily broad discretion or when the statutory command is framed as a single, nonjusticiable determination (for example, “if the President finds…entry would be detrimental”), courts have often treated the matter as committed to the political branches and thus effectively insulated from searching judicial review.

In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit relied on Trump v. Hawaii to explain why the President’s exercise of § 1182(f) receives exceptional deference. The court observed that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” because its “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that the entry of certain aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” When a statute conditions executive action on a named official’s explicit “finding,” courts often conclude there is little room for judicial second-guessing — the statutory text leaves no manageable standard beyond the executive’s own determination. That is the classic § 701(a)(2) profile: a politically assigned determination with no judicially administrable standard.

But § 701(a)(2) is not a blanket exclusion. Courts must ask whether the statute and governing law furnish a standard by which to judge the agency’s action. If there is “law to apply” — i.e., statutory or constitutional guideposts that would permit meaningful judicial assessment — then the action is reviewable. Pacito illustrates this principle by bifurcating the litigation: the court concluded the President was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions under § 1182(f), because that statutory provision presents the kind of discretionary presidential determination courts historically treat as nonjusticiable or at least deserving of extreme deference. At the same time, the court concluded that plaintiffs were likely to succeed on their APA claim regarding the defunding and termination of cooperative agreements under § 1522. Why? Because § 1522 itself contains statutory duties and reporting requirements (and a web of implementing regulations) that supply law to apply and thus permit judicial review of whether the government is meeting those statutory obligations.

Final agency action. Even when § 701(a)(2) does not bar review, the APA’s review provisions require that the challenged action be “final.” Bennett v. Spear provides the modern test for finality: (1) the action must mark the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process (it cannot be tentative or interlocutory), and (2) the action must have direct and appreciable legal consequences — it must alter the party’s legal position or rights. In Pacito, the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and the Department of State/HHS’s suspension of funding were concrete, consummated decisions producing immediate legal and practical effects on resettlement providers and refugees — the sort of action that passes Bennett’s finality test. The plaintiffs’ challenge thus presented a paradigmatic case of final agency action susceptible to APA review.

Applying these tests in practice: When confronting an executive immigration or funding decision, a lawyer should first map the statutory text that purportedly authorizes the action. If the statute (like § 1182(f)) centers the determination on an executive “finding,” courts may treat it as committed to executive discretion; if the statute (like § 1522) sets forth specific duties, conditions, or reporting requirements, it is more likely to furnish law to apply and to permit review. Next, ask whether the challenged action is a completed decision with tangible legal consequences — an agency’s policy statement may be too tentative, but termination of a funding agreement, the revocation of an application approval, or the denial of benefits that alters legal relations will likely be final.

## Standards of review: arbitrary and capricious, and statutory interpretation post–Loper Bright

Once reviewability and finality are established, the court determines the appropriate standard of review. Two distinct axes exist: (1) the standard for reviewing an agency’s factual or policy-based decisions — typically the “arbitrary and capricious” standard of 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A); and (2) the standard for reviewing agency statutory interpretation.

Arbitrary and capricious review. The APA directs courts to set aside agency actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” Under the Supreme Court’s precedents, arbitrary-and-capricious review requires that an agency have examined relevant data, articulated a satisfactory explanation for its action (including a rational connection between facts and choice), considered important aspects of the problem, and responded to significant countervailing evidence or arguments. Typical defects include failure to consider relevant factors, reliance on plainly wrong facts, unexplained departures from prior policy, and failure to assess alternatives or to justify reliance on new evidence.

But the intensity of review varies with context. Courts give more searching review when the agency failed to address important reliance interests, reversed course without explanation, or acted on a record that suggests pretext. Conversely, in domains touching core executive responsibilities — foreign affairs, national security, and matters requiring confidentiality or expertise — courts ordinarily exercise a more deferential form of arbitrary-and-capricious review. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito exemplified this modulation: it declined to engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications” when addressing § 1182(f), invoking prior hands-off treatment of national-security and entry decisions, but it scrutinized the government’s termination of resettlement funding more carefully because § 1522 created statutory responsibilities that the agencies had to satisfy.

Statutory interpretation post–Loper Bright: Chevron’s decline and Skidmore persuasion. The second axis — reviewing agency statutory interpretation — has undergone a fundamental shift in recent years. Historically, Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council (1984) instructed courts to apply a two-step inquiry: if Congress’s intent is unambiguous, follow the statute; if ambiguous, defer to an agency’s reasonable interpretation. Pacito leans on deference doctrines in the national-security/immigration context through Trump v. Hawaii, but statutory interpretation in many other contexts is now governed by the post–Loper Bright regime.

Following Loper Bright, courts across the federal judiciary have treated Chevron deference as no longer the controlling approach; agencies’ statutory interpretations are to be evaluated under a framework resembling Skidmore v. Swift. Under Skidmore persuasion, the weight accorded an agency’s interpretation depends on its persuasiveness — the thoroughness of its reasoning, consistency with earlier positions, expertise, and the overall fit with statutory text and structure. That means courts undertake independent statutory interpretation, but they may give the agency’s view considerable persuasive weight when it is well-reasoned and grounded in expertise.

Practical consequences. Under Skidmore-style review, litigants cannot rely on automatic, near-conclusive deference to an agency reading. Courts will closely analyze statutory text, structure, and the presence or absence of clear Congressional direction. In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit did not apply any species of Chevron deference to § 1522; rather, it examined the statutory language setting out specific duties and funding mechanisms and found there was “law to apply” that permitted judicial enforcement. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent further illustrates statutory interpretive techniques: she emphasized textual signals — titles such as “Authorization,” the repeated use of “is authorized,” and the qualifying phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” — to conclude § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory. That contest between the panel’s majority and the concurrence demonstrates how post–Loper Bright review requires courts to parse statutory grammar and structure, apply canons of construction, and weigh the persuasiveness of agency interpretations rather than simply accepting them under a deferential Chevron step-two.

## Foreign affairs and national security considerations in APA cases: scope but not elimination of review

Foreign-affairs and national-security contexts complicate administrative review but do not make it impossible. Courts recognize constitutional and prudential reasons for judicial caution when reviewing executive actions implicating national security, diplomatic relations, or immigration. The executive’s institutional competence and constitutional prerogatives support substantial deference. But deference is not a moratorium on review: where statutory obligations exist, or where executive action effects private rights in ways that are judicially manageable, courts retain authority to examine and, when appropriate, to enjoin agency conduct.

Pacito’s dual result vividly demonstrates the balance. The court emphasized that § 1182(f) confers broad presidential authority to suspend entry, and that prior precedent counsels reluctance to probe the persuasiveness of the President’s stated national-security justifications. Accordingly, the panel found the government likely to succeed on the merits of the challenge to the moratorium on admissions — a conclusion rooted in statutory text and national-security deference. Yet the panel also concluded that national-security power did not extinguish statutory obligations the executive owes to refugees who have already been admitted. The statutory duties in § 1522 — which address reception and placement services, accountability, and reporting — are not swallowed by the President’s § 1182(f) power. The court therefore concluded that the APA challenge to defunding and contract termination could proceed and that, at least preliminarily, judicial relief ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees was appropriate.

Key takeaways. When national-security or foreign-affairs considerations are asserted to shield agency action from review, courts ask (1) whether Congress has assigned the particular determination to the political branches in a way that leaves no judicially manageable standard and (2) whether the case involves legal interests (statutory or constitutional rights) that courts are equipped to protect. If a statute creates specific duties (for example, funding and programmatic duties for resettlement services), courts may review whether those duties are being satisfied even if the government’s broader policy decisions (for example, suspending admissions) remain highly protected.

## Winter v. NRDC and Nken v. Holder: preliminary injunctions and stays pending appeal

Emergency equitable relief — preliminary injunctions and stays pending appeal — is governed by well-established factors, and courts tailor their analysis to the interplay between likelihood-of-success questions and the balance of harms.

Preliminary injunctions: Winter’s framework. The Supreme Court in Winter v. NRDC clarified the preliminary-injunction standard. To obtain a preliminary injunction, the movant must show: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the movant is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief; (3) that the balance of equities tips in the movant’s favor; and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest. Winter rejected a formulation that permitted courts to grant injunctions where there were “serious questions” going to the merits as long as the balance of harms tipped sharply in the movant’s favor; while some circuits still use a sliding-scale variant, Winter requires courts to consider likelihood of success as a critical threshold. The irreparable-harm requirement is prominent: monetary losses are generally not enough; the harm must be non-compensable and imminent.

Stays pending appeal: Nken’s four-factor test. When a party seeks a stay pending appeal, the Supreme Court’s Nken v. Holder decision supplies parallel considerations, often stated as four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken clarifies that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are the most critical, but that the balance-of-interests and public-interest factors also play an important role.

Pacito as illustration. The Ninth Circuit’s stay pending appeal in Pacito applied the Nken factors. The panel concluded the government made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), citing both the statute’s broad presidential discretion and Supreme Court precedent that counsels deference in entry-suspension contexts. The panel also concluded that the remaining Nken factors—irreparable injury to the government (interpreted to include foreign and domestic policy implications), injury to third parties, and public interest—tilted in favor of a stay. At the same time, the panel held that the government was not likely to prevail on the APA challenge to termination of cooperative agreements under § 1522, and therefore the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees already admitted — a narrow remedial carveout. This outcome shows how courts can grant broad deference in one dimension (admission suspensions) while preserving relief where concrete statutory duties and imminent harms are at stake.

Practical advice for litigators. When seeking a preliminary injunction or opposing a stay, counsel should calibrate argument to the four-factor framework. Emphasize the strength of the merits, but do not neglect irreparable harm: produce concrete evidence (testimony, affidavits, operational details) showing imminent, non-compensable injury. Identify the balance of equities, including harms to third parties, and make persuasive public-interest arguments. In cases implicating national security or foreign affairs, anticipate the court’s sensitivity to diplomatic and safety considerations and tailor evidentiary submissions accordingly.

## Consular nonreviewability and its limits; review of generally applicable policies vs. individual visa denials

The consular nonreviewability doctrine bars courts from second-guessing a consular officer’s decision to grant or deny visas to particular applicants. Kleindienst v. Mandel established the core rule: when the Executive head of the political department (usually the Secretary of State) provides a “facially legitimate and bona fide” reason for denying a visa, courts will not probe the merits. Subsequent cases (for example, Kerry v. Din, Trump v. Hawaii) have fleshed out and complicated this doctrine, but its central thrust is that individual visa denials and certain admission decisions are not amenable to searching judicial review because they implicate the political branches’ authority over entry and foreign relations.

Nonetheless, consular nonreviewability is not absolute. Several important limits should be noted and were implicitly engaged by the issues in Pacito:

- Individual vs. programmatic or generally applicable policies. Courts are more likely to abstain from reviewing specific denial determinations than to decline review of generally applicable policies or statutory implementation. A facial challenge to an agency policy that applies broadly and affects statutory rights can be reviewable even if individual visa decisions are not. Pacito illustrates this difference: the court was unwilling to entertain deep scrutiny of the President’s statutory power to suspend entry under § 1182(f), but it did entertain APA challenges to the executive’s funding decisions that affected refugees already admitted — a programmatic, statutory-duty context rather than a classic consular visa denial.

- Constitutional claims and clear statutory entitlements. Consular nonreviewability does not bar all judicial remedies for constitutional violations, nor does it extinguish review where Congress has crafted a statutory scheme that creates enforceable rights and duties. For example, if an individual’s constitutional rights are implicated by an exclusion, or if statute creates particular entitlements to a benefit or procedure, courts retain the ability to review. The doctrine also does not immunize arbitrary administration of programs once aliens are within the United States or where domestic statutory protections apply.

- The “facially legitimate and bona fide” test has limits. Where the Executive's stated rationale for denial is pretextual, contradicted by the record, or not bona fide, courts have been willing to probe further. This is rare and entails high evidentiary thresholds, but it signals that the political-branch insulation is not absolute.

In short, consular nonreviewability gives the Executive special protection in individual entry and visa determinations, but programmatic policies that implicate statutory duties or that affect aliens already admitted fall outside the doctrine’s protective shield.

## Applying the doctrines in Pacito: the remedy the court ordered and the split over statutory interpretation

Pacito’s outcome was a nuanced application of these principles. The Ninth Circuit stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions against the suspension of refugee admissions pending appeal — concluding substantial deference to the President under § 1182(f) and relying on Trump v. Hawaii. But the panel also held that the plaintiff refugees and resettlement organizations were likely to succeed on an APA claim because the government may have violated statutory duties set out in § 1522 to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The remedy was correspondingly narrow and pragmatic: the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees who had been admitted to the United States, and to provide “the usual and customary services” afforded under the prior agreements.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent exposes the statutory-interpretation crux that many cases will present post–Loper Bright. She argued that § 1522 is better read as permissive, not mandatory, because its heading uses the word “Authorization,” several specific program provisions “authorize” the Director to provide services, and the text repeatedly qualifies duties by the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” From her perspective, reading § 1522 as mandating the provision of services would disregard textual signals and improperly constrain executive discretion — a point that underscores how post–Loper Bright review requires courts to parse statutory text and structure and to evaluate the persuasiveness of competing interpretations rather than simply defer to agency characterizations.

For practitioners, Pacito supplies a model for crafting remedies in cases that interlock foreign-affairs discretion and statutory protections. When a court finds limited review is appropriate, it will tailor relief narrowly: it may preserve executive latitude in the nationally sensitive domain (suspending admissions) while enforcing statutory duties that protect concrete domestic interests (resettlement services for already admitted refugees). Remedial orders are likely to be field-specific and limited to what the statute requires and the evidence shows.

## Practical roadmap for litigating and deciding cases like Pacito

When you confront a challenge to an immigration or agency funding decision, proceed through the following steps (each of which Pacito exemplifies):

1. Identify the challenged action and the actor. Is the action presidential, agency (regulation, policy), or an operational decision (termination of cooperative agreements, funding rescission, consular denial)? Is the actor the President, a cabinet secretary, or a subordinate agency official?

2. Determine reviewability under the APA.
   - Ask whether § 701(a)(2) applies: does the statute commit the particular decision to agency or executive discretion with no judicially manageable standard? If the statutory command centers on an official’s unreviewable “finding,” courts may treat it as not subject to meaningful review.
   - If § 701(a)(2) does not bar review, determine whether the challenged action is final under Bennett.

3. Define the “law to apply.” If statute, regulation, or constitutional text provides guideposts (duty, procedure, reporting requirement), then there is law to apply and courts can review the agency’s compliance. If not, reviewability is jeopardized.

4. Choose the standard of review.
   - For factual and policy choices, apply arbitrary-and-capricious review; examine whether the agency considered relevant factors, explained itself, and did not act arbitrarily or inconsistently.
   - For statutory questions, apply post–Loper Bright methodology: independently interpret the statute while assessing the persuasiveness of the agency’s interpretation (Skidmore-type weight), giving more deference to well-reasoned, expertise-grounded agency interpretations but not automatic Chevron step-two deference.

5. Account for foreign-affairs and national-security contexts. Recognize that courts will be hesitant to engage in searching review of presidential or high-level foreign-affairs determinations, but that statutory mandates and domestic interests can still be enforceable.

6. If seeking emergency relief, frame your arguments around Winter (preliminary injunction) or Nken (stay pending appeal) factors. Emphasize evidence of irreparable harm and the practical harms to third parties and the public interest. Expect courts to balance national-security or diplomatic concerns against statutory protections and imminent harms.

7. Craft remedies carefully. Courts will construe injunctions to avoid trenching on core political-branch authority while protecting statutory rights and preventing irreparable injury. Narrow, tailored relief that preserves executive discretion in sensitive domains while enforcing concrete statutory obligations stands a greater chance of surviving appeal.

## Conclusion

Pacito provides a concise case study in modern administrative and immigration law. It shows that courts will often apply a context-sensitive approach: deferring to the political branches in matters of entry and national security while retaining the capacity to enforce statutory duties and protect concrete rights, especially once aliens are lawfully within the United States. Post–Loper Bright, courts will not routinely accord Chevron-style deference to agencies’ statutory interpretations; instead, agencies’ interpretations must persuade. Finally, the equitable standards of Winter and Nken require litigants to develop solid evidence of likelihood of success and irreparable harm, and courts will tailor relief to respect both national-security prerogatives and enforceable statutory obligations.
