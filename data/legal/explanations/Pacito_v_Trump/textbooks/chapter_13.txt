Chapter 13: # Statutory Interpretation Toolkit for Refugee Services Cases

This chapter equips you with the interpretive tools lawyers and judges used (and disputed) in Pacito v. Trump to resolve whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes mandatory duties on the federal government to provide reception and placement services to refugees, or instead simply authorizes such programs. Pacito is a convenient, contemporary lens because the panel split mirrors common interpretive fault lines: textualism versus purposivism, granular grammatical parsing versus structural/functional reading, and skepticism toward agency post hoc justifications following changes in administrative practice. Your task as a student is to master the doctrinal vocabulary and tests that appear in these arguments so you can apply them to new statutory provisions and fact patterns.

We proceed through five interpretive topics that are especially important in refugee-services litigation and agency counseling: (1) modal verbs and force of command, (2) the interpretive value of headings and statutory structure, (3) the meaning of common qualifiers that temper statutory language, (4) the role of legislative history and statutory purpose in refugee law, and (5) how courts evaluate agency interpretations in the post–Loper Bright environment (Skidmore-like “practical persuasion” analysis). At the end of the chapter is a short practical checklist for drafting litigation and administrative arguments.

## Parsing modal verbs: ‘shall’ vs. ‘may’ vs. ‘is authorized to’

Modal verbs and formulaic phrases are the most immediate signals the parties and courts rely on to decide whether Congress imposed a binding duty or granted permissive discretion. Pacito turns on these words.

- The basic starting rule. When Congress uses “shall” in a statutory provision, courts often presume a mandatory command; when Congress uses “may,” the presumption is permissive. But the presumption is rebuttable. Context, structure, and the surrounding text can transform or qualify a facially mandatory “shall” into a contingent or hortatory provision.

- How Pacito applies the rule. The Ninth Circuit majority emphasized that § 1522(a) uses “shall” to describe duties the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must undertake: “The Director shall…make available sufficient resources…” and “shall…insure that cash assistance is made available….” The majority treated those “shall” provisions as expressing congressional obligation—supporting an injunction directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to furnish those services.

  Judge Lee’s partial dissent, by contrast, placed emphasis on the statutory pattern in the rest of § 1522. Many substantive subsections say the Director “is authorized to” provide certain programs or the Director “may” enter into grants, contracts, or cooperative agreements. From that pattern, Lee argued that Congress intended § 1522 to be a framework authorizing discretionary programs: the “shall” in § 1522(a) sets conditions or aims that should inform program design only “if” the government chooses to fund a program.

- How to analyze modal verbs in practice. When you read “shall,” ask these layered questions:
  1. What is the grammatical subject of “shall,” and is it directing an executive actor?
  2. Are there express qualifications in the same sentence—e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” “as needed”—that plausibly limit compulsion?
  3. Does the “shall” appear in a section labeled with an “authorization” caption or surrounded by other permissive language? If so, is the “shall” best read as a condition upon discretionary action rather than a freestanding duty?
  4. Is there a clear statutory mechanism for enforcement (criminal penalties, private right of action, mandatory reports to Congress), suggesting a mandatory scheme?

  These questions track the Ninth Circuit disagreement: the majority stresses unqualified “shall” language directing programmatic outcomes; the dissent stresses the frequency of express authorizations and the header “Authorization for programs,” which suggest permissive intent.

- Canon of construction and practical tip. When modal verbs conflict within a statutory scheme, apply the whole-text canon—look at how the statute as a whole treats the actor and program. If Congress consistently uses “may” or “is authorized” elsewhere but slips a “shall” into a provision that begins, “In providing assistance under this section…,” the safer reading may be that the “shall” identifies conditions for discretionary funding rather than imposing a free-standing funding duty. But that inference is contextual, not mechanical.

## Effect of headings, structure, and cross-references on operative text

Headings, placement, and the statutory architecture are powerful interpretive tools. They can corroborate what the grammar suggests or pull the meaning in another direction.

- The role of section headings and titles. Section headings themselves are not law, but they are a permissible aid to interpretation. A caption such as “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” (the title of § 1522) signals congressional intent to authorize programs. Courts treat such headings as evidence of context—useful but not dispositive. In Pacito, Judge Lee relied heavily on the title to argue that § 1522 authorizes rather than mandates programs. The majority, however, read the title as a backdrop but gave determinative weight to the operative “shall” verbs and to reporting and programmatic detail that point toward mandatory duties when funding exists.

- Structural reading: preambles, “conditions and considerations,” and operative subsections. Many statutes begin with a “conditions and considerations” clause that frames how discretionary powers shall be exercised “in providing assistance under this section.” Focus on whether such preambles are introductory—setting parameters for discretionary action—or whether they are themselves operative commands. In § 1522, the “conditions and consideration” heading is linguistically connected to the rest of the section: it can be read as “if you provide assistance under this section, you shall…,” which supports the dissent’s argument; but the majority reads some of those conditions as mandatory operational duties because they are phrased without express contingency and because they have downstream programmatic counterparts (e.g., reporting and program requirements).

- Cross-references and related provisions. Cross-references can settle ambiguity. If another statute conditions the exercise of the power on appropriations or creates enforcement mechanisms, that can indicate Congress’s design. In refugee law disputes, look to related sections for:
  • appropriations language (do other sections require Congress to appropriate funds?),
  • reporting or oversight requirements (detailed reporting requirements often suggest a mandatory statutory program), and
  • enforcement provisions (if Congress provides remedies or mandates, courts are more apt to treat duties as mandatory).

  In Pacito, the presence of § 1523’s detailed reporting requirement—an annual report to congressional committees—was one of the factors the majority used to support the conclusion that Congress expected the statute to have substantive, ongoing operation rather than mere authorization on paper.

- In practice. Map the statute. Create a table or flowchart: titles → introductory clauses → operative verbs by subsection → cross-referenced provisions (appropriations, reporting, enforcement). That map will show whether the textual ecosystem supports a mandatory or permissive reading.

## Reading qualifiers like ‘as needed’ and ‘to the extent of available appropriations’

Qualifiers are common devices that limit obligations. They are often decisive in disputes over mandatory funding. Three qualifiers appear in § 1522 and in many statutes: “to the extent of available appropriations,” “as needed,” and “if necessary.”

- “To the extent of available appropriations.” This is the archetypal appropriations qualifier. It can mean several things:
  1. Congress is specifying that a substantive duty is conditioned on the availability of appropriated funds—the duty is mandatory but only when funds are actually available.
  2. Congress is signaling that the statute is merely an authorization of programs—a permissive grant of spending authority contingent upon appropriations.
  3. Congress expects the executive to spend appropriated funds consistent with the statute—that is, once Congress actually appropriates funds for the statutory program, the executive must implement the statutory program consistent with the statutory directives.

  Courts parse these possibilities by looking at (a) whether Congress has provided a separate appropriation that unambiguously funds the statutory program and (b) whether the statute manifests an expectation of programmatic operation (detailed program structure, reporting, accountability). In Pacito, the majority noted that Congress had appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (albeit subject to subsequent rescission of a portion), and concluded that an appropriations condition did not render the relevant “shall” entirely toothless.

  Practically, a litigant should (i) identify the specific appropriation(s) in the annual appropriations acts that can be traced to the program, (ii) demonstrate whether funds are actually available or have been rescinded, and (iii) show how much discretion the executive has in allocating available funds among competing programs.

- “As needed” and “if necessary.” These phrases often limit a mandatory verb to circumstances in which the action is necessary. Courts interpret them by asking whether Congress intended a standard of judgment for the agency or left the existence of necessity to judicial review. If the phrase is tied to emergency or interim duties (for example, “shall assume legal responsibility…if necessary”), an agency will have some room to determine necessity, but the adequacy of that determination can be reviewed for reasonableness under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). In Pacito, subsection (d)(2)(B)’s “if necessary” language was central to Judge Lee’s point that the Director’s duties are contingent. The majority read the “if necessary” language as framed narrowly—applying only to certain temporary duties concerning unaccompanied minors—thus not abrogating the broader “shall” obligations elsewhere.

- Practical application. When the text uses qualifiers:
  • Quantify appropriations. Show whether funds were appropriated and whether they were directed to the program at issue.
  • Define “necessity.” In litigation, press the agency to explain the factual basis for claiming that action is not “necessary” (or is necessary).
  • Beware of agency-friendly glosses. An agency’s assertion that funds are unavailable or actions unnecessary should be tested against contemporaneous internal documents and budgetary allocations; inconsistent post hoc explanations are less persuasive.

## Use of legislative history and purpose in refugee legislation

Refugee legislation has both technical elements and a clear humane policy purpose; courts sometimes read the Refugee Act of 1980 and related statutes through that purpose. Legislative history can be a tie-breaker but not a first resort.

- When legislative history helps. If textual indicators are ambiguous or conflicting, legislative history—committee reports, floor debates, contemporaneous executive branch statements—can illuminate congressional intent. In the refugee context, helpful sources include: House and Senate Judiciary Committee reports, the legislative history of the Refugee Act of 1980, and contemporaneous executive communications about how the statute should operate in practice. Courts will look for specific legislative evidence that Congress intended mandatory duties or, conversely, that Congress intended a permissive framework subject to appropriations.

- Purpose and canons. Refugee law is often read against the backdrop of statutory purpose: providing humane, orderly resettlement and preventing statelessness or harm to vulnerable persons. The Supreme Court in Negusie recognized the Refugee Act as a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees.” Purpose can cut both ways:
  • Purpose can support a mandatory interpretation if Congress’s humanitarian aim would be frustrated by a reading that allows the executive to dismantle resettlement supports without congressional approval.
  • Purpose can support a permissive interpretation if Congress intended to create a flexible program the executive could tailor to foreign policy or resource constraints.

  In Pacito, plaintiffs emphasized the Refugee Act’s remedial purpose and the historical practice of cooperative agreements to show that Congress expected an operational resettlement program. The government pointed to the statute’s appropriations language and the President’s national-security prerogatives to argue for flexibility.

- Limits of legislative history. Be cautious: courts differ in their willingness to rely on legislative history. Some judges treat it as useful but cannot overcome clear statutory language; others give it considerable weight. Use history to fill textual gaps or show contemporaneous understanding by both Congress and the executive.

## Agency interpretations post–Loper Bright: Skidmore weight and practical persuasion

How much deference does an agency’s interpretation deserve? Pacito illustrates how courts evaluate agency explanations—both prior consistent practice and sudden reversals—when deciding whether an agency has permissibly construed a statute or whether the court should enforce statutory duties.

- The deference landscape. Over recent years the Supreme Court has recalibrated judicial deference to agency interpretations. In the post–Loper Bright environment courts have increasingly scrutinized agency positions and frequently treat agency interpretations as deserving persuasive weight rather than automatic controlling deference. Whether on construction of a statute or interpretation of an agency’s own regulation, the guiding inquiry turns on the agency’s expertise, the consistency and contemporaneity of its interpretation, the thoroughness of its reasoning, and the practical consequences of deferring.

- Skidmore factors (practical persuasion). Even where Chevron-style deference is reduced or confined, courts often apply Skidmore v. Swift: an agency’s interpretation may be persuasive depending on its:
  1. Thoroughness of explanation,
  2. Consistency over time,
  3. Formulation process (formal adjudication and notice-and-comment rulemaking weigh more heavily),
  4. Expertise of the agency, and
  5. Fit with statutory design and practical consequences.

  In Pacito the government had a recent, abrupt policy and practice change: termination of longstanding cooperative agreements and suspension of funding. At argument the government could not point to a developed plan for replacing those arrangements. These facts weakened the persuasive force of the government’s present-day assertions that § 1522 is merely permissive: the agency had not furnished a reasoned, contemporaneous explanation tying its actions to statutory aims or budgetary constraints. The majority relied on that absence to conclude the agency had not demonstrated the statutory discretion it claimed; it therefore reinstated agreements to effectuate statutory obligations. Judge Lee, by contrast, found the statutory text and headings sufficient to justify a permissive reading and thought courts should not police policy choices of the executive in a field where the President enjoys constitutional authority.

- Contemporaneity and reversals. A sudden reversal in agency practice without reasoned explanation diminishes the persuasive weight of the new interpretation. Conversely, a long-standing, consistent agency practice that aligns with a permissive reading of the statute can be highly persuasive, especially where the practice was known to and relied upon by Congress.

- Practical litigation posture. When representing plaintiffs challenging an agency roll-back:
  • Emphasize the lack of reasoned decisionmaking and contemporaneous record support for the agency’s position.
  • Present historical practice and program structure to show the agency previously treated the statute as mandatory in effect.
  • Use Skidmore factors to argue that the agency’s new interpretation is unpersuasive.

  When representing an agency defending change:
  • Produce contemporaneous evidence explaining the change (budget analyses, safety assessments, foreign policy memoranda).
  • Tie the reinterpretation to statutory purposes and appropriations constraints; explain why a permissive reading better fits the statute as a whole.
  • Show that practical necessities (national security, diplomacy, emergency funding changes) compelled the action.

## Applying the tools to § 1522 (how the arguments line up)

It is instructive to set out the competing arguments you would make on behalf of each side in Pacito, applying the interpretive tools above.

- Plaintiffs’ textualist (majority) approach. Start with the “shall” verbs in § 1522(a), read without an across-the-board contingency: “The Director shall make available sufficient resources…shall provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire…shall insure that cash assistance is made available…shall insure that women have the same opportunities as men….” Those are programmatic commands. Cross-references buttress the conclusion: § 1522(b) authorizes funding instruments but does not negate obligations; § 1523 requires detailed reporting to Congress on activities under the subchapter—Congress would not impose such accountability if the programs were purely optional. Congress appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, so the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier does not render the duties hollow. Historical practice—the long-standing cooperative agreements—shows that the statute was implemented as an ongoing program. The government’s abrupt termination, lacking a contemporaneous plan, fails Skidmore factors: the agency’s new interpretation is recent, unexplained, and inconsistent with prior practice.

- Government’s permissive (dissent) approach. Emphasize the statutory title (“Authorization for programs”) and repeated use elsewhere of “is authorized to” and “may” language. Read § 1522(a) as an expression of “conditions and consideration” that apply “in providing assistance under this section”: these are normative constraints for program design, not unconditional mandates. The “to the extent of available appropriations” phrase makes clear that Congress exercised budget control and intended the executive to act when funds are appropriated, not to be forced to create programs. Subsections with “as needed” and “if necessary” language confirm that Congress expected the executive to make discretionary judgments about the scope of assistance. Finally, where a statute implicates the Executive’s foreign policy and border-control prerogatives, courts should refrain from second-guessing policy choices.

- How a court resolves the dispute. The court must weigh text, structure, appropriations history, programmatic features (like reporting), and agency practice and explanations. Given ambiguity, a court will give weight to practical consequences—can the agency accomplish the statutory purposes while suspending the contracts? Can Congress be seen as having intended the Executive to have such unilateral power? The outcome often depends on how persuasive the evidence of congressional intent and contemporaneous agency practice is.

## Practical checklist for litigators and counselors

When you confront a dispute like Pacito, use a disciplined checklist:

1. Textual inventory
   - Identify every instance of “shall,” “may,” “is authorized,” “as needed,” “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary.”
   - Parse who the grammatical subject is and whether the verb is hortatory, conditional, or operative.

2. Structural mapping
   - Note the section title and the header language (e.g., “Conditions and consideration”).
   - Cross-reference related provisions (reporting, appropriations, enforcement).
   - Map programmatic architecture: funding instruments, grant/contract authority, and oversight.

3. Appropriations audit
   - Identify specific appropriations bills and text; determine whether funds were appropriated and if rescissions or reprogramming applied.
   - Prepare a budget timeline to show whether the program had funds when the agency acted.

4. Historical practice and contemporaneous evidence
   - Compile the agency’s historical practice and the existence of longstanding cooperative agreements.
   - Collect contemporaneous internal memos explaining the policy change; absence of such documents is strategically important for plaintiffs.

5. Legislative history and purpose
   - Pull committee reports, floor statements, and floor amendments from the Refugee Act and subsequent amendments.
   - Frame the statutory purpose both as a humanitarian aim and as a policy balancing exercise with foreign policy/defense concerns.

6. Agency interpretive posture
   - If defending the agency, present a well-reasoned explanation—use formal rulemaking or adjudication if possible; otherwise, produce detailed contemporaneous justification.
   - If attacking the agency, highlight inconsistency, lack of reasoned explanation, lack of planning, and reliance on late-breaking policy rationales.

7. Remedies strategy
   - For plaintiffs: build record evidence of irreparable harms to refugees and resettlement organizations; connect textual obligations to specific, remediable programmatic failures; request narrow, tailored injunctive relief.
   - For defendants: show public-interest harms of judicial micromanagement in national-security-sensitive fields and present feasible alternatives to specific injunctive relief.

Conclusion

Pacito is a modern exercise in statutory interpretation where modal verbs, headings, qualifiers, legislative purpose, and the agency’s interpretive posture intersect. There is no single canonical algorithm; instead, careful statutory mapping, a rigorous appropriations audit, and a persuasive account of agency practice are decisive. Master these techniques, and you will be able to litigate or counsel effectively whenever Congress uses garden-variety statutory language—“shall,” “may,” “is authorized”—around sensitive, resource-constrained programs like refugee resettlement.
