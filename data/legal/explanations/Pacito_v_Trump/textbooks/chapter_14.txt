Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

Chapter 14: # Policy, Administration, and Practical Consequences

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order in Pacito v. Trump provides a vivid illustration of how legal disputes over immigration and refugee policy translate into acute administrative and humanitarian dilemmas. The opinion confronts two paired but distinct legal questions: the President’s broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend classes of aliens from entry, and the statutory architecture that funds and directs domestic resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court’s treatment — treating the admission moratorium as presumptively within executive power while simultaneously concluding that the government may not be free to abandon post‑admission resettlement obligations — produces an operational problem that agencies must plan for and courts must manage: how to suspend admissions without creating a dereliction of care for refugees already in the United States or conditionally admitted abroad.

This chapter examines the operational and humanitarian effects of a pause in admissions coupled with continuing statutory duties, and the capacity challenges agencies face when law and policy diverge. The material is doctrinal and practical. It uses the Pacito litigation to show how statutory text, judicial deference, injunctive relief, and appropriations law converge to shape administrative choices. It also develops governance principles and concrete risk‑management options that agencies and their local partners commonly confront when admissions slow or stop.

## Maintaining a resettlement infrastructure during admissions pauses

A legal suspension of admissions — even if lawfully executed under § 1182(f) — does not automatically extinguish the statutory or contractual frameworks that enable refugees’ domestic reception and placement. Section 1522 contemplates a federal resettlement infrastructure: competitive or cooperative awards to public and private nonprofit agencies, case management systems, employment and language programs, and state and local coordination. When a President suspends refugee admissions, the pipeline of new arrivals may be interrupted, but the organizational and legal scaffolding that receives, places, and supports refugees remains for those already admitted or conditionally approved. That was the crux of the district court’s injunction and the panel’s partial reversal: the government cannot assert a policy choice to cease admissions while simultaneously asserting that it has no responsibility to provide statutorily described reception and placement services for refugees who are already in the United States or lawfully arriving.

Administratively, maintaining a basic resettlement infrastructure during a pause in admissions requires explicit decisions about scope and funding. Contracts, cooperative agreements, and grants that funded reception and placement (R&P) typically include performance periods, deliverables, and billing mechanisms tied to caseloads. If admissions stop, caseloads fall, and cooperative partners face imminent fiscal shortfalls. Unless the agency has authority to reprogram funds, use carryover balances, or draw on supplemental appropriations or other sources, the agency may be forced to terminate agreements. But the legal consequences of termination depend on whether the underlying statutory regime imposes mandatory duties. If a court — as the Pacito panel suggested — interprets § 1522 as imposing nondiscretionary obligations for certain cohorts (for example, refugees admitted in the period immediately before the suspension), then termination can produce litigation risk and injunctive relief forcing reinstatement. Agencies must therefore weigh the legal risk of termination against authorization-based arguments and practical fiscal constraints.

Operationally, the prudent administrative response is to preserve a minimum, scalable infrastructure — a skeleton of staff, data systems, and cooperative relationship — that can continue to deliver essential functions (case tracking, emergency cash and medical assistance, housing placement pipeline management, and coordination with states and local service providers) while minimizing costs. That approach obliges agencies to design contracting instruments with contingency provisions: bridge agreements or short-term extensions, termination-for-convenience clauses with phased wind‑down, and options for reduced scope of work that permit core services to continue even when new arrivals are paused.

## Continuity of care for admitted refugees: housing, employment, language services

Continuity of care is the human heart of resettlement law. Section 1522 enumerates a bundle of services — employment training and placement, English language training, cash and medical assistance for refugees in their first 36 months, and special care for unaccompanied minors — that are designed to promote early economic self‑sufficiency and protect vulnerable populations. These services are not merely bureaucratic outputs; they are time‑sensitive interventions that, if interrupted, can produce harm to refugees’ housing stability, health, and labor market integration.

From a programmatic perspective, certain services must be delivered on schedule. Employment and language training are most effective when offered soon after arrival; delays decrease labor market outcomes and slow the path to self‑sufficiency. Temporary housing and initial cash assistance are essential to preventing homelessness or medical crisis during the critical first weeks and months. Unaccompanied minors require immediate placement and protective care, the legal contours of which Congress recognized in § 1522(d)(2)(B). The statutory text and regulatory framework therefore create an implicit temporal structure: early interventions are qualitatively different from long‑term integrations and cannot simply be deferred until admissions resume.

When an agency contemplates pausing admissions, it must therefore triage services. One institutionally sensible principle is to prioritize continuity of time‑dependent services for refugees already admitted or imminently arriving under pre‑existing approvals. That principle matches the district court’s injunction in Pacito and the panel’s directive that cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” be reinstated. Even where appropriations are constrained, agencies can seek to prioritize expenditures within the statutory “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification by documenting the compelling needs of admitted refugees, notifying appropriations committees, and requesting emergency supplemental funds or permissive reprogramming.

Ensuring continuity also requires coordination with local service providers and state and municipal agencies. Housing authorities, public health clinics, school systems, and workforce partners often serve as the frontline of refugee integration. Agencies should put in place memoranda of understanding and communication protocols to preserve those local relationships — a task that is administratively simple but politically and legally consequential when federal funding is cut. Failure to maintain these links can cause gaps in services that are difficult and costly to remediate.

## Risk management for agencies: termination vs. bridge mechanisms

Agencies faced with a policy decision to halt admissions must confront immediate legal and contractual risks. The choice to terminate cooperative agreements or to keep them in force through reduced or bridge funding carries different legal exposures and administrative costs.

Termination raises contract law and administrative law risks. Terminating cooperative agreements without observing statutory constraints and without adequate justification may expose the government to claims under the APA (arbitrary and capricious action), breach of contract litigation, and injunctive relief restoring funding or compelling performance. The Pacito panel relied on APA principles to order reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees — an example of how courts can use equitable relief to protect vested statutory expectations and reliance interests. Agencies confronted with such risk must carefully document the legal basis for termination, including budgetary necessity, alternative means of compliance, and consultative steps taken with grantees, and consider the equitable factors courts apply when evaluating preliminary injunctions and stays (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable harm; balance of equities; public interest), as articulated in Nken v. Holder and applied in the Pacito stay.

Bridge mechanisms offer an alternative risk management path. Bridge agreements, short‑term extensions, and phased reallocation clauses allow agencies to preserve continuity while managing costs. From a contracting perspective, agencies should build contingency clauses into RFPs and cooperative agreements that permit temporary scope reductions, phased staffing, or the suspension of intake activities while preserving essential services. Where existing contracts lack these clauses, agencies should consider executing short-term modification agreements, using available contract administrative tools to avoid abrupt cessation. Bridge mechanisms also reduce political fallout and protect vulnerable populations during policy transitions.

A further risk-management tool is clear recordkeeping and interbranch communication. If an agency intends to curtail services due to policy choices, it should build an evidentiary record demonstrating how appropriations constraints, programmatic priorities, and statutory authorities were considered and why certain services can or cannot be maintained. Such documentation is not merely good governance; it can determine whether a court views the agency’s action as a permissible exercise of discretion or an arbitrary revocation of statutory obligations.

## Intergovernmental impacts: state and local partners, private sponsors, and NGOs

The resettlement system is not purely federal. Its success depends on an ecosystem of state and local governments, nonprofit resettlement agencies, volunteer networks, and private sponsors. A suspension of admissions and any associated federal defunding therefore has ripple effects that extend well beyond the Department of State or HHS headquarters.

Local resettlement agencies typically operate on thin margins. Their budgets commonly rely on a mix of federal cooperative agreement dollars, private donations, and in‑kind contributions. When federal funding is withdrawn, NGOs may respond with short‑term fundraising, volunteer mobilization, or redeployment of staff. But these are stopgap measures: sustained resettlement operations require predictable funding. The forced termination of cooperative agreements thus threatens organizational viability, staff retention, and institutional knowledge. In Pacito, the organizational plaintiffs were precisely such agencies, and the district court’s injunction recognized that terminating their funding would jeopardize the services they provide to admitted refugees.

State and local governments also feel the impact. Housing authorities, school districts, and local health providers carry the immediate cost of service provision when federal programs lapse. Although some states are sympathetic to continued resettlement and may adopt complementary programs, many lack the fiscal capacity to absorb large numbers of arrivals in the face of federal retrenchment. Private sponsorship models can offset federal reduction in some instances, but they too are limited by volunteers’ bandwidth and by legal restrictions on what private sponsors may be expected to deliver.

The interdependency of actors suggests two administrative solutions. First, federal agencies should consult and coordinate with state and local partners before implementing admissions pauses or program terminations. Consultation reduces surprises and allows for contingency planning at the local level. Second, agencies should explore temporary cost‑sharing arrangements or pass‑through grants to local partners to ensure continuity of critical services — a legally and politically feasible mechanism when federal appropriations permit such flexibility.

## Budgetary planning amidst rescissions and shifting executive priorities

Appropriations law is central to understanding how far agencies may go in sustaining or suspending services. Section 1522 repeatedly uses the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations,” signaling that Congress’s appropriation choices constrain executive action. But that qualification is not a blank check for unilateral defunding. Agencies must grapple with the mechanics of budget availability, rescissions, and anti‑deficiency limitations.

Pacito’s factual matrix included specific appropriations context: for FY 2025 Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, subsequently reduced by $800 million in a rescissions statute. The availability of funds, timing of rescissions, and the agency’s internal budgeting decisions determine whether contracts can lawfully be funded. Agencies must therefore maintain a clear nexus between statutory obligations, appropriations law, and contract obligations. Where appropriations are inadequate, agencies should provide Congress with timely notice and exercise available administrative flexibilities (e.g., carryover of unobligated balances, reprogramming within statutory limits, or requesting emergency supplemental appropriations).

From a risk perspective, invoking appropriations constraints as a basis for termination requires clear documentary support. The anti‑deficiency statute limits obligations in the absence of appropriations; nonetheless, a bare assertion that funding is insufficient will be insufficient if unobligated balances or reprogramming authority exists. Moreover, courts often expect agencies to use available statutory mechanisms to mitigate harm to beneficiaries before resorting to contract terminations.

Budget planning should therefore anticipate policy shifts. Agencies that operate in politically volatile domains ought to maintain contingency reserves, negotiate flexible contracts with NGOs (including options for reduced scope), and build triggers for ramp‑down and ramp‑up that minimize service interruptions. Budget officials should also coordinate closely with program leaders to ensure that legal interpretations of statutory duties (such as whether § 1522 is mandatory or permissive) are reconciled with fiscal strategy and with ongoing communications to grantees and appropriations committees.

## Applying the Pacito dispute: practical governance lessons and legal tests

Pacito emphasizes several doctrinal touchstones with immediate governance consequences. First, the court’s invocation of Trump v. Hawaii shows how expansive § 1182(f) is: courts give substantial deference to presidential findings that entry of a class of aliens is detrimental to national interests. For agencies, this means that pauses in admissions grounded in § 1182(f) will seldom be overturned on the merits. But that executive latitude does not automatically excuse neglect of statutory duties to persons already admitted.

Second, the debate over statutory interpretation of § 1522 — whether it creates mandatory duties or is merely an authorization for discretionary programming — is pivotal. The majority’s reading, which the Pacito panel applied in ordering cooperative agreements reinstated for admitted refugees, treats at least portions of § 1522 as imposing non‑discretionary duties to ensure the immediate care and assistance of refugees already in the United States. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent highlights the counterargument: the statute’s title and many provisions use the term “authorize,” suggesting permissive rather than mandatory obligations. For administrators, this split in judicial interpretation signals litigation risk and suggests prudence: absent a clear statutory reading in one’s favor, agencies should not assume unfettered discretion to defund or cease services for admitted refugees without exposing themselves to injunctive relief.

Third, equitable remedies play a major role. The district court’s preliminary injunctions and the Ninth Circuit’s directed reinstatement of some cooperative agreements show that courts can and will tailor relief to preserve statutory expectations and to prevent irreparable harm, even while respecting executive prerogatives on admissions. The four Nken factors — with particular emphasis on likelihood of success and irreparable injury — will remain the lodestar in stay and injunction settings. Agencies should therefore assess not just the legal strength of their position, but the practical likelihood that their actions will cause irreparable harm to vulnerable populations, which can tip the balance toward court‑ordered relief.

Operationally, this jurisprudence suggests a conservative administrative posture when admitting refugees is paused: preserve essential services for those already in the system; create bridge agreements and phased termination protocols; document appropriation constraints and interbranch communications; and coordinate with state, local, and nonprofit partners to ensure continuity of critical housing, medical, and child protection services.

## Conclusion: reconciling legal authority and administrative duty

The Pacito litigation demonstrates a central paradox of refugee governance. The President’s power to curtail entry under § 1182(f) is broad and receives judicial deference, yet the federal government simultaneously stands behind a statutory commitment — reflected in § 1522 and associated regulations — to provide immediate reception and placement services to recently admitted refugees. When admission policy and statutory care obligations diverge, agencies and courts become arbiters of how to allocate scarcity without abdicating legal and humanitarian responsibilities.

For administrators, the principal lesson is one of anticipatory governance: design contracts and budgets with contingencies; prioritize time‑sensitive services for admitted refugees; document and communicate appropriation limitations to Congress; and build operational flexibility to scale services down and back up without creating gaps that courts will seek to cure. For lawyers and judges, Pacito reaffirms that broad executive authority on entry does not erase statutory commitments to persons already within the United States or lawfully arriving, and that equitable remedies remain potent tools for protecting vulnerable populations when policy changes endanger continuity of care.
