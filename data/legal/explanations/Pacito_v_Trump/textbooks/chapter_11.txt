Chapter 11: # Separation of Powers and the Youngstown Lens

The Pacito litigation is a useful classroom vehicle for thinking about the separation of powers in practice. At its core this case presents a familiar trio of issues: (1) the scope of presidential authority to restrict entry of non‑citizens (the statutory grant in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)); (2) the scope of statutory duties imposed on the executive by the Refugee Act (most importantly 8 U.S.C. § 1522), and the consequences when an executive policy decision collides with such duties; and (3) the limits that appropriations law and impoundment principles place on the Executive’s ability to refuse to spend money that Congress has appropriated. Pacito illustrates how courts sort these competing claims through the Youngstown framework, basic canons of statutory interpretation, doctrines of administrative law, and the law governing the federal purse. This chapter explains those doctrinal tools and shows how they were deployed (and contested) in Pacito so that you can apply them to new fact patterns.

## Youngstown categories and their application to immigration and refugee policy

Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer remains the touchstone for structuring judicial review of presidential action that operates in a domain where Congress has also legislated. The familiar tripartite framework divides presidential action into three categories:

- Category 1: the President acts pursuant to an express or implied authorization of Congress — the Executive stands at its maximum constitutional authority and courts will give considerable weight to the President’s position;  
- Category 2: the President acts where Congress has not spoken — a zone of twilight in which both branches may have concurrent authority and the court’s scrutiny depends on the circumstances;  
- Category 3: the President acts against the express or implied will of Congress — the Executive’s power is at its lowest ebb and the action must be supported solely by his constitutional powers absent congressional authorization.

Apply this framework in immigration/regulatory settings by asking first whether Congress has given the President an explicit statutory grant of authority that authorizes the Executive’s action, and second whether Congress elsewhere has legislated in a way that constrains or contradicts that action. In Pacito the panel divided the issues along those lines. The President pointed to § 1182(f), the century‑old provision that permits the President, “whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend the entry of any class of aliens. That is an express statutory delegation and places the suspension of refugee admissions squarely in Youngstown Category 1 — the Executive’s action is taken pursuant to an express congressional authorization. The panel repeatedly invoked Trump v. Hawaii, where the Supreme Court sustained analogous entry restrictions under § 1182(f) and described that statutory authority as one that “exudes deference” to the President.

But Youngstown analysis does not stop with an initial classification. The existence of one grant of authority (here § 1182(f)) does not necessarily immunize the Executive from all other statutory constraints that Congress has enacted. If another statute imposes duties that the Executive’s policy frustrates, courts must ask whether the President’s exercise of § 1182(f) is nonetheless in conflict with Congress’s will expressed elsewhere — and whether that conflict reduces the action to Youngstown Category 3. In Pacito the court confronted precisely that tension: § 1182(f) provided a clear pathway for suspending admissions, but § 1522 imposed obligations relating to domestic reception and placement services for refugees admitted to the United States. Where the Executive suspends admissions and defunds resettlement activities in a way that prevents the provision of statutorily required services to already‑admitted refugees, the operative question becomes whether the President’s action, though authorized to stop future admissions, transgresses congressionally imposed duties regarding persons already admitted. The panel concluded that on the admissions question the government was likely to prevail (Youngstown Category 1), but that it was not likely to prevail on the claim that the government may refuse to perform statutory obligations to refugees already admitted (which puts the Executive’s conduct into closer proximity to Youngstown Category 3, or at least removes Category 1’s protective force).

When you analyze similar cases, use Youngstown as an organizing framework but resist treating it as a mechanically exclusive test. The key analytic moves are identifying statutory authorizations and statutory constraints, comparing them for conflict or compatibility, and calibrating the level of judicial deference accordingly.

## Delegation via § 1182(f) vs. statutory constraints via § 1522

To understand the interplay between § 1182(f) and § 1522 you must do two things simultaneously: (1) read the delegation to the President to determine what actions it permits; and (2) read the statutory scheme addressing refugee admissions and resettlement to see what duties Congress has imposed on the Executive. The two readings may pull in different directions.

Section 1182(f) is unusually broad on its face. It authorizes the President, after finding that entry of “any aliens or of any class of aliens” would be “detrimental” to the United States, to suspend their entry “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” Congress has thus delegated a sweeping power to the President over immigration admission decisions. When a plaintiff challenges a presidential action taken pursuant to § 1182(f), courts often concentrate on whether the President in fact made the requisite finding, and then — because of statutory deference and the political character of immigration decisions — afford substantial deference to the President’s national‑security and foreign‑policy determinations. That is why courts treating § 1182(f) see the President’s action as operating in Youngstown Category 1 and why the Pacito panel found a likelihood of success on the merits of the suspension of refugee admissions.

Section 1522, by contrast, is part of the Refugee Act of 1980 and sets out Congress’s expectations for domestic resettlement programs and services, including a funding mechanism and a structure under which the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) and other agencies provide reception and placement services. Key features include “shall” language in subsection (a) purporting to impose duties on the Director “to the extent of available appropriations,” specific language authorizing grants and cooperative agreements with public and private non‑profit agencies, and statutory reporting requirements. Historically, the federal government has implemented § 1522 by contracting or entering cooperative agreements with non‑profit resettlement agencies that deliver reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

These two statutes thus occupy different functional spaces: § 1182(f) controls who may enter the country; § 1522 requires the Executive to provide specific services to those who have been admitted. When the Executive uses § 1182(f) to suspend admissions it is acting within the sphere Congress created for the President; when the Executive suspends or refuses to perform duties described in § 1522 (e.g., terminating cooperative agreements for reception and placement services), the Executive may be preventing Congress’s statute from operating as intended. Resolving the tension between those statutes requires statutory interpretation and separation‑of‑powers reasoning about whether the President’s delegated authority is broad enough to override, or is constrained by, statutory duties Congress has already imposed.

Pacito illustrates both sides of this coin. The majority panel read § 1182(f) as a commanding grant supporting the suspension, but read § 1522’s provisions — in particular the requirement to provide reception and placement services “to the extent of available appropriations” and the specific language regarding legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minors — as creating enforceable statutory obligations that the Executive could not simply refuse to perform. Judge Lee’s partial dissent recognized § 1182(f)’s breadth but read § 1522 as largely permissive, arguing that much of § 1522 uses authorization language (e.g., “is authorized”) and that the “shall” clauses are best read as conditions upon which funding, if provided, must be used. That disagreement centers on statutory interpretation doctrines that courts commonly apply: the significance of the statutory title and headings, the use of “shall” versus “is authorized,” the import of the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier, and the contextual reading of the statutory scheme.

When approaching similar disputes, first parse the text of each relevant provision with attention to operative verbs, qualifying phrases, cross‑references, and the statute’s place within the larger statutory framework. Then ask whether the circumstances present a true conflict (one statute necessarily prevents the other from operating) or whether the statutes can be read to coexist (e.g., the President suspends admissions prospectively under § 1182(f) while the Executive continues to satisfy § 1522’s duties as to persons already admitted).

## When executive orders collide with statutory duties: supremacy of statute and remedial consequences

A central separation‑of‑powers principle is simple: the Executive cannot nullify statutory obligations by issuing an executive order that conflicts with or frustrates the intent of Congress. When an executive order purports to alter the operation of a statute — for example, by depriving a statutorily protected group of services Congress intended them to receive — courts apply established rules to resolve the dispute.

First, statutory supremacy. If Congress has plainly required a particular program, payment, or duty, the Executive lacks authority to disregard or abrogate that requirement through an executive order. Youngstown Category 3 is the conceptual locus of this rule: when the President acts contrary to the will of Congress, his power is at its nadir and the courts must be prepared to enjoin ultra vires conduct. Pacito shows this principle in operation. The panel concluded the government was unlikely to prevail on the APA claims concerning § 1522 and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements to ensure reception and placement services for refugees already admitted. That relief is a classic example of judicial enforcement of statutory duties: the court did not purport to second‑guess the President’s assessment about future admissions under § 1182(f), but it refused to allow an executive policy to thwart a statutorily mandated domestic resettlement program.

Second, remedies. When courts find that executive action violates statutory requirements or the APA, they typically fashion prospective equitable relief tailored to restore the status quo ante and to ensure compliance with statutory duties. In the preliminary injunction context — the posture of the district court’s initial orders in Pacito — courts weigh the Nken factors (likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, balance of equities, and public interest) in deciding whether to enjoin the government. The Pacito panel applied that framework on stay‑pending‑appeal review; the district court’s initial injunctions were stayed in part while the appellate panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for admitted refugees because the panel concluded the government had not shown it could meet statutory duties otherwise.

Third, judicial review and remand. When the government terminates programs without reasoned explanation, courts will often remand under the APA for a reasoned explanation or will set aside agency action as arbitrary and capricious. Even where the Executive has discretion, that discretion normally must be exercised consistent with statutory mandates and must be accompanied by reasoned decisionmaking. Pacito rests on that principle: the panel signaled that the government has not provided an adequate record showing that it has fulfilled or can fulfill statutory duties, and thus the court required remedial measures to ensure compliance.

For a student confronted with a hypothetical where an executive order appears to conflict with a statute, the proper sequence is: identify the statutes at issue and their text; determine whether the executive action is authorized or is in conflict; apply Youngstown to determine deference; and, if conflict exists, consider whether judicial relief should be fashioned to vindicate the statute (injunction, reinstatement of programs, remand for explanation). Remember that courts will not lightly substitute their policy judgments for the President’s in matters of immigration and national security, but they will enforce statutory obligations that are unambiguous and mandatory.

## Appropriations and the Anti‑Deficiency Act: operating mandates “to the extent of available appropriations”

Many statutory duties are expressly qualified by appropriations language: Congress commonly writes “to the extent of available appropriations” or “subject to the availability of appropriations.” That qualifying phrase plays a pivotal role in how courts resolve disputes about the Executive’s obligation to fund programs. Pacito turns on exactly this kind of language: § 1522(a) conditions the Director’s responsibilities on “the extent of available appropriations.” How should courts read that qualification?

Start with the ordinary meaning. “To the extent of available appropriations” makes performance contingent on funds being available; it does not, however, mean that the Executive may ignore appropriated funds that Congress has in fact made available. Congress can both authorize and appropriate. Authorization statutes (like § 1522, depending on how it is read) permit the program to be run; appropriations statutes provide the money to run it. If Congress has appropriated funds to accomplish a statutory program, the Executive cannot decline to spend those funds for that purpose without observing the procedures for altering spending (such as seeking a rescission or reprogramming consistent with appropriations law). The key is whether appropriations for the program exist and whether they are lawfully available for the aggrieved statutory purpose.

The Anti‑Deficiency Act (ADA), 31 U.S.C. § 1341 et seq., prohibits federal officers from obligating or expending funds in advance of appropriation or in excess of an appropriation. It also forbids accepting voluntary services for the United States. The ADA thus bars an agency from legally obligating funds if there is no appropriation to support the obligation. Conversely, when Congress appropriates money and the agency has authority to obligate funds for a program, the ADA does not permit the Executive to refuse to obligate appropriated funds simply because of policy disagreement; to do so would be an illegal withholding of funds that Congress intended to be spent.

Train v. City of New York (421 U.S. 775 (1975)) is a canonical case on this point. There, the Secretary of Transportation reduced obligations under a statutory program despite Congress having appropriated funds and having authorized the Secretary to make grants. The Supreme Court held the Secretary could not refuse to carry out the statutory plan when Congress had appropriated money for that purpose. That holding stands for the proposition that the Executive cannot effectively nullify statutory programs by declining to spend appropriated funds without lawful authority to do so.

Pacito signals this dynamic. The panel noted the FY 2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance and the subsequent rescission that reduced the total. The presence of an appropriation (even a reduced one) is important: the Executive’s decision to terminate cooperative agreements and to suspend funding for reception and placement services is not proof that appropriations are unavailable to satisfy § 1522 duties. Where the record shows that funds exist and were appropriated, courts will scrutinize the Executive’s decision to stop implementing statutory programs to ensure the decision comports with appropriations law.

A few practical points for applying these principles:

- Determine whether Congress has appropriated funds for the statutory program and whether those funds are currently available (not rescinded or otherwise legally restricted). Legislative history and appropriations committee reports can be relevant but are secondary to the text of the appropriation.

- Ask whether the executive branch has a lawful procedure for redirecting or deferring spending. The President cannot simply impound or permanently withhold appropriation funds; the Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974 and OMB rules set out procedures for deferrals, rescissions, and reprogramming. Those procedures ordinarily must be followed.

- If funds have been lawfully appropriated and are available to fund statutory duties, the Executive must explain any decision not to obligate them. A bare policy disagreement is an insufficient basis to withhold funds that Congress has allocated for a statutory program.

In short, “to the extent of available appropriations” makes obligations contingent on whether Congress has provided money, not a license for the Executive to nullify a statutory program when funds are in fact available.

## Impoundment principles and limits on executive non‑expenditure of appropriated funds

The history of impoundment — and the congressional reaction to perceived executive impoundment — is essential background for Pacito. Impoundment refers to executive branch withholding or delaying the obligation or expenditure of funds that Congress has appropriated. In the Nixon years, Presidents frequently refused to spend appropriated funds for policy reasons; Congress responded with the Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, which sought to constrain executive impoundment by requiring the President to transmit proposed rescissions to Congress and by creating rules governing deferrals.

Legal limits on impoundment derive from a combination of the Anti‑Deficiency Act, Train, and the Budget Act. The Executive may not unilaterally suppress expenditures in a way that frustrates clear congressional intent. If the Executive believes that funds should not be spent for policy reasons, the constitutional and statutory route is to seek a rescission from Congress; absent that, the Executive’s lawful options are limited to temporary deferments within the procedures established by the Budget Act and OMB rules.

Applied to Pacito, those principles mean that the termination of cooperative agreements and the halt in funding for domestic resettlement required careful attention to the appropriations and to the procedures used. If the Executive terminated funding and cooperative agreements without a lawful basis (for example, absent a contemporaneous determination that appropriations were unavailable or without following deferral/rescission procedures), the district court could find that the Executive had unlawfully impounded funds or otherwise failed to carry out a statutory duty. The Pacito panel’s directive that cooperative agreements be reinstated (as necessary to fulfill § 1522 duties) reflects courts’ willingness to remedy situations where the Executive has not shown that appropriation‑based constraints justify non‑spending.

A couple of practical guideposts follow:

- Distinguish between three forms of non‑expenditure: (1) genuine non‑availability of appropriations (e.g., funds were rescinded or otherwise legally restricted); (2) lawful, temporary deferral under Budget Act procedures that have been properly invoked; and (3) unlawful impoundment or refusal to spend appropriated funds for policy reasons without following statutory procedures. Only the first two can be legally sustainable in most circumstances.

- When agencies terminate contracts or cooperative agreements, determine whether they are simply declining to enter into new obligations (which can be lawful if no appropriations exist) or whether they are terminating existing obligations that Congress meant to continue. Termination of already‑valid obligations funded by appropriations raises different legal questions.

- Courts will look for a reasoned administrative record. If the government asserts that funds are not available, the record should explain why: the appropriations that would otherwise fund the program were rescinded, reprogrammed, or otherwise rendered unavailable pursuant to law. Unsupported assertions that the Executive lacks money or that a policy change justifies non‑spending are unlikely to satisfy judicial review.

## Applying the Pacito lessons to future fact patterns

Pacito offers a roadmap for analyzing future disputes that involve presidential directives, statutory duties, and appropriations law. Here is a step‑by‑step approach to analyzing similar problems:

1. Identify the relevant statutes. Does the President rely on an express statutory grant (e.g., § 1182(f))? Does another statute impose duties (e.g., § 1522)? Identify the operative text: “may,” “is authorized,” “shall,” and any appropriations qualifiers.

2. Place the case in the Youngstown frame. If the President acts under an express statutory authorization, you are in Category 1 and give substantial deference to the Executive on that discrete power. If Congress has legislated contrary to the President’s action, treat the situation as moving toward Category 3 and apply stricter review.

3. Read the statutes together. Can the statutes coexist, or do they present an irreconcilable conflict? If they can coexist (for example, the President may suspend future admissions under § 1182(f) while still providing reception services to those already admitted), courts will usually order the Executive to take steps to satisfy both statutes.

4. Examine the appropriations. Has Congress appropriated funds for the statutory duties? Are those funds available? Did the Executive follow the Budget and Impoundment Control Act if it wished to defer or rescind spending? If the funds are available and the Executive has not lawfully impounded them, courts will be skeptical of refusals to spend.

5. Evaluate the administrative record. Under the APA, agencies must provide reasoned explanations for major policy shifts that affect statutory programs. Absent a reasoned explanation tied to statutory constraints or available appropriations, agency action is vulnerable to a finding of arbitrary and capricious or ultra vires conduct.

6. Consider remedies and equitable balancing. If the plaintiff is likely to succeed on the claim that a statutory duty has been violated, equitable relief (injunction, reinstatement of agreements, remand) is a typical remedy, balanced against the national‑security and foreign‑policy interests the Executive identifies.

Pacito thus demonstrates the interplay of statutory interpretation, separation of powers, administrative law, and appropriations law. The case reminds scholars and students that the President’s broad delegated powers in immigration and national security are not unbounded; statutory duties, the availability of appropriations, and the courts’ duty to ensure that the Executive acts within the law all remain potent constraints. Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito highlights the line‑drawing difficulty — many statutory provisions use both permissive and mandatory language and the line between authorization and obligation is often disputed — but the controlling lesson is institutional: the Executive can exercise broad discretion, but courts will enforce clear statutory mandates and the procedural and substantive rules that govern spending of appropriated funds.
