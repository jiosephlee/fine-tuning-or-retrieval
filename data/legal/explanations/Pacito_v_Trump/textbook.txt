Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

# Background and Procedural Posture

This chapter explains the executive actions, the parties and their claims, the district court’s preliminary injunctions, and the Ninth Circuit’s interlocutory stay practice that produced the panel order in Pacito v. Trump. The opinion underlying this chapter is an interim, expedited order that (1) stays most of the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending appeal, (2) vacates earlier administrative and partial stays, and (3) preserves a limited remedial requirement arising from statutory obligations to provide certain resettlement services. The legal points that matter for this procedural posture are the President’s asserted authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the statutory framework for refugee resettlement (principally 8 U.S.C. § 1522), the nature of cooperative agreements that have historically funded reception-and-placement services, and the standards for stays pending appeal.

## Executive Order 14163 (Jan. 20, 2025): suspension of refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f), 1185(a)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The Order declared that entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and directed suspension of refugee admissions “pending further findings.” The President cited 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (which authorizes the President to suspend entry of aliens whenever he finds such entry would be detrimental) and § 1185(a) as his authority. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order treats the § 1182(f) authority as broad and entitled to substantial deference, relying on Trump v. Hawaii for the principle that the statute “exudes deference” to the President’s national-security and foreign-affairs judgments.

Understanding § 1182(f) is central to the stay analysis: it is a statutory delegation that, at least according to the panel, imposes only the single textual prerequisite that the President “find” detriment to U.S. interests. That perceived breadth underlies the panel’s conclusion that the government is likely to succeed on the merits of a challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions.

## Executive Order 14169 (Jan. 24, 2025): suspension of USRAP-related funding as part of foreign aid reevaluation

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended funding related to the USRAP. That action was taken, the government said, pursuant to a separate executive order concerning foreign aid. The practical effect was to halt federal funding for domestic reception-and-placement services and some overseas processing support for the refugee program, including termination of cooperative agreements with non‑profit resettlement organizations. Those funding terminations are central to plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act (APA) claims and to the district court’s remedial orders.

## Plaintiffs: admitted refugees, refugees approved but abroad, and three resettlement organizations with State Department cooperative agreements

The plaintiffs include three categories: (1) refugees who had already been admitted to the United States; (2) individuals who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the United States (conditional approvals); and (3) three nonprofit organizations that historically provided overseas processing and domestic reception-and-placement services pursuant to cooperative agreements with the State Department. The organizations are the typical contractors for the USRAP’s “reception and placement” work—services Congress contemplated could be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii).

Plaintiffs sued in the Western District of Washington in February 2025. Their operative theories were that the E.O. suspending admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980 (as incorporated in the INA and described by the Supreme Court as the “general rule” governing refugees), and that the State Department’s defunding and termination of cooperative agreements violated the APA (e.g., through unlawful agency action or failure to follow required procedures).

## District court actions: Feb. 25 oral PI enjoining E.O. 14163 §§ 3(a)–(c), 4; Feb. 28 written PI specifying funding suspension prohibited; Mar. 24 PI ordering reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements

The district court moved quickly. On February 25, 2025, it orally issued a preliminary injunction that blocked enforcement or implementation of several provisions of Executive Order 14163 (identified as §§ 3(a)–(c) and § 4). The government responded administratively by terminating cooperative agreements; by February 26 the State Department had terminated all domestic cooperative agreements and nearly all overseas processing agreements.

To clarify scope, the district court issued a written order on February 28, 2025, that expressly prohibited suspension of USRAP funding (i.e., enjoined the State Department’s defunding activity). After further briefing on the fallout from the terminations, the court entered a second preliminary injunction on March 24, 2025, ordering the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the court’s February 25 ruling. Those remedial directives—prohibiting the funding suspension and requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements—are the subject of the government’s appeals and many of the procedural skirmishes that followed.

## Government appeals (Feb. 28 and Mar. 25 notices) and motions panel’s Mar. 25 partial stay with carveout for individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before Jan. 20, 2025; Apr. 21 and May 9 clarifications

The government appealed the district court’s orders, filing notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025. It also moved for a stay pending appeal. On March 25 a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted a partial stay. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, the panel emphasized the deference owed to the President under § 1182(f) and granted a stay to the extent the district court’s injunction interfered with the President’s suspension of refugee admissions. But the panel denied the stay insofar as the preliminary injunction applied to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) before January 20, 2025. That carveout left in force a narrow class of plaintiffs—those with prior conditional approvals—who could still seek enforcement of the district court’s injunction.

The motions panel later issued orders on April 21 and May 9 clarifying the scope and operation of that carveout. Those clarifications concerned the practical mechanics of enforcing relief for the covered individuals and reflected the court’s attempt to limit interference with core presidential authority while preserving relief for those the district court had determined to be particularly affected.

## Post-briefing developments: July 14 enforcement framework and magistrate appointment; July 17 emergency motion; July 18 administrative stay; Sept. 3 expedited oral argument

While the appeal was pending and briefing complete, the district court on July 14 issued an order setting out a framework to implement and enforce the carveout relief for conditionally approved individuals. The court appointed a magistrate judge to oversee this enforcement framework. The government responded with an emergency motion (filed July 17) asking the Ninth Circuit to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay. The Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order on July 18 while it considered the emergency motion. The panel then moved the case onto an expedited track, heard oral argument on September 3, 2025, and submitted the matter for decision—while indicating that a fuller opinion would follow.

During the interim, factual and administrative details continued to matter. The government told the district court on March 10 it was preparing a request for proposals for new reception-and-placement providers, but at oral argument represented uncertainty about any progress toward securing such providers. The panel’s interim order references that uncertainty in assessing whether the government can meet the statutory resettlement obligations without reinstating existing cooperative agreements.

## Current order: vacates prior stays (Mar. 25 and amendments; July 18), issues new stay pending appeal with an exception for statutorily required services, and denies the July 17 emergency motion as moot

On September 12, 2025, the Ninth Circuit issued the interim order now under study. The court vacated its earlier administrative stay (July 18) and the March 25 partial stay and its April/May clarifications, and then issued a new stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions “in their entirety,” except as noted. The court denied as moot the government’s July 17 emergency motion.

The panel applied the familiar four-factor Nken v. Holder standard for stays pending appeal—likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury, balance of harms, and public interest—emphasizing that the first two factors are the most critical. The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of the Executive Order’s suspension of refugee admissions, invoking the broad textual scope and high deference associated with § 1182(f) (again citing Trump v. Hawaii). The court therefore stayed the district court’s injunction insofar as it disrupted the President’s authority to suspend refugee entry.

But the panel also concluded the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA challenges—specifically, that the government has a statutory duty under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees who have already been admitted. Section 1522 (and related regulations) contemplates various services—employment training, English-language training, cash and medical assistance, and special care obligations for unaccompanied minors—and authorizes (and, depending on the court’s analysis, may sometimes obligate) the federal government to fund these services “to the extent of available appropriations.” Accounting for that statutory framework and the current uncertainty about the government’s ability to provide mandated services (and noting the existence of appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, subject to rescission activity), the panel directed reinstatement of those cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522 for refugees already admitted to the United States. The order required provision of the “usual and customary services” under the prior cooperative agreements for admitted refugees, while otherwise staying the district court’s remedial orders pending final adjudication of the appeal.

In short, the Ninth Circuit’s interlocutory order does three things: (1) it recognizes strong presidential authority under § 1182(f) and stays the district court’s injunction to the extent it curtailed the President’s suspension of refugee admissions; (2) it preserves a narrow statutory-provision remedy (reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees) because the government is not likely to prevail on that APA/§ 1522 claim; and (3) it vacates and replaces prior interim stays, leaving an opinion on the merits to follow.

# Legal Questions and Governing Law for a Stay Pending Appeal

This chapter explains the legal framework the Ninth Circuit applied in its expedited stay order in Pacito v. Trump. The court stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending appeal, while preserving a limited requirement that the government continue to provide certain reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. To understand that outcome you must be able to (1) apply the four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder; (2) see how the court treated the President’s statutory authority to suspend entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) in light of Trump v. Hawaii; and (3) see why the court found that statutory and APA constraints on terminating refugee resettlement services under the Refugee Act (chiefly 8 U.S.C. § 1522) required a narrower injunction than the district court ordered. Each of those subjects is summarized below with attention to how the panel used them in its interim ruling.

## The stay standard under Nken v. Holder
A stay pending appeal is governed by the four-factor test articulated in Nken v. Holder: (1) the likelihood the stay applicant will succeed on the merits, (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable harm absent a stay, (3) whether issuance of a stay will substantially injure other parties, and (4) where the public interest lies. The Ninth Circuit emphasized that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable harm—are “the most critical.” In this case the court concluded that the government satisfied those critical factors with respect to plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That finding supplied the core justification for staying the district court’s injunctions in their entirety, except for the limited requirement to restore services for refugees already admitted.

How the factors were applied here:
- Likelihood of success: The panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on the legality of the admission suspension because § 1182(f) plainly authorizes the President to “suspend the entry of . . . any class of aliens” once he “finds” entry would be detrimental. The court declined to subject the President’s justification to searching substantive review, citing deference principles from Trump v. Hawaii.
- Irreparable harm: The court identified national-security, foreign-relations, and other governmental interests as injuries the government would suffer absent a stay—harms that cannot be fully remedied on appeal.
- Injury to others and public interest: The panel weighed these factors but found that, overall, they favored a stay on the admissions moratorium question because of the broad discretion Congress vesting the President in this area. Nevertheless, the court balanced the public interest in statutory protections for refugees when it preserved obligations relating to services for refugees already admitted.

The practical lesson: a stay pending appeal requires an integrated, fact-sensitive judgment. Even where a movant is likely to prevail on one core claim (here, the President’s authority under § 1182(f)), the court may still carve out relief if other legal obligations (here, statutory duties to provide certain services) are likely to survive review.

## Presidential power to suspend entry: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and Trump v. Hawaii
The government invoked 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as the source of the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions. The statute says that whenever the President “finds” that the entry of any aliens or class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” he “may” suspend entry or impose restrictions. The panel relied on Trump v. Hawaii for two related propositions: (1) § 1182(f) plainly grants broad, discretionary authority to the President to restrict or suspend entry of aliens, and (2) courts accord substantial deference to the President’s national-security and foreign-relations judgments made under § 1182(f), limiting judicial review of the President’s explanations.

In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit invoked that precedent to conclude that the President had satisfied § 1182(f)’s textual prerequisite (he made the requisite “find[ing]”) and that the court could not substitute a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” On the stay record the panel thus found a strong likelihood that the government would prevail on its claim that the refugee-admissions suspension was within § 1182(f).

Important point for readers: § 1182(f) is worded in permissive terms (“may” suspend) and includes only the single textual threshold that the President “find” detriment. When Trump v. Hawaii is invoked, courts generally limit scrutiny of the President’s rationale and treat the statute as a broad grant of discretion in immigration-entry decisions.

## The Refugee Act framework: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1522 and 1523 and implementing regulations
While § 1182(f) governs admission decisions, the Refugee Act of 1980 (and implementing regulations) governs domestic resettlement services for refugees once they are admitted. The key provisions at issue in the stay order are in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and § 1523 and related regulations at 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.

- Purpose and structure of § 1522: The statute is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” It establishes a program under the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) for reception and placement and other services. The provision contains several different types of language:
  - Substantive directives in § 1522(a) (called “Conditions and consideration”): certain actions are described with “shall” language (for example, directives about making available resources for employment training and English instruction “to the extent of available appropriations”).
  - Programmatic subsections (b)–(e) creating mechanisms (grants, contracts, project funding) and describing specific programs (e.g., cash and medical assistance, unaccompanied children).
  - A reporting requirement in § 1523 that requires the Secretary to report to Congress on activities under the subchapter.
- Regulations: 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. implement the statute and describe the federal funding streams available for refugee services and the administrative processes by which ORR and other agencies operate resettlement programs.

Why § 1522 mattered here: Plaintiffs alleged (and the panel preliminarily accepted as likely meritorious) that § 1522 imposes statutory obligations to ensure reception-and-placement services are provided to refugees who have been admitted. Because those services are commonly delivered through cooperative agreements with nonprofit resettlement organizations, the February 2025 termination of all cooperative agreements posed a direct risk to delivering statutorily required services.

## APA challenges to defunding and termination of cooperative agreements
The plaintiffs advanced Administrative Procedure Act (APA) claims challenging the executive actions that suspended USRAP funding and terminated cooperative agreements. The APA gives courts authority to set aside agency action that is “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law” (5 U.S.C. § 706(2)), or contrary to statutory mandates.

Two types of APA theories are most directly implicated by the district court’s injunction and the Ninth Circuit’s carveout:
- Contrary-to-law (statutory-violation) theory: Plaintiffs argued termination of cooperative agreements conflicts with statutory duties in § 1522—i.e., agencies cannot eliminate programs or stop providing services that the statute requires. If the statute imposes a mandatory duty and the agency’s action defeats that duty, the action can be set aside as not in accordance with law.
- Arbitrary-and-capricious review: Even if the statute is permissive in some respects, plaintiffs can argue that the termination was arbitrary and capricious because the agencies failed to consider relevant factors (e.g., statutory obligations, reliance interests of resettlement organizations, and the immediate needs of refugees already admitted), provided no reasoned explanation, or failed to follow required procedures for changes affecting cooperative agreements.

The panel’s interim decision indicates it viewed at least one APA-based challenge to the terminations as likely to succeed: the court concluded that the government had not shown it could lawfully stop the reception-and-placement services required by § 1522 for refugees who have already been admitted. That conclusion justified directing the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to furnish those services pending appeal.

For students: when an agency changes course on programmatic funding or cooperative agreements tied to statutory duties, courts commonly examine both whether the statute authorizes or compels the challenged action and whether the agency supplied a reasoned explanation for the change consistent with the APA’s “hard look” requirement.

## Appropriations context and the meaning of “to the extent of available appropriations”
A pivotal textual and practical issue in the litigation was the phrase in § 1522(a) “to the extent of available appropriations.” That phrase appears to condition some duties on the availability of funds—raising the question whether § 1522 creates absolute, judicially enforceable duties or only contingent obligations that depend on Congressional appropriations.

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order addressed appropriations contextually: it noted that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 and that a later rescission reduced that amount by $800 million (Rescissions Act of 2025). The presence of a significant appropriation—even after a rescission—bore on whether funds were “available” for programs under § 1522. The panel’s interim conclusion—that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim—implies the court viewed appropriations as sufficient (or at least not obviously absent) to trigger statutory obligations for services to refugees already in the United States.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the competing statutory interpretations: she emphasized that § 1522’s title (“Authorization for programs...”) and many provisions use permissive language (“is authorized”), arguing the statute is principally discretionary and that the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier further supports non-mandatoriness. The majority, however, read some provisions of § 1522 (and related subsections addressing immediate care for unaccompanied minors and short-term support) as imposing obligations that cannot be nullified simply by administrative termination of cooperative agreements—at least where appropriations exist and refugees are already present.

Practical takeaway: the availability (or lack) of appropriated funds is often decisive when statutes tie agency duties to appropriations. A court assessing an APA or statutory-duty claim will ask whether Congress has supplied funds adequate to make the statutory duties operative and whether the agency’s funding decisions are consistent with statutory commands and reasonable administrative practice.

## Mechanisms for delivering services: contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)
Section 1522 explicitly contemplates that reception-and-placement services can be provided through “contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies” (statutory citation in the panel order). Historically, the federal government has used cooperative agreements with national and local resettlement organizations to perform intake, placement, employment services, language instruction, cash and medical assistance connections, and other immediate-support functions.

Why the delivery mechanism matters here:
- Cooperative agreements create a reliance ecosystem: resettlement agencies organize staffing, local placements, leases, and service networks on the expectation of federal cooperation/funding. Abrupt termination disrupts that ecosystem and can be a ground for APA claims that an agency failed to consider reliance interests.
- Statutory linkage: when statutory duties (in § 1522) are framed so that services “may” be provided through cooperative agreements, termination of those agreements—without a lawful alternative—can be treated as inconsistent with the statute if the underlying duty remains operative and appropriations exist.
- Remedial implications: because cooperative agreements are the means by which services have been historically delivered, the district court ordered and the Ninth Circuit required reinstatement of those agreements necessary to furnish services to admitted refugees pending appeal.

The panel’s interim directive—that the government reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to deliver the services described in § 1522 for refugees already admitted—reflects the court’s view that the statute, read with the appropriations picture and the government’s admissions about its lack of an alternative, created enforceable duties that could not be suspended wholesale without further explanation or lawful authority.

*Conclusion (how these questions shaped the stay)*

The panel’s stay order reflects a split legal analysis: it deferred to the President’s broad authority under § 1182(f) (and Trump v. Hawaii) regarding suspension of refugee admissions and therefore was willing to stay the district court’s injunction forbidding that suspension. At the same time, the court concluded that statutory obligations under § 1522—and the APA consequences of terminating the cooperative agreements that historically delivered those obligations—likely prevented the government from cutting off reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted, especially where appropriations existed. That mixed result—full stay of the district court’s orders except for a limited requirement to reinstate agreements and services for admitted refugees—follows from applying Nken’s stay factors together with the interaction between presidential admission authority and Congress’s statutory framework for resettlement services.

# Interim Merits Assessment: Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

This chapter explains the Ninth Circuit’s interim assessment that the government is likely to prevail on its defense of Executive Order No. 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and how that assessment shaped the court’s decision to stay the district court’s injunction against the suspension of refugee admissions. The opinion relies principally on the text of § 1182(f), Supreme Court precedent emphasizing judicial deference in immigration and national-security contexts (most notably Trump v. Hawaii), and the four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder. Reading the court’s reasoning requires attention to what § 1182(f) actually says, how courts treat presidential “findings” under that provision, and how likelihood of success on the merits interacts with the other stay factors.

## Textual breadth of § 1182(f): suspension of entry of any class of aliens based on presidential detriment finding

Section 1182(f) authorizes the President to act whenever he “finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The statute then permits the President, “by proclamation,” to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.” The panel’s interim order reads this language broadly: Congress plainly gave the President authority to suspend entry of “any class” of aliens, and the provision’s operative condition—the President’s finding of detriment—is the “sole prerequisite” spelled out in the text. The court therefore treats § 1182(f) as a grant of sweeping power to suspend entry, subject to the single textual requirement that the President make the requisite finding.

For students, the key takeaway is that statutory language matters here: the statute speaks in universal terms (“all aliens or any class of aliens”) and places the triggering decision squarely with the President (“whenever the President finds”). The panel relied on those textual features to conclude that Executive Order 14163 falls within the statute’s plain scope.

## Limits on judicial review: no “searching inquiry” into the President’s justifications per Trump v. Hawaii

Because § 1182(f) vests the determinative “finding” in the President, the panel limited the scope of judicial inquiry into the Executive’s justification. The court quoted Trump v. Hawaii for the principle that courts should not engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Trump underscored that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and vests “ample power” in him to impose entry restrictions. The Ninth Circuit adopted that posture in this interim order: the question for the stay analysis is not whether the President’s policy is politically appealing or factually optimal, but whether the statutory prerequisites are satisfied and whether the statute’s text bounds the President’s action.

Practically, this means the court’s preliminary review focuses on two simple legal checks: did the President make the formal finding that entry “would be detrimental,” and does the order fall within the textual authority conferred by § 1182(f)? The court expressed that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the statutory finding here and that the executive order does not exceed “any textual limit” on the President’s authority. The court therefore declined to require a searching evidentiary record or to second-guess the substance of the President’s national-security or foreign-policy judgments at the preliminary stage.

## Application to E.O. 14163: the President made the requisite detriment finding; the order fits § 1182(f)’s scope

Applying those principles to Executive Order 14163, the panel concluded that the President satisfied § 1182(f)’s formal condition. The order expressly states a presidential finding that refugee entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and directs suspension of refugee admissions pending further findings. That language tracks the statutory trigger; accordingly, the court treated the President’s declaration as adequate for the purposes of § 1182(f).

The panel also found the order’s substance to be within § 1182(f)’s scope. Because the statute authorizes suspension of the “entry of . . . any class of aliens,” the court read the refugee class described in EO 14163 to be a class that § 1182(f) covers. In short, under the court’s preliminary legal assessment, (1) the Executive made the finding § 1182(f) requires, and (2) the operational step taken—suspending entry of a class of aliens (refugees)—is the very remedial power the statute confers. That conclusion provided the foundation for the court’s view that the government is “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.”

Students should note the limited nature of this assessment: the panel made a preliminary, likelihood-of-success determination for stay purposes, not a final merits disposition. The court signaled that a fuller opinion will elaborate the merits later, but the interim reasoning relied on the straightforward textual fit and on deference principles from Trump v. Hawaii.

## Balancing the Nken factors: domestic and foreign policy interests, irreparable harm to the government, and public interest favor a stay of the injunction against the entry suspension

A federal court deciding whether to stay a district-court injunction pending appeal applies the four-factor Nken test: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) likelihood of irreparable harm to the movant absent a stay, (3) potential injury to other interested parties from granting the stay, and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken makes the first two factors “the most critical.”

Because the panel concluded the government is likely to succeed on the § 1182(f) claim, that critical first factor weighed strongly in favor of a stay. The court then linked that conclusion to the second factor—irreparable harm—by invoking the national-security, foreign-relations, and immigration policy interests that § 1182(f) empowers the President to protect. The panel characterized those interests as substantial and as matters where the President is afforded “broad discretion.” In that context the court viewed the government as likely to suffer irreparable injury if the injunction remained in force and prevented the President from implementing a suspension tied to those policy judgments.

For the remaining factors, the panel concluded that neither the harm to plaintiffs nor the public interest counseled against a stay. Although the district court had granted an injunction to protect plaintiffs’ asserted statutory and APA rights, the panel weighed those interests against the government’s interest in preserving its chosen immigration and foreign-policy posture while the appeal proceeds. Because these decisions often implicate foreign relations and national security, the court viewed the public interest as favoring the maintenance of executive flexibility and cautioned that courts should be reluctant to obstruct such executive acts at the preliminary stage.

Putting the Nken factors together, the Ninth Circuit found that the government had shown both a strong likelihood of success (based on the text of § 1182(f) and deference doctrines) and a risk of irreparable harm if the stay were denied. Those two central factors, combined with considerations about competing harms and the public interest, justified a stay of the district court’s injunction insofar as it barred implementation of the entry suspension.

Conclusion

In sum, the panel’s interim order treats § 1182(f) as a broad statutory grant allowing the President to suspend the entry of a class of aliens so long as he makes the statutory “detriment” finding. Drawing on Trump v. Hawaii, the court limited judicial inquiry into the substance of that finding and, on that basis, concluded the government is likely to prevail on the merits of its § 1182(f) defense. Because likelihood of success and the prospect of irreparable injury favor the government, those core Nken factors supported issuing a stay of the district court’s injunction against the refugee-entry suspension while the appeal proceeds.

# Interim Merits Assessment: Statutory Duties to Provide Post-Admission Services

This chapter explains why the Ninth Circuit panel concluded, in its interim order, that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge—namely, that the executive branch violated statutory duties to provide certain post‑admission refugee services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522—and how that conclusion produced a narrow exception to the court’s stay of the district court injunctions. The discussion stays close to the panel’s reasoning: it identifies the specific statutory provisions the panel relied on, summarizes the implementing regulations and appropriations context that supply the “availability” predicate, shows how cooperative agreements have historically delivered those services, reviews the relevant record facts (especially termination of agreements and the government’s representations), and explains the tailored relief the panel ordered while the appeal proceeds.

## Statutory mandates identified by the panel

The panel anchored its APA concern in three statutory provisions within 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that, taken together, create substantive duties to provide certain reception and placement services for refugees once they are admitted to the United States. First, § 1522(a)(1)(A) directs that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement “shall,” to the extent of available appropriations, make available core resources—employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance structured not to discourage self‑sufficiency, and gender equality protections. The panel treated the use of “shall” in § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing a mandatory obligation on the executive when appropriations are available.

Second, § 1522(d)(2)(B) requires that, during an interim period while an unaccompanied minor refugee is in the United States or in transit and before placement, the Director “shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary,” and is “authorized to make necessary decisions” for the child’s immediate care. The panel read this language as creating at least a conditional mandatory duty (triggered when assumption of responsibility is “necessary”) that cannot be ignored if the condition is present.

Third, § 1522(e)(7)(A) commands that the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects” providing interim support, medical services, and case management for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months “as needed.” The panel viewed this as another statutory command to furnish certain services for recently admitted refugees, subject to the “as needed” qualification but nonetheless mandatory when the need exists.

Taken together, these provisions supply the statutory substantive baseline that underlies the plaintiffs’ APA claim: that the executive may not abandon statutorily required reception and placement functions for refugees already admitted to the United States, particularly where appropriations are available and needs exist.

## Implementing regulations: 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.

The statute’s operational content is reinforced by the implementing regulations in 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which describe the federally funded refugee services that state and national resettlement agencies have historically provided. Those regulations define program components—case management, cash and medical assistance coordination, employment and language training, and other reception and placement functions—that map onto the statutory duties identified in § 1522. The panel relied on that regulatory framework to show that Congress did not write hollow directives: the statute and regulations together describe the types of services the executive has been expected to furnish to facilitate refugees’ integration and care after admission.

## Appropriations context: availability of funds in FY2025

Section 1522 repeatedly conditions duties on the availability of appropriations (“to the extent of available appropriations”), so the availability of funds is a crucial legal predicate. The panel pointed to the FY2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance: Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance (in the cited statutes), of which $800 million was later rescinded. The panel treated those enacted appropriations and the partial rescission as relevant record facts supporting the conclusion that funds existed at the time the government terminated cooperative agreements—i.e., that the “to the extent of available appropriations” condition was satisfied, so the statutory “shall” language could be operative. The panel thus considered appropriations context in assessing whether the government could lawfully stop providing services without running afoul of § 1522.

## Delivery mechanism: cooperative agreements under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

Section 1522 explicitly contemplates that reception and placement services may be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” Historically, the federal government has relied on cooperative agreements with national and local voluntary resettlement agencies to carry out the § 1522 duties. The panel emphasized this established delivery mechanism because it bears on what it is reasonable to order in a preliminary remedy: if cooperative agreements have been the usual vehicle for delivering federally‑mandated services, and if the government abruptly terminates those agreements while funds remain available, a court may require temporary reinstatement of those agreements to preserve statutorily required care for already‑admitted refugees during the pendency of judicial review.

## Record facts: termination, the RFP, and uncertainty at argument

The record facts drove the panel’s practical concern. On February 26, 2025—after the district court’s initial preliminary injunction—the State Department terminated every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States and nearly all overseas processing cooperative agreements. On March 10, 2025 the government told the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency” to provide those services. But at oral argument before the panel the government could not say whether any substantive progress had been made toward securing replacement agencies. The panel viewed that sequence—termination of the existing agreements, a promise to solicit replacements, and uncertainty about whether replacements were near— as strengthening the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the government’s termination left refugees admitted to the United States without the statutorily required services, at least in the short term.

In short, the panel did not need to resolve the ultimate legal question on the merits at this stage; it concluded that the factual record showed a credible risk that, absent interim relief, the executive branch would not be meeting the statutory duties the plaintiffs invoked.

## Why the panel concluded the government is not likely to prevail on the APA challenge

Under the APA, a plaintiff can prevail on a claim that an agency action was “contrary to law” if the action is inconsistent with a statutory mandate. The panel identified that risk here based on three linked points: (1) § 1522 contains categorical “shall” commands to make certain services available to refugees, (2) appropriations for refugee assistance existed in FY2025 (so the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification did not foreclose relief), and (3) the government had terminated the practical means of delivering those services and could not credibly show replacement capacity. Given those considerations, the panel determined that the government was not likely to succeed on an APA defense that its terminations and defunding were lawful because § 1522 either did not impose a duty or because appropriations were unavailable. The panel therefore treated the plaintiffs’ APA claim as sufficiently compelling on the merits to weigh against a blanket stay of the district court’s injunctions.

Importantly, the panel separated this APA result from its view on the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions. It found the government likely to prevail on the § 1182(f) issue (and therefore stayed the district court’s injunction to the extent it blocked a presidential suspension of admissions), but concluded the government was unlikely to prevail on plaintiffs’ statutory/APA challenge concerning services owed to refugees already admitted.

## Targeted relief: reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees

As a result of the foregoing, the panel issued narrowly tailored interim relief while the appeal proceeds: it stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions in their entirety except for one carveout. The government was directed to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel described those services as “the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements,” which ties the required relief to the statutory duties and the implementing regulations (rather than imposing novel obligations).

Two aspects of this remedy are important to keep in mind. First, the relief is targeted to refugees already admitted — the panel left intact the government’s ability, subject to other injunction limits, to pause future admissions under the president’s § 1182(f) authority. Second, the remedy is pragmatic and limited: it seeks to restore the preexisting operational relationships (cooperative agreements) the government used to fulfill § 1522 duties while the appellate process runs its course, rather than prescribing a new permanent structure or adjudicating in full whether § 1522 creates an unfetterable duty in all circumstances.

## Takeaway for students

At the preliminary‑stage, courts frequently must balance competing legal questions and practical consequences. Here, the panel applied that approach: it accepted the government’s strong arguments (and precedent) about the President’s broad § 1182(f) authority but at the same time recognized a separate statutory channel—§ 1522 together with its regulations and the appropriation record—through which Congress required the executive to provide certain services to refugees already admitted. The combination of statutory “shall” language, an implementing regulatory scheme, available appropriations, an established delivery mechanism (cooperative agreements), and the government’s record of terminating those agreements without demonstrable replacements persuaded the panel that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim concerning post‑admission services. That determination justified a limited, practical judicial remedy: temporary reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to carry out the usual reception and placement services for admitted refugees while the appeal proceeds. The panel signaled it will explain the full merits reasoning in an opinion to follow.

# Operative Order: What Is Stayed and What Must Proceed

This chapter explains precisely what the Ninth Circuit did in its interim order: which prior appellate stays it vacated, which district-court injunctions it put on hold, and the narrow exception the court left in place requiring the federal government to restore certain resettlement services for refugees already in the United States. It also notes the disposition of the government’s emergency motion and the procedural posture going forward.

## Vacatur of prior appellate stays

The panel began by withdrawing the temporary stays it had earlier entered. Two categories of prior appellate action were vacated: an administrative stay the court issued on July 18, 2025, and an earlier stay issued March 25, 2025 (as later amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025). Vacatur of those orders means they no longer have any operative effect; the new interim order replaces them. For a student, the key point is that vacatur here does not resolve the merits of the underlying dispute — it simply removes those earlier temporary stays from the record and substitutes a fresh stay tailored to the court’s present ruling.

## Global stay of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions (with one exception)

The core operative move in the order is a stay pending appeal of the district court’s two written preliminary injunctions dated February 28 and March 24, 2025. Those district-court injunctions had (1) prohibited implementation of substantial portions of President Trump’s Executive Order 14163 suspending refugee admissions and had (2) required the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements that had been terminated to provide reception and placement services to refugees. The Ninth Circuit’s order now stays those district-court injunctions “in their entirety,” meaning that, as a general matter, the district court’s work is paused while the appeal proceeds and the government may not be constrained by those injunctions during the appeal.

It is important to read that sweeping language with the next section in mind: the stay is comprehensive except for a deliberately narrow carveout. The panel’s reasoning for granting the stay rests on the familiar four-factor framework for stays pending appeal from Nken v. Holder: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, balance of equities, and public interest — with the first two being most critical. The panel concluded (as previewed in the order) that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, and that the equities and public-interest factors favor the government on that question. That conclusion is why the injunctions that would have halted the suspension of admissions are stayed.

## Carveout and mandate: reinstating cooperative agreements to provide § 1522 reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted

Although the district-court injunctions are stayed generally, the Ninth Circuit left intact one narrow but concrete obligation: the government must reinstate those cooperative agreements that are necessary to provide the reception and placement services required by statute to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The order directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and it specifies that such services “shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.”

Two features of that carveout merit careful attention.

First, who is covered. The court’s order applies only to refugees who have already been admitted into the United States. It does not require the government to resume overseas processing or to admit refugees who remain outside the United States; it does not resurrect any obligation to process conditionally approved applicants abroad. The difference between “admitted” refugees and those eligible or conditionally approved but still outside the country is important: the court confined the reinstatement mandate to persons already present in the United States and to services that assist their domestic resettlement.

Second, what services are covered. The panel tied the injunction’s carveout to the statutory scheme in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement and assistance and authorizes (and in some subsections instructs) certain reception-and-placement services. The order does not catalogue every item but expressly requires the government to restore the “usual and customary services” under the prior cooperative agreements. In the district-court briefing and in the appellate opinion the panel flagged illustrative statutory provisions — such as employment training and placement, English-language instruction, cash and medical assistance and case management for recently resettled refugees, and specific authority concerning unaccompanied refugee children — as the kinds of programs § 1522 contemplates. Practically, therefore, the government must reestablish the contractual relationships (cooperative agreements) needed to deliver the core reception-and-placement services that historically were provided under those agreements for refugees who are already in the United States.

The order’s wording also limits the scope in a second way: the government must reinstate only the cooperative agreements that are “necessary to provide” the § 1522 services. That language signals that the court expects the government to restore the functionality needed to deliver statutory services, rather than to reopen every programmatic element of the prior agreements regardless of need. The panel’s direction responds to the record evidence that the State Department had terminated cooperative agreements and that, at oral argument, the government could not identify whether a replacement network of providers was in place.

Finally, the panel’s carveout reflects a legal split in the court. The majority concluded that plaintiffs are likely to succeed on at least one of their APA-based challenges tied to § 1522 (hence the mandate to provide services), whereas it concluded the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the executive’s authority under § 1182(f) to suspend admissions (hence the stay of the broader injunctions). Judge Lee filed a partial concurrence and partial dissent taking a different view of § 1522’s scope (reading it as permissive rather than mandatory), but the operative order is the majority’s.

## Emergency motion (July 17) denied as moot

The government had filed an emergency motion on July 17, 2025, seeking relief concerning enforcement or clarification of the existing stay regime. Because the panel’s new order now supersedes the prior administrative stay and replaces the earlier March 25 stay with the present stay-and-carveout order, the court disposed of that emergency motion as moot. In other words, the panel addressed the underlying issues by issuing this interim order, making the separate emergency motion unnecessary.

## Case submitted after expedited argument; full merits opinion to follow

Procedurally, the Ninth Circuit heard expedited oral argument and, by this order, submitted the case for decision. The panel expressly stated that a full written opinion on the merits will issue in due course. That means two things for readers. First, this stay order is interim and limited to preserving the parties’ positions while the appeal proceeds; it does not finally resolve the substantive statutory and administrative-law questions. Second, the order telegraphs the court’s preliminary conclusions on two main legal points: (1) substantial deference accompanies the President’s invocation of § 1182(f) and the court is inclined to uphold the suspension of admissions; and (2) the court perceives stronger plaintiffs’ claims under the APA tied to § 1522’s statutorily defined reception-and-placement duties, which is why the court compelled the government to restore services for admitted refugees. The forthcoming opinion will develop the full legal analysis that undergirds those tentative conclusions.

Conclusion

In short, the Ninth Circuit vacated its earlier temporary stays and issued a new interim stay that suspends the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions pending appeal, except for a narrow, concrete requirement: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services required by § 1522 for refugees already admitted to the United States, restoring the usual and customary services previously provided under those agreements. The emergency motion is moot, the case has been submitted after expedited argument, and a full merits opinion will follow.

# Separate Opinion: The § 1522 Dispute and Its Implications

## Agreement on the entry-suspension issue (§ 1182(f))
Judge Lee begins from common ground with the other members of the panel: the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend classes of admissions is entitled to substantial deference. Both the majority and Judge Lee accept that, under the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii and the text of § 1182(f), the President may suspend refugee admissions and that courts should not perform a searching review of the President’s determinations about national interests. That concession narrows the dispute on appeal: Lee does not challenge the stay of the district court’s injunction ordering an end to the admissions suspension; rather, his disagreement is focused entirely on whether the federal government has a statutory duty, separate from the admission decision, to provide domestic reception-and-placement services to refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

## Core disagreement: § 1522 as permissive authorization, not a mandate
At the heart of Judge Lee’s partial dissent is a textualist argument about statutory form and signal words. He emphasizes that § 1522’s heading—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—is itself a textual clue. “Authorization,” as Lee quotes Black’s Law Dictionary, connotes permission, not compulsion. That framing, he argues, is reinforced throughout the substantive subsections: subsections (b) through (e), which set out particular resettlement programs and services, repeatedly state that the Director or Secretary “is authorized” to take certain actions. For Lee, the prevalence of permissive phrasing—“is authorized”—shows Congress intended to grant the executive branch discretion to design and fund refugee-resettlement programs, not to command that those programs must be operated in all circumstances.

## Reading § 1522(a): “shall” is conditional on choosing to fund
The majority rests part of its conclusion on § 1522(a), which uses the mandatory verb “shall” to describe certain duties of the Director “in providing assistance under this section.” Lee reads that provision differently: he treats § 1522(a)’s “shall” as a clause of conditions and considerations that governs how programs must operate if the government elects to provide assistance under the discretionary authorities of subsections (b)–(e). In his view, the statutory sequence matters: the mandatory language is prefaced by “In providing assistance under this section,” which ties the “shall” obligations to the decision to provide assistance (i.e., to exercise the discretionary authorities later set out). Put simply, for Lee the “shall” creates required operating rules for funded programs, not an independent, freestanding spending mandate that obligates the executive branch to run programs or maintain contractual mechanisms irrespective of its policy choices or appropriations constraints. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” further reinforces, to his reading, that Congress contemplated executive discretion tied to funding availability.

## Qualified subsections: (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) preserve discretion
Judge Lee addresses two specific provisions the majority highlights—§ 1522(d)(2)(B), concerning temporary legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), concerning cash and medical assistance projects. He reads both as qualified obligations that leave significant room for executive judgment. Subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume certain responsibilities “if necessary” and authorizes the Director to make “necessary” decisions—language that, Lee argues, is explicitly contingent and deferential to executive assessment of necessity. Subsection (e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to “develop and implement” projects “as needed,” again signaling that the duty to act pertains to identified needs and is governed by programmatic discretion. For Lee, these qualifiers undercut the majority’s view that § 1522 imposes an enforceable duty to maintain contractual reception-and-placement mechanisms at all times.

## Remedial consequence: no judicially compelled reinstatement of cooperative agreements
Because Lee reads § 1522 as authorizing programs rather than mandating them, he reaches a straightforward remedial conclusion: the district court erred in ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements and enjoining the government from suspending the statutory program. On Lee’s view, the court lacks the statutory predicate to compel execution or funding of resettlement contracts when the executive has chosen to suspend or reprioritize those programs. Practically, this means he would have granted a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal—allowing the government to keep cooperative agreements terminated and to withhold reception-and-placement funding during the appeal—whereas the majority preserves a limited carveout that obligates the government to reinstate agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services to refugees already admitted.

## What to watch for in the forthcoming opinion
Judge Lee flags several issues that the full opinion will need to resolve, and these are the points that will shape the precedential significance of the case:

- Definitive construction of § 1522. Will the court accept Lee’s textualist account that § 1522 is predominantly permissive, or will the majority adopt a reading that treats some or all of § 1522 as imposing enforceable duties even when the executive has suspended program operations? How the panel resolves the interplay between the section title, repeated “is authorized” language, the “shall” language in subsection (a), and the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification will control the overall statutory posture.

- APA reviewability and the “contrary to law” question. If § 1522 is permissive, the termination of cooperative agreements is likely committed to agency discretion and therefore not “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” If § 1522 is mandatory, termination could be a final agency action “contrary to law” under the Administrative Procedure Act. The panel’s resolution will determine whether plaintiffs can succeed on APA claims.

- Scope of judicial authority to compel contractual mechanisms. Even if the court finds some statutory duty, it must decide whether and how a court may fashion equitable relief to compel the government to enter or reinstate contracts and cooperative agreements with private partners in order to fulfill that duty. Lee’s concurrence suggests skepticism that courts should compel the mechanics of program delivery where Congress did not plainly require them.

- Harmonization of entry power and post-admission obligations. The case implicates a structural question: how does the President’s broad authority to restrict entry under § 1182(f) sit alongside statutory obligations relating to refugees after admission? If the executive can suspend admissions but Congress has required certain post-admission services, courts may face competing commands. Lee’s position treats the post-admission statute as granting discretion that avoids a direct collision; the forthcoming opinion will need to decide whether a genuine conflict exists and, if so, how to reconcile or prioritize the statutes and the branch prerogatives they reflect.

Conclusion
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent advances a tight, text-focused argument that § 1522 authorizes—rather than compels—domestic refugee resettlement programs. That interpretive stance leads him to oppose the district court’s remedy that would have reinstated cooperative agreements and required program operations during the appeal. The forthcoming merits opinion will have to choose between Lee’s permissive construction and the majority’s view that certain statutory duties are enforceable, and that choice will determine both the parties’ fate on the APA claims and the scope of judicially enforceable obligations for refugee resettlement programs.
