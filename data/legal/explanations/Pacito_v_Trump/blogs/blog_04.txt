Title: Blog about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

# Appropriations as Constraint: The Ninth Circuit’s Pacito stay and what it means for defunding USRAP, §1522, and the APA

## Why Pacito matters
The Ninth Circuit’s emergency stay in Pacito v. Trump, issued September 12, 2025, is more than an interlocutory skirmish over refugee policy. The panel split the difference: it accepted the Executive’s broad authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (following Trump v. Hawaii), but it balked at permitting the Administration to abandon the domestic resettlement apparatus for refugees already admitted to the United States. Practically, the court’s interim directive — requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” — marks a judicial willingness to enforce statutory resettlement duties even while deferring to the President on border-entry decisions. Doctrinally, the decision raises a key question: when a statute uses both mandatory phrasing (“shall”) and qualifying language (“to the extent of available appropriations”), what limits do appropriations principles place on executive flexibility to halt programs? And what Administrative Procedure Act (APA) problems arise when the government abruptly terminates cooperative agreements without a clear plan to honor statutory obligations?

## What the Ninth Circuit ordered and why
The panel granted a stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal except for one substantive carve-out: the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted. The court reasoned that, while the President likely has authority under §1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, the government was unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge grounded in 8 U.S.C. §1522. Section 1522, the court emphasized, contains specific provisions (and implementing regulations) that require the federal government, through the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) and related mechanisms, to make various services available to refugees — employment assistance, English-language training, cash and medical programs, and special responsibilities for unaccompanied children. Faced with the State Department’s February 2025 termination of cooperative agreements that historically delivered these services, the court concluded there was a real risk that the Executive had not lawfully complied with §1522’s statutory duties. Against that backdrop, the court ordered reinstatement of agreements necessary to meet §1522 obligations for refugees who are already in the United States.

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part, taking the opposite view on §1522’s character: he reads the statute (and its title, “Authorization for programs…”) as permissive, not mandatory, arguing the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification and repeated use of “authorized” signal discretion. The panel’s majority, however, treated at least some of §1522’s provisions as creating enforceable obligations when appropriations exist — an interpretive posture the stay reflects.

## How appropriations language and statutory “shall” interact
At the heart of the dispute is a familiar interpretive tension: statutory prescriptions framed as duties (often using “shall”) that are simultaneously qualified by “to the extent of available appropriations.” That qualification ordinarily means that funding constraints limit a statutory program — Congress must appropriate money before an agency may spend it. But it does not automatically render every “shall” a nullity.

Two points flow from the Ninth Circuit’s interim approach. First, when Congress has actually appropriated funds for the statutory program, a “to the extent of available appropriations” clause will not necessarily give the Executive free rein to zero out services unilaterally through an internal policy decision or an unrelated executive order. The opinion notes Congress’s FY2025 appropriation for migration and refugee assistance (and an $800 million rescission), suggesting the court was attentive to whether appropriated resources remained available to satisfy §1522 responsibilities. If Congress has provided funds specifically for the relevant programs in a fiscal year and those funds have not been lawfully rescinded or reprogrammed, an agency abandon­ment of statutory duties may be reviewable and enjoinable.

Second, the qualification matters for scope. The government’s ability to change program structure or prioritize spending may be broader when appropriations are limited or have been lawfully rescinded. But where appropriated funds remain and the statute lays out discrete responsibilities (especially for vulnerable populations, such as unaccompanied children), courts are more likely to treat certain statutory commands as operational constraints that the Executive must follow, and to require a reasoned explanation under the APA for any abrupt termination of services.

In short: “to the extent of available appropriations” is a real limit on enforcement only insofar as appropriations are in fact unavailable; where appropriations exist, the “shall” language can be operationally binding.

## APA risks in terminating cooperative agreements
The court’s order rests on an APA theory: the Termination of longstanding cooperative agreements that implement §1522 services may be arbitrary and capricious if the Executive fails to account for statutory obligations and to provide a reasoned plan for continuity of services for refugees already admitted. Several legal levers are relevant:

- Final agency action. The termination of a cooperative agreement that affects the legal rights and duties of recipients and beneficiaries can constitute final agency action subject to judicial review under the APA. Plaintiffs can and did press that theory here.

- Arbitrary-and-capricious review. When an agency changes course on an established policy or program that serves statutory mandates, it must supply a reasoned explanation showing that it considered the relevant statutory scheme and the reliance interests at stake. A blanket termination that ignores demonstrable statutory duties (e.g., legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors; cash and medical assistance programs required under §1522(e)) risks being vacated as arbitrary or procedurally deficient.

- Procedural obligations. Some cooperative agreements may be contract-like; others implicate grant or regulation regimes that require notice, opportunity to respond, or adherence to statutory procedures before termination. Abrupt termination that leaves beneficiaries without statutorily contemplated services raises procedural APA questions as well.

The Ninth Circuit’s direction to reinstate “necessary” cooperative agreements reflects concern that the government did not adequately account for these APA obligations before slashing funding or canceling agreements.

## The interagency wrinkle: HHS duties versus State’s cooperative agreements
Pacito also highlights a messy bureaucratic mismatch. Section 1522 vests the Director of ORR (within HHS) with responsibilities for many resettlement functions. Operationally, however, the State Department has historically managed cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies to handle reception-and-placement work on the ground. When State — perhaps acting under a different executive directive — terminates those agreements, it may jeopardize HHS’s ability to meet statutory obligations.

The Ninth Circuit’s order implicitly recognizes that an interagency split cannot be used to sidestep statutory duties. If HHS has a statutory obligation under §1522 and the only practical delivery mechanism is State-managed cooperative agreements, the Executive branch must ensure continuity — whether by keeping agreements in place, transferring funds and responsibility formally to HHS, or otherwise establishing a lawful alternative. Absent a clear restructuring accompanied by reasoned administrative action, courts are likely to insist on the status quo ante for services already in motion.

## Doctrinal implications and likely downstream effects
Pacito’s interim posture portends several doctrinal and practical consequences:

- Splitting admissions from services. Courts may be inclined to leave the Executive broad authority over who enters the country while policing statutory obligations owed to persons already admitted. That bifurcation reduces the practical bite of an admissions moratorium in protecting resident beneficiaries.

- Appropriations are not an escape hatch by default. Executive attempts to rely on internal policy pronouncements to nullify congressionally created programmatic duties — absent a clear congressional rescission or reprogramming of funds — face uphill APA review. Agencies must document not only policy justifications but also how appropriations realities shape their actions.

- Interagency coordination will matter more. Agencies that depend on another department’s contracting machinery to satisfy statutory duties will need clear delegation, funding transfers, or joint plans before one agency unilaterally cancels contracts. Failure to coordinate invites litigation and provisional relief.

- Litigation strategy. Plaintiffs challenging sudden program terminations will press (and courts will scrutinize) whether statutory duties remain operable given appropriations. Plaintiffs may obtain relief limited to individuals already admitted or covered by pending cooperative agreements even if courts defer to the Executive on future admissions.

- Congress’s leverage. If the Executive wishes to make the changes permanent, it can — and Congress can — do so more cleanly through appropriations and statutory amendment rather than through executive orders that fray statutory implementation and invite APA challenges.

## Bottom line for agencies and litigants
For agencies: when you tinker with programs that implement statutory duties, don’t treat appropriations language as a one-way ticket to instant suspension. If appropriations exist, you need a reasoned administrative record explaining how statutory obligations will be met (or lawfully ceased), and you must coordinate across agencies when program delivery mechanisms live in another department.

For litigants: Pacito illustrates a promising pathway. Even if courts defer to the Executive on border-entry policy under §1182(f), they will enforce statutory service obligations under §1522 where funds and longstanding reliance interests exist. Emergency relief to restore services for already-admitted refugees remains a live and effective remedy.

The Ninth Circuit’s forthcoming opinion on the merits will be the real test of the boundaries the stay sketches. But the stay itself already signals that appropriations phrasing and statutory “shall” obligations are not mere semantic theater: courts will parse them to determine whether the Executive has lawfully suspended programs or simply walked away from congressionally structured humanitarian duties.
