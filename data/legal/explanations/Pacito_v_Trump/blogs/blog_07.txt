# From Travel Bans to Program Halts: The Trajectory of Judicial Review in National Security Immigration

## Why Pacito matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim stay in Pacito v. Trump is the latest—and instructive—chapter in a decade-long realignment of how federal courts approach presidential authority over immigration. On one hand, Pacito reaffirms the powerful presumption of deference to the President’s determinations about who may enter the country, echoing the Supreme Court’s approach in Trump v. Hawaii. On the other, it signals that such deference has limits: courts remain willing to police statutory obligations governing what the government must do inside the United States, especially where domestic programs, benefits, and contracts are implicated and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) provides a vehicle for challenge.

That combination—maximal latitude at the border coupled with robust judicial enforcement of statutory constraints on domestic implementation—is now a pattern. Pacito illustrates the consequences: an executive order halting refugee admissions can be shielded by sweeping deference, while contemporaneous programmatic steps—defunding resettlement service providers and terminating cooperative agreements—remain vulnerable to judicial intervention. The case therefore offers a roadmap for both government litigation strategy and for advocates seeking to hold agencies accountable when entry restrictions are matched by domestic programmatic consequences.

## What the Ninth Circuit did (and did not) say

The panel issued a stay of the district court’s injunctions against President Trump’s Executive Order No. 14163 (suspending refugee admissions) and the State Department’s defunding actions, pending appeal. In doing so the court applied the familiar four-factor Nken test for a stay and placed particular emphasis on the two “most critical” factors: likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm.

On the merits of the entry suspension under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the Ninth Circuit was unequivocal: the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the statute’s sole textual prerequisite—the President’s finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Quoting Trump v. Hawaii, the panel stressed that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power” to impose entry restrictions. That language drove the court’s conclusion that the government is likely to prevail on challenges to the EO’s suspension of admissions.

But the panel also identified a statutory line the government had trouble crossing. Relying on 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related regulations, the court found that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on APA claims challenging the termination of cooperative agreements and defunding of reception-and-placement services. The opinion framed § 1522 as creating an obligation—subject to appropriations—that requires the Office of Refugee Resettlement (and the responsible Secretaries) to make certain services available to refugees after admission. Given the government’s apparent uncertainty about replacing contract partners and ensuring service delivery, the court directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to furnish “usual and customary” reception-and-placement services to refugees who had already been admitted.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent crystallizes the tension animating the case. He agreed with the panel’s deference to presidential authority on admissions but argued that § 1522 is a permissive authorization, not a mandate. For Lee, the statute authorizes—but does not compel—the executive to operate refugee resettlement programs, and many of § 1522’s provisions read as conditional on appropriations and executive choice. His view would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full.

## Why this reasoning matters doctrinally

Pacito does three doctrinal things worth noting.

First, it continues the post-Hawaii trend of generous judicial deference to pure entry restrictions framed as national-security determinations under § 1182(f). Courts confronted with facial or as-applied challenges to high-level presidential proclamations barring admission will likely find it difficult to prevail if the government can point to an express presidential finding and national-security framing.

Second, Pacito reaffirms the viability of the APA and statutory-enforcement claims as a counterbalance to that deference. Even where the government can lawfully suspend entry, it cannot necessarily dismantle the domestic infrastructure Congress created for admitted refugees without confronting statutory constraints. The panel’s reading of § 1522 as imposing enforceable duties (at least to the extent of appropriations) provides a blueprint for litigants to convert programmatic harms into judicially cognizable claims.

Third, the opinion sharpens the importance of statutory drafting and appropriations language. The government’s obligations under § 1522 are qualified—“to the extent of available appropriations”—but the panel treated those qualifications as important, not dispositive: Congress’s allocation of migration and refugee funds, together with statutory reporting and programmatic design provisions, can create enforceable standards that courts will police if the executive takes sudden programmatic action inconsistent with those statutes.

## Downstream effects and likely future litigation targets

Pacito points to a likely division of labor in future immigration litigation: entry policy will continue to be a domain of expansive executive discretion; domestic program implementation will remain fair game for APA and statutory claims.

What will litigants challenge next?

- Reception-and-placement and benefits administration. Lawsuits will continue to target terminations of cooperative agreements, halts in grants, and abrupt changes in cash, medical, and employment services for newly admitted refugees. These are the fact-patterns that translate easily into APA claims and statutory-entitlement arguments.

- Grantmaking, procurement, and cooperative agreements. Agencies that abruptly cancel agreements without statutory or regulatory process risk suits alleging arbitrary-and-capricious or procedurally defective action. The Pacito injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements exemplifies this vulnerability.

- Unaccompanied minors and custodial duties. Statutory formulations that impose caretaker duties (even phrased as discretionary) will attract litigation if agencies decline to act and harm or neglect is plausibly alleged.

- Reporting and oversight provisions. Statutory accountability measures—reporting requirements, inspection regimes, and Congressional earmarks—can supply plaintiffs with causes of action or at least strong support in equitable relief efforts.

Where litigation is less likely to succeed: pure challenges to admission suspensions framed as national-security or foreign-affairs determinations. Unless plaintiffs can show that the proclamation or EO exceeded a clear textual limit in § 1182(f) or was based on an insincere or pretextual “finding,” courts will be reluctant to substitute their judgment.

## Final observations

Pacito cements a predictable pattern: the judiciary will bow out of second-guessing presidential findings about who gets in, but it will not abdicate when the executive’s actions touch the domestic statutory architecture Congress has erected. That division of labor channels contestation toward programmatic and administrative corners of immigration law—where statutory text, appropriations, cooperative agreements, and the APA remain powerful tools for accountability.

For practitioners and advocates, the lesson is strategic. Where the avenue of challenging admissions is narrow, litigation energy will remain productively spent on protecting service delivery, benefits, and contract-backed supports for those already within the United States—or already approved for admission. For agencies, Pacito is a warning: national-security deference does not immunize programmatic retrenchment from judicial scrutiny.
