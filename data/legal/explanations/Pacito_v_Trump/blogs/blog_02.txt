Title: Blog about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

# Presidential Orders, Agency Action, and the APA: How Pacito Channels Review Through Implementing Agencies

Why this matters: Pacito is an important, if interim, lesson in how courts can police presidential policymaking by targeting the implementing bureaucracy rather than the President himself. The Ninth Circuit’s expedited stay opinion signals two doctrinal moves that will shape future challenges to sweeping executive immigration or foreign-affairs directives. First, the panel assumes that broad presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is largely insulated from a searching merits review—echoing Trump v. Hawaii—but it carves out a conventional path for APA litigation by treating the agencies’ dismantling of the resettlement apparatus as reviewable agency action under the APA. Second, the panel takes the unusual remedial step of ordering agencies to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutorily mandated reception-and-placement services. Both choices reflect a pragmatic, separation-of-powers approach: the court will not second-guess the President’s immigration judgment but will enforce statutory duties that Congress placed on agencies.

What the panel did (and didn’t) decide: The court’s stay opinion—decided on an expedited record and with a fuller opinion promised later—stays the district court’s injunctions against the suspension of refugee admissions, finding that the government is likely to prevail on that point under § 1182(f). At the same time the panel concluded that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA theory: that the agencies violated statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 by terminating cooperative agreements that provided reception-and-placement services. Because the agencies appear uncertain about their plans to replace the terminated contractors, the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services for refugees already admitted to the United States. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent underscores the statutory interpretive tension: she reads § 1522 as largely authorizing, not mandating, agency programs and would have stayed the injunctions in full.

How the panel channels review toward agencies: Several doctrinal lines converge in the court’s ruling. The panel accepts the starting premise from Trump v. Hawaii that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and supplies broad, largely nonjusticiable space for national-entry decisions. Faced with that frontal barrier to APA review of a presidential admission moratorium, the court shifts the fight to agency implementation: even if the President can lawfully suspend admissions, agencies implementing refugee resettlement programs still must operate within the bounds of statutes that Congress enacted for those programs. That is a familiar judicial strategy—declining to treat the President as an “agency” under the APA while recognizing that subordinate agencies’ actions (or inactions) remain subject to statutory and APA review.

Two threshold APA questions follow from that move, and the panel’s interim ruling presumes favorable answers to each for the plaintiffs: (1) was the termination of cooperative agreements “final agency action” subject to APA review, and (2) if so, was that action “committed to agency discretion by law” under Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), or instead presumptively reviewable because the statute supplies judicially manageable standards?

Final agency action: The termination of cooperative agreements looks very much like final agency action. It is a concrete, discrete decision with present legal effects: withdrawal of funding and termination of the arrangements that historically delivered services to admitted refugees. The Ninth Circuit’s remedy presumes finality—if the terminations were merely ephemeral exercise of internal judgment or interlocutory planning, a court could not properly enjoin or compel their reversal. The court’s reliance on the statutory framework—§ 1522’s affirmative provisions, the grant/cooperative-agreement mechanism in § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), and the appropriation and reporting structure—supports the view that the termination of agreements is a consummation of agency decisionmaking that produces rights and obligations (for contractors, refugee recipients, and the agencies obliged by Congress to provide services).

Heckler v. Chaney and the discretion barrier: Heckler held that an agency’s decision not to enforce is presumptively nonreviewable if it is committed to agency discretion by law, and that doctrine typically protects prosecutorial-style declinations and resource-based judgments. But Heckler is not a universal shield. Where Congress has supplied substantive directives and “shall”-type duties, courts can review agency action (or inaction) to ensure statutory compliance. The Pacito panel implicitly treats § 1522 as supplying such duties—at least for some services and populations—so Heckler’s discretion bar does not foreclose review.

Whether Heckler applies will turn on statutory text and structure. Sections of § 1522 use permissive language (“is authorized”), but the statute also contains “shall” provisions and specific programmatic obligations (e.g., duties for the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to make resources available, requirements for care of unaccompanied minors “if necessary,” and for the Secretary to develop interim projects for cash and medical assistance “as needed”). The panel reads this statutory texture as supplying at least some non- discretionary obligations that courts can enforce. Judge Lee’s partial dissent illustrates the opposing view: she emphasizes § 1522’s title (“Authorization for programs...”) and the many “authorized” clauses to argue that the statute primarily grants discretion to craft programs, so Heckler would most likely bar review.

Are the statutory standards judicially manageable? This is the sticking doctrinal question. Even if the termination of agreements is final agency action and not shielded by Heckler, courts may still refuse to adjudicate if the statute lacks manageable legal standards. The panel suggests that § 1522 contains judicially manageable hooks—e.g., “shall make available sufficient resources,” duties “to the extent of available appropriations,” the specific programmatic descriptions for employment, language training, cash and medical support, and the fiscal-reporting obligation in § 1523. Together those elements arguably permit judicial comparison between what Congress required and what agencies have done.

On the other hand, manageability objections are plausible. “To the extent of available appropriations” injects a funding contingency; Congress appropriated sizable migration funds for FY 2025 but also enacted rescissions, and appropriations politics is a classic area of agency discretion and political judgment. Moreover, assessing whether “sufficient resources” exist, or what interim projects are “needed,” invites line-drawing and policy balancing—tasks some judges will be reluctant to undertake. The panel’s preliminary holding navigates these tensions by limiting its conclusion: it says only that the government is not likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ APA claim—enough, under Nken, to warrant a stay exception and an interim directive—leaving the full merits analysis for the forthcoming opinion.

The remedial choice: reinstating cooperative agreements and the § 706 frameworks

The panel didn’t merely declare that agencies likely violated the APA; it ordered a concrete remedial step: reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and- placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. That remedy sits at the intersection of two distinct § 5 U.S.C. § 706 provisions.

First, § 706(1) authorizes the court to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” If the agency had a nondiscretionary duty to fund or arrange reception-and-placement services for admitted refugees, the court can order the agency to act (e.g., to award cooperative agreements, make payments, or otherwise perform). Compel relief is particularly apt where an agency has declined to do something the statute required—“here, reinstate agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.”

Second, § 706(2) permits courts to “hold unlawful and set aside” agency actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” If the terminations themselves were final agency actions, the court could set them aside on APA grounds. Practically speaking, compulsion under § 706(1) and vacatur under § 706(2) often overlap: vacating an unlawful termination and ordering agencies to take steps to restore the status quo both achieve the same ends. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order reads like a blend: it requires reinstatement (compel/vacatur feel) while acknowledging the larger executive prerogatives the government claims.

Is reinstatement unusual? Yes and no. Courts have on multiple occasions vacated unlawful agency deregulatory moves and required agencies to restore prior programs pending lawful rulemaking or statutory compliance. But ordering agencies to reinstate cooperative agreements raises special practical and separation-of-powers questions. Cooperative agreements involve private parties who may have been released from obligations, may have spent or lost funds, and may be subject to complex procurement and performance constraints. Courts commanding agencies to re-enter specific bilateral arrangements inevitably micromanage administrative contracting, which courts normally avoid unless the statutory duty is clear and the agency’s refusal is unlawful. The Ninth Circuit’s directive is therefore a bold, targeted remedy: it aims to protect statutory beneficiaries (admitted refugees) and the integrity of congressionally authorized programs without upending the President’s broader suspension of admissions.

Potential downstream effects and practical friction points

Pacito’s doctrinal posture—insulating the President’s moratorium while enforcing agency duties—offers a blueprint for future litigation over executive orders with wide sweep. Plaintiffs will likely bring dual claims: (1) direct attacks on the presidential directive (where success will be difficult post-Hawaii), and (2) APA challenges to agency implementation decisions that have concrete, localized effects on beneficiaries. Courts sympathetic to enforcing statutory directives will, as here, treat the second path as the more workable route to relief.

But this route is not frictionless. Three practical issues will recur:

1) The funding caveat. Congress’s repeated use of “to the extent of available appropriations” complicates judicial enforcement. Where appropriations are present but politically reallocated (e.g., rescissions), courts must decide how much deference to accord agency resource allocations and whether appropriations politics create a nonjusticiable barrier.

2) Manageability and remedial scope. If courts are willing to police agencies’ programmatic choices, they must still avoid crafting detailed programmatic blueprints. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order tries to thread that needle by limiting relief to providing “the usual and customary services” under prior agreements for refugees already admitted. But clarifying the contours of “usual and customary” services will likely generate further fact-intensive litigation and implementation disputes.

3) Separation-of-powers optics. The court’s selective intervention—enjoining agency rollbacks while leaving the President’s moratorium intact—may be criticized by some as constitutionally awkward: it allows the executive to control border entries while constraining how it treats those already inside. That split leaves open politically sensitive questions about immigration policy coherence, resource allocation, and accountability between the political branches.

Where the law may land on appeal

Given the Ninth Circuit’s indication that the government is likely to prevail on the § 1182(f) claim but not on at least part of the APA claim, much will turn on the full opinion the panel promised. Key issues to watch on appeal or further proceedings:

- Whether the court ultimately treats the termination of cooperative agreements as final, reviewable agency action.
- How the court resolves the Heckler tension—does it find specific, nondiscretionary duties in § 1522 that displace the discretion presumption, or does it accept a narrower, program-by-program reading?
- How the court allocates responsibility between State, HHS (Office of Refugee Resettlement), and other agencies whose authorities and contracts intersect with reception-and-placement services.
- The durability of the reinstatement remedy in light of contracting law, appropriations constraints, and any higher-court review that might view such an order as intrusive into executive contracting discretion.

Conclusion

Pacito illustrates a pragmatic judicial strategy: protect the structural prerogatives of the political branches on national-entry and foreign-affairs questions while preserving a judicial role in enforcing statutory obligations that Congress imposed on implementing agencies. That doctrinal channel—from presidential order to agency action to APA enforcement—keeps courts in the business of contesting the concrete effects of big-picture executive policymaking without converting themselves into a super-policy-maker on immigration. The approach is doctrinally defensible, but it will generate hard line-drawing disputes about statutory mandates, manageability, and remedial propriety. The forthcoming full opinion in Pacito should be read carefully for how the Ninth Circuit resolves those disputes; its reasoning will be a template for the next wave of litigation that tests the outer boundaries of presidential power while holding agencies accountable to statutory duties.
