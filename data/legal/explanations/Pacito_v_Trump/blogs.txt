Title: Blog about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

## Hawaii’s Long Shadow: The Ninth Circuit Reaffirms Expansive 1182(f) Power in Pacito v. Trump

Why this matters: At the intersection of immigration authority and statutory refugee protections, the Ninth Circuit’s interim stay in Pacito v. Trump is a high-stakes preview of how lower courts will treat presidential power to suspend classes of entrants under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The panel, invoking Trump v. Hawaii, moved quickly to stay a district court’s preliminary injunction against President Trump’s January 20, 2025 executive order suspending refugee admissions. The ruling underscores two linked strands of doctrine: (1) the continued force of near-absolute deference to presidential findings under § 1182(f); and (2) the limits of that deference when the government’s action collides with other statutory schemes—here, the Refugee Act’s reception-and-placement provisions (8 U.S.C. § 1522) and related APA claims.

What the court did: The three-judge panel stayed the district court’s February and March preliminary injunctions in their entirety, except for a narrowly tailored remand-style directive that the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel explained that, on the stay factors laid out in Nken v. Holder, the government showed the requisite likelihood of success on the merits of its challenge to the district court’s block on the suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f). The court explicitly relied on Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and that the only statutory prerequisite is the President’s finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” At the same time, the panel recognized that the government was unlikely to prevail on an APA challenge tied to the statutory obligations in § 1522, and therefore ordered interim relief to ensure statutorily mandated reception-and-placement services for refugees already in the United States.

How the panel reasoned: The opinion — brief because a fuller merits opinion is promised — leans heavily on Trump v. Hawaii’s textualist and national-security deference. It reads § 1182(f) as expansive: the statute authorizes the President to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens,” and the sole textual predicate for use of that authority is the President’s finding of detriment. The Ninth Circuit therefore declined to undertake a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications,” essentially treating the President’s finding as dispositive for the stay analysis on the admissions suspension.

But the panel did not treat the case as a one-way ticket for the administration. The court flagged that the Refugee Act’s reception-and-placement provisions (8 U.S.C. § 1522) impose statutory obligations that may constrain executive action, at least where the government has already admitted refugees and Congress has enacted detailed programmatic provisions and appropriations. Because the government had terminated cooperative agreements that historically delivered services after admission, and could not assure the court a replacement infrastructure was ready, the panel concluded the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA challenge to that defunding and ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services for refugees already admitted.

A notable dissent-in-part: Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent stakes out a narrower view of § 1522. Lee reads § 1522 as primarily authorizing (not mandating) resettlement programs and argues the district court exceeded judicial authority by ordering the executive to operate those programs. Lee would have stayed the injunctions in full. That disagreement foreshadows the exact doctrinal battleground that a merits panel will face: whether § 1522 creates a judicially enforceable duty to provide reception-and-placement services, or instead leaves the matter to executive discretion “to the extent of available appropriations.”

Analytical perspective: The panel’s approach is doctrinally unsurprising but consequential. It doubles down on the post-Hawaii trajectory: when the President acts under § 1182(f), courts will give enormous deference to the President’s assessment that a class’ entry would be detrimental. That deference narrows the universe of successful statutory and constitutional challenges to admission suspensions to fairly limited lines: clear statutory text that imposes specific obligations that the presidential order actually displaces (as the panel suggests may be true for § 1522), or constitutional defects (e.g., discrimination that clearly violates the Due Process or Equal Protection principles in a nonnational-security context). Absent such anchors, plaintiffs face the uphill task of contesting the sufficiency of the President’s finding.

The panel’s careful carveout on § 1522, and Judge Lee’s opposing reading, reveals tentative limits to the Hawaii approach. The government need not have unbounded power to suspend entries if suspension implicitly or explicitly nullifies other, comprehensive statutory schemes that Congress created for admitted refugees. The panel’s interim directive to reinstate cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted is a practical compromise: it preserves executive suspension authority going forward, while protecting on-the-ground reliance interests and statutory duties post-admission. That remedy threads a needle between deference to the President on admissions and judicial enforcement of concrete statutory entitlements.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects: Pacito will be read in three ways by future litigants and courts. First, it reaffirms that § 1182(f) is a potent tool for presidents who prioritize border control or selective entry restrictions. Future plaintiffs seeking to challenge broad entry suspensions will face a hard threshold: the President’s finding will be difficult to rebut on the merits unless challengers can tie the suspension to a conflicting statute or show the decision falls outside the statute’s text.

Second, the case highlights the importance of statutory specificity. When Congress embeds detailed programmatic mandates — coupled with appropriations — courts may be more willing to police the executive’s compliance with those statutes, even while deferring on core admission decisions under § 1182(f). That means challengers should sharpen claims around statutory duties that govern post-admission treatment, funding, and services. Agencies that abruptly terminate statutorily contemplated programs risk an easier path for plaintiffs under the APA.

Third, this decision will intensify pressure on the administrative record and agency planning. The Ninth Circuit was concerned that the government had no clear plan to replace the resettlement infrastructure after terminating cooperative agreements. That practical deficiency likely swayed the court toward protecting admitted refugees’ services. Future administrations considering suspension should document contingencies for statutorily required functions to avoid injunctive relief restoring programmatic obligations.

Finally, the split among the panel—albeit narrow—signals the legal questions to watch on appeal: Is § 1522 coercive enough to restrict § 1182(f) suspensions as applied? How far does the “finding” requirement limit courts’ ability to test executive rationale? And to what extent will courts demand administrative evidence of continuity-of-services planning when admissions are suspended?

Bottom line: Pacito confirms that Hawaii’s deference doctrine remains alive and influential in the Ninth Circuit, making it difficult to enjoin admissions suspensions brought under § 1182(f). But the court’s simultaneous willingness to enforce statutory resettlement obligations for admitted refugees suggests that the judiciary still has a role policing concrete statutory programs that intersect with—but do not wholly fall within—presidential immigration proclamations. The upcoming merits opinion promised by the Ninth Circuit will be the place to watch for a fuller articulation of where that policing line is drawn.



# When “Authorized” Becomes “Required”? Parsing § 1522 and the Refugee Services Mandate

## Why this case matters

Last week’s Ninth Circuit interim order in Pacito v. Trump — staying the district court’s injunction of the President’s suspension of refugee admissions but directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception and placement services — puts front and center a knotty statutory question with real-world consequences: does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 create judicially enforceable duties to provide resettlement services for refugees admitted before a suspension, or is it merely an authorization that leaves funding and programmatic choices to the political branches? The panel’s answer — that the government is unlikely to prevail on an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge because § 1522 imposes substantive obligations the executive cannot simply abandon — was enough to keep relief for already-admitted refugees alive while the broader policy dispute over admissions is litigated. Judge Lee’s partial dissent frames the opposite view: § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory. How courts read the statute matters: it determines whether courts can require the executive to deliver services critical to newly arrived refugees and whether a presidential moratorium can be implemented by cutting off the machinery that helps resettle people already here.

## What the panel said (and didn’t)

The Ninth Circuit’s stay opinion splits the issues. Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, the panel concluded the President is likely to succeed on the merits of authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions — a domain where substantial deference to the President historically obtains. But the panel also concluded the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim: the claim that the government unlawfully refused to provide reception-and-placement services mandated by § 1522 to refugees already admitted. On that narrow point, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services while the appeal proceeds.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent differs sharply on the statutory reading. Lee reads § 1522 as an authorization statute. He emphasizes the statute’s title — “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” — and repeatedly notes the use of the verb “is authorized” in the key funding and program subsections. For Lee, the statute tells the executive branch what it may do when appropriations are available; it does not convert those options into a judicially enforceable command to maintain programs or contracts.

## Textual cues: “shall,” “authorized,” and “to the extent of available appropriations”

Much of the dispute turns on a classical statutory-drafting puzzle: how do courts reconcile the mix of permissive language (“is authorized”) with occasional mandatory verbs (“shall”) and the persistent appropriations qualifier (“to the extent of available appropriations”)?

- “Authorized.” The statute’s repeated phrase — that the Director or Secretary “is authorized” to carry out certain programs, grants, and contracts — is an unmistakably permissive formulation. Historically, “authorization” provisions have been treated as enabling, not mandatory; that is, they permit the executive to create and fund programs, subject to later appropriations. Judge Lee leans on that conventional gloss.

- “Shall.” By contrast, subsection 1522(a) uses “shall” in setting out certain duties and “conditions and considerations” that apply “In providing assistance under this section.” The panel takes those “shall” statements to be operative duties — concrete obligations the executive must satisfy to the extent it provides assistance. The majority’s approach treats those mandatory terms as signaling enforceable substantive commitments when the government elects to provide the covered assistance.

- “To the extent of available appropriations.” That phrase is a critical limiter. It signals Congress’s recognition that any duties in § 1522 are constrained by available funding. Both sides can point to the phrase: permissivists (like Judge Lee) stress that this makes § 1522 contingent and thus non-self-executing absent appropriations; the panel majority can say that the phrase does not eliminate all judicially enforceable obligations, but rather calibrates the executive’s duties to the reality of funding.

The result is a classic statutory tug-of-war. The majority reads the statutory scheme as creating enforceable duties for reception and placement once the executive has, in effect, committed to resettlement operations — a reading anchored in the operational reality that Congress created a statutory architecture for resettlement programs and accountability mechanisms (e.g., reporting requirements). Lee reads the same text as a structural authorization that allows the executive to design programs but does not bind it to keep them in place.

## Structural context across subsections (a)–(e)

Parsing § 1522 in context matters. Subsection (a) sets out “conditions and consideration[s],” while subsections (b)–(e) elaborate particular programmatic authorities: grants and contracts for services, initial resettlement programs, cash and medical assistance, and special provisions for unaccompanied minors. Subsection (c), for instance, directs the Director to make available resources for employment training “in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible.” Subsection (d)(2)(B) speaks to the Director’s legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minors “if necessary.” Subsection (e)(7)(A) instructs the Secretary to “develop and implement alternative projects” for recent refugees “under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.”

Two interpretive paths flow from that structure. One treats subsections (b)–(e) as the substantive program vehicles Congress authorized; subsection (a)’s “shall” clauses impose standards or duties on the execution of those programs — conditions that become judicially enforceable when programs exist or when the government has bound itself contractually. The alternative treats subsection (a) as a directional provision that explains how to run authorized programs without converting the statutory scheme into a command to run them at all.

The panel’s practical hook is the government’s own conduct. The government historically operated through cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies; the sudden termination of those agreements and the State Department’s admitted uncertainty about replacement capacity suggested to the court that the executive had not merely declined to create a program but effectively abandoned statutory obligations for refugees already admitted. That factual posture makes the statutory text look less like a mere permission slip and more like the sort of statutory framework courts can enforce through the APA.

## Legislative history and the “law to apply” inquiry

The APA authorizes review of “agency action” unless “committed to agency discretion by law.” When an agency’s action is challenged as arbitrary and capricious or contrary to statute, courts ask whether there is “law to apply” — i.e., whether Congress set a standard by which agency action can be judged. The Ninth Circuit’s interim view was that § 1522 supplies such law for the limited domain of services to admitted refugees.

On legislative history, the Pacito opinion (so far) stays careful. The panel’s reasoning stays rooted in statutory language and the existing statutory architecture for refugee resettlement programs. The opinion did not purport to rely on a deep dive into contemporary legislative history; it framed § 1522 as part of the Refugee Act’s “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” In short, the panel treated the statutory text and structure — reinforced by the government’s longstanding federal-state-nonprofit resettlement regime — as supplying the “law to apply.” Absent a clear legislative-history signal that Congress intended § 1522 to be purely hortatory, the court was willing to read the statute as creating standards the executive must satisfy when it provides (or purports to cease providing) services.

Those are the right textual starting points. But it is also fair to say that legislative history in the refugee context is mixed and not dispositive: the Refugee Act was enacted to establish a uniform federal policy and processes for refugee resettlement, but Congress also consistently tied programs to appropriations and left detailed program design to the executive. Given that background, courts deciding whether § 1522 imposes judicially enforceable duties will necessarily weigh statutory text and structure heavily; legislative history will likely be a tie-breaker only when the text is ambiguous.

## My analytical perspective

The panel’s pragmatic reading has force. Congress created a durable regulatory and funding apparatus for resettlement that presumes ongoing governmental involvement: program authorities, cooperative-agreement mechanisms, regulatory standards, and reporting obligations. When the executive abruptly terminates cooperative agreements and disavows replacement plans — but Congress has appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in the relevant fiscal year — a court can reasonably conclude there is “law to apply” to assess whether the government’s action comports with statutory duties and the APA.

Judge Lee is also right to underscore the limits of statutory language. The repeated “is authorized” phrasing and explicit appropriations qualifier are powerful indicators that Congress expected the executive to retain discretion. If § 1522 were read to compel perpetual funding and program operation in every circumstance, that would flatten a deliberate legislative design that couples authorization with annual appropriations and executive administration. There is a legitimate separation-of-powers sensitivity in ordering the executive to maintain particular cooperative agreements when appropriations could be reallocated or when the political branches have divergent policy preferences about refugee intake.

Reconciling these perspectives suggests a middle road that the Ninth Circuit’s interim order reflects in practice: treat § 1522 as supplying enforceable standards in particular, concrete circumstances — notably where funding exists (or at least has recently been appropriated) and where the government has historically operated through specific cooperative arrangements and then abruptly terminated them without a demonstrable plan to fulfill statutory duties through other means. That approach respects congressional control over appropriations and executive discretion in program design, while preserving judicial review to prevent the executive from evading statutory responsibilities by unilateral termination of the operational machinery Congress created.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

If the Ninth Circuit’s eventual merits opinion adopts the panel’s interim posture, we should expect several downstream consequences:

- Judicial enforcement of programmatic duties. Courts may be more willing to find “law to apply” in statutes that mix permissive and mandatory language where government conduct effectively dismantles a long-standing statutory regime and Congress has appropriated money for related purposes.

- Narrower executive escape routes. The executive branch would be constrained from implementing policy shifts by simply terminating cooperative agreements or cutting off contractors when those agreements implement statutory programs tied to prior appropriations and statutory standards.

- Pressure on appropriations and political branches. At the same time, courts will remain sensitive to the “to the extent of available appropriations” limiter. The more Congress explicitly reduces or withholds funds, the harder it will be for plaintiffs to sustain claims that the executive must run programs. Litigation thus may shift to appropriations battles and to plaintiffs’ showing that funds were in fact available for the covered purposes.

- Fragmentation across circuits. Other courts may parse § 1522 differently. A circuit read that embraces Judge Lee’s view could create a split over whether authorization statutes with embedded “shall” clauses are enforceable absent express mandatory language or unambiguous appropriations mandates.

- Practical effects on refugee resettlement. For refugees themselves, a ruling that § 1522 imposes enforceable duties means courts can require the government to restore services essential for early integration. Practically, that preserves the on-the-ground capacity of resettlement agencies that federal contracts and grants sustain.

## Bottom line

Pacito puts a spotlight on a familiar but consequential interpretive thicket: how to treat statutes that mix authorization, directive language, and appropriations-based constraints. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order leans toward enforceability where the government’s conduct shows an abdication of a statutory resettlement architecture and where appropriations remain relevant. Judge Lee’s dissent is a cautionary counterweight, reminding courts not to read authorization provisions as perpetual funding mandates. The ultimate merits opinion, when it issues, will be important not only for the refugees and resettlement organizations at issue, but for the broader question of when a permissive statutory scheme becomes, in practice, a legal duty that courts can enforce under the APA.



# Presidential Orders, Agency Action, and the APA: How Pacito Channels Review Through Implementing Agencies

Why this matters: Pacito is an important, if interim, lesson in how courts can police presidential policymaking by targeting the implementing bureaucracy rather than the President himself. The Ninth Circuit’s expedited stay opinion signals two doctrinal moves that will shape future challenges to sweeping executive immigration or foreign-affairs directives. First, the panel assumes that broad presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is largely insulated from a searching merits review—echoing Trump v. Hawaii—but it carves out a conventional path for APA litigation by treating the agencies’ dismantling of the resettlement apparatus as reviewable agency action under the APA. Second, the panel takes the unusual remedial step of ordering agencies to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutorily mandated reception-and-placement services. Both choices reflect a pragmatic, separation-of-powers approach: the court will not second-guess the President’s immigration judgment but will enforce statutory duties that Congress placed on agencies.

What the panel did (and didn’t) decide: The court’s stay opinion—decided on an expedited record and with a fuller opinion promised later—stays the district court’s injunctions against the suspension of refugee admissions, finding that the government is likely to prevail on that point under § 1182(f). At the same time the panel concluded that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA theory: that the agencies violated statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 by terminating cooperative agreements that provided reception-and-placement services. Because the agencies appear uncertain about their plans to replace the terminated contractors, the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services for refugees already admitted to the United States. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent underscores the statutory interpretive tension: she reads § 1522 as largely authorizing, not mandating, agency programs and would have stayed the injunctions in full.

How the panel channels review toward agencies: Several doctrinal lines converge in the court’s ruling. The panel accepts the starting premise from Trump v. Hawaii that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and supplies broad, largely nonjusticiable space for national-entry decisions. Faced with that frontal barrier to APA review of a presidential admission moratorium, the court shifts the fight to agency implementation: even if the President can lawfully suspend admissions, agencies implementing refugee resettlement programs still must operate within the bounds of statutes that Congress enacted for those programs. That is a familiar judicial strategy—declining to treat the President as an “agency” under the APA while recognizing that subordinate agencies’ actions (or inactions) remain subject to statutory and APA review.

Two threshold APA questions follow from that move, and the panel’s interim ruling presumes favorable answers to each for the plaintiffs: (1) was the termination of cooperative agreements “final agency action” subject to APA review, and (2) if so, was that action “committed to agency discretion by law” under Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), or instead presumptively reviewable because the statute supplies judicially manageable standards?

Final agency action: The termination of cooperative agreements looks very much like final agency action. It is a concrete, discrete decision with present legal effects: withdrawal of funding and termination of the arrangements that historically delivered services to admitted refugees. The Ninth Circuit’s remedy presumes finality—if the terminations were merely ephemeral exercise of internal judgment or interlocutory planning, a court could not properly enjoin or compel their reversal. The court’s reliance on the statutory framework—§ 1522’s affirmative provisions, the grant/cooperative-agreement mechanism in § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), and the appropriation and reporting structure—supports the view that the termination of agreements is a consummation of agency decisionmaking that produces rights and obligations (for contractors, refugee recipients, and the agencies obliged by Congress to provide services).

Heckler v. Chaney and the discretion barrier: Heckler held that an agency’s decision not to enforce is presumptively nonreviewable if it is committed to agency discretion by law, and that doctrine typically protects prosecutorial-style declinations and resource-based judgments. But Heckler is not a universal shield. Where Congress has supplied substantive directives and “shall”-type duties, courts can review agency action (or inaction) to ensure statutory compliance. The Pacito panel implicitly treats § 1522 as supplying such duties—at least for some services and populations—so Heckler’s discretion bar does not foreclose review.

Whether Heckler applies will turn on statutory text and structure. Sections of § 1522 use permissive language (“is authorized”), but the statute also contains “shall” provisions and specific programmatic obligations (e.g., duties for the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to make resources available, requirements for care of unaccompanied minors “if necessary,” and for the Secretary to develop interim projects for cash and medical assistance “as needed”). The panel reads this statutory texture as supplying at least some non- discretionary obligations that courts can enforce. Judge Lee’s partial dissent illustrates the opposing view: she emphasizes § 1522’s title (“Authorization for programs...”) and the many “authorized” clauses to argue that the statute primarily grants discretion to craft programs, so Heckler would most likely bar review.

Are the statutory standards judicially manageable? This is the sticking doctrinal question. Even if the termination of agreements is final agency action and not shielded by Heckler, courts may still refuse to adjudicate if the statute lacks manageable legal standards. The panel suggests that § 1522 contains judicially manageable hooks—e.g., “shall make available sufficient resources,” duties “to the extent of available appropriations,” the specific programmatic descriptions for employment, language training, cash and medical support, and the fiscal-reporting obligation in § 1523. Together those elements arguably permit judicial comparison between what Congress required and what agencies have done.

On the other hand, manageability objections are plausible. “To the extent of available appropriations” injects a funding contingency; Congress appropriated sizable migration funds for FY 2025 but also enacted rescissions, and appropriations politics is a classic area of agency discretion and political judgment. Moreover, assessing whether “sufficient resources” exist, or what interim projects are “needed,” invites line-drawing and policy balancing—tasks some judges will be reluctant to undertake. The panel’s preliminary holding navigates these tensions by limiting its conclusion: it says only that the government is not likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ APA claim—enough, under Nken, to warrant a stay exception and an interim directive—leaving the full merits analysis for the forthcoming opinion.

The remedial choice: reinstating cooperative agreements and the § 706 frameworks

The panel didn’t merely declare that agencies likely violated the APA; it ordered a concrete remedial step: reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and- placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. That remedy sits at the intersection of two distinct § 5 U.S.C. § 706 provisions.

First, § 706(1) authorizes the court to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” If the agency had a nondiscretionary duty to fund or arrange reception-and-placement services for admitted refugees, the court can order the agency to act (e.g., to award cooperative agreements, make payments, or otherwise perform). Compel relief is particularly apt where an agency has declined to do something the statute required—“here, reinstate agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.”

Second, § 706(2) permits courts to “hold unlawful and set aside” agency actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” If the terminations themselves were final agency actions, the court could set them aside on APA grounds. Practically speaking, compulsion under § 706(1) and vacatur under § 706(2) often overlap: vacating an unlawful termination and ordering agencies to take steps to restore the status quo both achieve the same ends. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order reads like a blend: it requires reinstatement (compel/vacatur feel) while acknowledging the larger executive prerogatives the government claims.

Is reinstatement unusual? Yes and no. Courts have on multiple occasions vacated unlawful agency deregulatory moves and required agencies to restore prior programs pending lawful rulemaking or statutory compliance. But ordering agencies to reinstate cooperative agreements raises special practical and separation-of-powers questions. Cooperative agreements involve private parties who may have been released from obligations, may have spent or lost funds, and may be subject to complex procurement and performance constraints. Courts commanding agencies to re-enter specific bilateral arrangements inevitably micromanage administrative contracting, which courts normally avoid unless the statutory duty is clear and the agency’s refusal is unlawful. The Ninth Circuit’s directive is therefore a bold, targeted remedy: it aims to protect statutory beneficiaries (admitted refugees) and the integrity of congressionally authorized programs without upending the President’s broader suspension of admissions.

Potential downstream effects and practical friction points

Pacito’s doctrinal posture—insulating the President’s moratorium while enforcing agency duties—offers a blueprint for future litigation over executive orders with wide sweep. Plaintiffs will likely bring dual claims: (1) direct attacks on the presidential directive (where success will be difficult post-Hawaii), and (2) APA challenges to agency implementation decisions that have concrete, localized effects on beneficiaries. Courts sympathetic to enforcing statutory directives will, as here, treat the second path as the more workable route to relief.

But this route is not frictionless. Three practical issues will recur:

1) The funding caveat. Congress’s repeated use of “to the extent of available appropriations” complicates judicial enforcement. Where appropriations are present but politically reallocated (e.g., rescissions), courts must decide how much deference to accord agency resource allocations and whether appropriations politics create a nonjusticiable barrier.

2) Manageability and remedial scope. If courts are willing to police agencies’ programmatic choices, they must still avoid crafting detailed programmatic blueprints. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order tries to thread that needle by limiting relief to providing “the usual and customary services” under prior agreements for refugees already admitted. But clarifying the contours of “usual and customary” services will likely generate further fact-intensive litigation and implementation disputes.

3) Separation-of-powers optics. The court’s selective intervention—enjoining agency rollbacks while leaving the President’s moratorium intact—may be criticized by some as constitutionally awkward: it allows the executive to control border entries while constraining how it treats those already inside. That split leaves open politically sensitive questions about immigration policy coherence, resource allocation, and accountability between the political branches.

Where the law may land on appeal

Given the Ninth Circuit’s indication that the government is likely to prevail on the § 1182(f) claim but not on at least part of the APA claim, much will turn on the full opinion the panel promised. Key issues to watch on appeal or further proceedings:

- Whether the court ultimately treats the termination of cooperative agreements as final, reviewable agency action.
- How the court resolves the Heckler tension—does it find specific, nondiscretionary duties in § 1522 that displace the discretion presumption, or does it accept a narrower, program-by-program reading?
- How the court allocates responsibility between State, HHS (Office of Refugee Resettlement), and other agencies whose authorities and contracts intersect with reception-and-placement services.
- The durability of the reinstatement remedy in light of contracting law, appropriations constraints, and any higher-court review that might view such an order as intrusive into executive contracting discretion.

Conclusion

Pacito illustrates a pragmatic judicial strategy: protect the structural prerogatives of the political branches on national-entry and foreign-affairs questions while preserving a judicial role in enforcing statutory obligations that Congress imposed on implementing agencies. That doctrinal channel—from presidential order to agency action to APA enforcement—keeps courts in the business of contesting the concrete effects of big-picture executive policymaking without converting themselves into a super-policy-maker on immigration. The approach is doctrinally defensible, but it will generate hard line-drawing disputes about statutory mandates, manageability, and remedial propriety. The forthcoming full opinion in Pacito should be read carefully for how the Ninth Circuit resolves those disputes; its reasoning will be a template for the next wave of litigation that tests the outer boundaries of presidential power while holding agencies accountable to statutory duties.



# A Stay With Strings: Tailoring Interim Relief to Preserve Statutory Entitlements

## Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s September 12, 2025 order staying most of the district court’s injunctions in Pacito v. Trump, while carving out a narrow, mandatory obligation to reinstate cooperative agreements that provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States, is consequential for two reasons. First, it illustrates how a federal court of appeals can thread the needle between the broad deference owed to the President in immigration and national-security-adjacent judgments (under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)) and the more ordinary rule that the executive must follow statutes it administers (here, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and its implementing regulations). Second, the order suggests a pragmatic, remediation-oriented approach to stays pending appeal: rather than an all-or-nothing posture, the panel fashioned a stay that preserves the national policy the government asserts while protecting statutorily conferred benefits for a discrete class of individuals during the pendency of expedited appellate review. That hybrid approach could influence how courts handle conflicts between sweeping executive action and statutory programs in future, fast-moving litigation.

## The court’s reasoning, in brief

The panel applied the familiar four-factor framework for a stay pending appeal from Nken v. Holder, identifying the likelihood of success on the merits and the prospect of irreparable injury as the most critical considerations. On the presidential authority issue, the panel concluded that the government is likely to prevail. Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, the court emphasized that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and requires only that the President “find” entry of a class of aliens would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The panel therefore stayed the district court’s injunctions barring the suspension of refugee admissions.

But the panel also recognized a separate problem: the executive’s withdrawal of funding and termination of cooperative agreements delivering domestic reception-and-placement services creates statutory friction with § 1522, which governs federal resettlement assistance and authorizes (and in some respects directs) specified offices to ensure that refugees receive services “to the extent of available appropriations.” The panel concluded that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge tied to the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and uncertainty about its ability to provide statutorily required services. As a result, the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States, while staying the rest of the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending appeal.

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part, emphasizing statutory text and structure to argue that § 1522 is best read as an authorization rather than a mandatory command — a claim that, if accepted, would counsel against ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements.

## Why the panel’s “stay-with-strings” is notable

Two doctrinal tensions run through the panel’s approach. First is deference in immigration/national-security contexts: courts frequently give the President substantial leeway under the INA and related statutes when the action purports to affect national security or the admission of aliens. The panel hews to that precedent by acknowledging the government’s likely success under § 1182(f) and by staying the injunction against the suspension of admissions.

Second is ordinary administrative-law and statutory-enforcement logic: even where executive action is privileged by deference in setting immigration policy, the executive is not a law unto itself. When Congress enacts statutory programs and allocates funds (or contemplates appropriations) for particular benefits and assigns implementation responsibilities to executive agencies, courts have long been willing to police whether the executive has complied with statutory obligations, at least where plaintiffs show a plausible claim of statutory entitlement or where APA review is available.

The panel’s order operationalizes this distinction. It gives the government deference on the sweeping policy question of whether to suspend refugee admissions altogether, but it refuses to bless a sudden dismantling of the existing statutory infrastructure that serves refugees already admitted — at least while the appeal proceeds. This balancing act can be read as a careful division between (1) the President’s broad policy discretion under § 1182(f) and (2) the executive’s duty to administer statutes that Congress designed to protect refugees within the United States.

## Institutional and remedial implications

The Ninth Circuit’s remedy — conditioning a stay on reinstatement of cooperative agreements — raises practical and institutional questions worth flagging.

First, enforcement. The court ordered the government to reinstate “such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That relief is targeted and, on its face, manageable: it directs agencies to restore the status quo ante for a definable class. But the order leaves open several thorny implementation questions. Which cooperative agreements must be reinstated? For how long? Must the agreements be reinstated on identical terms, or may agencies revise terms consistent with statutory requirements? The district court’s July 14 enforcement framework — and the magistrate judge appointed to oversee it — will matter greatly to operationalize the panel’s command. The appellate panel itself recognized some of these practical uncertainties by explicitly directing reinstatement “to provide the usual and customary services” and by noting the district court’s role in defining scope, which shores up the remedial structure needed to make the stay meaningful without micromanaging agency operations.

Second, appropriations constraints. The panel relied in part on § 1522’s affirmative language and statutory purpose, and it cited available appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY2025 (subject to a post-enactment rescission). Yet § 1522 repeatedly and explicitly limits obligations “to the extent of available appropriations.” That caveat is central to Judge Lee’s partial dissent: to him, § 1522 is predominantly authorizing, and where mandatory language appears it is tethered to appropriations and to the conditional framing in subsection (a). The panel’s remedial choice implicitly presumes that appropriations — or emergency measures short of new congressional action — exist to fund reinstated agreements for those already admitted. If the executive’s practical argument rests on lack of appropriations, the government can still press that defense on remand or appeal. But courts ordering provisional relief in appropriation-sensitive contexts inch into separation-of-powers tensions: courts can require the executive to resume statutorily contemplated administration of programs only so long as there are funds to do so — and judges must be careful not to convert budgetary scarcity into judicially enforceable excuses for noncompliance.

Third, institutional comity and speed. The order reveals how appellate courts can tailor interim relief in expedited contexts to avoid either (a) undermining national-security- or immigration-driven policy changes by blocking the government outright, or (b) permitting irrevocable harm to individuals who have already relied on statutory entitlements. The approach preserves the government’s ability to pursue its policy in the long run while keeping administrative machinery operating for a group that, practically speaking, cannot be “unmade.” That is an institutionally sensible compromise, and it gives both parties an incentive to litigate responsibly on an expedited timetable: the government avoids being enjoined wholesale, and plaintiffs receive limited but tangible protection.

## Doctrinal ripple effects and future litigation

If other circuits or future panels adopt this “stay-with-strings” template, a few doctrinal effects are plausible.

First, it may reinforce a two-track analytical reflex in immigration-related APA litigation: courts will be more likely to accord sweeping policy deference on the merits to executive decisions tied to admission and national-security judgment, yet still entertain robust statutory claims where the executive’s policy change entails stopping benefits or services for persons already within the United States. That distinction builds on settled principles (the domestic reach of statutory entitlements vs. deference on entry restrictions), but the Ninth Circuit’s remedial mapping makes the point practically palpable.

Second, the order may influence preliminary-injunction practice by emphasizing narrow remedial tailoring as a way to satisfy equity and preserve public interests. Rather than framing stays as defeats for the enjoined party or victors’ spoils for the enjoined, appellate courts may increasingly craft interim orders that preserve the status quo for vulnerable classes (those already admitted, long-residing, or otherwise reliant on benefits) while allowing executive policy changes to proceed prospectively.

Third, though, courts that replicate this approach will need to be vigilant about admin-law doctrines that constrain judicial second-guessing — particularly where statutory language is genuinely ambiguous. The panel’s decision rests on a provisional finding that plaintiffs are likely to succeed on at least one APA claim tied to § 1522; but the statutory question is close enough that Judge Lee wrote separately. That split suggests the approach will be most defensible in cases where (a) there is a clear statutory text or history showing Congress intended specified protections for people inside the U.S., or (b) the executive’s abrupt operative steps (terminating long-standing cooperative agreements without a plausible transition plan) make judicial intervention necessary to avoid immediate street-level harms.

## A model with limits

The Ninth Circuit’s order is an attractive model insofar as it seeks proportionality: it vindicates deference to presidential judgments about admission while protecting the statutory program for those already residing here. But the model has limits. It depends on courts being able to identify discrete, administrable classes whose harms are both imminent and remediable; it depends on agencies having feasible ways to reinstate operations consistent with appropriations law; and it depends on judges avoiding undue micromanagement of agency decisionmaking. Moreover, where statutory obligations are genuinely permissive (Judge Lee’s textual reading of § 1522 is a live argument), courts must be careful not to transform programmatic authorizations into judicially enforced mandates simply because of perceived policy equities.

## Bottom line

The panel in Pacito illustrates a pragmatic way courts can reconcile the usual deference to presidential immigration judgments with the obligation of the executive to administer statutory programs for people within the United States. By staying the district court’s injunctions on admissions while insisting that the government maintain reception-and-placement services for already-admitted refugees, the Ninth Circuit crafted a narrowly tailored interim remedy that preserves both the public interest in national decisionmaking and the statutory protections for vulnerable individuals. That approach — a stay with strings attached — may guide future courts faced with fast-moving conflict between sweeping executive orders and statutory entitlements. But its durability will turn on how lower courts navigate the hard questions this order leaves open: definitional scope, appropriations constraints, and the limits of judicial oversight of programmatic implementation.



# The Purse Versus the Pen: Can the Executive Zero Out a Funded Refugee Services Program?

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s expedited stay order in Pacito v. Trump thrusts squarely into a live constitutional and statutory tension: how far can the President go in suspending refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) while also stopping the machinery that Congress created to receive, resettle, and support refugees once they are here? The panel’s interim ruling—staying district-court injunctions against the entry suspension but directing the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted—signals that courts will not treat authority over admissions as an automatic license to dismantle statutorily-mandated post‑admission programs. That tension implicates the separation of powers in an especially raw way: the executive’s plenary immigration authorities and national-security deference on one side, and Congress’s purse and programmatic commands on the other.

The immediate stakes are concrete. The State Department terminated virtually all cooperative agreements providing reception and placement services after the district court first enjoined parts of the President’s January 20, 2025, executive order. The Ninth Circuit panel found the government likely to prevail on the validity of the admissions suspension, but not on the argument that the Administration can suspend or defund services the statute requires for refugees already admitted. The court therefore ordered the reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees while staying the district court’s larger injunctions pending appeal. That split ruling captures the jurisprudential balancing act: great deference on decisions to admit, less deference when statutory duties to care for persons already admitted are at issue.

## What the court said — the reasoning in brief

The panel applied the familiar stay factors from Nken, emphasizing that likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm are most critical. On the Section 1182(f) challenge to Executive Order No. 14163, the panel followed Trump v. Hawaii: § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and requires only that the President “find[]” entry would be detrimental. The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail there and stayed the district court’s injunctions insofar as they sought to restrain the admissions suspension.

On the other hand, the panel flagged a different result under the Administrative Procedure Act for the statutory framework Congress created for domestic resettlement. The panel focused on 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions that authorize or direct the Office of Refugee Resettlement and other agencies to provide reception-and-placement services (employment training, English instruction, cash and medical assistance for newly arrived refugees, and specified responsibilities for unaccompanied minors). The court noted that for FY2025 there were appropriations in the billions for migration and refugee assistance (reduced, the panel observed, by $800 million in a 2025 rescissions act), and concluded that the government had effectively terminated cooperative agreements without a clear replacement plan—raising a plausible APA claim that the Executive had acted unlawfully in cutting off services statutorily associated with admitted refugees. Accordingly, the panel directed immediate reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees already admitted into the United States.

Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent highlights the crux of statutory interpretation: is § 1522 mandatory (forcing obligations to be performed, subject to “available appropriations”) or merely permissive authorization of programs? Lee reads § 1522 as an authorization scheme—“authorization” in its title, recurring use of “is authorized,” and qualifying language such as “to the extent of available appropriations”—and would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full. The majority (or at least the panel’s interim order) reads § 1522 as plausibly imposing duties the Executive cannot simply drop where appropriations exist and statutory language uses “shall” in relevant subsections.

## Analytical perspective: statute, appropriation, and the Impoundment Control Act

The Ninth Circuit’s interim posture is careful but consequential. It recognizes that immigration enforcement and border-policy decisions receive substantial deference, but refuses to let that deference swallow the programmatic commands and accountability measures Congress placed in the refugee-resettlement regime. That posture raises several doctrinal flashpoints.

First, the interpretation of § 1522 matters. The statute is a mixed text: its title and much of the text use permissive language—authorizations for programs, “to the extent of available appropriations,” and repeated instances of authorizing grants, contracts, and cooperative agreements. But interspersed are “shall” provisions that, on their face, impose particular requirements or conditions of assistance once an agency elects to provide funding. The panel’s interim remedy seems to treat those “shall” clauses—especially the duty to make available reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted—as operational obligations that cannot be met by unilateral executive cessation of cooperative agreements, at least where appropriated funds remain available (even if partially rescinded elsewhere).

Second, appropriations and rescissions complicate the analysis. The panel notes the FY2025 appropriation for migration and refugee assistance and the $800 million rescission enacted later. That matters for two reasons. If Congress has appropriated funds for “migration and refugee assistance” and the executive tries to leave those funds unobligated or unspent in order to effect policy changes (for example, halting domestic resettlement services), courts may treat that as a problematic circumvention of Congress’s power of the purse. That raises an Impoundment Control Act (ICA) question: the ICA constrains the President’s ability to refuse to obligate or spend available appropriations, and imposes procedures for deferrals and rescissions. If the Administration’s termination of cooperative agreements is intended to effect a de‑facto withholding of appropriated refugee-program funds, litigants could argue, as the Ninth Circuit intimates, that the action is less a programmatic policy shift and more an unlawful refusal to carry out Congress’s appropriations directive without compliance with the ICA.

Third, the APA framework matters. The panel’s tentative conclusion that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim reflects the idea that agency action terminating cooperative agreements and defunding established services may be arbitrary and capricious if it lacks reasoned explanation, ignores statutory duties, or fails to consider reliance interests. The government told the district court it was preparing requests for new resettlement agencies but, at oral argument, said its actual progress was uncertain. That sort of record makes a court more comfortable concluding that the termination was not a thoughtful, administratively defensible plan, but a blunt and unexplained cessation that frustrates statutory objectives.

Fourth, separation-of-powers stakes cut both ways. The President’s broad § 1182(f) authority gives the Executive considerable latitude to protect national security through admissions controls. At the same time, Congress has assigned programmatic responsibilities and reporting requirements in the refugee statutes, including oversight to Congress (annual reporting obligations under § 1523). The Ninth Circuit’s interim approach respects both: it leaves the Executive’s admissions-suspension power untouched (for now) while policing the Executive’s compliance with statutory duties concerning persons already admitted and with Congress’s appropriation choices.

## Downstream effects and practical issues

If the Ninth Circuit’s eventual merits opinion sustains the panel’s interim view, several downstream effects are likely.

Agency operations: Departments will need clearer internal process before terminating long-standing cooperative agreements that implement statutory programs. Agencies may have to follow notice-and-comment procedures, reasoned decisionmaking, or at least explain how a termination comports with statutory duties and with any remaining appropriations.

Impoundment litigation: Expect renewed ICA litigation. Plaintiffs and amici may press the view that an across-the-board termination of cooperative agreements—when Congress has appropriated funds for refugee assistance—constitutes an unlawful non‑obligation or constructive rescission if it leaves appropriations unspent without complying with the ICA. The Executive may counter that program design decisions and shifting priorities reflect permissible exercise of executive discretion to prioritize spending within broad appropriation categories.

Political-accountability and oversight: The dispute sharpens Congress’s oversight role. The reporting obligations in § 1523 and the visibility of cooperative-agreement terminations will likely prompt hearings and appropriations riders. Congress may respond by clarifying mandatory versus discretionary aspects of refugee assistance, tightening statutory language, or conditioning funds more aggressively.

Forum shopping and nationwide relief: The case also highlights how preliminary relief can be tailored. The panel’s carve-out for refugees already admitted shows courts can fashion narrower remedies that avoid wholly intruding on contested executive immigration policy, while still enforcing statutory duties. That tailoring could become a model in other contexts where statutes govern services tied to immigration status—e.g., health or education programs for noncitizens.

Potential Supreme Court review: The decision—once the Ninth Circuit issues a full opinion—could present clean issues for higher review: the scope of § 1182(f) post-Hawaii, the interpretation of § 1522 in light of appropriations, and the application of the ICA. The Supreme Court will be attentive to structural separation-of-powers claims as well as national‑security deference principles.

## Practical takeaways

For practitioners and policymakers the case offers a few immediate lessons. First, an administration that wants to alter an operating federal program that implements a statutory scheme should document a reasoned plan that accounts for statutory duties, appropriations, and reliance interests—especially where Congress has earmarked funds or the statute uses mandatory language. Second, where appropriations remain available, courts are likely to scrutinize abrupt terminations that leave beneficiaries without statutory entitlements, and agencies risk APA and ICA challenges. Third, legislative drafting matters: the recurring use of “authorized” and “to the extent of available appropriations” will generate litigation over whether statutes create programmatic mandates or mere authorizations. If Congress wants to avoid such disputes, it must be explicit.

## What to watch next

The Ninth Circuit will issue a full merits opinion in due course; that decision will be the key to doctrinal clarity. If the court ultimately holds that § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations that the Executive may not suspend absent appropriation or proper ICA procedure, expect further litigation on the scope of those obligations (who counts as an “admitted refugee” for purposes of services, how long obligations run, how to sequence funding cuts and program changes). If the court sides with the government, the decision will embolden administrations to use admissions suspensions in tandem with budgetary changes to effect policy goals without engaging Congress.

For now the panel’s interim order underscores an important principle: even where the President’s “pen” is wide in matters of admissions, the purse—Congress’s appropriations power and statutory programmatic mandates—remains a constitutionally and practically potent constraint. The Ninth Circuit’s ruling does not resolve the entire clash between those powers, but it reaffirms that one side of the ledger cannot be unilaterally wiped clean without encountering judicially enforceable statutes and appropriations rules.



## Two Tracks, Two Doctrines: Entry Suspensions vs. Domestic Resettlement Obligations

The Ninth Circuit’s expedited stay last week in Pacito v. Trump epitomizes a growing doctrinal divide in immigration law: when the President acts at the border—suspending entry of an entire class of aliens under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—courts reflexively grant sweeping deference; but when the government takes action that affects persons who are already within the United States, ordinary statutory and administrative-law frameworks reassert themselves. The panel’s order, while temporary, is a lucid statement of that bifurcation. It stays the district court’s injunction curbing the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, leaning heavily on Trump v. Hawaii’s deference-in-immigration principle, but it refuses to let the government off the hook as to federally mandated domestic resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

Why this matters
The practical stakes are immediate: the government sought to halt refugee admissions and to defund or terminate the cooperative agreements that deliver reception-and-placement services to newly arrived refugees. For refugee advocates and the organizations that administer resettlement programs, the question is not merely academic—the availability of cash assistance, English-language training, case management, and care for unaccompanied minors can determine whether recent arrivals are housed, fed, and integrated. Legally, the case sharpens two separate doctrines that litigants and courts will invoke in future challenges—one governing sovereign power over entry and the other governing statutory duties to persons on U.S. soil.

What the Ninth Circuit did
The panel applied the familiar four-part stay standard, emphasizing that the first two factors—likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm—are “the most critical.” On the entry issue, the court said the government was likely to prevail. Echoing Trump v. Hawaii, the panel treated § 1182(f) as yielding “deference” to the President’s assessment that admitting refugees “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The panel concluded the Executive had fulfilled the statute’s solitary textual prerequisite—the President’s “find[ing]”—and that § 1182(f)’s language imposes no meaningful textual limits on a suspension of entry. In short: the President has broad, arguably plenary authority to suspend refugee admissions, and courts should refrain from undertaking a “searching inquiry” into the Executive’s policy justifications.

At the same time, the panel declined to stay certain aspects of the district court’s relief concerning domestic reception-and-placement services. Section 1522, which authorizes (and, the district court read, requires) a suite of services for refugees “to the extent of available appropriations,” was read by the Ninth Circuit to contain enforceable obligations. Because the government had terminated cooperative agreements and had not demonstrated that it had secured new implementers, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522 for refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel signaled that, unlike a suspension of entry, the government’s decision to defund and dismantle domestic service delivery is subject to ordinary statutory analysis and, where statutory duties exist, judicial enforcement.

A sharp disagreement in the margins
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent crystallizes the competing interpretive lines. He agreed with the panel that deference on entry suspensions was appropriate, and would have stayed the injunctions in full. But on § 1522 he reads the statutory architecture differently: the chapter title (“Authorization for programs…”), repeated use of “is authorized,” and provisions conditioned on “available appropriations” suggest permissive congressional authorization rather than a compulsory mandate. Under his reading, the Secretary and the Director have discretion whether and how to run such programs; § 1522 imposes mandatory procedural or substantive constraints only when the Executive chooses to provide assistance. For Lee, the court’s remedial step—ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements—oversteps the judicial role because the statute is principally hortatory and discretionary.

How to reconcile these tracks
Pacito illustrates a sensible analytic separation: immigration-entry doctrines preserve maximal Executive discretion because the text of the INA, historical practice, and national-security concerns point toward singular presidential competence over who enters the country. Trump v. Hawaii and the Ninth Circuit’s order follow that playbook. Conversely, once individuals are physically within the United States, ordinary statutory text and administrative-law principles regain force; Congress can, and sometimes does, assign affirmative duties to federal agencies to deliver benefits, protect welfare, or care for vulnerable populations. Courts, in turn, are equipped to enforce those duties under the APA and related statutory-enforcement frameworks.

Doctrinal implications
Several doctrinal takeaways flow from the Ninth Circuit’s approach.

First, litigants will increasingly split claims into two tracks. Challengers who want to protect noncitizens will press APA and statutory claims that target domestic conduct—defunding programs, terminating contracts, or altering benefits and services for people already inside the United States—because the entry-deference doctrine is unlikely to yield relief against suspension of admissions. Refugee plaintiffs and service organizations will focus on statutes like § 1522 (and on appropriations-law arguments where relevant) to compel the executive branch to continue delivering services.

Second, courts will draw sharper lines between “entry” questions governed by § 1182(f) and “in-country” questions governed by ordinary statutory interpretation. That means assessing whether the government’s action operates at the point of legal-physical entry or instead affects settled expectations and statutory entitlements of people present in the U.S. The former will attract high deference; the latter, ordinary textual and APA review.

Third, the Pacito order underscores the importance of statutory drafting and titles. Judge Lee’s textualist emphasis on “authorization” and residual “available appropriations” language will encourage litigators to marshal careful canons of construction—titles, uses of “shall” vs. “may,” and context within a statutory scheme—to argue either for or against judicially enforceable mandates. Courts will confront disputes over when “authorized” programs are transformed into enforceable obligations by other textual cross-references (for example, a § 1522(a) “shall” tied to “in providing assistance under this section”), and whether the “to the extent of available appropriations” gloss is a limiting clause or a contingent caveat.

Practical downstream effects
Practitioners should take away two practical strategies from Pacito.

For would-be challengers to entry suspensions: don’t rely solely on immigration-entry doctrines. If the relief sought is to assist individuals already admitted (or conditionally admitted), frame claims around domestic statutory duties, contractual or cooperative-agreement rights, and procedural obligations under the APA. Seek injunctions that compel the government to continue programmatic infrastructure, not to reverse broad geopolitical judgments about admissions.

For administrations seeking to implement restrictive immigration policy: anticipate that actions neatly targeted at “entry” will be insulated, but changes touching the domestic delivery apparatus (contracts, grants, cooperative agreements) may be vulnerable. If the goal is to wind down domestic programs, administrations will need clear statutory authority, documented contingency plans for program continuity, and careful adherence to statutory notice-and-termination procedures to reduce the risk of immediate injunctions.

Wider governance questions
Pacito also raises normative questions about separation of powers and accountable governance. Section 1182(f) grants the President broad authority over who may enter, but § 1522 reflects congressional choice to create an administrative infrastructure for integrating refugees once admitted. Where those two powers collide—when the Executive simultaneously restricts entry and retrenches domestic obligations—courts become the arbiter of competing institutional prerogatives. The Ninth Circuit’s bifurcated posture reflects a moderate institutional balance: robust deference for presidential determinations about admission, paired with a willingness to police statutory duties where Congress has prescribed the treatment of people on U.S. soil.

Looking ahead
The Ninth Circuit promised a fuller opinion on the merits. That forthcoming opinion will be important for two reasons. First, it will (one hopes) flesh out the panel’s reasoning about the text and structure of § 1522: when does authorization morph into an enforceable obligation? Second, it will likely refine the boundary between “entry” and “in-country” conduct—an analytic line that future litigants will contest in cases involving asylum processing overseas, parole decisions, interior enforcement practices, and targeted defunding of domestic programs.

Pacito is not the final word on either doctrine, but it is a timely reminder that immigration law is not monolithic. The legal rules that govern who may cross the border are different in character and origin from the statutory frameworks that govern what the government must do for people here. For advocates, policymakers, and courts, recognizing and litigating along those two tracks will be central to the next wave of immigration disputes.



# Cooperative Agreements as a Litigation Battleground: Grants, Procurement, and Judicial Mandates

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s emergency stay order in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) crystallizes a suite of legal fault lines that will shape refugee policy litigation for years to come. At a high level, the panel divided the battlefield: it showed deference to the President’s sweeping use of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, but it also signaled that certain statutory obligations governing domestic resettlement services—codified in 8 U.S.C. § 1522—may be judicially enforceable under the Administrative Procedure Act. Practically, the court ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted, even as it stayed the district court’s injunctions pending appeal. That mix—deference to the executive on admission policy plus a willingness to require the restoration of service agreements—raises immediate and recurring questions: What exactly are “cooperative agreements” in this context? Are they grants, procurement contracts, or a sui generis government instrument? And to what extent can a court compel the executive to reinstate or manage those arrangements without running afoul of separation-of-powers and practical administrability concerns?

The answer the Ninth Circuit previews is consequential. If §1522 imposes legal duties that courts can enforce, organizations that rely on predictable federal grants and cooperative agreements (from refugee resettlement agencies to health and social-service providers) gain a new litigation lever to challenge executive retrenchment. Conversely, if §1522 is read as permissive, the executive retains broader discretion to realign or pause programs in pursuit of policy goals. The dispute in Pacito highlights how the line between discretionary funding and mandatory programmatic duties can become a litigation battleground.

## What the Ninth Circuit said (and did not say)

The panel’s short, stay-order opinion resolves immediate stakes while signaling how the merits may break down. On the immigration side, the court emphasized the deference afforded to presidential findings under §1182(f)—quoting Trump v. Hawaii—and concluded the government is “likely to prevail” on the challenge to the suspension of admissions. That reasoning justified staying the district court’s injunction against the admissions moratorium.

On the resettlement-services side, however, the court reached the opposite preliminary conclusion: the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based challenge concerning §1522. The panel stressed how §1522 and related regulations authorize provision of reception-and-placement services—employment training, English training, cash and medical assistance, and special responsibilities for unaccompanied minors—and observed that Congress appropriated substantial funding for migration and refugee assistance in FY2025 (subject to later rescissions). Given the government’s termination of cooperative agreements and apparent uncertainty about immediate alternatives, the court directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522” to refugees who have already been admitted.

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part. He agreed with deference on admissions but read §1522 as primarily permissive—emphasizing titles, repeated use of “is authorized,” and qualifications like “to the extent of available appropriations.” From his perspective, §1522 creates a framework of permitted programs and conditions but does not impose a judicially enforceable duty to run those programs; the district court therefore lacked authority to compel their operation.

The panel did not issue a full merits opinion in the stay order; rather, it canvassed the landscape sufficient to justify a stay pending appeal while reserving a full explanation for later.

## Cooperative agreements, grants, and procurement: doctrinal distinctions that matter

At the heart of the dispute are legal and practical differences among three common government instruments: procurement contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements. The Ninth Circuit’s remedial prescription—reinstating “cooperative agreements”—invites immediate legal scrutiny about what courts can require and how.

- Cooperative agreements (and grants) typically implement federal assistance programs under the statutes that authorize them. They are often governed by agency grants regulations (e.g., 2 C.F.R. for uniform grant guidance, agency-specific grant manuals) and by the statutory terms that authorize awarding funds to “public or private nonprofit agencies” to carry out programs. They are distinguishable from procurement contracts, which are governed by procurement statutes and rules (e.g., the Federal Acquisition Regulation) and are intended to acquire goods or services for the government’s direct use.

- Cooperative agreements differ from grants in that they envisage substantial federal involvement in performance. But they are not, for most legal purposes, federal procurement contracts subject to procurement law’s remedies; instead, they sit within the administrative law/grant regime. That regime typically includes less judicially enforceable rights for third parties than procurement law provides for bid protestors or contract claimants. Whether a cooperative agreement gives rise to private enforceable rights depends on statutory text, binding agency commitments, and whether the relevant instrument creates a specific entitlement.

- The Ninth Circuit’s directive to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522” treats the cooperative agreements as the functional mechanism for carrying out statutory duties. If §1522 imposes enforceable obligations on agencies (e.g., to make certain services available “to the extent of available appropriations,” or to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children when “necessary”), courts may order agencies to effectuate statutory duties through the established programmatic vehicles—cooperative agreements among them.

These distinctions matter for relief and administration. Courts can and do order agencies to comply with statutory duties; they can also order agencies to restore specific contractual or grant relationships where those are necessary to fulfill the statutory duty. But ordering wholesale re-contracting, micromanaging selection processes, or directing how proposals are evaluated steps into sensitive territory: it risks substituting judicial management for executive program administration.

## The court’s authority to require reinstatement: legal and practical limits

The Ninth Circuit’s interim direction is modestly tailored: reinstate those cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” Still, even a limited command raises thorny questions.

First, judicial authority under the APA to set aside agency action can translate into affirmative relief—ordering agencies to comply, restore unlawfully terminated agreements, or reopen processes if an agency’s action was arbitrary, capricious, or unlawful. If the district court (and, tentatively, the Ninth Circuit) finds the terminations unlawful under the APA because they contravene statutory duties in §1522, courts can order reinstatement as a remedy. That is conventional equitable relief where it corrects unlawful agency action and restores the status quo while a party’s substantive rights are sorted out on the merits.

Second, the statutory caveat “to the extent of available appropriations” matters. Appropriations law is a limiting factor: a court cannot compel agencies to spend money Congress has not appropriated. But the record in Pacito showed appropriations for migration and refugee assistance for FY2025 (albeit reduced by rescissions). The court invoked those appropriations in concluding the government had not established it could meet its §1522 obligations absent the cooperative agreements. Thus, if appropriations exist and the court concludes the agency unlawfully disrupted statutorily mandated programs funded by those appropriations, a judicial order to reinstate relevant cooperative agreements is within the traditional remedial toolbox.

Third, however, courts should be cautious about imposing operational micromanagement—directing how the State Department should re-compete agreements, select providers, or run day-to-day placement logistics. The judicially administrable relief doctrine counsels courts to confine orders to what is necessary to correct legal violations and to avoid substituting judicial preferences for agency expertise. In practice, careful tailoring matters: ordering reinstatement of specific cooperative agreements that were terminated without lawful basis is different from requiring the agency to run a new procurement on a court-drafted timetable.

Fourth, the interplay of grants-era doctrines and procurement law can complicate third-party expectations. Organizations that had cooperative agreements will argue they had a reliance interest protected by the APA or by specific contractual rights. The government, in contrast, will argue that cooperative agreements were discretionary and revocable—particularly where the statute repeatedly uses “authorized” and where programs depend on available appropriations. Judge Lee’s concurrence underscores this textual argument. Ultimately, the merits will turn on statutory interpretation of §1522’s text and structure, on the significance of the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification, and on the record showing whether the agencies acted arbitrarily or violated statutory process.

## Practical questions: re-competes, continuity, and transitional arrangements

Assuming the district court’s injunction is ultimately affirmed in part, the practical task of reinstating or otherwise ensuring service continuity invites several operational questions:

- Can the State Department simply re-award the terminated cooperative agreements?
  If the terminated agreements were validly entered and the government lacks lawful reasons for termination, reinstatement by mutual agreement or court order is feasible. Where terminations were unilateral and contested, courts sometimes order agencies to reinstate agreements or to reopen competitions. But restoration may be complicated if the original awardees have redeployed staff or shifted capacity.

- Does the government have to re-run procurements or re-open grant competitions?
  Not necessarily. If the cooperative agreements are grant-like and the agencies have statutory duty plus appropriations, the agency can often extend or restore funding to prior providers as an administrable remedy. If procurement statutes govern a particular instrument, re-competition obligations under procurement law might apply. The key is tailoring relief to what is necessary to remedy the statutory violation while minimizing disruption to service continuity.

- Who bears operational responsibility in the interim?
  The court’s order targets reinstatement for refugees already admitted. That is a narrow class compared to prospective applicants overseas. Agencies might preserve continuity by reactivating a subset of agreements focused on reception-and-placement for admitted refugees while deferring broader program-level decisions. Such a piecemeal approach aligns with judicially cautious relief: remedy the immediate statutory injury without deciding broader policy questions on admissions.

- How do appropriations and rescissions affect practical relief?
  Where appropriations exist and have not been lawfully reallocated, courts can order agencies to use those funds for statutorily mandated purposes. But rescissions or explicit congressional instructions could constrain remedial options. Agencies will press those limits vigorously in subsequent filings.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Pacito spotlights several doctrinal and policy implications that will ripple beyond this case.

First, the case sharpens the conflict between immigration exceptionalism—where courts traditionally afford extraordinary deference to the political branches—and ordinary administrative-law norms that allow judicial review of statutorily mandated programs. The Ninth Circuit’s bifurcated approach—deferring on admissions while policing statutory administration of resettlement services—reflects an attempt to thread that needle. If courts broadly accept that statutory implementation duties (even when phrased as “authorization” with appropriations caveats) can be enforced, executive maneuvering through defunding or terminating cooperative agreements will face a new check.

Second, the decision elevates cooperative agreements and grant-like instruments as vehicles for legal challenge. Agencies that attempt to change policy by cutting off grant or cooperative agreement funding should expect litigation that focuses not just on the propriety of the policy, but on whether the agency lawfully administered statutorily mandated programs. That dynamic could lead to more early-stage litigation aimed at preserving program continuity for beneficiaries while broader policy disputes proceed.

Third, the case poses separation-of-powers tensions about the extent to which courts can micromanage program administration. Trial and appellate courts will need to craft remedies that correct statutory violation without intruding on programmatic discretion. The eventual merits opinion will be critical in articulating principled boundaries for such remedial relief.

Finally, the case raises practical pressures on agencies to build contingency plans: maintain records showing deliberative rationales for program terminations, tie programmatic changes clearly to appropriations or statutory authority, and plan transition arrangements that avoid abrupt cessation of services to statutorily protected populations. Litigation risks and operational exigencies may incentivize agencies to adopt slower, more documented transitions when shifting refugee-related programs.

## Bottom line

Pacito is a reminder that the legal character of government instruments matters: cooperative agreements and grants are not mere paper titles, they are the levers through which statutory programs are delivered—and they can be the levers plaintiffs pull to obtain judicial relief. The Ninth Circuit’s interim posture—defer on admissions but order restoration of critical resettlement services—reflects an effort to respect presidential authority in immigration while enforcing administrative-law limits on how the executive implements (or discontinues) statutory programs.

The coming merits opinion will be the test. Will the court adopt Judge Lee’s textualist skepticism—reading §1522 as permissive and therefore beyond the courts’ remedial reach—or will it confirm that §1522, read with its regulatory and appropriations context, creates judicially enforceable obligations that authorize courts to order reinstatement of cooperative agreements? The answer will determine whether cooperative agreements become a durable litigation strategy to temper abrupt programmatic shifts, or whether agencies retain broader latitude to realign assistance programs by reallocating or suspending cooperative agreements. Either way, refugee resettlement providers, litigators, and agencies should be watching closely—both for doctrinal guidance and for the practical shape of any remedial orders the courts ultimately fashion.



# Harmonizing the Refugee Act with 1182(f): How Much Room Is Left for Congress’s Framework?

## Why this matters

Last week’s Ninth Circuit stay in Pacito v. Trump—issued while the panel prepares a full opinion—put front and center a recurring and high-stakes tension in immigration law: how to reconcile the President’s sweeping § 1182(f) authority to suspend the entry of classes of aliens with the Refugee Act’s careful statutory framework for admitting and assisting refugees. The stay, and the court’s preliminary reading, signal that lower courts will continue to treat presidential entry suspensions with heavy deference after Trump v. Hawaii, while still probing whether Congress has imposed judicially enforceable statutory obligations that the executive cannot simply sidestep by suspending admissions or slashing program funding.

Two features of the Ninth Circuit’s interim order bear emphasis. First, the panel reiterated that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and that the President’s finding that refugee admissions “would be detrimental” satisfies the statutory prerequisite identified in Hawaii; the government is “likely to prevail” on the claim that the President can suspend refugee admissions. Second, the court concluded the government is not likely to prevail on all fronts: at least one claim under the Administrative Procedure Act (and tied to 8 U.S.C. § 1522) appears plausible — namely that the government’s termination of cooperative agreements providing reception-and-placement services may have violated statutory duties to provide certain services to refugees already admitted.

That dual posture—extreme deference to the suspension power coupled with scrutiny of statutory service obligations—frames the doctrinal questions explored below: can a global moratorium under § 1182(f) coexist with the Refugee Act’s admissions ceiling, consultation processes, and statutory duties? If so, how should courts draw lines that give meaning to both statutes?

## The statutory tension: breadth versus framework

At the heart of the problem is a clash of statutory imagery. Section 1182(f) is capacious: whenever the President “finds” that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens “would be detrimental,” he “may” suspend entry “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” It speaks in plain, sweeping terms about entry and grants the President unilateral, largely unreviewable discretion to bar classes of entrants.

By contrast, the Refugee Act (the INA’s refugee provisions and companion statutes like 8 U.S.C. § 1522) is procedural and programmatic. It establishes an admissions ceiling (the presidential determination setting annual refugee admissions), an interbranch consultation process (the President’s consultative responsibilities with Congress), mechanisms for allocating visas and admissions, and a panoply of domestic resettlement services—some described in mandatory language in certain contexts and other provisions couched as authorizations “to the extent of available appropriations.” Congress envisioned an ongoing admissions program tied to appropriations, reports, and specified duties to facilitate resettlement.

Read bluntly, the two statutes can point in different directions: § 1182(f) authorizes an abrupt cessation of entry; the Refugee Act envisions a continuing system of admission and resettlement. The question for courts is whether one statute displaces the other, whether they can be harmonized, or whether statutory and constitutional doctrines constrain one when applied to the other.

## Arguments for reading the statutes in pari materia (harmonization)

There are persuasive reasons to look for a harmonious reading that gives effect to both statutes rather than allowing one to swallow the other.

First, ordinary canons of construction favor reading statutes to avoid surplusage and conflict where possible. The Refugee Act is Congress’s comprehensive effort to fix immigration and resettlement policy for refugees; it would be surprising if Congress meant to vest the President with a freely exercisable unilateral power effectively to nullify the carefully constructed statutory program without subjecting that power to any limiting principle.

Second, many of the Refugee Act’s provisions are tied to appropriations and reporting requirements. Courts could hold that § 1182(f) permits temporary, specific suspensions of entry for demonstrated security or foreign-policy reasons but does not permit the executive to abrogate statutory duties that Congress has tied to appropriations and that ensure accountability—e.g., periodic reporting, consultation with Congress, and the maintenance of services to refugees already admitted. In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit appears willing to treat obligations concerning reception and placement services as independently enforceable, at least for refugees already admitted.

Third, harmonization can be achieved by parsing the different spheres of the statutes: § 1182(f) is about the admission decision, while the Refugee Act governs the structure and operation of refugee resettlement inside the United States. Under that reading, a lawful suspension of admissions might coexist with ongoing statutorily mandated duties to those already admitted (and possibly to processing and applicants abroad, in limited circumstances), preserving Congress’s policy choices regarding resettlement support without trenching on the President’s foreign-policy and national-security prerogatives.

## Risks of a broad reading of § 1182(f)

Despite arguments for harmonization, there are real hazards in allowing § 1182(f) to be read as an essentially plenary override of the Refugee Act.

If courts treat § 1182(f) as an unbounded trump card, Congress’s carefully constructed levers become hollow. The annual presidential determination evincing a refugee ceiling, the consultation obligations, and the statutory architecture for domestic services would be reduced to mere formalities that the President could suspend at will. That is especially troubling where Congress has provided specific statutory duties—such as reporting and funding authorizations—that reflect legislative choices about the nation’s humanitarian commitments.

There are also practical consequences. A permissive view of § 1182(f) encourages administrations to use procedural devices—funding withdrawals, termination of inter-agency cooperative agreements, or broad findings of “detriment”—to halt refugee resettlement while avoiding the political and diplomatic frictions of explicit permanent bans. That risks destabilizing longstanding cooperative frameworks with NGOs and foreign partners and could lead to a churn of litigation about who qualifies as a “class” of aliens or what “finding” suffices.

Finally, an unbounded suspension power implicates separation-of-powers and rule-of-law concerns: if the President may, by fiat, undo statutory programs without meaningful constraints, Congress’s lawmaking power is weakened and private parties cannot rely on statutory schemes that Congress designed to be durable. These are the sorts of structural considerations courts should consider when resolving statutory tension.

## Doctrinal pathways for courts to cabin suspension power

Lower courts confronting this tension will likely draw on several doctrinal tools to cabin § 1182(f) while respecting presidential authority recognized in Hawaii. Those limiting principles are not mutually exclusive.

1. Textual limitation and statutes-in-pare-materia: Courts can distinguish between the President’s power over admission (entry) and Congress’s power to structure what happens after admission. Under that approach, a suspension under § 1182(f) could lawfully bar future admissions but would not allow the executive to avoid duties Congress has tied to admitted refugees’ welfare—especially where statutory language uses mandatory terms or imposes reporting/appropriations-based constraints.

2. Chevron/deference plus major-questions skepticism: When an agency interprets § 1522 or similar provisions to forgo statutorily indicated duties, courts may apply heightened scrutiny (the “major questions” framework) because the stakes—national refugee policy and large-scale suspension of a statutory program—are politically and economically significant. That could limit agency readings that effectively nullify the Refugee Act, even where some deference would ordinarily be due.

3. Constitutional-avoidance and findings requirements: Courts might require that § 1182(f) finders be specific and supported by adequate factual findings when the suspension would substantially frustrate congressional objectives. Deference to a national-security finding remains substantial, but the more a suspension undermines Congress’s statutory program, the greater the justification the executive must provide. This is roughly consistent with Hawaii’s recognition of deference but also respects the text and purpose of congressional enactments.

4. Temporal and scope-based limits: Courts can cabin suspensions by insisting they be temporary, targeted, and tailored to particular risks (for example, specific regions, entry pathways, or categories tied to demonstrated threats), rather than global and indefinite moratoria that eviscerate statutory programs.

5. Reliance and administrative-law protections: Where Congress has authorized discrete cooperative agreements with NGOs and required accountability/reporting, courts may enforce reliance interests and procedural protections under the APA, as the Ninth Circuit did insofar as it entertained an APA-based claim regarding terminated cooperative agreements.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito illustrates the other side of the doctrinal debate: he reads § 1522 primarily as authorizing programs, not commanding them—the title, “Authorization for programs,” and repeated “is authorized” constructions indicate permissive, discretionary authority. That textual posture makes it harder to force the executive to continue funding or to mandate the operation of cooperative agreements. Even if § 1522 is largely permissive, however, some subsections arguably use “shall” and impose obligations in specific contexts (e.g., duties regarding unaccompanied minors or programs “as needed”), and the interplay of those provisions with appropriations creates room for judicial enforcement in particular cases.

## Potential downstream effects

How courts resolve this statutory tension will shape a number of real-world consequences.

For the executive branch, a permissive reading of § 1182(f) will encourage greater reliance on blanket suspensions and funding maneuvers to achieve policy ends with little judicial pushback. That may yield rapid policy shifts but at the cost of programmatic instability and strained relations with resettlement partners.

For Congress, such judicial readings increase the incentive to legislate more clearly—either by expressly limiting § 1182(f), by setting non-waivable statutory mandates for services to admitted refugees, or by using appropriations riders to constrain executive action. Expect legislative proposals to pin down the interaction between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act in response to high-profile suspensions.

For the courts, the tension will generate recurring cases testing the contours of “finding” under § 1182(f), the enforceability of statutory resettlement duties, and the appropriate standard of review. Pacito’s approach—deferring to admission suspensions while policing statutory obligations tied to residency and services—offers a template: afford the President broad latitude over who may enter, but do not permit that latitude to be exercised in a way that eviscerates Congress’s post-admission statutory architecture without sufficient justification.

For refugees and resettlement organizations, doctrinal outcomes matter a great deal. A judicial rule that allows a suspension to wipe out cooperative agreements and programs would upend lives and institutional capacity. A rule that protects statutorily mandated services for already-admitted refugees, while allowing temporary suspensions of new admissions, protects settled expectations and the continuity of humanitarian assistance.

## Conclusion

Pacito does not resolve the doctrinal contest between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act, but it crystallizes the approach courts are likely to adopt: strong deference to presidential findings that bar entry, paired with willingness to enforce Congress’s statutory framework where the executive’s actions reach into the post-admission sphere Congress has regulated. That middle course preserves the President’s authority to protect the national interest while preventing the executive from using entry suspensions as a backdoor to nullify statutory programs and obligations. The more concrete question now is how the forthcoming Ninth Circuit opinion will articulate limiting principles—textual, procedural, or prudential—that balance respectful deference against Congress’s role in framing refugee policy. The answer will shape the future of refugee law, executive power, and the durability of the statutory commitments Congress has made to those admitted into the United States.



# From Travel Bans to Program Halts: The Trajectory of Judicial Review in National Security Immigration

## Why Pacito matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim stay in Pacito v. Trump is the latest—and instructive—chapter in a decade-long realignment of how federal courts approach presidential authority over immigration. On one hand, Pacito reaffirms the powerful presumption of deference to the President’s determinations about who may enter the country, echoing the Supreme Court’s approach in Trump v. Hawaii. On the other, it signals that such deference has limits: courts remain willing to police statutory obligations governing what the government must do inside the United States, especially where domestic programs, benefits, and contracts are implicated and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) provides a vehicle for challenge.

That combination—maximal latitude at the border coupled with robust judicial enforcement of statutory constraints on domestic implementation—is now a pattern. Pacito illustrates the consequences: an executive order halting refugee admissions can be shielded by sweeping deference, while contemporaneous programmatic steps—defunding resettlement service providers and terminating cooperative agreements—remain vulnerable to judicial intervention. The case therefore offers a roadmap for both government litigation strategy and for advocates seeking to hold agencies accountable when entry restrictions are matched by domestic programmatic consequences.

## What the Ninth Circuit did (and did not) say

The panel issued a stay of the district court’s injunctions against President Trump’s Executive Order No. 14163 (suspending refugee admissions) and the State Department’s defunding actions, pending appeal. In doing so the court applied the familiar four-factor Nken test for a stay and placed particular emphasis on the two “most critical” factors: likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm.

On the merits of the entry suspension under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the Ninth Circuit was unequivocal: the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the statute’s sole textual prerequisite—the President’s finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Quoting Trump v. Hawaii, the panel stressed that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power” to impose entry restrictions. That language drove the court’s conclusion that the government is likely to prevail on challenges to the EO’s suspension of admissions.

But the panel also identified a statutory line the government had trouble crossing. Relying on 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related regulations, the court found that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on APA claims challenging the termination of cooperative agreements and defunding of reception-and-placement services. The opinion framed § 1522 as creating an obligation—subject to appropriations—that requires the Office of Refugee Resettlement (and the responsible Secretaries) to make certain services available to refugees after admission. Given the government’s apparent uncertainty about replacing contract partners and ensuring service delivery, the court directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to furnish “usual and customary” reception-and-placement services to refugees who had already been admitted.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent crystallizes the tension animating the case. He agreed with the panel’s deference to presidential authority on admissions but argued that § 1522 is a permissive authorization, not a mandate. For Lee, the statute authorizes—but does not compel—the executive to operate refugee resettlement programs, and many of § 1522’s provisions read as conditional on appropriations and executive choice. His view would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full.

## Why this reasoning matters doctrinally

Pacito does three doctrinal things worth noting.

First, it continues the post-Hawaii trend of generous judicial deference to pure entry restrictions framed as national-security determinations under § 1182(f). Courts confronted with facial or as-applied challenges to high-level presidential proclamations barring admission will likely find it difficult to prevail if the government can point to an express presidential finding and national-security framing.

Second, Pacito reaffirms the viability of the APA and statutory-enforcement claims as a counterbalance to that deference. Even where the government can lawfully suspend entry, it cannot necessarily dismantle the domestic infrastructure Congress created for admitted refugees without confronting statutory constraints. The panel’s reading of § 1522 as imposing enforceable duties (at least to the extent of appropriations) provides a blueprint for litigants to convert programmatic harms into judicially cognizable claims.

Third, the opinion sharpens the importance of statutory drafting and appropriations language. The government’s obligations under § 1522 are qualified—“to the extent of available appropriations”—but the panel treated those qualifications as important, not dispositive: Congress’s allocation of migration and refugee funds, together with statutory reporting and programmatic design provisions, can create enforceable standards that courts will police if the executive takes sudden programmatic action inconsistent with those statutes.

## Downstream effects and likely future litigation targets

Pacito points to a likely division of labor in future immigration litigation: entry policy will continue to be a domain of expansive executive discretion; domestic program implementation will remain fair game for APA and statutory claims.

What will litigants challenge next?

- Reception-and-placement and benefits administration. Lawsuits will continue to target terminations of cooperative agreements, halts in grants, and abrupt changes in cash, medical, and employment services for newly admitted refugees. These are the fact-patterns that translate easily into APA claims and statutory-entitlement arguments.

- Grantmaking, procurement, and cooperative agreements. Agencies that abruptly cancel agreements without statutory or regulatory process risk suits alleging arbitrary-and-capricious or procedurally defective action. The Pacito injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements exemplifies this vulnerability.

- Unaccompanied minors and custodial duties. Statutory formulations that impose caretaker duties (even phrased as discretionary) will attract litigation if agencies decline to act and harm or neglect is plausibly alleged.

- Reporting and oversight provisions. Statutory accountability measures—reporting requirements, inspection regimes, and Congressional earmarks—can supply plaintiffs with causes of action or at least strong support in equitable relief efforts.

Where litigation is less likely to succeed: pure challenges to admission suspensions framed as national-security or foreign-affairs determinations. Unless plaintiffs can show that the proclamation or EO exceeded a clear textual limit in § 1182(f) or was based on an insincere or pretextual “finding,” courts will be reluctant to substitute their judgment.

## Final observations

Pacito cements a predictable pattern: the judiciary will bow out of second-guessing presidential findings about who gets in, but it will not abdicate when the executive’s actions touch the domestic statutory architecture Congress has erected. That division of labor channels contestation toward programmatic and administrative corners of immigration law—where statutory text, appropriations, cooperative agreements, and the APA remain powerful tools for accountability.

For practitioners and advocates, the lesson is strategic. Where the avenue of challenging admissions is narrow, litigation energy will remain productively spent on protecting service delivery, benefits, and contract-backed supports for those already within the United States—or already approved for admission. For agencies, Pacito is a warning: national-security deference does not immunize programmatic retrenchment from judicial scrutiny.
