Law Stack Exchange Q&A: Pacito_v_Trump

### How does § 1182(f) interact with the Refugee Act’s admissions framework under § 1157?
Question:
The panel says the President likely has authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee entry and cites Trump v. Hawaii. But refugee admissions are governed specifically by 8 U.S.C. § 1157 (annual caps, consultations). Why didn’t the more specific Refugee Act framework limit or displace the more general § 1182(f) authority here? What canons did the court implicitly apply to reconcile these statutes?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treats §1182(f) and the Refugee Act as complementary, not contradictory. It relies on Trump v. Hawaii to read §1182(f) as a broad, stand‑alone grant that can suspend the entry of a class of aliens (including refugees), while leaving the Refugee Act’s procedural provisions (caps, admissions procedures, and post‑admission service statutes) operative for refugees who are admitted. That reconciliation rests on ordinary canons: harmonize statutes where possible; the specific programmatic provisions of the Refugee Act govern how refugees are processed and what services are provided after admission, but they do not implicitly repeal or cabin the clear, textual suspension power in §1182(f) absent a contrary (and clear) congressional statement.

What the opinion actually says (and relies on)
- The panel quotes Trump v. Hawaii and repeatedly emphasizes the breadth of §1182(f): it “’exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. at 684). On the merits the panel again says, quoting Hawaii, that “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry ... ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Slip op. at DISCUSSION (pp. ___) (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 685).
- At the same time the panel takes the Refugee Act seriously for what it is: programmatic law governing resettlement services. It cites 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services §1522 describes. DISCUSSION (pp. ___).

How the statutes are reconciled (the implicit canons and reasoning)
1. Harmonization (read statutes to avoid conflict). The court treats §1182(f) as addressing the Executive’s authority to control entry and the Refugee Act (§1157, §1522, etc.) as addressing how refugees are processed and what services are provided once admitted. Because the two statutes operate in different spheres (admission suspension vs. administration of resettlement services), the court reads them together where possible rather than holding one displaced by the other. The opinion’s twin holdings — (a) the President likely may suspend refugee entry under §1182(f) (citing Hawaii), and (b) the government must provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted under §1522 — reflect that harmonizing approach.

2. Specific vs. general only controls when there is a true conflict. The traditional canon (“specific governs general”) would limit a general grant only when a more specific provision directly conflicts. The panel treats §1157/Refugee Act as specific on admission process, caps, and resettlement programming, but not as a specific limitation on the textual, standalone suspension authority in §1182(f). Instead, the panel follows Trump v. Hawaii’s reading that §1182(f) can be exercised “in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA],” i.e., it supplements rather than is superseded by other INA provisions. See Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684).

3. Disfavor of implied repeal / clear‑statement principle. The opinion implies (consistent with settled canons) that Congress would speak plainly if it intended to strip the President of a power as broad as §1182(f). Because the Refugee Act contains admissions mechanics and appropriations‑qualified program authorizations (see, e.g., §1522’s “to the extent of available appropriations” language cited in the opinion), the court does not infer a congressional intent to negate §1182(f)’s suspension power without an explicit statutory limit. That approach is visible where the panel treats §1182(f)’s “sole prerequisite” (the President’s finding) as dispositive. DISCUSSION at pp. ___ (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 685).

4. Functional separation of powers / deference in immigration. The court leans on the strong judicial deference recognized in Hawaii for national‑security and entry decisions—an interpretive posture that colors its reconciliation: §1182(f) is read to give the Executive broad discretion over entry, while statutory program obligations (like §1522) constrain how the government must act with respect to those already admitted. See DISCUSSION (pp. ___) (“we cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.’ Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686”).

What this means in practice (how the panel applied those principles)
- The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on the claim that the President can suspend refugee admissions under §1182(f). That follows from Trump v. Hawaii’s interpretation of §1182(f) as a broad, independent grant of authority.
- The panel nonetheless held that the Refugee Act’s program provisions (here §1522) impose obligations concerning services to refugees who have been admitted, and the court ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide those statutory reception-and-placement services to admitted refugees. That illustrates the harmonizing approach: §1182(f) governs entry; §1522 governs post‑admission services.

Limitations of the panel’s approach (and why Judge Lee dissented in part)
- Judge Lee’s separate opinion underscores the tension: she reads §1522 as largely discretionary (authorization, not mandate), and would therefore not order reinstatement of cooperative agreements. That disagreement shows the thin line between treating Refugee Act provisions as programmatic (and thus compatible with a suspension) and treating some Refugee Act language as imposing enforceable duties for admitted refugees.

Bottom line
The panel reconciles §1182(f) and the Refugee Act by: (1) reading §1182(f) per Trump v. Hawaii as a broad textual grant to suspend entry (including refugee admissions), (2) treating the Refugee Act as the governing framework for admissions mechanics and for services to refugees once admitted, and (3) applying ordinary canons—harmonization, disfavoring implied repeal, and deference in immigration—to avoid treating the Refugee Act as displacing §1182(f) unless Congress clearly said so. The opinion therefore allows a presidential suspension of refugee entries while still enforcing statutory obligations under §1522 for refugees already admitted.

### What standard of review applies to the President’s § 1182(f) findings post-Hawaii?
Question:
The order says the court cannot engage in a “searching inquiry” into the President’s justifications and quotes Hawaii’s deferential language. Is this effectively rational-basis review? How does the court decide whether the President has “undoubtedly fulfilled” the sole prerequisite (a detriment finding) without probing pretext or evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applies the extremely deferential review that the Supreme Court spelled out in Trump v. Hawaii — not ordinary rational‑basis review, but a near‑noninquiry focused on two narrow legal questions: (1) did the President make the statutory “finding” that §1182(f) requires, and (2) does the President’s action exceed any textual limit in the statute. The court accepts the President’s stated finding as sufficient (“the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here”) and refuses to engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” That is deference to the existence and legal scope of the finding, not an assessment of whether the finding is factually true under a typical merits test.

Explanation of the controlling doctrine and how the opinion applies it
- The governing precedent is Trump v. Hawaii, which the panel cites repeatedly. Hawaii teaches that §1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and that courts should not undertake a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The panel quotes that language and frames its analysis around it. See opinion’s recitation: “We observed that the authority under which the President issued Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), ‘exudes deference’ …” and later: “we cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.’ Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686.”  

- The panel identifies the statutory test as a single, textual prerequisite: the President must “find[] that the entry of any aliens … would be detrimental to the interest of the United States.” The court treats that requirement as a threshold, formal element of the statute: “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” In other words, the court’s review is limited to whether the statutory form/condition exists, not to a searching factual review of the merits that led the President to the finding.

- That limited review is distinct from classic rational‑basis review. Rational‑basis inquiry normally asks whether a government action is rationally related to a legitimate purpose and can involve looking at factual record and competing justifications. Here, by contrast, the court (following Hawaii) declines to weigh the persuasiveness of the President’s national‑security or foreign‑policy reasons, and instead asks only whether the President acted within the text of §1182(f). The opinion says as much: the court concluded that “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” That is legal, text‑based review — not a fact‑intensive balancing test.

How the court decides “undoubtedly fulfilled” without probing pretext or evidence
- The phrase “undoubtedly fulfilled” signals acceptance of the President’s formal finding as legally sufficient on its face. Practically, the panel looks for (a) the President’s formal statement or proclamation that he has made the finding required by §1182(f), and (b) whether the proclamation stays within the statute’s textual bounds (e.g., applies to “entry” of a class of aliens and does not contravene other clear statutory limits). Because the statute vests the President with “broad discretion” and contains a clear, single prerequisite (a finding), the court will not probe the factual basis for that finding — it will accept the President’s stated determination unless the action exceeds the statute’s text or other controlling limits. See opinion: “The sole prerequisite … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here,” and later: “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.”

- In short, the court performs a narrow, legalistic check: (1) Is there a formal, cognizable finding? (2) Does the President stay within the textual scope of §1182(f)? If both answers are yes, the court will not excavate the evidence behind the President’s assertion or treat the finding as subject to a searching factual inquiry. That is precisely what the panel does here — it finds the formal elements present and concludes the government is likely to prevail on that statutory claim.

What this is not
- This is not an open‑ended rational‑basis or pretext inquiry. The court expressly refuses to engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.”  
- It also does not mean the President’s actions are immune from all judicial review. The panel distinguishes the §1182(f) question from other statutory or administrative claims (e.g., APA claims about providing resettlement services under §1522), where ordinary review may apply. The opinion illustrates that deferential §1182(f) review is narrow and does not displace other statutory obligations that the government may have to third parties.

Bottom line for practice
- After Hawaii, and as reflected in this opinion, a court reviewing a §1182(f) suspension will apply extreme deference: it will verify that the President made a formal statutory finding and will check only whether the executive action exceeds the statute’s text or other clear legal limits. Courts will not undertake a searching factual or “persuasiveness” inquiry into the President’s national‑security or foreign‑policy justifications.

### Why does the court evaluate the § 1182(f) challenge outside the APA framework?
Question:
Franklin v. Massachusetts says the APA doesn’t apply to the President. Here, plaintiffs also brought APA claims, but the panel’s § 1182(f) analysis seems to rest on non-APA review (constitutional/statutory). What is the jurisdictional basis for reviewing presidential action here, and are agencies’ implementing actions the real target for judicial review?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treated the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as a matter for statutory/constitutional review rather than APA rulemaking review because the APA does not govern the President himself. At the same time the court made clear that the implementation steps taken by agencies (the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and the HHS duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522) are subject to ordinary judicial review and can be enjoined if they conflict with statutory obligations. In other words: the proclamation is evaluated on statutory/textual and constitutional grounds (with the extreme deference described in Trump v. Hawaii); the agencies’ implementing actions are the proper, judicially reviewable targets when plaintiffs challenge discrete agency conduct (and the court in fact ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements under § 1522).

Why the court steps outside the APA for the § 1182(f) claim
- The APA provides a mechanism for reviewing “agency” action, not actions of the President. Franklin v. Massachusetts stands for that separation: the APA does not apply to the President. The opinion here relies on that principle (and on Trump v. Hawaii) in treating the President’s invocation of § 1182(f) as a distinct, non-APA inquiry. See the opinion’s reliance on Trump v. Hawaii language that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and that the “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Op. at 2–3 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684–85). That framing is statutory/textual review of the President’s delegated power, not APA notice-and-comment or arbitrary-and-capricious review of an agency rule.

What law governs review of the President’s § 1182(f) action
- The court frames the § 1182(f) question as one of statutory scope and constitutional limits: does the order exceed what § 1182(f) permits (and if so, does that raise constitutional problems)? The panel used Trump v. Hawaii to explain that § 1182(f) vests broad, deferential presidential authority to suspend entry, and concluded the President satisfied the statutory predicate (“the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here”). Op. at 3. That is the classic approach when the plaintiff challenges the President’s statutory authority: analyze the statute’s text and any constitutional constraints, not the APA’s procedural standards.

What provides jurisdiction to the courts here — and why agencies matter
- Plaintiffs sued not only the President but also challenged concrete agency actions (the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements; HHS responsibilities under 8 U.S.C. § 1522). Those agency actions are classically reviewable under the APA and under statutory-compulsory-duty principles. The panel explicitly addresses § 1522 duties and funding provisions, treats the agencies’ conduct as subject to judicial enforcement, and directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522. Op. at 10–13 (directing reinstatement in light of § 1522 and the government’s admitted termination of agreements).

- Put another way: the President’s proclamation is a policy decision (reviewed as a statutory/textual matter with strong deference). But when that policy is executed through agency acts—terminating contracts, cutting funding, or failing to perform statutory duties—those discrete agency acts fall within the courts’ ordinary review power (APA and statutory enforcement). The opinion embodies precisely that split: substantial deference to the President under § 1182(f) (and thus a high bar to invalidate the proclamation itself), while enforcing statutory obligations by agencies under § 1522 when those obligations appear unfulfilled (hence the order to reinstate cooperative agreements).

Relevant parts of the opinion supporting this split
- “Franklin … the APA doesn’t apply to the President” is the premise for treating the proclamation differently from agency action (see the panel’s reliance on Trump v. Hawaii and the court’s description of § 1182(f) as conferring “broad discretion” and supplying only a single prerequisite: the President’s finding). Op. at 2–3.
- The panel’s conclusion that the government is “likely to prevail” on the § 1182(f) claim follows from that statutory/textual reading: “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Op. at 3.
- By contrast, the panel explicitly treats the § 1522/agency claims as justiciable: it explains the statutory duties in § 1522, recounts the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements, notes the government’s representation about seeking new vendors, and then directs reinstatement of agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” Op. at 10–13.

A procedural takeaway for law students
- When litigation challenges immigration policy tied to a presidential proclamation under § 1182(f), expect two different analytical tracks: (1) the proclamation itself will be reviewed on statutory and constitutional grounds with heavy deference (not under the APA); (2) any implementing agency conduct—contract terminations, funding decisions, failure to perform statutory duties—remains subject to APA/statutory review and injunction. This opinion follows that pattern: it sustains strong deference to the President’s § 1182(f) finding while policing agency compliance with § 1522 and granting relief aimed at agency implementation.

Note on internal disagreement
- Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent highlights that reasonable statutory interpretation questions remain about whether § 1522 is mandatory or permissive; that debate explains why the court gave careful attention to agency duties rather than collapsing all review into a single APA or presidential-review box. Op. at 13–15 (Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

Conclusion
- The APA doesn’t provide the framework for reviewing the President’s § 1182(f) invocation; statutory/textual and constitutional analysis does. But courts retain jurisdiction to review and enjoin agency implementing acts (and to enforce statutory duties like § 1522), and the opinion expressly exercises that power by ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements even while upholding the President’s discretion under § 1182(f).

### What gives the Ninth Circuit authority to order affirmative relief (reinstating cooperative agreements) in a stay order?
Question:
The panel stays the district court’s injunctions but simultaneously directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements needed to provide § 1522 services to already-admitted refugees. Under what authority can an appellate court issue such mandatory, interim relief while granting a stay pending appeal? Is this grounded in the All Writs Act or the court’s equitable power to preserve the status quo ante?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel relied on its ordinary equitable authority to grant interim (mandatory) relief as part of a stay pending appeal — i.e., the appellate court’s power to fashion relief necessary to preserve the status quo and to protect rights that the district court’s injunction was meant to secure — rather than on the All Writs Act. The opinion frames that authority through the familiar stay factors (Nken) and the court’s conclusion that the APA/§1522 claim is likely meritorious as to services for already‑admitted refugees, and then directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements as the narrowly tailored interim relief needed to protect those statutory interests while the appeal proceeds.

Why that is what the opinion says
- The opinion opens the legal analysis with the stay standard and explicitly applies the four Nken factors (slip op. § “The standards for issuing a stay are well established. We consider four factors… Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009).”). That framing shows the court treated its order as a stay-pending-appeal decision, not as a final merits ruling under a different statute.
- The panel found (for purposes of the stay) that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s §1182(f) authority, but it also concluded the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. §1522. The court therefore limited the stay and explained why affirmative interim relief is necessary: the statute (§1522) creates statutory duties concerning reception-and-placement services for refugees (the opinion quotes §1522(a)(1)(A) and cites (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A)), Congress has appropriated funds for migration/refugee assistance, and the government had terminated cooperative agreements and conceded uncertainty about whether replacement arrangements were in place. See slip op. ¶¶ describing §1522 and government’s termination of agreements; slip op. (“the government advised the panel … it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services”).
- Against that factual and legal backdrop, the panel “directed the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” (slip op., final paragraph). That relief is presented as interim, narrow, and tied to preserving the relief the district court granted for refugees already admitted — i.e., keeping in place the services that §1522 protects while the §1522/APA issue is litigated on the merits.

Why this is equitable stay authority (not the All Writs Act)
- The opinion never cites or invokes the All Writs Act; instead it repeatedly grounds the action in the stay framework and in §1522’s statutory text. The sequence is: apply Nken stay factors → conclude a portion of the district court injunction should be stayed → but, because the §1522 claim likely succeeds and because the government eliminated cooperative agreements and cannot show replacement services, order reinstatement of the specific cooperative agreements necessary to provide §1522 services to admitted refugees. That is the classic equitable-court approach to interim relief: preserve the status quo and protect the parties’ rights pending appeal when otherwise irreparable harm would occur.
- The court’s remedy is tailored to preserve the district court’s protective aim for a class of beneficiaries (refugees already admitted) rather than to rewrite policy across the board. That tailoring reflects equitable remedial authority an appellate court exercises when granting or conditioning a stay (see the opinion’s use of the Nken factors and the selective carve‑outs described earlier in the opinion).

Additional context in the opinion
- The panel’s injunction-directed relief is explicitly limited: the district court’s injunctions “are stayed in their entirety, except as specified,” and the affirmative direction only applies to cooperative agreements needed to deliver §1522 services to refugees already admitted (slip op.). Judge Lee’s partial dissent confirms the panel’s framing as a statutory‑interpretation dispute over whether §1522 is mandatory or permissive — he disputes the merits but not that the panel fashioned the interim relief in light of its reading of §1522 (Concurring/Dissenting opin.).
- That confirms the panel viewed its order as an equitable, interlocutory remedy tied to its preliminary assessment of the likelihood of success on the §1522 claim and the need to prevent irreparable injury while the appeal proceeds.

Bottom line
The authority for ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements in this stay order comes from the court’s equitable power to grant and condition stays pending appeal — specifically to preserve the status quo and the practical effectiveness of the district court’s injunction for persons the panel deemed likely protected by §1522 — not from any All Writs Act citation. The opinion grounds the directive in the Nken stay framework plus the panel’s preliminary statutory conclusion that §1522 likely imposes enforceable duties as to services for already‑admitted refugees.

### Why apply Nken’s stay factors instead of Winter’s preliminary injunction test here?
Question:
The panel frames its analysis under Nken v. Holder. Given the posture (appeal from preliminary injunctions), how do the Nken factors differ from Winter’s test, and why are success on the merits and irreparable harm treated as the “most critical” on a stay pending appeal?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the panel was deciding a stay pending appeal (whether the government — the stay applicant — should be allowed to suspend the district court’s preliminary injunctions while the appeal is resolved), Nken governs. Winter governs whether a district court may issue a preliminary injunction in the first instance. The two tests ask similar questions but operate in different procedural postures and place different burdens and emphases on the moving party — which is why the opinion correctly frames its analysis under Nken and explains that the two “most critical” factors are success on the merits and irreparable injury.

Why Nken (not Winter) applies here
- Procedural posture: The court is ruling on a motion for a stay pending appeal of district-court preliminary injunctions. Nken v. Holder sets the controlling standard for stays pending appeal; the panel explicitly cites and applies Nken’s four-factor test (opinion § “The standards for issuing a stay are well established. We consider four factors…” (citing Nken, 556 U.S. at 434)). Winter, by contrast, governs whether a district court should grant a preliminary injunction in the first instance. The opinion therefore uses the correct doctrinal hook for the question before it.

How the tests differ (practical and doctrinal differences)
- Same four considerations, different emphasis and burden. Both Winter and Nken look to (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable harm, (3) balance of harms/equities, and (4) public interest. But:
  - Burden: In Winter the movant seeking a preliminary injunction (usually the plaintiff) must show it is likely to succeed on the merits and likely to suffer irreparable harm absent an injunction. In Nken the movant for a stay pending appeal (here the government) must show a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits and that it will be irreparably injured without a stay. The appellate stay context often requires a higher “strong showing” because a stay disturbs the district court’s order.
  - Purpose and scope: Winter assesses whether forward-looking, prophylactic relief is justified as an equitable remedy. Nken focuses on preserving the status quo and the parties’ appellate rights while an appeal is decided; it therefore tailors the inquiry to factors relevant to a temporary suspension of district-court relief.
  - Deference and caution: Nken recognizes additional institutional considerations (the interest in not upsetting a district court’s interim decision absent strong reasons), and courts frequently describe the first two factors (merits and irreparable injury) as “the most critical” in the stay context because those justify overriding the district court’s interim decision while an appellate court reviews it.

Why success on the merits and irreparable harm are “most critical” here
- The panel follows Nken’s express directive: “Of these factors, the first two—the showing of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are ‘the most critical.’” The panel therefore focuses its primary analysis on whether the government has made a strong showing of likely success (so that upsetting the injunction is warranted) and whether the government will suffer irreparable harm without a stay (so that delay of appellate review would cause non-compensable injury).
- Application in the opinion: the court finds the government “is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” relying on deference expressed in Trump v. Hawaii and the plain language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (opinion text noting the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the §1182(f) prerequisite and that the language is “clear”). That is the merits prong of Nken. The court then notes the remaining factors (balance of harms and public interest) also favor the government with respect to the suspension of admissions. At the same time the opinion identifies a different merits result on one APA claim (§1522), explaining why the government is not likely to prevail on that discrete statutory claim and therefore directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for those admitted refugees — demonstrating the panel’s application of Nken’s merits focus to the specific legal questions presented.
- Why irreparable injury matters: In stay practice, irreparable injury is central because a stay postpones the district court’s remedial order and can produce non-recoverable consequences (for the parties or the public interest) during the appeal. Nken’s emphasis reflects that practical reality: absent a strong showing of both merits and irreparable harm, the appellate court should not upset the district court’s interim relief.

Bottom line
- Winter governs whether a district court should issue a preliminary injunction. Nken governs whether an appellate court should stay (i.e., suspend) a district court’s injunction pending appeal. The opinion correctly invokes Nken, quotes its four factors, and — consistent with Nken — treats likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury as the “most critical” because those are the core justifications required to override a district court’s interim decision while appellate review proceeds. The panel then applies those factors to the specific statutory provisions at issue (8 U.S.C. §1182(f) and §1522), explaining where the government has a strong merits showing and where it does not.

### How does the panel justify limiting relief to refugees already admitted to the U.S.?
Question:
The order preserves services for “refugees who have been admitted” but not for those abroad or conditionally approved. Is that because § 1522’s obligations only attach upon admission? How do provisions like § 1522(d)(2)(B) (unaccompanied minor refugees “in transit”) factor into this boundary?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel limits mandatory relief to refugees already physically in the United States because, as the panel reads the statute, the operative duties in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 are tied to post‑admission presence (or, in a narrow category, transit into the United States). The court therefore directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements only to provide the reception-and-placement services § 1522 requires for refugees “after their admission” (and the related provisions that apply to children “in the United States or in transit”), rather than to people who remain abroad or are only conditionally approved.

Why the panel treats § 1522 obligations as attached to admission
- The court frames § 1522 as a statute that “must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States.” (Opinion, Discussion ¶1–2.) That sentence frames the operative statutory obligation as post‑admission.
- The panel then cites specific provisions that impose duties tied to presence in the United States: § 1522(a)(1)(A) (listing employment, English training, cash assistance, etc.), § 1522(e)(7)(A) (projects “for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months”), and the implementing regulations. The court’s remedial command tracks those textual triggers by directing the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” (Order ¶¶ immediately preceding the command.)
- The court’s remedy therefore follows the statute’s words: where the statute speaks of services “in the United States,” or for refugees “who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months,” the court enjoins the government to supply those services to persons who actually are in the United States.

How § 1522(d)(2)(B) (unaccompanied minors “in transit”) fits in
- The opinion calls out § 1522(d)(2)(B) and quotes its operative language: during an interim period “while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (Opinion, quoting § 1522(d)(2)(B)).
- That passage shows the court recognizes a statutory exception: Congress expressly addressed children who are already in the United States or en route (“in transit”) and imposed a duty for interim care in that narrow circumstance. The court’s order, however, does not broaden relief beyond people in the United States (or those in the limited “in transit” category expressly mentioned by the statute). In short, the statute contains a specific in‑transit rule for unaccompanied minors, but the general § 1522 duties the court enforces are triggered by being in the United States post‑admission.
- The panel’s remedy therefore respects the textual boundary Congress drew: it enforces duties that apply to refugees who have been admitted (and, implicitly, the narrowly expressed in‑transit rule for certain children), but it does not treat § 1522 as creating a free‑standing obligation to provide domestic reception services to refugees who remain abroad or are only conditionally approved.

Practical and doctrinal context the court relied on
- The opinion places its remedy beside the President’s broad entry authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (citing Trump v. Hawaii), distinguishing the government’s power to suspend entry from statutory duties that operate once refugees are in the United States. The stay analysis therefore preserves the executive’s suspension of admissions while holding the government to statutory duties that arise after admission. (See Discussion on § 1182(f) and the later discussion of § 1522.)
- The court also grounds its directive in the government’s termination of cooperative agreements and uncertainty about replacing them: because the government had terminated agreements that historically provided the § 1522 services, the court ordered reinstatement of the agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” (Order ¶.)

What this does—and does not—mean
- It means the panel will enforce § 1522 obligations that Congress tied to presence in the United States (and it recognizes the more specific in‑transit rule for unaccompanied minors).
- It does not mean the court imposed a duty under § 1522 to provide domestic reception services to people who remain abroad or to require admission of refugees; those matters are governed by the President’s § 1182(f) authority and by the text of § 1522, which the panel read as triggering duties principally upon admission.

A note on the concurrence
- Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes a different statutory interpretation—treating much of § 1522 as authorization rather than mandate—but that disagreement goes to whether § 1522 compels funding at all. The majority, as the order shows, assumed enough of a mandatory duty for § 1522 services post‑admission to justify reinstating cooperative agreements for refugees already in the United States.

Bottom line
The panel limits relief to admitted refugees because the language and structure of § 1522 tie the statutory reception‑and‑placement duties to refugees already in the United States (with a narrowly worded statutory exception for unaccompanied minors “in transit”). The court’s remedy simply enforces those post‑admission duties rather than interfering with the President’s suspension of entry.

### Does the ‘authorization’ language in § 1522(b)–(e) negate the ‘shall’ in § 1522(a)?
Question:
The majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties to provide reception and placement services (subject to appropriations), while Judge Lee argues § 1522 merely authorizes programs and funding. How should courts reconcile the title (“Authorization...”) and the repeated “is authorized” phrasing in (b)–(e) with the “shall” directives in (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)?
Answer:
Short answer
They can be read together. The statute both (1) authorizes programs and funding in subsections (b)–(e) and (2) imposes statutory duties stated with “shall” in (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A) — but those duties are expressly limited by the statutory proviso “to the extent of available appropriations” and by the fact that the substantive programs are created (and funded) through the authorizing subsections. In practice that means the “shall” is mandatory when and to the extent Congress (or the Executive under an appropriation) supplies funds or otherwise activates the authorized programs; it is not an absolute command to spend irrespective of appropriations or the statutory scheme.

Explanation and how the opinion supports it
1) The text shows a two-part structure: authorization + conditional mandates
- The opinion quotes § 1522(a)(1)(A) as requiring the Director of ORR “to the extent of available appropriations” to perform several tasks and reproduces those “shall” duties (employment training, English training, cash assistance rules, equal opportunity for women). That phrasing places the mandatory language inside an explicit funding qualification. (Op. at ¶ starting “Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires…”)
- By contrast, the opinion also notes that the “resettlement services… may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements” and that the specific program provisions in (b)–(e) “state that the ‘Director is authorized’ to provide assistance.” (Op. at ¶s describing §1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) and the later discussion of (b)–(e).)

2) Ordinary meaning: “shall” vs “is authorized,” as the panel describes
- The majority treats “shall” as imposing duties, subject to appropriations: the court directs the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522” because of the government’s uncertainty about providing those statutorily mandated services. (Op. at ¶ beginning “In light of the government’s uncertainty…”)
- Judge Lee emphasizes the opposite inference from the title and repeated “is authorized” phrasing: “Authorization is permissive, not mandatory,” and, in his view, §1522(a) merely prescribes conditions to be observed “in providing assistance under this section” — i.e., if the Executive chooses to fund the programs. (Lee partial dissent ¶s beginning “We start at the beginning…” and “In arguing that Section 1522 imposes mandatory funding…”)

3) How courts should reconcile the language
- Read the provisions together: subsections (b)–(e) establish the statutory programs and authorize the Executive to fund and implement them; subsection (a)’s “shall” prescribes how those programs must operate once they are funded or otherwise activated. The operative modal is therefore conditional: performance is mandatory insofar as appropriations are available and the programs are put into effect.
- The opinion itself applies that synthesis: it recognizes statutory “shall” duties (quoting (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)) and also notes the statutory appropriation level for FY2025. The court then directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements because the government had terminated them and could not show it had a funded alternative in place, which undermined its ability to satisfy the §1522 duties. (Op. at ¶s citing the FY2025 appropriation and directing reinstatement.)

4) Practical consequences under the opinion
- If Congress appropriates funds and the Executive activates the authorized programs, the “shall” provisions impose enforceable duties (the majority’s view).
- If no appropriations are available or the Executive has not put the authorized program into operation, the “shall” language does not create a freestanding, unconditional obligation to spend money; Lee’s point about the permissive import of “authorization” and the limiting clause “to the extent of available appropriations” explains why courts must pay attention to funding and activation.

Bottom line
The statutory scheme is best read as authorization-plus-conditional-mandate: (b)–(e) authorize programs and means of providing services; (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A) state mandatory operational rules that apply when the programs are funded or otherwise put into effect. The panel majority enforces those duties in the factual posture before it because the government terminated cooperative agreements and could not show functioning, funded alternatives; Judge Lee reasonably reads the same text as leaving more discretion to the Executive when appropriations or program activation are lacking. Both readings rely on text the opinion quotes; the reconciliation adopted here follows the majority’s approach of treating “shall” as mandatory but expressly limited by the authorization/appropriations framework the statute creates.

### What role does ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ play in making § 1522 duties mandatory?
Question:
The panel notes Congress appropriated funds (with a subsequent rescission) and points to § 1522(a)(1)(A)’s qualifier. Does that phrase establish a justiciable floor (i.e., some level of services must be provided if money remains), or does it simply preserve executive discretion to scale services down to zero if the Executive deems funds ‘unavailable’ through apportionment or reprogramming?
Answer:
Short answer
The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” makes the §1522 duties contingent on Congress having appropriated money, but it does not turn those duties into mere non‑justiciable suggestions. Where Congress has appropriated funds (and those funds remain lawfully available for the purpose), the statute’s mandatory verbs (e.g., “shall” in §1522(a)(1)(A)) create an enforceable obligation. Conversely, if there are no appropriations lawfully available for the program, the statutory obligation cannot be enforced. The panel’s opinion treats the qualifier as a condition precedent — not as a license for the Executive to declare appropriations “unavailable” in order to nullify a statutory duty without review.

Explanation and textual grounding
1) The panel reads the statute as conditional, not empty. The panel quotes §1522(a)(1)(A) and emphasizes the “to the extent of available appropriations” limiter immediately before listing mandatory duties (make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement; provide English-language training; insure cash assistance, etc.). See Opinion (quoting §1522(a)(1)(A)). That quotation shows the statute ties the duty to Congress’s appropriations.

2) But the panel also treats the statute’s mandatory language as enforceable when appropriations in fact exist. The court notes Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY2025 (reduced by an $800 million rescission) and then—in the very next paragraphs—directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide the §1522 reception-and-placement services for refugees admitted before the suspension. Opinion pp. __ (citing Pub. L. No. 119‑4 §1101(a)(11); Rescissions Act Pub. L. No. 119‑28 §2(b)(6); and directing reinstatement). That remedy reflects the court’s view that the statute’s “to the extent” phrase does not strip the “shall” of enforceability where appropriations remain.

3) The court’s remedy implicitly rejects the argument that the Executive may unreviewably scale services to zero by declaring funds “unavailable.” The panel found the government “uncertain regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. §1522” and, rather than accepting an executive reallocation or suspension as dispositive, directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522.” Opinion (ordering reinstatement). That demonstrates the panel viewed judicial review as appropriate to determine whether appropriations actually make the statutory duties enforceable.

4) The concurrence/dissent highlights the counterargument — authorization vs. mandate — but the majority treats §1522 as imposing duties when funding exists. Judge Lee argues §1522 is “authorization, not a mandate” (pointing to the statute’s title and many subsections that say the Director “is authorized”), and reads the “to the extent” language as a limit on how those discretionary programs must be run if funded. Dissent ¶__. That is a legitimate textual reading, but it is the court majority that controls here: the majority found at least some APA claim likelier to succeed and issued relief tied to the existing appropriations.

Practical legal rule for litigation
- If Congress has appropriated funds for the covered programs and those funds remain lawfully available for use, the “to the extent of available appropriations” language does not prevent the court from enforcing §1522’s mandatory provisions. Plaintiffs can challenge an executive decision that refuses to provide statutorily required services despite available appropriations.
- If there are no appropriations at all, or the appropriations have been lawfully rescinded/redirected such that no funds are lawfully available for the purpose, then the statute’s command cannot be enforced.
- A key contested question in any case will be whether the Executive has lawfully made the funds “unavailable” (by lawful rescission, apportionment decisions under the Impoundment Control Act, lawful reprogramming, etc.). The opinion shows the court is prepared to examine that issue rather than treat the Executive’s unilateral statement of unavailability as dispositive.

Bottom line
“To the extent of available appropriations” makes §1522’s duties conditional on congressional funding, but it does not render the duties advisory or non‑justiciable when appropriation dollars in fact exist and are intended for those programs. The panel’s reinstatement order — grounded in the FY2025 appropriation figures the opinion cites — embodies that reading. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent emphasizes the opposing, more deferential reading, which the panel rejected for the relief it granted.

### Is the termination of refugee cooperative agreements reviewable under the APA or committed to agency discretion?
Question:
State terminated all domestic R&P agreements and most overseas processing agreements. Are those termination decisions ‘final agency action’ not committed to agency discretion by law (§ 701(a)(2))? What legal standards (statute, regulation, uniform guidance) cabin the agency’s discretion enough to permit arbitrary-and-capricious or contrary-to-law review?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — at least according to the panel majority, the termination of the cooperative agreements is reviewable under the APA and is not committed to agency discretion under 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2). The court treated the terminations as final agency action subject to arbitrary-and-capricious and contrary-to-law review because Congress and agency law (statute plus regulations and reporting requirements) supply legal standards that cabin the Executive’s discretion. Judge Lee’s partial dissent offers the opposing view that §1522 is merely permissive, but the majority directed reinstatement of the agreements for the subset of refugees covered by the injunction, relying on mandatory statutory language and implementing regulations.

Why this is reviewable (legal doctrine and how it applies to the opinion)
Two separate doctrines control whether an agency decision like termination of cooperative agreements is judicially reviewable under the APA:

1) Final agency action (Bennett v. Spear)
- The decision must (a) consummate the agency’s decisionmaking process, and (b) produce legal consequences or rights and obligations.
- The opinion shows the terminations were consummated and produced concrete legal consequences: the State Department “terminated all cooperative agreements” (Opinion Background ¶—Feb. 2025 terminations), and the panel directed the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That remedy presumes the terminations were final and caused legal effects (loss of statutorily mandated services).

2) The “committed to agency discretion” exception, 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)
- If a statute “is drawn so that a court would have no meaningful standard against which to judge the agency’s exercise of discretion,” review is foreclosed.
- The panel majority rejected that exception here because the Refugee Act/INA provisions and agency rules supply standards. The court relied on 8 U.S.C. § 1522’s substantive provisions (see Discussion ¶¶ on §1522), the implementing regulations (45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.), and statutory accountability/reporting in §1523 as furnishing legal benchmarks. The court therefore concluded the terminations were not immune from review under §701(a)(2).

What statutory/regulatory/other standards the court relied on as constraining discretion
The opinion identifies (and uses) specific legal materials that cabin agency discretion:

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (statute)
  - §1522(a)(1)(A): requires the Director of ORR, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, provide English-language training, insure cash assistance is made available in a way that does not discourage self-sufficiency, and insure equal opportunity for women (Opinion Discussion ¶1 on §1522).
  - §1522(d)(2)(B): for unaccompanied minors, the Director “shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) … and is authorized to make necessary decisions” during interim placement (Opinion Discussion ¶ on §1522(d)(2)(B)).
  - §1522(e)(7)(A): the Secretary “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for cash/medical assistance for refugees who have been in the U.S. less than 36 months, “under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed” (Opinion Discussion ¶ on §1522(e)(7)(A)).
  - The court treated these “shall” provisions and the statutory structure (including the title “Authorization for programs…” and the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier) as giving concrete duties and limits (the majority and Judge Lee disagree on interpretation, see Lee concurrence/dissent).

- 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (regulations)
  - The opinion cites these implementing regulations as describing “the federal funding available for refugee services,” i.e., concrete programmatic rules against which the agency’s termination can be judged (Opinion Discussion ¶ referencing 45 C.F.R.).

- Appropriations and reporting provisions
  - The opinion notes Congress appropriated funds for FY2025 (Pub. L. No. 119-4 §1101(a)(11), later reduced by a rescission), and references §1523’s reporting requirement, which imposes accountability duties on the Secretary (Opinion Discussion ¶ on appropriations and §1523). Those statutory and appropriations details supply context and objective benchmarks.

How those materials translate into judicial standards
- The court found (at least preliminarily for preliminary-injunction purposes) that §1522 contains mandatory language (“shall”) and program-specific duties that are not so vague as to leave “no law to apply.” That means the arbitrary-and-capricious standard (and review for actions “contrary to law”) applies: the court can ask whether the agency’s termination was reasonable, adequately explained, and consistent with statutory duties (see opinion’s direction to reinstate agreements in light of the government’s uncertainty about meeting §1522 obligations).

Counterpoint from Judge Lee
- Judge Lee (concurring/dissenting) argues §1522 is largely authorizing, not obligatory: the title uses “Authorization,” many subsections read “is authorized,” and the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier signals permissiveness. Lee would treat the statute as leaving the Executive discretion not to operate the programs and therefore would find the terminations non-reviewable or at least not remediable by court order. The majority, however, read §1522 as imposing enforceable duties in the circumstances before the court (Opinion ¶s on §1522 and the remedial direction).

Practical takeaways for future APA challenges
- Finality: If you can show the agency’s action is a consummated decision that produces concrete legal effects (loss of funding, loss of specifically described services, legal responsibility to certain classes), Bennett’s finality test is satisfied.
- Law to apply: Demonstrate statutory text or binding regulations that impose duties or conditions (mandatory verbs, program-specific requirements, reporting/accountability provisions, implementing regs) so a court has standards to judge the agency. The panel majority relied on §1522’s “shall” language, the 45 C.F.R. regime, appropriations context, and reporting requirements to conclude there is “law to apply.”
- Beware of the “to the extent of appropriations” language: courts will consider it, but it does not automatically defeat review if the statute otherwise imposes duties and Congress has appropriated funds (the opinion discusses appropriations for FY2025 and the rescission, which the court viewed as relevant background rather than an absolute bar).

Conclusion
Under the panel majority, the February 2025 terminations are reviewable APA final agency actions because they were final and produced legal consequences, and §1522 together with 45 C.F.R. §400.200 et seq., appropriations history, and §1523 reporting requirements provide judicially manageable standards to test the lawfulness of the terminations. Judge Lee offers the contrary statutory-interpretation view (that §1522 is permissive), but the court’s interim remedy — reinstatement of agreements to provide §1522 services — shows the majority’s conclusion that agency discretion here is sufficiently cabined to permit arbitrary-and-capricious and contrary-to-law review.

### Does the Tucker Act or procurement law preclude APA review of terminating cooperative agreements?
Question:
Cooperative agreements are assistance instruments, but they are still government agreements. Are disputes over termination of such agreements considered contract claims that must go to the Court of Federal Claims, thereby barring APA equitable relief like reinstatement? How do courts draw the line between contract remedies and APA challenges to programmatic policy changes effected via terminations?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the Tucker Act and procurement jurisdiction do not automatically bar APA review or equitable relief (like reinstatement) when challengers press a programmatic statutory claim that the agency unlawfully ended funding or otherwise failed to carry out a statutory duty. Courts draw the line by asking whether the plaintiff’s claim is essentially a contract claim (enforcing private contractual rights or seeking money damages for breach) — which belongs in the Court of Federal Claims under the Tucker Act — or instead is a challenge to agency action (a programmatic or statutory-policy decision) that is reviewable under the APA and susceptible to prospective equitable relief.

Why and how the opinion supports that answer
- The panel treated the organizational plaintiffs’ challenge as an APA/programmatic claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, not a pure procurement/contract enforcement action. The opinion reviews § 1522’s substantive duties and funding framework (pp. 6–11 of the excerpt) and directed the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That is prospective, programmatic relief restoring a statutorily defined program — the kind of remedy courts routinely grant under the APA.
- The court expressly tied the plaintiffs’ likely APA success to the statutory obligations in § 1522 (not to the private contract terms of particular cooperative agreements). See the panel’s discussion: § 1522 requires certain reception-and-placement services and authorizes the use of “contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements” to carry them out; the court ordered reinstatement because the government’s termination left uncertainty about compliance with § 1522 (pp. 6–11). The remedy was framed as restoring statutory program services, not as enforcing particular contractual entitlements or awarding money damages.
- The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee illustrates the relevant delimiting inquiry. Judge Lee does not say Tucker Act or procurement law automatically forecloses APA review; instead he disputes the legal characterization of § 1522 (authorization vs. mandate). That debate shows two things: (1) courts look to the statutory source of the duty (is there a nondiscretionary statutory obligation?) and (2) if the plaintiff’s claim rests on a nondiscretionary statutory duty, the APA is an appropriate vehicle for injunctive relief even when the government uses cooperative agreements to implement the program (Lee’s opinion disputes whether § 1522 imposes that duty, not whether APA is categorically displaced).

Doctrinal rule-of-thumb from the opinion and related doctrine
- If the plaintiff’s claim requires interpretation or enforcement of the parties’ contractual rights (e.g., bid protests, entitlement to contract performance, or money damages for breach), Tucker Act/COFC jurisdiction and procurement rules typically control — such claims are generally routed to the Court of Federal Claims.
- If the plaintiff challenges an agency’s change in program policy or statutory implementation (alleging the agency acted unlawfully under a statute and seeking prospective relief to conform agency action to that statute), the APA is the right path and district courts can issue equitable relief such as reinstatement of the program or cooperative agreements — provided the statutory duty at issue is not wholly discretionary and the requested relief is prospective (as here).
- Practical markers courts use (illustrated by this opinion):
  - Source of the right: Is it contractual (private agreement) or statutory (public program requirement)? The panel focused on § 1522 as the statutory source.
  - Nature of relief sought: Monetary damages or contract-specific remedies point toward Tucker Act/COFC; prospective injunctive relief to compel statutory compliance supports APA/district-court review. The panel directed reinstatement, a prospective remedy.
  - Whether relief would require judicial resolution of complex contract administration, bid selection, or money-mandating questions. The panel did not purport to micromanage contract performance; it ordered reinstatement to meet statutory obligations.
  - The presence of statutory language making performance “to the extent of available appropriations” or otherwise discretionary can undercut a claim that the statute mandates relief — see Judge Lee’s dissent explaining why he reads § 1522 as authorizing rather than mandating programs. That statutory construction question is often dispositive.

Bottom line
This opinion demonstrates that the existence of cooperative agreements does not automatically convert every termination dispute into a Tucker Act/procurement-only controversy. When plaintiffs press that an agency’s termination undermines a nondiscretionary statutory duty and seek prospective relief to restore programmatic services, courts can and will treat that as an APA claim and grant equitable relief (here, reinstatement), unless the statute or facts show the duty is purely discretionary or the relief intrudes into classic contract adjudication reserved to the Court of Federal Claims.

### Which agencies actually have the statutory duty under § 1522: State (PRM) or HHS (ORR)?
Question:
The order cites 45 C.F.R. part 400 (HHS/ORR regulations) yet discusses State’s termination of R&P cooperative agreements. If § 1522(a) duties are lodged in ORR/HHS, can the court compel State to reinstate its agreements to fulfill § 1522 obligations? How do the distinct roles of PRM’s R&P program and ORR’s domestic services interface under § 1522?
Answer:
Short answer
- The opinion treats the substantive statutory duties in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as incumbent on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) within HHS (see the court’s text describing § 1522(a)(1)(A) duties).  
- At the same time, § 1522 expressly contemplates that reception-and-placement services may be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements (§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)), and the State Department historically administered those cooperative agreements. The district court therefore ordered the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements it had terminated because those agreements were the mechanism by which the government—through ORR’s statutory duties—actually delivered R&P services.

Why (legal reasoning tied to the opinion)
1. Who the statute names as the duty‑bearer: the opinion identifies the Director of ORR/HHS as the actor to whom § 1522’s substantive duties are directed. The opinion quotes § 1522(a)(1)(A) and ties provisions about employment training, English instruction, cash assistance, and equal opportunities directly to “the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—an office within the Department of Health and Human Services” (Opinion, ¶ beginning “For reasons to be explained…” and the paragraph that follows). That plainly frames ORR/HHS as the statutory office charged with those resettlement responsibilities.

2. The role of cooperative agreements and State: the opinion also explains that “these resettlement services…may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies.’” (citing § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)). It then records that the organizational plaintiffs are agencies that historically had cooperative agreements “with which the Department of State has had cooperative agreements,” and that the State Department terminated those agreements in February 2025. The district court concluded (and this Court directed) that the government must “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522” because the State’s termination impaired the government’s ability to provide services statutorily required under § 1522.

3. Remedy logic in the opinion: the Court’s remedial directive is grounded in the interaction of (a) the statute assigning substantive duties to ORR/HHS and (b) the fact that those duties were being fulfilled via State‑administered cooperative agreements that the government terminated. The opinion explicitly links ORR’s statutory obligations with the cooperative agreements State had used and directs reinstatement on the ground that the government is “uncertain regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522” and had “terminated all cooperative agreements to provide such services.” Thus the Court ordered restoration of the practical apparatus (the agreements) by which the statutory duties were being performed.

Counterpoint the opinion records: discretionary/authorization argument
- Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent argues § 1522 is best read as authorizing programs, not mandating them—pointing to the statute’s title (“Authorization for programs…”) and the repeated use of “is authorized” in subsections (b)–(e), and characterizes the mandatory‑looking language in § 1522(a) as conditional (“in providing assistance under this section… ‘shall’ operate in a particular way”). The concurrence therefore disputes that § 1522 imposes a judicially enforceable affirmative duty to operate R&P programs at all.

Practical synthesis
- Under the Court’s opinion: the substantive legal obligations under § 1522 are those of ORR/HHS; State’s role is operational (administering cooperative agreements that effectuate those obligations). Because State had been the vehicle for providing the statutorily required R&P services and then terminated its agreements, the court ordered State to reinstate the agreements as necessary to allow the government to meet § 1522 responsibilities.  
- The opinion also records disagreement: one judge views § 1522 as authorizing rather than mandating action, which is relevant to the merits and to whether a court may compel the executive to act.

Bottom line for your question
- Statutory duty (substantive obligations) — HHS/ORR.  
- Operational responsibility for delivering those services can rest with State through cooperative agreements; the court can order reinstatement of those agreements if their termination frustrates the government’s ability to carry out ORR’s § 1522 duties (as the opinion directs).

### Post–Loper Bright, what deference (if any) applies to agency interpretations of § 1522?
Question:
The order doesn’t mention Chevron, and Loper Bright overruled Chevron deference. If there’s an existing agency interpretation (HHS/ORR or State/PRM) reading § 1522 as discretionary or mandatory, what level of deference (Skidmore?) would a court give when interpreting ‘shall’ vs. ‘authorized’ in this context?
Answer:
Short answer
After Loper Bright overruled Chevron, a court presented with an agency interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 would not receive Chevron’s mandatory-step-two deference. Instead the court will (1) decide the statute’s meaning using the ordinary tools of statutory interpretation and (2) may give the agency’s reading persuasive (Skidmore-type) weight—the amount depending on the agency’s expertise, consistency, thoroughness of its reasoning, and the timing of the interpretation.

How that plays out in this opinion
- The panel majority treats § 1522 as imposing legal obligations the government must meet for recently admitted refugees: it cites the statutory text (e.g., § 1522(a)(1)(A) using “shall,” § 1522(d)(2)(B), § 1522(e)(7)(A)) and the statutory/designated appropriation language to conclude the government must provide reception and placement services and thus directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements. See Discussion (paragraphs summarizing § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), (e)(7)(A)) and the court’s directive that the government “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.”
- Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent reads the same statutory text differently, emphasizing the statute’s title—“Authorization for programs…”—and repeated use of the verb “authorized” in §§ 1522(b)–(e) to argue that § 1522 is permissive rather than mandatory. He relies on textual indicators and context (title and “authorized” language) in support of that interpretation. See Lee C.J. Concurrence/Dissent (paragraphs citing title and §§ 1522(b)–(e) as “authorization” language).

Why Skidmore-type deference (not Chevron) applies
- Loper Bright removes Chevron’s framework that required courts to accept a reasonable agency interpretation when a statute was ambiguous and the agency spoke through formal rulemaking or adjudication. Once Chevron is gone, courts return to independent statutory interpretation as the primary task.
- When courts do look to the agency’s interpretation, they typically apply a persuasive-weight inquiry of the sort described in Skidmore v. Swift: the agency’s view is considered and may be persuasive to the extent it is well-reasoned, consistent with earlier positions, grounded in expertise, and timely. The opinion’s two competing readings illustrate the precise inquiry a court would perform: the panel majority rests on textual commands (“shall”) and specific subsections to find a duty; Judge Lee focuses on the statutory title and repeated statutory “authorization” language to support discretion. A court evaluating an agency interpretation would weigh similar textual indicators and then decide how persuasive the agency’s posture is.
- The opinion also shows that context matters: the panel relied on statutory structure, subsection text, and appropriations language; Judge Lee relied on title and the distribution of “authorized” vs “shall.” That is the kind of textual/structural analysis a court performs before or alongside any Skidmore-style consideration of an agency position.

Practical implications for litigants and the agency
- If HHS/ORR or State/PRM advances a § 1522 interpretation post-Loper Bright, litigants should brief (and a court will examine) the statute’s plain text, structure, titles, and context first. If ambiguity remains, the court will ask how persuasive the agency’s interpretation is by looking at:
  - whether the interpretation is reasoned and explained,
  - whether it’s consistent with the agency’s prior positions and practice,
  - whether it invokes agency expertise (e.g., programmatic experience delivering reception and placement services),
  - whether it was contemporaneous with Congressional choices (including appropriations),
  - and whether it arose through notice-and-comment rulemaking or only through litigation positions.
- The panel’s outcome shows that two reasonable textual readings can coexist; absent Chevron, an agency cannot compel deference simply by asserting its construction. Its interpretation must persuade the court on the merits.

Bottom line
Post-Loper Bright, courts confronting agency readings of § 1522 will apply ordinary statutory interpretation and, if they consider the agency’s view, treat it as persuasive under a Skidmore-type standard—not as binding under Chevron. The Ninth Circuit opinion itself exemplifies this approach: judges reached opposite conclusions by applying textual and structural analysis, and an agency’s later explanation would be evaluated for its persuasive force rather than afforded Chevron deference.

### How do the Impoundment Control Act and appropriations law constrain the Executive’s defunding of USRAP?
Question:
The panel references enacted appropriations and a later rescission but suggests the Executive can’t simply suspend funding. Is the court implicitly invoking the Impoundment Control Act’s prohibition on policy impoundments? How does ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ intersect with the Executive’s apportionment authority and the Anti-Deficiency Act in determining whether funds are ‘available’ for § 1522 services?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the panel’s ruling rests on the same structural limits that the Impoundment Control Act (ICA) and appropriations law impose, even though the opinion does not walk through the ICA statutory text. By flagging §1522’s directive “to the extent of available appropriations,” quoting the FY‑2025 appropriation and the later $800 million rescission, and directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements because the government cannot show the services are not required or funded, the court is treating “available appropriations” as meaning funds that Congress has appropriated and the Executive has not lawfully withheld or otherwise made unavailable under the statutory apportionment/impoundment rules. Put simply: the Executive cannot defeat a statutory duty by an informal policy suspension of funding; funds are “available” unless Congress rescinds them or the Executive follows the ICA/OMB apportionment process that lawfully withholds or delays spending.

Relevant legal doctrine and how it applies here

1) Where the court locates the constraint
- The opinion repeatedly rests on §1522 and the district court’s finding that reception-and-placement services are statutorily required for refugees admitted before the suspension. The panel explains that §1522 requires (or at least conditions) provision of services “to the extent of available appropriations,” then notes the FY2025 appropriation ($3.178 billion) and the later rescission of $800 million. The court then orders reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522” because the government has not shown it can otherwise meet its statutory obligations. (Op. pp. 6–9 (statutory discussion) & order near the end directing reinstatement.)

2) The Impoundment Control Act and policy impoundments
- The ICA (2 U.S.C. §§681–688) was enacted to prevent the Executive from unilaterally withholding or deferring spending for policy reasons that would frustrate congressional intent. Under the ICA an executive deferral or rescission of funds for policy reasons is subject to specified procedures and to congressional approval; absent those procedures the Executive may not simply “impound” appropriated funds.
- The panel does not recite the ICA statutory numbers, but its practical holding — that the Executive cannot simply suspend funding for resettlement services and must reinstate cooperative agreements needed to perform §1522 obligations when the government has not shown the appropriations are unavailable — is squarely consistent with the ICA’s limitation on unilateral policy impoundments. The court treats the Executive’s suspension as an impermissible withholding of funds needed to carry out a statutory program unless the government can show a lawful basis (e.g., a valid rescission or apportionment) for non‑spending.

3) “To the extent of available appropriations,” apportionment authority, and the Anti‑Deficiency Act
- “To the extent of available appropriations” is a standard congressional qualification: it means agencies must carry out the statutory duties if and only if Congress has appropriated funds that are lawfully available for that purpose.
- Whether funds are “available” has three components in practice:
  a. Congressional action: has Congress appropriated or rescinded funds? (The opinion cites Congressional action: $3.178B appropriated, then a $800M reduction in the Rescissions Act.)  
  b. Lawful Executive apportionment/administration: OMB (or the Executive agency) may apportion or defer obligations under the apportionment/deferral rules (31 U.S.C. chapters governing apportionment, formerly implemented through OMB Circulars and the ICA procedures), but those actions must comply with the ICA/OMB procedures — they cannot be a pretext for policy impoundment.  
  c. Anti‑Deficiency Act constraints (31 U.S.C. §§1341, 1342): agencies may not obligate or expend funds not lawfully available; conversely, they may not refuse to obligate funds that are lawfully available for a statutory duty simply to avoid spending unless a lawful apportionment/deferral or rescission applies.
- The panel’s directive tracks this framework: because Congress had appropriated funds (with an identified post‑appropriation rescission) and the government made no showing that the remaining appropriations were lawfully unavailable or apportioned away for a lawful reason, the court treated those funds as “available” for §1522 services and enjoined the Executive’s suspension. The court explicitly relied on the government’s “uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. §1522” and therefore ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements. (Op. pp. 7–9.)

Practical effect in this case
- The court does not declare every use of “to the extent of available appropriations” to create a mandatory spending obligation regardless of Executive posture. Rather, it requires the government to show that funds are not lawfully available (by reason of a valid rescission, lawful apportionment, or other statutory limit) before it may stop performing statutorily required services.
- Because the opinion records: (1) a still‑identified FY2025 appropriation and a Congressional rescission of a specified amount, and (2) the government’s failure to demonstrate that the remainder has been lawfully made unavailable, the panel orders the immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements to effectuate §1522 obligations. That remedy is consistent with preventing an unlawful policy impoundment under the ICA and enforcing the rule that appropriated (and lawfully available) funds must be used to carry out statutory duties; the Anti‑Deficiency Act then prevents agencies from incurring obligations beyond what is available.

Conclusion
The panel’s reasoning functions as an application of appropriations law principles — including the limits on unilateral Executive “impoundment” and the interplay of apportionment and the Anti‑Deficiency Act — even if it does not quote the ICA or OMB statutes verbatim. By treating “available appropriations” as meaning appropriations that remain lawfully available to carry out §1522 and by ordering reinstatement when the government failed to show otherwise, the court enforces the long‑standing rule that the Executive cannot nullify statutory programs through informal suspension of funding without following the statutory mechanisms (rescission or lawful apportionment/deferral) that the ICA and appropriations law require.

### What is the basis for organizational standing for the resettlement agencies under the APA?
Question:
The organizational plaintiffs had cooperative agreements with State. Do they have Article III standing based on lost funding and programmatic disruption, and do their interests fall within the zone of interests of § 1522 and the APA? Does their standing hinge on whether § 1522 creates enforceable obligations rather than pure authorization?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel’s majority treated the organizations as having organizational Article III standing: they suffered a concrete, particularized injury (termination of cooperative agreements, lost funding, and programmatic disruption); that injury was caused by the Executive Order and the State Department’s termination of the agreements; and a favorable court order reinstating cooperative agreements and funding would redress the harm. The court also held the organizations’ interests fall within the “zone of interests” of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the APA because § 1522 expressly contemplates use of cooperative agreements and targets the provision of reception-and-placement services that the organizational plaintiffs perform. Judge Lee disagreed on statutory interpretation—reading § 1522 as authorization rather than a mandatory duty—but that disagreement is about the legal predicate for relief, not the core standing analysis.

Why the organizations clear Article III standing here
The opinion identifies the three familiar Article III elements and ties them to the organizations’ facts:

- Injury-in-fact: The government “terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services” (opinion, facts) and “terminated all cooperative agreements” in February 2025 — a concrete financial loss and programmatic disruption to organizations whose mission and operations are to provide those services. That is a classic tangible injury for organizational standing (lost funds and lost ability to provide services).

- Causation: The injury was caused by the Executive Order and the State Department’s subsequent termination of the cooperative agreements (the opinion attributes the terminations to the government’s actions and describes the government’s suspension of funding).

- Redressability: The panel directed that the government “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522,” indicating that a judicial remedy (reinstatement) would cure the organizations’ injury. The court therefore treated redressability as satisfied.

Why the organizations’ interests fall within the zone of interests of § 1522 / APA
The opinion rests the organizations’ entitlement to relief on § 1522’s structure and text:

- § 1522 expressly contemplates that resettlement “services . . . may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies.’” The organizational plaintiffs are precisely such agencies and were operating under cooperative agreements with the State Department.

- The opinion quotes and summarizes § 1522(a) (the “shall” language about making available resources “to the extent of available appropriations”) and examples of specific service provisions (duty language about unaccompanied minors, subsection (e)(7)(A) on alternative projects). The court concluded the government “is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA” because § 1522 requires the government to provide certain reception-and-placement services and the government’s conduct threatened the statutory scheme.

Because § 1522 targets the provision of the very services that these organizations perform and expressly authorizes use of cooperative agreements with agencies like them, the organizations’ interests are within the zone of interests that § 1522 protects; they are therefore proper plaintiffs under the APA.

Does standing depend on whether § 1522 is mandatory or merely permissive?
No — standing and the merits are distinct questions, and the panel treated them separately:

- Standing: The organizations’ concrete injury, causation, and redressability exist regardless of whether § 1522 is read as mandatory or permissive. The panel’s majority found those Article III prerequisites satisfied by the terminated agreements, lost funding, and the prospect of reinstatement.

- Merits (enforceability of § 1522): Whether § 1522 imposes an enforceable duty that the court can compel is a merits question. The majority read § 1522 as imposing obligations sufficient to support relief and so ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements. Judge Lee, in partial dissent, argued § 1522 is best read as authorizing—permissive—action by the executive (citing the statute’s title “Authorization for programs…” and the repeated “is authorized” phrasing), and would therefore deny the district court power to mandate government provision of services. That disagreement affects whether the organizations ultimately win relief, but it does not negate that they have Article III standing to raise their APA claims.

Bottom line
Under the panel majority: organizational standing is grounded in the concrete economic and programmatic harms caused by the termination of their cooperative agreements, and those harms fall within the zone of interests of § 1522 because Congress expressly contemplated cooperative agreements with agencies like the plaintiffs and set statutory expectations about reception-and-placement services. Whether the statutory language is construed as mandatory or merely authorizing is a merits issue (on which the panel is divided) that determines entitlement to relief, not the existence of Article III standing.

### Do refugees outside the United States have standing given consular nonreviewability?
Question:
Some plaintiffs are approved for resettlement but remain abroad. Given the deference under Hawaii and the consular nonreviewability doctrine, how did (or could) the court handle standing and justiciability for those plaintiffs challenging the suspension of entry or the termination of overseas processing support?
Answer:
Short answer
Consular nonreviewability did not automatically defeat the plaintiffs’ ability to obtain relief for refugees who had already been conditionally approved for resettlement and remained abroad. The Ninth Circuit treated reviewability and justiciability as distinct from the high degree of deference owed to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (the Hawaii line of cases). The panel concluded (1) the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the President’s suspension of refugee entry under § 1182(f) and thus stayed most of the district court injunction, but (2) it would not stay the injunction insofar as it applied to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee resettlement before January 20, 2025. That carveout reflects the court’s view that consular nonreviewability/immigration deference limits merits review of entry suspensions but does not automatically deprive already-approved refugees abroad of standing or equitable relief against programmatic actions (like suspension of processing or funding) that block their imminent entry.

Legal doctrine and how the opinion applies it
1. Consular nonreviewability and § 1182(f)/Hawaii deference
- The opinion reiterates the Hawaii rule that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and vests “ample power” to suspend entry of classes of aliens; the court said it “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.’” (Opinion, quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684, 686–88.)
- Because of that deference, the panel concluded the government was “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” and therefore stayed (most of) the district court injunction as to the suspension of admissions.

2. Standing/justiciability for those already conditionally approved abroad
- The court’s orders (March 25 and later clarifications) expressly denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by USCIS before January 20, 2025.” (March 25 Order, cited in the opinion and described in this opinion’s background.) The panel later clarified and preserved a “limited carveout” for those individuals.
- That disposition shows the panel treated conditionally approved refugees abroad differently from a garden‑variety consular visa denial. These plaintiffs had a special status: USCIS approval for resettlement before the President’s suspension, which creates an imminent, particularized injury (loss of a promised opportunity to enter) distinct from a mere challenge to a consular officer’s discretionary visa decision. The court therefore left intact preliminary relief protecting those approved applicants.

3. Why consular nonreviewability did not foreclose relief here
- The opinion distinguishes two things: (a) merits review of the President’s statutory authority to suspend entry (where Hawaii deference controls), and (b) review of agency action under the APA and statutory duties to provide services and to process refugees. The panel said it would reach the merits on some APA claims (notably § 1522 issues) and concluded the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim (which justified directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services to refugees already admitted).
- For applicants abroad who already have conditional USCIS approval, the injury is not a simple consular discretionary denial but an executive suspension of the program that prevents their lawful admission. The carveout indicates the court viewed that injury as cognizable and equitably remediable at the preliminary-injunction stage.

What the court did (practical outcome)
- Stayed the district court’s injunctions in most respects because the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the § 1182(f) entry suspension (Hawaii deference).
- Refused to stay the injunction insofar as it applied to refugees who had been conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025 — i.e., those plaintiffs remained protected by the district court’s preliminary relief.
- On a related but separate track, the panel ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees who have been admitted, because the court concluded the government was not likely to prevail on APA/statutory claims concerning § 1522 duties for admitted refugees (and the government admitted uncertainty about replacing those services).

Takeaway for future litigants
- Consular nonreviewability and presidential immigration deference remain powerful limits on merits review of entry suspensions, but they do not categorically eliminate standing or equitable relief for people who already possess a particularized, pre-existing approval (e.g., USCIS conditional approval) and face a programmatic suspension that prevents their imminent lawful entry.
- Courts will treat (1) a facial or programmatic suspension of admission relying on § 1182(f) as highly deferential on the merits, while (2) statutory/APA challenges to how the government funds or implements resettlement and processing programs (and claims by those already approved) can present justiciable injuries that survive preliminary‑relief scrutiny. The Ninth Circuit’s carveout for conditionally approved refugees embodies that division.

### Can a merits panel revisit a motions panel’s earlier stay ‘carveout’ without violating law-of-the-case?
Question:
An earlier motions panel denied a stay as to individuals conditionally approved before Jan. 20, but this panel vacates that prior stay and issues a different interim order. What doctrine allows the merits panel to alter the motions panel’s interim stay ruling, and are there limits under law-of-the-case to doing so?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. A merits panel may revisit and vacate a motions‑panel stay carveout when the merits panel exercises its inherent authority to manage the appeal and applies the stay factors (here, Nken). That authority is not unlimited: law‑of‑the‑case principles still restrain a later panel from relitigating settled, decision‑on‑the‑merits holdings unless there are the usual exceptional grounds for departure (intervening authority, new facts, or clear error). The opinion itself embodies both points — the merits panel expressly vacated the earlier motions‑panel stay after full briefing and argument while explaining that it was applying the stay standards (Nken), and it left open that some merits questions (e.g., the APA/§1522 issue) will be resolved in the forthcoming opinion.

Why the merits panel could alter the motions panel’s stay
- The court’s order shows the merits panel exercising its supervisory power over interlocutory relief pending appeal. The panel began by saying it had “heard oral argument … on an expedited basis” and that “An opinion will issue in due course. In the interim, this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court.” It then stated plainly: “We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. We vacate our prior stay issued March 25, 2025, as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025.” That is a straight exercise of the court’s authority to modify or vacate temporary stay orders it previously entered.
- The panel justified revisiting the prior stay by applying the familiar stay framework: it recited the four Nken factors (likelihood of success, irreparable injury, balance of harms, public interest), identified the first two as “the most critical,” and explained why those factors now support a different interim posture (noting both deference to the President on §1182(f) and a separate APA/§1522 concern). In short, the panel changed the interim relief after reconsidering the stay factors on the record now before it.

Limits under law‑of‑the‑case
- The opinion also shows the natural limit: the panel is addressing interlocutory stay relief pending a full merits opinion. It repeatedly signals that a full merits opinion will follow (“An opinion on the merits will issue in due course,” “For reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow…”). Law‑of‑the‑case restraints persist where an issue has been finally decided on the merits within the same case. Here, the panel’s action was interlocutory management of the stay pending a merits decision, not a repudiation of a final merits judgment.
- Although the opinion does not recite a multi‑factor law‑of‑the‑case test, it demonstrates the practical limits: the panel distinguished the stay question from the ultimate merits questions (it vacated the prior stay while reserving full resolution of the APA/§1522 claim for its forthcoming opinion). That approach reflects the settled practice that later panels should not lightly relitigate prior panels’ controlling merits holdings, but may revisit interlocutory administrative rulings (like stays) when circumstances, briefing, or argument warrant a different interim course.

Takeaway for similar situations
- If a motions panel issues an interlocutory stay or carveout, a later merits panel sitting on the same appeal can revisit and revise that interlocutory relief by exercising the court’s supervisory authority and applying the governing stay standard (Nken here).  
- That power is cabined by ordinary law‑of‑the‑case concerns: a later panel cannot cavalierly overturn an earlier panel’s on‑the‑merits holding absent the exceptional reasons that justify departing from the prior decision (e.g., intervening controlling authority, materially different facts, or clear error). The opinion’s structure — vacatur of the prior stay plus promise of a full merits opinion addressing statutory and APA issues — illustrates those limits.

### Why cite § 1185(a) if the § 1182(f) analysis is dispositive?
Question:
The background references both 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and § 1185(a) as the Executive Order’s authority, but the analysis rests entirely on § 1182(f). What distinct authority, if any, does § 1185(a) contribute in this context, and why didn’t the court analyze it?
Answer:
Short answer
The court treated § 1182(f) as both sufficient and dispositive for the stay because that provision squarely authorizes a presidential suspension of “the entry of . . . any class of aliens,” the panel relied on the Supreme Court’s deferential framing in Trump v. Hawaii, and there was no need to decide any additional statutory question. § 1185(a) was cited as part of the Executive Order’s asserted statutory basis, but the opinion never had to reach (and therefore did not analyze) whatever separate or overlapping authority § 1185(a) might supply.

Why the panel focused on § 1182(f)
- The opinion explicitly anchors its merits analysis on § 1182(f). It quotes Hawaii and states that “the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” (Discussion ¶¶ 2–3.) The court further says that “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority” to suspend entry. (Discussion ¶ 3.)
- Because § 1182(f) gives the President broad, self‑executing authority to suspend entry after making a finding, the panel concluded this statute alone made the government “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.” That showing was central to the stay analysis (Nken factors), so the court did not need an alternative statutory justification.

What distinct role § 1185(a) could play (and why the court left it untouched)
- The opinion itself does not interpret § 1185(a), so we must limit conclusions to plausible, bounded points consistent with the opinion: § 1185(a) was cited in the Executive Order’s recitals (Background ¶1), so it functions as an alternative or supplementary statutory claim of authority in the Order’s text. The panel’s omission of any analysis of § 1185(a) reflects that § 1182(f) was adequate to resolve the dispositive question before it (likelihood of success on the question whether the President could suspend admissions).
- Courts commonly avoid reaching additional statutory (or constitutional) questions once a dispositive ground has been resolved. The opinion shows that approach here: after resolving the stay factors largely by reference to § 1182(f) and Hawaii, the panel moved on to the APA/resettlement‑services issue that required further, more specific statutory parsing (Discussion ¶¶ 4–10). There was therefore no need to parse § 1185(a)’s scope.

Practical upshot for reading the opinion
- Treat § 1185(a) in this opinion as a cited but unused alternative basis in the Executive Order. The panel relied on § 1182(f)’s clear, broad grant of suspension authority and the Supreme Court’s deferential treatment in Hawaii to resolve the stay question; it neither adopted nor rejected any distinct power that § 1185(a) might confer because doing so was unnecessary to its disposition. (See Background ¶1; Discussion ¶¶2–3.)

If you need textual comparison between the two statutes to predict how a later opinion might treat § 1185(a), say so and I’ll sketch the relevant statutory language and typical judicial uses — but that would go beyond what this opinion itself decides.

### What constitutional constraints (if any) cabin § 1182(f)’s breadth after Hawaii?
Question:
Judge Lee emphasizes the President’s vast discretion, and the panel quotes Hawaii’s deference. Are there still nondelegation or separation-of-powers limits on § 1182(f)? What is the ‘intelligible principle’ that courts rely on to uphold § 1182(f) against nondelegation challenges, and how would a court police the outer bounds?
Answer:
Short answer
Hawaii left §1182(f) a very broad delegation to the President, but it is not unconstrained. After Hawaii the main constitutional constraint is the same as before: the statute supplies an “intelligible principle” — the President may act only when he “finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Courts will give that finding substantial deference (Hawaii), but they still can police the outer bounds by (1) insisting on a genuine presidential finding and some record support for it, (2) ensuring the President’s action stays within the statutory text, and (3) enforcing other statutory limits Congress has imposed (for example, the Refugee Act/§1522 duties invoked in this opinion).

What the opinion says (and why it matters)
- This Ninth Circuit stay opinion repeatedly quotes and relies on Trump v. Hawaii for the basic framework: §1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power,” and courts may not engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Op. at 2–3 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–86). The panel therefore treats compliance with the statute’s single textual prerequisite — that the President “find” detriment to U.S. interests — as the dispositive threshold for §1182(f) authority: “The sole prerequisite … is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry … ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Op. at 3 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 685).
- Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent emphasizes the same point: the President “enjoys vast discretion and deference in immigration matters.” Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part (last paragraph). That concurrence underscores the court’s reluctance to substitute its policy judgment for a presidential determination under §1182(f).

What counts as the “intelligible principle”
- The intelligible principle courts rely on is the statutory phrase itself: the President may act only after he “finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” That phrase provides a textual limit — it identifies the subject-matter (entry of aliens or a class of aliens) and the standard that must be found (detrimental to U.S. interests).
- Hawaii treated that statutory phrase as a sufficient guiding principle to avoid nondelegation worry and to justify deference to the President’s national-security/foreign-affairs judgment. This opinion adopts Hawaii’s approach (Op. at 2–3).

How courts would police the outer bounds (practically)
- Demand a bona fide “finding.” Courts will not be reduced to pure rubber stamps; they can require that the President have made a genuine, formal finding as the statute conditions. The panel treats that “finding” as the statutory “sole prerequisite” (Op. at 3), so absence of any finding or a sham finding would be reviewable.
- Require some connection to the statutory standard. Even with deference, courts can insist the action actually concerns “entry” and a purported link to U.S. interests. The court here declined to conduct a searching inquiry into persuasiveness (Hawaii), but it still framed review around the statutory text and the President’s finding (Op. at 3).
- Enforce other statutory limits. The opinion shows how judicial policing often occurs not by second-guessing national-security judgments under §1182(f) but by enforcing other statutes that Congress has enacted. The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on the suspension of admissions under §1182(f) but identified an APA/statutory problem under 8 U.S.C. §1522 (and related appropriations/administration duties) that limits how the executive can act toward refugees already admitted or for whom services are statutorily required (Op. at 4–8). That is a concrete example of how courts cabin executive action: where the President’s exercise of §1182(f) collides with other statutory obligations, courts can (and did) scrutinize and enjoin the executive.
- Use ordinary judicial doctrines. Separation-of-powers and nondelegation claims still exist, but post-Hawaii they face a high hurdle: the statutory “find” clause supplies an intelligible principle and the national-security/immigration context commands deference. Thus, in practice, courts will police the outer bounds through familiar tools — requiring a real finding, checking for conformity to statutory text, reviewing for procedural flaws under the APA, and enforcing other congressional constraints — rather than by substituting their policy views for presidential judgments.

Bottom line for a nondelegation/separation challenge
- A pure nondelegation attack on §1182(f) will be difficult after Hawaii: the statute’s “find” requirement has been treated as an intelligible principle and the immigration/foreign-affairs context draws substantial deference (see Op. at 2–3). But that does not mean §1182(f) is limitless: courts will still require a bona fide finding, enforce the text (the President can suspend “entry”), and police conflicts with other statutes (as this opinion did with §1522 and APA claims).

### How did the court weigh the public interest and equities where national security and humanitarian harms conflict?
Question:
The panel concludes the remaining Nken factors favor the United States ‘as well’ because of the President’s broad discretion. What framework governs weighing national-security assertions against concrete harms to refugees and service providers, and does the court’s deference effectively collapse the equities into the merits for § 1182(f) cases?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applied the ordinary Nken stay framework (four-factor test) but treated the merits question under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) with the heightened institutional deference announced in Trump v. Hawaii. That deference made the court reluctant to probe the President’s national‑security finding and therefore helped carry the “likely to succeed” and public‑interest prongs in favor of the Government on the suspension of admissions. Crucially, however, the court did not simply collapse the equities into the merits. It separately analyzed—and in one respect ruled for the plaintiffs—under the APA and § 1522 (statutory duties to provide reception and placement services), concluding the Government had not shown it could meet those statutory obligations and therefore ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted. So deference narrowed the merits inquiry under § 1182(f) but did not eliminate a distinct equities/public‑interest analysis where concrete statutory and humanitarian harms were at stake.

What the opinion does (the governing framework)
- Legal framework: the panel explicitly applied the four Nken v. Holder stay factors: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) harm to other parties; and (4) the public interest. The opinion prints Nken’s list and says the first two are “the most critical.” (Order, Discussion ¶1–2 (citing Nken, 556 U.S. at 434).)
- Deference on the § 1182(f) merits: for the merits question whether the President validly suspended refugee admissions under § 1182(f), the court relied on Trump v. Hawaii’s principle that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power” to impose entry restrictions, and thus refused to engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The panel concluded the President “undoubtedly fulfilled” the statutory prerequisite (that he “find[]” entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States”) and therefore the Government is “likely to prevail” on that merits claim. (Order, Discussion ¶¶2–4 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–86).)
- Separate statutory/APA inquiry and equities: the court did not stop with the § 1182(f) analysis. It examined statutory duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related regulations governing reception and placement services, and found the Government’s February 2025 termination of cooperative agreements left serious doubt whether the Government could meet its statutory obligations. Because the Government could not show that it could provide required reception and placement services, the panel directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” (Order, Discussion ¶¶6–12.)

How the court balanced national‑security assertions against humanitarian harms
- Deference narrowed—but did not end—the balancing. On the question whether the President may suspend admissions, the court’s deference to the President’s national‑security finding meant that factor favored the Government: the panel treated the § 1182(f) finding as fulfilling the statutory threshold and therefore weighed the public interest and equities “as determined by the ‘broad discretion’ conferred on the President.” (Order, Discussion ¶4 (citing Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84).)
- But the panel separately considered concrete harms to admitted refugees and service providers under the APA and statutory framework for resettlement services. The court identified specific statutory text (§ 1522’s requirements), the Government’s termination of cooperative agreements, the Government’s statement that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals,” and the Government’s subsequent admission of uncertainty about progress. Given that record, the court concluded the public interest and equities required reinstating cooperative agreements for those already admitted because otherwise the Government could not satisfy the statutory resettlement duties and concrete harms would follow. (Order, Discussion ¶¶6–12.)
- In short: deference affected the merits inquiry under § 1182(f) (making the Government’s national‑security position presumptively strong), but the court continued to weigh irreparable harms and statutory obligations independently. That is why the court stayed the district court’s injunctions in part (on admissions) but ordered relief in part (reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees in the United States).

Does deference “collapse the equities into the merits” in § 1182(f) cases?
No, not in this opinion. The court used deference to limit the scope of judicial inquiry into the President’s § 1182(f) determination (thereby strengthening the Government’s likelihood‑of‑success showing), but it nevertheless performed an independent Nken balancing on irreparable injury and the public interest where statutory duties and concrete humanitarian harms were implicated. The panel’s remedy reflects that split: it stayed the district court’s injunctions regarding the admissions suspension (reflecting deference on the § 1182(f) merits) but ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements to honor § 1522’s requirements and to avert concrete harms to refugees already admitted (reflecting an independent equities/public‑interest assessment). (See Order, Discussion ¶¶4, 6–12.)

One more point (responding to the concurrence/dissent)
Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes a different statutory reading—characterizing § 1522 as authorizing rather than mandating relief—but the majority nonetheless concluded the record showed statutory duties and current inability to meet them, and thus required reinstatement. That illustrates that the court treated deference to national‑security judgments and the independent judicial responsibility to enforce clear statutory obligations as distinct tasks.

Bottom line
The governing framework is Nken’s four‑factor stay test, applied with Trump v. Hawaii deference on the § 1182(f) merits. That deference makes it harder for courts to second‑guess national‑security findings, but it does not automatically merge the public‑interest/equities analysis into the merits: where concrete statutory duties and humanitarian harms exist (here, § 1522 and terminated cooperative agreements), the court will independently weigh equities and may order relief despite deference on the admissions question.

### Why frame the § 1522 argument as an APA claim rather than as a direct statutory cause of action?
Question:
The order says the government is unlikely to prevail on ‘at least one’ APA challenge related to § 1522’s mandates. Is there no private right of action under § 1522, making the APA’s ‘not in accordance with law’ path the only vehicle? How does the court identify the specific ‘final agency action’ for this APA claim (e.g., termination of agreements, funding suspension, or both)?
Answer:
Short answer
The opinion treats plaintiffs’ challenge to § 1522 obligations as an APA claim because § 1522 does not itself speak to a private right of action in this record and the plaintiffs are attacking discrete executive acts (the funding suspension and the termination of cooperative agreements) that the district court treated as agency action subject to APA review. The court therefore asks whether those executive actions are “not in accordance with law” under the APA — i.e., whether they contravene § 1522’s statutory duties or conditions. The “final agency action” at issue, as reflected in the opinion, is the State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding and its termination of the cooperative agreements providing reception-and-placement services (and the district court’s consequent order that those agreements be reinstated).

Why plaintiffs framed it as an APA claim (and not a standalone private cause of action under § 1522)
- The opinion does not identify an express private right of action in § 1522. The panel’s analysis focuses on whether the executive branch’s conduct (suspending funding, terminating agreements) is lawful under the statute, and thus frames the dispute as reviewable agency action under the APA. See, e.g., “The government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA” (emphasis added).
- The statutory text is disputed as to whether § 1522 creates a mandatory duty or merely authorizes programs. Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes that § 1522’s title (“Authorization for programs…”) and repeated use of “is authorized” support a discretionary reading. Because the parties and judges treat the issue as whether executive action is consistent with § 1522, the APA’s “not in accordance with law” provision is the natural vehicle for that contention in the absence of an asserted, clearly conferred private cause of action. See Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part (analyzing the text and structure of § 1522 and explaining why it “is most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate”).
- In short: plaintiffs are asking a court to set aside specific agency decisions as unlawful under a statute; that is the classic posture of an APA § 706(2)(A) claim asserted where no express private remedy in the statute is established in the opinion.

What the court treated as the “final agency action” (i.e., what the APA claim targets)
- The opinion identifies two concrete executive moves that motivated the APA challenge:
  - The January 24, 2025 Department of State suspension of all USRAP funding, which “included funding for domestic resettlement services” (Background ¶2).
  - The February 26, 2025 termination by the government “of every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad” (Background ¶3).
- The district court’s remedies likewise focused on those decisions: it “prohibit[ed] the suspension of USRAP funding” (Feb. 28 order) and later ordered the State Department “to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling” (Mar. 24 order). The panel’s remedial direction in the stay opinion likewise requires the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522” insofar as those services are provided to refugees already admitted (Discussion ¶—final paragraphs).
- Thus, as reflected in the opinion, the APA challenge is directed at (1) the suspension of funding and (2) the termination of cooperative agreements — both treated as agency actions that can be reviewed for whether they are consistent with § 1522.

Practical import
- If § 1522 imposes a mandatory statutory duty as the district court majority reads it, then terminating funding or agreements that effectuate those statutory duties may be “not in accordance with law” and thus unlawful agency action under the APA — which is exactly the panel’s point in saying the government “is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”
- If, however, § 1522 is discretionary as Judge Lee argues, then those same executive decisions are less vulnerable to being set aside as inconsistent with the statute — which is why the panel divides on the underlying statutory question.

Bottom line
The opinion frames the dispute as an APA claim because the contested conduct consists of discrete executive/agency acts (funding suspension and termination of cooperative agreements) whose legality under § 1522 is contested, and the opinion does not identify an express private cause of action under § 1522 itself. The “final agency action” the plaintiffs attacked (and that the district court sought to remedy) is the State Department’s suspension of funding and termination of the cooperative agreements that implement reception-and-placement services.

### What is the significance of vacating the administrative stay and denying the emergency motion as moot?
Question:
The panel vacates its July 18 administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and then issues a new stay pending appeal, denying the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot. Procedurally, how do those steps interact, and why was the emergency motion mooted by the panel’s new stay order?
Answer:
Short answer
Vacating the July 18 administrative stay removed the temporary order whose terms the parties were disputing, and the panel’s contemporaneous issuance of a new stay pending appeal replaced that temporary relief with a formal stay whose terms now control. Because the new stay superseded the earlier administrative stay (and disposed of the underlying dispute over enforcement/clarification), the government’s separate emergency motion asking the court to enforce, clarify, or amend the earlier stay presented no longer a live controversy and was therefore denied as moot.

Why — doctrinal and textual explanation
- Administrative stays are temporary, internal tools; a court can vacate them and then enter a different stay. The opinion expressly says both things: “We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18, 2025.” (Order ¶1) and shortly thereafter: “We issue this stay pending final resolution of this appeal. The district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety, except as noted below.” (Discussion ¶¶1–2). That shows the court intended the new stay to replace the earlier administrative stay and to fix the controlling terms going forward.

- Mootness: Federal courts do not decide abstract or hypothetical disputes. If an event (here, entry of a new stay with its own terms) eradicates the practical effect of a pending motion (here, the government’s July 17 emergency motion “to enforce, clarify or amend the stay order”), there is no longer a live controversy for the court to resolve. The opinion reflects this: after vacating the prior stays and issuing the new stay, the court states simply: “The government’s emergency motion of July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.” (Front matter and Order ¶1). That is classic mootness practice — the motion asked the court to act with respect to a prior stay that the court has now superseded.

- Practical interaction between the steps: vacatur + new stay accomplish two related things. Vacating the earlier (administrative and March) stays removes their force; issuing a new stay creates a single, authoritative stay pending appeal with the scope the panel intends (here: staying the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions “in their entirety, except as noted below”). Because the new stay both supersedes and clarifies the relief the parties were contesting, any motion aimed at the prior administrative stay (enforcement/clarification/amendment) has been overtaken by events.

How the opinion supports this
- The opinion’s opening lines perform the steps at issue: “We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. . . . The government’s emergency motion of July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.” (Order ¶1).
- The Background explains the sequence: the government filed the July 17 emergency motion after the district court’s July 14 enforcement order; the court issued an administrative stay July 18; the panel then considered briefing and argument and “in the interim” replaced those temporary measures with a stay pending appeal. (Background & Discussion).
- The Discussion confirms the new stay’s scope and the reason for mooting the motion: after summarizing standards and the court’s conclusions, the panel states it has “vacate[d] our administrative stay of July 18” and “issue[d] this stay pending final resolution of this appeal,” thereby creating the new operative order that renders the emergency motion unnecessary. (Discussion ¶¶1–2).

Bottom line
Vacating the administrative stay and issuing a new stay are not inconsistent — the latter simply supersedes the former. Once the panel entered the new, controlling stay (with its defined scope), there was nothing left for the emergency motion to do with respect to the old administrative stay, so the panel properly denied that motion as moot.
