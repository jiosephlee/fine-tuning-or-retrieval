Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

### Do refugees abroad and resettlement organizations have standing and fall within the zone of interests to challenge a § 1182(f) suspension and funding terminations?
Question:
Plaintiffs include refugees admitted to the U.S., refugees conditionally approved but still abroad, and organizations with cooperative agreements. For those still outside the U.S., do they have standing to challenge entry suspensions after Trump v. Hawaii? For the organizational plaintiffs, does the APA’s zone-of-interests test allow them to invoke § 1522 or related provisions to challenge termination of their agreements, or are such claims too attenuated or contractual in nature?
Answer:
Short answer

- Refugees who have been conditionally approved for resettlement but remain abroad: yes — the Ninth Circuit treated them differently from the general class barred by § 1182(f) and preserved the district-court injunction as to those individuals. The panel’s stay orders show the court found their claims sufficiently concrete and redressable to avoid a stay even while recognizing the broad deference afforded the President under Trump v. Hawaii. That does not mean the refugees have won the merits; the panel said the government is likely to prevail generally under § 1182(f), but it expressly declined to stay relief for conditionally approved applicants. (Pacito order, March 25, 2025 stay order quoted in the panel order.)

- Organizational plaintiffs with terminated cooperative agreements: yes — the court treated § 1522 as supplying a legally cognizable claim under the APA and directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services required by § 1522 for refugees already admitted. The opinion grounds this on § 1522’s substantive provisions (which refer expressly to using “public or private nonprofit agencies” and enumerate duties for the Director) and on the concrete injury caused by termination of those agreements. A dissenting judge argued the statutory text is permissive, but the majority (in the interim order) concluded the government is not likely to prevail on the APA claim and ordered reinstatement. (Pacito order, discussion of § 1522 and district-court relief.)

Why — the legal doctrine and how the opinion applied it

1. Article III standing (injury, causation, redressability)
- Injury-in-fact and imminence: conditionally approved refugees abroad had applications adjudicated and were in the pipeline; the court’s refusal to stay the injunction as to that subgroup reflects a view that their injury was concrete and imminent enough to satisfy Article III. The motions panel explicitly “denied the motion for a stay… to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by [USCIS] before January 20, 2025.” That carveout implies the panel saw a different standing calculus for those with conditional approval than for the general class of non‑admitted aliens.
- Causation and redressability: the panel’s carveout and the district court’s injunction (kept in place for conditionally approved applicants) show the court concluded an injunction could alleviate the plaintiffs’ injury (i.e., allow their resettlement to proceed), so redressability was satisfied as to them.

2. Zone‑of‑interests under the APA (5 U.S.C. § 702 / test from Match–Zone cases)
- The APA’s zone‑of‑interests test asks whether the plaintiff’s interests are arguably within the interests Congress sought to protect in the statute invoked. The panel’s treatment of § 1522 supports a straightforward fit for the organizational plaintiffs: § 1522 expressly contemplates that reception-and-placement services “may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies’.” The organizations at issue were exactly such partners and suffered immediate contractual and programmatic harms when the State Department terminated the agreements.
- The panel concluded the government was “not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” described the statutory duties in § 1522 (and its subsections) and, based on uncertainty about replacement arrangements, directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That relief confirms the court viewed the organizational plaintiffs’ interests as falling within § 1522’s zone.

What the opinion does (and does not) resolve
- The panel repeatedly cites Trump v. Hawaii to stress that § 1182(f) vests broad presidential authority and says the government is “likely to prevail” on challenges to the suspension of refugee admissions under that provision. So for the broad class of aliens the President has suspended, the merits are uphill after Hawaii.
- But the panel preserved preliminary relief for a narrow subset — refugees conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025 — and left intact the district court’s reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted. Those rulings show the court is willing to recognize standing and statutory/APA claims where the plaintiffs have been admitted or have a more concrete entitlement (conditional approval + imminent entry) or where Congress’ statutory scheme explicitly contemplates third‑party nonprofit providers.
- Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the counterargument: § 1522’s title and many subsections use “authorize,” and he reads the statute as permissive rather than mandatory. The interim order does not finally resolve that textual debate; it signals the majority will view § 1522 as imposing sufficient legal duty (at least for preliminary relief) to support the organizations’ APA claims.

Practical takeaways for briefing or litigation
- Emphasize imminence and concrete planning for any refugee plaintiff still abroad (conditional USCIS approval, tickets, port-of-entry arrangements) to strengthen injury/redressability.
- For organizational plaintiffs, emphasize the close textual fit: § 1522 contemplates cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies and enumerates programmatic duties (employment training, language, cash/medical assistance, care for unaccompanied children). That alignment supports both standing and the APA zone‑of‑interests.
- Be ready to address the Lee dissent’s statutory‑interpretation argument — the government will press that § 1522 is discretionary and that appropriations language limits duty. The panel’s interim order shows that, at least at the preliminary‑injunction stage, the majority found those counterarguments insufficient.

Bottom line
Pacito’s orders indicate (1) the Ninth Circuit treats conditionally approved refugees abroad as having a stronger Article III case than the general class of aliens barred under § 1182(f) (the panel preserved injunction relief for that subgroup), and (2) the court found organizational plaintiffs injured and within the APA’s zone‑of‑interests with respect to § 1522‑based claims, directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees. The panel also signaled that the government remains likely to prevail on the general § 1182(f) challenge under Trump v. Hawaii, and a vigorous textual dispute over § 1522’s mandatory vs. permissive character remains an important counterargument.
