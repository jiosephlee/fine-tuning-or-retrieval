Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

### What is the legal basis for the panel’s earlier carveout for conditionally approved refugees and the district court’s appointment of a magistrate to oversee compliance?
Question:
Earlier, the motions panel carved out from the stay individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before Jan. 20, 2025, and the district court then set an enforcement framework and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee it. On what authority can an appellate court tailor a stay with such a carveout, and what standard governs district court managerial orders (like appointing a magistrate or special master) to administer that carveout while the merits are on appeal? Does such supervision risk contravening the stay or exceed ancillary enforcement powers?
Answer:
Short answer
- The motions panel relied on its equitable authority under the familiar stay framework (Nken’s four-factor test) to tailor the stay and leave a narrow “carveout” in place for refugees conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025. See Pacito order (quoting Nken; March 25 order language quoted in the opinion).
- The district court’s appointment of a magistrate to implement and police that limited injunction is an ordinary exercise of a trial court’s managerial and equitable authority to enforce injunctions and to administer the relief it has ordered — provided the appointment is (1) necessary to implement the un-stayed relief, (2) limited in scope, and (3) not used to expand relief that the appellate court has stayed. The panel treated the district court’s enforcement order as the kind of interlocutory enforcement activity that can be administratively stayed, and it ultimately left the carveout intact while vacating broader relief. See Pacito (describing the July 14 enforcement order, the panel’s administrative stay, and its later vacatur of earlier stays while preserving the carveout).
- Supervision does not automatically contravene the stay so long as it is confined to administering the portion of the injunction the appellate court left in effect; it risks contravening the stay only if the district court’s managerial measures (or a magistrate’s actions) reach beyond the carveout and attempt to implement relief the panel has stayed.

Explanation and textual support from the opinion
1) Appellate authority to tailor a stay (the carveout)
- The panel framed its decision in the Nken stay framework: it explicitly applied the four Nken factors (likelihood of success; irreparable injury; balance of harms; public interest) and called the first two “the most critical.” Pacito at “The standards for issuing a stay are well established.” (citing Nken, 556 U.S. 418).
- Applying that framework, the panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on the merits of its challenge to the Executive Order’s suspension of admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (invoking the deference described in Trump v. Hawaii), but that there was at least one APA-based claim (grounded in § 1522 statutory duties to provide reception-and-placement services) on which the government was not likely to prevail. Pacito explains both holdings: likely success on the § 1182(f) claim, and the court’s view that the government is not likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ §1522/APA challenge.
- Because a stay is an equitable, discretionary interlocutory remedy, the panel exercised that discretion by staying the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety except as specified” and explicitly preserving the injunction’s effect for the subclass of “individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status … before January 20, 2025.” Pacito (quoting the March 25 order and subsequent clarifications). In short, the panel used Nken-guided equitable discretion to tailor the stay to the balance of interests it found.

2) District court managerial orders and the standard that governs them while the merits are on appeal
- The opinion shows the district court responded to the panel’s carveout by entering a July 14 enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate to oversee compliance with the limited injunction. The panel treated that managerial order as enforcement activity that could be administratively stayed pending resolution (it issued an administrative stay of the July 14 order and then revisited the matter).
- The controlling standard, as reflected in the opinion, is practical and functional: a district court may structure and supervise implementation of the relief that remains operative (here, reception-and-placement services for the limited class) — including by appointing a magistrate or other officer to administer that relief — so long as the supervision is directed at effectuating the un-stayed injunction, is reasonably necessary to that end, and does not expand or modify relief that the appellate court has stayed. The panel’s handling of the enforcement order (administrative stay; then vacatur of earlier stays but preservation of the carveout; and eventual direction that the government “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522”) illustrates that the district court’s managerial steps are proper insofar as they implement the surviving scope of relief.
- The opinion also ties the propriety of such supervision to the underlying merits questions it identified: because the court found the government unlikely to prevail on the §1522-based APA claim, it was appropriate to preserve and to order implementation of the statutorily rooted reception-and-placement obligations for those covered by the carveout. That statutory basis limits and justifies managerial oversight aimed at securing compliance with those duties.

3) When supervision risks contravening the stay or exceeding ancillary powers
- The opinion demonstrates the boundary: the panel repeatedly stayed the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety except as specified” and made clear it could and would issue administrative relief (the July 18 administrative stay) to prevent enforcement activity that exceeded the stay. That sequence shows that appellate courts will intervene if a district court’s enforcement apparatus attempts to reach relief that the appellate court has stayed.
- Put plainly: appointing a magistrate to implement the un-stayed carveout is consistent with both the district court’s enforcement/managerial powers and the panel’s carveout. But supervisory measures that attempt to implement or expand the injunction beyond the narrowly preserved subclass would conflict with the stay and invite appellate correction (as the panel’s administrative stay of the July 14 order illustrates).

Bottom line for practitioners
- Appellate courts may and do tailor stays under Nken, including leaving limited portions of a district-court injunction operative for discrete classes when equity and the merits-supporting findings justify that result. Pacito is an example: the panel stayed the injunction broadly but preserved relief for conditionally approved refugees pre‑Jan. 20, 2025.
- District courts retain the ordinary authority to structure and supervise compliance with whatever relief remains in force, including appointing magistrates or other officers to administer that relief, so long as those measures are necessary, limited to effectuating the un‑stayed relief, and do not attempt to vindicate relief the appellate court has suspended — otherwise the appellate court can and will step in. Pacito shows both sides of that authority: the district court’s managerial appointment and the panel’s willingness to stay or vacate enforcement measures to keep the stay’s scope intact.
