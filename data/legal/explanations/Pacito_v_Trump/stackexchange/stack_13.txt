### How do the Impoundment Control Act and appropriations law constrain the Executive’s defunding of USRAP?
Question:
The panel references enacted appropriations and a later rescission but suggests the Executive can’t simply suspend funding. Is the court implicitly invoking the Impoundment Control Act’s prohibition on policy impoundments? How does ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ intersect with the Executive’s apportionment authority and the Anti-Deficiency Act in determining whether funds are ‘available’ for § 1522 services?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the panel’s ruling rests on the same structural limits that the Impoundment Control Act (ICA) and appropriations law impose, even though the opinion does not walk through the ICA statutory text. By flagging §1522’s directive “to the extent of available appropriations,” quoting the FY‑2025 appropriation and the later $800 million rescission, and directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements because the government cannot show the services are not required or funded, the court is treating “available appropriations” as meaning funds that Congress has appropriated and the Executive has not lawfully withheld or otherwise made unavailable under the statutory apportionment/impoundment rules. Put simply: the Executive cannot defeat a statutory duty by an informal policy suspension of funding; funds are “available” unless Congress rescinds them or the Executive follows the ICA/OMB apportionment process that lawfully withholds or delays spending.

Relevant legal doctrine and how it applies here

1) Where the court locates the constraint
- The opinion repeatedly rests on §1522 and the district court’s finding that reception-and-placement services are statutorily required for refugees admitted before the suspension. The panel explains that §1522 requires (or at least conditions) provision of services “to the extent of available appropriations,” then notes the FY2025 appropriation ($3.178 billion) and the later rescission of $800 million. The court then orders reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522” because the government has not shown it can otherwise meet its statutory obligations. (Op. pp. 6–9 (statutory discussion) & order near the end directing reinstatement.)

2) The Impoundment Control Act and policy impoundments
- The ICA (2 U.S.C. §§681–688) was enacted to prevent the Executive from unilaterally withholding or deferring spending for policy reasons that would frustrate congressional intent. Under the ICA an executive deferral or rescission of funds for policy reasons is subject to specified procedures and to congressional approval; absent those procedures the Executive may not simply “impound” appropriated funds.
- The panel does not recite the ICA statutory numbers, but its practical holding — that the Executive cannot simply suspend funding for resettlement services and must reinstate cooperative agreements needed to perform §1522 obligations when the government has not shown the appropriations are unavailable — is squarely consistent with the ICA’s limitation on unilateral policy impoundments. The court treats the Executive’s suspension as an impermissible withholding of funds needed to carry out a statutory program unless the government can show a lawful basis (e.g., a valid rescission or apportionment) for non‑spending.

3) “To the extent of available appropriations,” apportionment authority, and the Anti‑Deficiency Act
- “To the extent of available appropriations” is a standard congressional qualification: it means agencies must carry out the statutory duties if and only if Congress has appropriated funds that are lawfully available for that purpose.
- Whether funds are “available” has three components in practice:
  a. Congressional action: has Congress appropriated or rescinded funds? (The opinion cites Congressional action: $3.178B appropriated, then a $800M reduction in the Rescissions Act.)  
  b. Lawful Executive apportionment/administration: OMB (or the Executive agency) may apportion or defer obligations under the apportionment/deferral rules (31 U.S.C. chapters governing apportionment, formerly implemented through OMB Circulars and the ICA procedures), but those actions must comply with the ICA/OMB procedures — they cannot be a pretext for policy impoundment.  
  c. Anti‑Deficiency Act constraints (31 U.S.C. §§1341, 1342): agencies may not obligate or expend funds not lawfully available; conversely, they may not refuse to obligate funds that are lawfully available for a statutory duty simply to avoid spending unless a lawful apportionment/deferral or rescission applies.
- The panel’s directive tracks this framework: because Congress had appropriated funds (with an identified post‑appropriation rescission) and the government made no showing that the remaining appropriations were lawfully unavailable or apportioned away for a lawful reason, the court treated those funds as “available” for §1522 services and enjoined the Executive’s suspension. The court explicitly relied on the government’s “uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. §1522” and therefore ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements. (Op. pp. 7–9.)

Practical effect in this case
- The court does not declare every use of “to the extent of available appropriations” to create a mandatory spending obligation regardless of Executive posture. Rather, it requires the government to show that funds are not lawfully available (by reason of a valid rescission, lawful apportionment, or other statutory limit) before it may stop performing statutorily required services.
- Because the opinion records: (1) a still‑identified FY2025 appropriation and a Congressional rescission of a specified amount, and (2) the government’s failure to demonstrate that the remainder has been lawfully made unavailable, the panel orders the immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements to effectuate §1522 obligations. That remedy is consistent with preventing an unlawful policy impoundment under the ICA and enforcing the rule that appropriated (and lawfully available) funds must be used to carry out statutory duties; the Anti‑Deficiency Act then prevents agencies from incurring obligations beyond what is available.

Conclusion
The panel’s reasoning functions as an application of appropriations law principles — including the limits on unilateral Executive “impoundment” and the interplay of apportionment and the Anti‑Deficiency Act — even if it does not quote the ICA or OMB statutes verbatim. By treating “available appropriations” as meaning appropriations that remain lawfully available to carry out §1522 and by ordering reinstatement when the government failed to show otherwise, the court enforces the long‑standing rule that the Executive cannot nullify statutory programs through informal suspension of funding without following the statutory mechanisms (rescission or lawful apportionment/deferral) that the ICA and appropriations law require.
