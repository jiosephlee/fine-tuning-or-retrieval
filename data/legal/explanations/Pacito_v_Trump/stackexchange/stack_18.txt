Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

### What constitutional constraints (if any) cabin § 1182(f)’s breadth after Hawaii?
Question:
Judge Lee emphasizes the President’s vast discretion, and the panel quotes Hawaii’s deference. Are there still nondelegation or separation-of-powers limits on § 1182(f)? What is the ‘intelligible principle’ that courts rely on to uphold § 1182(f) against nondelegation challenges, and how would a court police the outer bounds?
Answer:
Short answer
Hawaii left §1182(f) a very broad delegation to the President, but it is not unconstrained. After Hawaii the main constitutional constraint is the same as before: the statute supplies an “intelligible principle” — the President may act only when he “finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Courts will give that finding substantial deference (Hawaii), but they still can police the outer bounds by (1) insisting on a genuine presidential finding and some record support for it, (2) ensuring the President’s action stays within the statutory text, and (3) enforcing other statutory limits Congress has imposed (for example, the Refugee Act/§1522 duties invoked in this opinion).

What the opinion says (and why it matters)
- This Ninth Circuit stay opinion repeatedly quotes and relies on Trump v. Hawaii for the basic framework: §1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power,” and courts may not engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Op. at 2–3 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–86). The panel therefore treats compliance with the statute’s single textual prerequisite — that the President “find” detriment to U.S. interests — as the dispositive threshold for §1182(f) authority: “The sole prerequisite … is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry … ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Op. at 3 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 685).
- Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent emphasizes the same point: the President “enjoys vast discretion and deference in immigration matters.” Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part (last paragraph). That concurrence underscores the court’s reluctance to substitute its policy judgment for a presidential determination under §1182(f).

What counts as the “intelligible principle”
- The intelligible principle courts rely on is the statutory phrase itself: the President may act only after he “finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” That phrase provides a textual limit — it identifies the subject-matter (entry of aliens or a class of aliens) and the standard that must be found (detrimental to U.S. interests).
- Hawaii treated that statutory phrase as a sufficient guiding principle to avoid nondelegation worry and to justify deference to the President’s national-security/foreign-affairs judgment. This opinion adopts Hawaii’s approach (Op. at 2–3).

How courts would police the outer bounds (practically)
- Demand a bona fide “finding.” Courts will not be reduced to pure rubber stamps; they can require that the President have made a genuine, formal finding as the statute conditions. The panel treats that “finding” as the statutory “sole prerequisite” (Op. at 3), so absence of any finding or a sham finding would be reviewable.
- Require some connection to the statutory standard. Even with deference, courts can insist the action actually concerns “entry” and a purported link to U.S. interests. The court here declined to conduct a searching inquiry into persuasiveness (Hawaii), but it still framed review around the statutory text and the President’s finding (Op. at 3).
- Enforce other statutory limits. The opinion shows how judicial policing often occurs not by second-guessing national-security judgments under §1182(f) but by enforcing other statutes that Congress has enacted. The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on the suspension of admissions under §1182(f) but identified an APA/statutory problem under 8 U.S.C. §1522 (and related appropriations/administration duties) that limits how the executive can act toward refugees already admitted or for whom services are statutorily required (Op. at 4–8). That is a concrete example of how courts cabin executive action: where the President’s exercise of §1182(f) collides with other statutory obligations, courts can (and did) scrutinize and enjoin the executive.
- Use ordinary judicial doctrines. Separation-of-powers and nondelegation claims still exist, but post-Hawaii they face a high hurdle: the statutory “find” clause supplies an intelligible principle and the national-security/immigration context commands deference. Thus, in practice, courts will police the outer bounds through familiar tools — requiring a real finding, checking for conformity to statutory text, reviewing for procedural flaws under the APA, and enforcing other congressional constraints — rather than by substituting their policy views for presidential judgments.

Bottom line for a nondelegation/separation challenge
- A pure nondelegation attack on §1182(f) will be difficult after Hawaii: the statute’s “find” requirement has been treated as an intelligible principle and the immigration/foreign-affairs context draws substantial deference (see Op. at 2–3). But that does not mean §1182(f) is limitless: courts will still require a bona fide finding, enforce the text (the President can suspend “entry”), and police conflicts with other statutes (as this opinion did with §1522 and APA claims).
