### Why frame the § 1522 argument as an APA claim rather than as a direct statutory cause of action?
Question:
The order says the government is unlikely to prevail on ‘at least one’ APA challenge related to § 1522’s mandates. Is there no private right of action under § 1522, making the APA’s ‘not in accordance with law’ path the only vehicle? How does the court identify the specific ‘final agency action’ for this APA claim (e.g., termination of agreements, funding suspension, or both)?
Answer:
Short answer
The opinion treats plaintiffs’ challenge to § 1522 obligations as an APA claim because § 1522 does not itself speak to a private right of action in this record and the plaintiffs are attacking discrete executive acts (the funding suspension and the termination of cooperative agreements) that the district court treated as agency action subject to APA review. The court therefore asks whether those executive actions are “not in accordance with law” under the APA — i.e., whether they contravene § 1522’s statutory duties or conditions. The “final agency action” at issue, as reflected in the opinion, is the State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding and its termination of the cooperative agreements providing reception-and-placement services (and the district court’s consequent order that those agreements be reinstated).

Why plaintiffs framed it as an APA claim (and not a standalone private cause of action under § 1522)
- The opinion does not identify an express private right of action in § 1522. The panel’s analysis focuses on whether the executive branch’s conduct (suspending funding, terminating agreements) is lawful under the statute, and thus frames the dispute as reviewable agency action under the APA. See, e.g., “The government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA” (emphasis added).
- The statutory text is disputed as to whether § 1522 creates a mandatory duty or merely authorizes programs. Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes that § 1522’s title (“Authorization for programs…”) and repeated use of “is authorized” support a discretionary reading. Because the parties and judges treat the issue as whether executive action is consistent with § 1522, the APA’s “not in accordance with law” provision is the natural vehicle for that contention in the absence of an asserted, clearly conferred private cause of action. See Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part (analyzing the text and structure of § 1522 and explaining why it “is most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate”).
- In short: plaintiffs are asking a court to set aside specific agency decisions as unlawful under a statute; that is the classic posture of an APA § 706(2)(A) claim asserted where no express private remedy in the statute is established in the opinion.

What the court treated as the “final agency action” (i.e., what the APA claim targets)
- The opinion identifies two concrete executive moves that motivated the APA challenge:
  - The January 24, 2025 Department of State suspension of all USRAP funding, which “included funding for domestic resettlement services” (Background ¶2).
  - The February 26, 2025 termination by the government “of every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad” (Background ¶3).
- The district court’s remedies likewise focused on those decisions: it “prohibit[ed] the suspension of USRAP funding” (Feb. 28 order) and later ordered the State Department “to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling” (Mar. 24 order). The panel’s remedial direction in the stay opinion likewise requires the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522” insofar as those services are provided to refugees already admitted (Discussion ¶—final paragraphs).
- Thus, as reflected in the opinion, the APA challenge is directed at (1) the suspension of funding and (2) the termination of cooperative agreements — both treated as agency actions that can be reviewed for whether they are consistent with § 1522.

Practical import
- If § 1522 imposes a mandatory statutory duty as the district court majority reads it, then terminating funding or agreements that effectuate those statutory duties may be “not in accordance with law” and thus unlawful agency action under the APA — which is exactly the panel’s point in saying the government “is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”
- If, however, § 1522 is discretionary as Judge Lee argues, then those same executive decisions are less vulnerable to being set aside as inconsistent with the statute — which is why the panel divides on the underlying statutory question.

Bottom line
The opinion frames the dispute as an APA claim because the contested conduct consists of discrete executive/agency acts (funding suspension and termination of cooperative agreements) whose legality under § 1522 is contested, and the opinion does not identify an express private cause of action under § 1522 itself. The “final agency action” the plaintiffs attacked (and that the district court sought to remedy) is the State Department’s suspension of funding and termination of the cooperative agreements that implement reception-and-placement services.
