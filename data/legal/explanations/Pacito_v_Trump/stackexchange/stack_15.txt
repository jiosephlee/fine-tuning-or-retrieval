### Do refugees outside the United States have standing given consular nonreviewability?
Question:
Some plaintiffs are approved for resettlement but remain abroad. Given the deference under Hawaii and the consular nonreviewability doctrine, how did (or could) the court handle standing and justiciability for those plaintiffs challenging the suspension of entry or the termination of overseas processing support?
Answer:
Short answer
Consular nonreviewability did not automatically defeat the plaintiffs’ ability to obtain relief for refugees who had already been conditionally approved for resettlement and remained abroad. The Ninth Circuit treated reviewability and justiciability as distinct from the high degree of deference owed to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (the Hawaii line of cases). The panel concluded (1) the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the President’s suspension of refugee entry under § 1182(f) and thus stayed most of the district court injunction, but (2) it would not stay the injunction insofar as it applied to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee resettlement before January 20, 2025. That carveout reflects the court’s view that consular nonreviewability/immigration deference limits merits review of entry suspensions but does not automatically deprive already-approved refugees abroad of standing or equitable relief against programmatic actions (like suspension of processing or funding) that block their imminent entry.

Legal doctrine and how the opinion applies it
1. Consular nonreviewability and § 1182(f)/Hawaii deference
- The opinion reiterates the Hawaii rule that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and vests “ample power” to suspend entry of classes of aliens; the court said it “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.’” (Opinion, quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684, 686–88.)
- Because of that deference, the panel concluded the government was “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” and therefore stayed (most of) the district court injunction as to the suspension of admissions.

2. Standing/justiciability for those already conditionally approved abroad
- The court’s orders (March 25 and later clarifications) expressly denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by USCIS before January 20, 2025.” (March 25 Order, cited in the opinion and described in this opinion’s background.) The panel later clarified and preserved a “limited carveout” for those individuals.
- That disposition shows the panel treated conditionally approved refugees abroad differently from a garden‑variety consular visa denial. These plaintiffs had a special status: USCIS approval for resettlement before the President’s suspension, which creates an imminent, particularized injury (loss of a promised opportunity to enter) distinct from a mere challenge to a consular officer’s discretionary visa decision. The court therefore left intact preliminary relief protecting those approved applicants.

3. Why consular nonreviewability did not foreclose relief here
- The opinion distinguishes two things: (a) merits review of the President’s statutory authority to suspend entry (where Hawaii deference controls), and (b) review of agency action under the APA and statutory duties to provide services and to process refugees. The panel said it would reach the merits on some APA claims (notably § 1522 issues) and concluded the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim (which justified directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services to refugees already admitted).
- For applicants abroad who already have conditional USCIS approval, the injury is not a simple consular discretionary denial but an executive suspension of the program that prevents their lawful admission. The carveout indicates the court viewed that injury as cognizable and equitably remediable at the preliminary-injunction stage.

What the court did (practical outcome)
- Stayed the district court’s injunctions in most respects because the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the § 1182(f) entry suspension (Hawaii deference).
- Refused to stay the injunction insofar as it applied to refugees who had been conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025 — i.e., those plaintiffs remained protected by the district court’s preliminary relief.
- On a related but separate track, the panel ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees who have been admitted, because the court concluded the government was not likely to prevail on APA/statutory claims concerning § 1522 duties for admitted refugees (and the government admitted uncertainty about replacing those services).

Takeaway for future litigants
- Consular nonreviewability and presidential immigration deference remain powerful limits on merits review of entry suspensions, but they do not categorically eliminate standing or equitable relief for people who already possess a particularized, pre-existing approval (e.g., USCIS conditional approval) and face a programmatic suspension that prevents their imminent lawful entry.
- Courts will treat (1) a facial or programmatic suspension of admission relying on § 1182(f) as highly deferential on the merits, while (2) statutory/APA challenges to how the government funds or implements resettlement and processing programs (and claims by those already approved) can present justiciable injuries that survive preliminary‑relief scrutiny. The Ninth Circuit’s carveout for conditionally approved refugees embodies that division.
