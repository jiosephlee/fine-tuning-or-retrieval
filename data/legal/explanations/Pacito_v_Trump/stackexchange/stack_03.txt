Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

### Why does the court evaluate the § 1182(f) challenge outside the APA framework?
Question:
Franklin v. Massachusetts says the APA doesn’t apply to the President. Here, plaintiffs also brought APA claims, but the panel’s § 1182(f) analysis seems to rest on non-APA review (constitutional/statutory). What is the jurisdictional basis for reviewing presidential action here, and are agencies’ implementing actions the real target for judicial review?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treated the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as a matter for statutory/constitutional review rather than APA rulemaking review because the APA does not govern the President himself. At the same time the court made clear that the implementation steps taken by agencies (the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and the HHS duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522) are subject to ordinary judicial review and can be enjoined if they conflict with statutory obligations. In other words: the proclamation is evaluated on statutory/textual and constitutional grounds (with the extreme deference described in Trump v. Hawaii); the agencies’ implementing actions are the proper, judicially reviewable targets when plaintiffs challenge discrete agency conduct (and the court in fact ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements under § 1522).

Why the court steps outside the APA for the § 1182(f) claim
- The APA provides a mechanism for reviewing “agency” action, not actions of the President. Franklin v. Massachusetts stands for that separation: the APA does not apply to the President. The opinion here relies on that principle (and on Trump v. Hawaii) in treating the President’s invocation of § 1182(f) as a distinct, non-APA inquiry. See the opinion’s reliance on Trump v. Hawaii language that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and that the “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Op. at 2–3 (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684–85). That framing is statutory/textual review of the President’s delegated power, not APA notice-and-comment or arbitrary-and-capricious review of an agency rule.

What law governs review of the President’s § 1182(f) action
- The court frames the § 1182(f) question as one of statutory scope and constitutional limits: does the order exceed what § 1182(f) permits (and if so, does that raise constitutional problems)? The panel used Trump v. Hawaii to explain that § 1182(f) vests broad, deferential presidential authority to suspend entry, and concluded the President satisfied the statutory predicate (“the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here”). Op. at 3. That is the classic approach when the plaintiff challenges the President’s statutory authority: analyze the statute’s text and any constitutional constraints, not the APA’s procedural standards.

What provides jurisdiction to the courts here — and why agencies matter
- Plaintiffs sued not only the President but also challenged concrete agency actions (the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements; HHS responsibilities under 8 U.S.C. § 1522). Those agency actions are classically reviewable under the APA and under statutory-compulsory-duty principles. The panel explicitly addresses § 1522 duties and funding provisions, treats the agencies’ conduct as subject to judicial enforcement, and directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522. Op. at 10–13 (directing reinstatement in light of § 1522 and the government’s admitted termination of agreements).

- Put another way: the President’s proclamation is a policy decision (reviewed as a statutory/textual matter with strong deference). But when that policy is executed through agency acts—terminating contracts, cutting funding, or failing to perform statutory duties—those discrete agency acts fall within the courts’ ordinary review power (APA and statutory enforcement). The opinion embodies precisely that split: substantial deference to the President under § 1182(f) (and thus a high bar to invalidate the proclamation itself), while enforcing statutory obligations by agencies under § 1522 when those obligations appear unfulfilled (hence the order to reinstate cooperative agreements).

Relevant parts of the opinion supporting this split
- “Franklin … the APA doesn’t apply to the President” is the premise for treating the proclamation differently from agency action (see the panel’s reliance on Trump v. Hawaii and the court’s description of § 1182(f) as conferring “broad discretion” and supplying only a single prerequisite: the President’s finding). Op. at 2–3.
- The panel’s conclusion that the government is “likely to prevail” on the § 1182(f) claim follows from that statutory/textual reading: “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Op. at 3.
- By contrast, the panel explicitly treats the § 1522/agency claims as justiciable: it explains the statutory duties in § 1522, recounts the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements, notes the government’s representation about seeking new vendors, and then directs reinstatement of agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” Op. at 10–13.

A procedural takeaway for law students
- When litigation challenges immigration policy tied to a presidential proclamation under § 1182(f), expect two different analytical tracks: (1) the proclamation itself will be reviewed on statutory and constitutional grounds with heavy deference (not under the APA); (2) any implementing agency conduct—contract terminations, funding decisions, failure to perform statutory duties—remains subject to APA/statutory review and injunction. This opinion follows that pattern: it sustains strong deference to the President’s § 1182(f) finding while policing agency compliance with § 1522 and granting relief aimed at agency implementation.

Note on internal disagreement
- Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent highlights that reasonable statutory interpretation questions remain about whether § 1522 is mandatory or permissive; that debate explains why the court gave careful attention to agency duties rather than collapsing all review into a single APA or presidential-review box. Op. at 13–15 (Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

Conclusion
- The APA doesn’t provide the framework for reviewing the President’s § 1182(f) invocation; statutory/textual and constitutional analysis does. But courts retain jurisdiction to review and enjoin agency implementing acts (and to enforce statutory duties like § 1522), and the opinion expressly exercises that power by ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements even while upholding the President’s discretion under § 1182(f).
