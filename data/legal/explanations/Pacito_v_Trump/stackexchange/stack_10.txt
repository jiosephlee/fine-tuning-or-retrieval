### Does the Tucker Act or procurement law preclude APA review of terminating cooperative agreements?
Question:
Cooperative agreements are assistance instruments, but they are still government agreements. Are disputes over termination of such agreements considered contract claims that must go to the Court of Federal Claims, thereby barring APA equitable relief like reinstatement? How do courts draw the line between contract remedies and APA challenges to programmatic policy changes effected via terminations?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the Tucker Act and procurement jurisdiction do not automatically bar APA review or equitable relief (like reinstatement) when challengers press a programmatic statutory claim that the agency unlawfully ended funding or otherwise failed to carry out a statutory duty. Courts draw the line by asking whether the plaintiff’s claim is essentially a contract claim (enforcing private contractual rights or seeking money damages for breach) — which belongs in the Court of Federal Claims under the Tucker Act — or instead is a challenge to agency action (a programmatic or statutory-policy decision) that is reviewable under the APA and susceptible to prospective equitable relief.

Why and how the opinion supports that answer
- The panel treated the organizational plaintiffs’ challenge as an APA/programmatic claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, not a pure procurement/contract enforcement action. The opinion reviews § 1522’s substantive duties and funding framework (pp. 6–11 of the excerpt) and directed the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That is prospective, programmatic relief restoring a statutorily defined program — the kind of remedy courts routinely grant under the APA.
- The court expressly tied the plaintiffs’ likely APA success to the statutory obligations in § 1522 (not to the private contract terms of particular cooperative agreements). See the panel’s discussion: § 1522 requires certain reception-and-placement services and authorizes the use of “contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements” to carry them out; the court ordered reinstatement because the government’s termination left uncertainty about compliance with § 1522 (pp. 6–11). The remedy was framed as restoring statutory program services, not as enforcing particular contractual entitlements or awarding money damages.
- The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee illustrates the relevant delimiting inquiry. Judge Lee does not say Tucker Act or procurement law automatically forecloses APA review; instead he disputes the legal characterization of § 1522 (authorization vs. mandate). That debate shows two things: (1) courts look to the statutory source of the duty (is there a nondiscretionary statutory obligation?) and (2) if the plaintiff’s claim rests on a nondiscretionary statutory duty, the APA is an appropriate vehicle for injunctive relief even when the government uses cooperative agreements to implement the program (Lee’s opinion disputes whether § 1522 imposes that duty, not whether APA is categorically displaced).

Doctrinal rule-of-thumb from the opinion and related doctrine
- If the plaintiff’s claim requires interpretation or enforcement of the parties’ contractual rights (e.g., bid protests, entitlement to contract performance, or money damages for breach), Tucker Act/COFC jurisdiction and procurement rules typically control — such claims are generally routed to the Court of Federal Claims.
- If the plaintiff challenges an agency’s change in program policy or statutory implementation (alleging the agency acted unlawfully under a statute and seeking prospective relief to conform agency action to that statute), the APA is the right path and district courts can issue equitable relief such as reinstatement of the program or cooperative agreements — provided the statutory duty at issue is not wholly discretionary and the requested relief is prospective (as here).
- Practical markers courts use (illustrated by this opinion):
  - Source of the right: Is it contractual (private agreement) or statutory (public program requirement)? The panel focused on § 1522 as the statutory source.
  - Nature of relief sought: Monetary damages or contract-specific remedies point toward Tucker Act/COFC; prospective injunctive relief to compel statutory compliance supports APA/district-court review. The panel directed reinstatement, a prospective remedy.
  - Whether relief would require judicial resolution of complex contract administration, bid selection, or money-mandating questions. The panel did not purport to micromanage contract performance; it ordered reinstatement to meet statutory obligations.
  - The presence of statutory language making performance “to the extent of available appropriations” or otherwise discretionary can undercut a claim that the statute mandates relief — see Judge Lee’s dissent explaining why he reads § 1522 as authorizing rather than mandating programs. That statutory construction question is often dispositive.

Bottom line
This opinion demonstrates that the existence of cooperative agreements does not automatically convert every termination dispute into a Tucker Act/procurement-only controversy. When plaintiffs press that an agency’s termination undermines a nondiscretionary statutory duty and seek prospective relief to restore programmatic services, courts can and will treat that as an APA claim and grant equitable relief (here, reinstatement), unless the statute or facts show the duty is purely discretionary or the relief intrudes into classic contract adjudication reserved to the Court of Federal Claims.
