Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 5: # Scope of Entry and Final Disposition

Having established that Lisa Coleman voluntarily consented to Officer Kizer's entry into the home, the analysis shifts to the boundaries of that consent. The Fourth Amendment does not grant police unlimited authority simply because a resident opens the door; rather, a search conducted under consent must remain within the scope of what a reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the consenting party. In *United States v. Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit applied this "objective reasonableness" standard to determine whether the officers' actions inside the home—specifically their canvas of the rooms and interaction with Jaison Coleman—exceeded the permission granted for a child welfare check. The court's resolution of this issue not only validated the discovery of evidence leading to a warrant but also underscored the deference afforded to district courts when reviewing factual findings regarding the scope of consent.

## Linking Stated Purpose (Child Welfare) to Actions Taken (Canvas and Pat-down)

The central question regarding the scope of consent was whether the officers' conduct aligned with the specific purpose for which entry was granted: ensuring the safety of Lisa's children. Officer Kizer explicitly framed his request to enter around this concern, stating he needed to "make sure" the kids were okay after Lisa expressed uncertainty about their status. Once inside, the officers did not immediately begin rummaging through drawers or searching for contraband; instead, their actions were strictly tethered to the stated objective of verifying occupant safety. Lisa directed them to her bedroom where the children were located, but the officers' duty to ensure safety logically extended beyond merely sighting the children. To confirm that no threat lingered within the home, it was necessary to account for all individuals present, particularly Jaison Coleman, who had been identified as the source of the threat. Consequently, when Coleman was not found in the room with the children, a quick canvas of the remaining areas to locate him was a direct and necessary extension of the welfare check. Similarly, once Coleman was located, his agreement to a protective pat-down served the immediate goal of neutralizing any potential danger he might pose to his family or the officers, fitting squarely within the ambit of a safety-oriented entry.

## 'Objective Reasonableness' in Locating All Occupants During a Safety Check

The court evaluated these actions through the lens of "objective reasonableness," asking what a typical reasonable person would have understood by Lisa's invitation for police to enter and verify her children's safety. Under this standard, consent to search for a specific purpose includes those acts reasonably necessary to execute that purpose. The Seventh Circuit reasoned that a reasonable person understanding an officer's intent to check on children in a domestic violence scenario would anticipate that the officer needs to see every part of the house where a threat could hide. One cannot genuinely verify that children are safe from an aggressive stepfather without confirming that stepfather's location and condition. Therefore, scanning the home and locating Coleman in a separate bedroom was not an exploratory fishing expedition but a logical component of the welfare check authorized by Lisa. The court noted that Lisa herself facilitated this process by identifying Coleman's likely location ("back bedroom") and guiding officers toward the children. Because finding and assessing Coleman was intrinsic to determining if the children were truly "okay," these steps remained within the scope of her consent.

## Transition from Consensual Entry to Warranted Search

The validity of consensual entry often serves as the precursor to obtaining judicial authorization for a more extensive search based on probable cause developed during that lawful presence. In *Coleman*, once officers were lawfully inside pursuant to Lisa's consent, they gathered critical information through their interactions with Cole-man and his stepchildren. The children disclosed that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened arson—details constituting fresh evidence of criminal activity and ongoing danger.
