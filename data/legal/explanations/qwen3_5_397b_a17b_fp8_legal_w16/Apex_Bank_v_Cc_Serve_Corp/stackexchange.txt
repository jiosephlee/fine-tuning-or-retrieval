Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What is the procedural posture of an appeal from the TTAB to the Federal Circuit, and what standard of review applies to factual findings versus legal conclusions?
Question:
I'm confused about how the Federal Circuit reviews decisions from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The opinion mentions reviewing legal conclusions *de novo* but factual findings for 'substantial evidence.' Can someone explain exactly what 'substantial evidence' means in this context? Does it mean the court just checks if there is *any* evidence, or do they re-weigh the evidence themselves? Also, why does the Federal Circuit have jurisdiction here instead of a District Court?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, here is the breakdown of the procedural posture, jurisdiction, and standards of review applicable to appeals from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to the Federal Circuit.

### Procedural Posture and Jurisdiction
The procedural posture of this case is a direct appeal from a final decision of the TTAB sustaining an opposition to trademark registration. Unlike civil actions filed in district courts under 15 U.S.C. § 1071(b), which allow for a trial *de novo* and the introduction of new evidence, an appeal to the Federal Circuit under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B) is strictly limited to the administrative record developed before the Board.

In *Apex Bank*, the court explicitly grounds its authority in **28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)**. Because this is an appellate review of an agency decision rather than a new lawsuit, the Federal Circuit does not hear witness testimony or accept new exhibits. Instead, it reviews the Board's application of law to the facts already established in the record (referenced throughout the opinion as "J.A." or Joint Appendix citations).

### Standards of Review
The Federal Circuit applies a bifurcated standard of review depending on whether the issue is one of law or fact:

**1. Legal Conclusions: *De Novo***
The court reviews all legal conclusions *de novo*, meaning it gives no deference to the Board's legal interpretations and decides the legal questions anew.
*   **Application in *Apex Bank*:** The court applied this standard when determining the proper scope of the sixth *DuPont* factor. The Board had committed a legal error by restricting its analysis of third-party marks only to "credit card services" while simultaneously finding in its analysis of the second factor that "banking services" and "credit card services" were "legally identical, in part." The Federal Circuit held that imposing a stricter standard of similarity for the sixth factor than for the second was a flaw in legal reasoning. Consequently, they vacated the Board's finding on this factor without deferring to the Board's narrower interpretation.

**2. Factual Findings: Substantial Evidence**
The court reviews factual findings for **substantial evidence**. This is a highly deferential standard.
*   **Definition:** As defined in *Apex Bank* (quoting *Consol. Edison Co. v. NLRB*), substantial evidence is "**such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.**"
*   **Does this mean "any" evidence?** No. It requires more than a mere scintilla, but it does **not** permit the court to re-weigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the Board. If there is enough evidence in the record that a reasonable person could rely on to reach the Board's conclusion, the finding stands—even if the Federal Circuit judges might have decided differently had they been sitting as the initial trier of fact.
*   **Application in *Apex Bank*:** The court affirmed the Board's finding regarding the **second *DuPont* factor** (similarity of goods/services). Apex argued that banking and credit card services were not competitive because CC Serve partners with banks rather than being a bank itself. However, looking at definitions and third-party registrations showing these services can emanate from a single source, the Federal Circuit concluded that "substantial evidence supports [this] finding." They did not re-evaluate whether they personally believed confusion was likely; they merely confirmed that reasonable minds could accept Congress's definitions and third-party registrations as adequate proof that Apex reached their conclusion correctly based on facts alone where no legal errors occurred below (like misinterpreting statutory terms).

### How can 'banking services' and 'credit card services' be considered 'legally identical' when the applicants operate differently (issuer vs. servicer)?
Question:
In Section III.A, the Board finds that Apex's 'banking and financing services' and CC Serve's 'credit card services' are 'legally identical, in part,' even though Apex doesn't currently issue credit cards and CC Serve partners with banks rather than being a bank itself. The court affirms this based on dictionary definitions and the idea that services need not be identical, only related enough to cause confusion. How broad is this interpretation? If two companies perform different functions in the same financial ecosystem, does that automatically make their services 'related' under the second *DuPont* factor?
Answer:
The short answer to your question is that under the second *DuPont* factor, "relatedness" is not determined by whether two companies perform the exact same operational function (e.g., issuer vs. servicer), but rather by whether the services are sufficiently connected in the minds of consumers that they might believe they originate from the same source.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's finding that "banking and financing services" and "credit card services" are "legally identical, in part," despite the structural differences you noted. The court's reasoning rests on three key pillars found in Section III.A of the opinion:

**1. Functional Overlap via Dictionary Definitions**
The court looked past the corporate structure (who holds the charter vs. who manages the account) and focused on the intrinsic nature of the services. The Board defined "credit card services" broadly to include issuing cards, processing payments, managing accounts, and extending credit. Crucially, the Board found that standard dictionary definitions of "banking" and "finance" inherently encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." Because Apex's identified services ("banking and financing") naturally cover the activities CC Serve performs (managing credit accounts and facilitating credit), the services overlap legally, even if Apex does not currently issue cards itself.

**2. The "Same Source" Standard**
You asked if different functions in the same ecosystem automatically make services related. The standard articulated in the opinion is slightly narrower but still broad: services are related if they are "of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." The Board supported this by citing third-party registrations where single entities register marks for both credit card and general banking services. This evidence suggests to the court that consumers are accustomed to seeing one brand provide both traditional banking and credit card management. Therefore, a consumer seeing "ASPIRE BANK" and "ASPIRE" credit cards could reasonably assume they come from the same entity, regardless of whether that entity technically issues the card or merely services it.

**3. Rejection of Operational Distinctions**
Apex argued precisely what you are asking: that CC Serve is merely a partner/servicer and not a bank, so the services aren't competitive. The Federal Circuit rejected this as "unavailing." The court held that substantial evidence supported the finding of high similarity because the *services offered to the public* (access to credit, account management) are highly similar, even if the backend business models differ. The legal test protects against consumer confusion regarding origin, not against confusion regarding corporate licensing agreements or regulatory charters.

**Conclusion**
This interpretation is indeed broad, but it is bounded by consumer perception rather than technical industry roles. Two companies performing different functions in the same financial ecosystem are not *automatically* related under *DuPont* factor two; however, they will be found related if:
1.  Their service descriptions overlap in common parlance (dictionary definitions); and
2.  Market reality shows that such services frequently emanate from a single source (third-party registrations).

In this case, because banking naturally includes financing via credit cards, and because consumers expect banks to offer credit products, the distinction between "issuer" and "servicer" was insufficient to break the chain of relatedness.

### Why did the Federal Circuit find error in the Board's definition of 'similar goods' when switching from DuPont Factor 2 to Factor 6?
Question:
This is the core of my confusion regarding the court's reasoning. In Factor 2 (similarity of goods), the Board ruled that banking and credit card services are highly similar (almost identical). However, for Factor 6 (third-party marks), the Board narrowed its focus only to marks used on 'credit card services,' excluding general banking marks. The Federal Circuit called this inconsistent. Why must the definition of 'similar goods' remain constant across all *DuPont* factors? Isn't it logical that third-party usage might look different depending on the specific niche being analyzed?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit found error because the Board applied **inconsistent legal standards** for what constitutes "similar goods" across two different prongs of the same *DuPont* likelihood-of-confusion analysis. While the scope of goods can vary depending on the specific factor being analyzed, the Board cannot make a factual finding that goods are "highly similar" (or even "legally identical") for one factor and then arbitrarily narrow that definition to exclude those same goods when analyzing a different factor.

Here is the breakdown of the court's reasoning based on *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*:

### 1. The Inconsistency in Factual Findings
In its analysis of **DuPont Factor 2** (similarity of goods), the Board conducted a detailed review of the services and concluded that Apex's "banking and financing services" and CC Serve's "credit card services" were **"highly similar"** and, in part, **"legally identical."** J.A. 27–28. The Board relied on dictionary definitions and third-party registrations showing that banking often encompasses issuing credit cards. Based on this broad definition of similarity, the Board ruled that Factor 2 weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion.

However, when analyzing **DuPont Factor 6** (number and nature of similar marks in use), the Board abruptly shifted gears. It defined the relevant universe of goods strictly as **"credit card services,"** thereby excluding third-party marks used on broader "banking and financing services." J.A. 47. By doing so, the Board ignored evidence of third-party "Aspire" marks in the broader banking sector that Apex had submitted.

### 2. The Legal Error: Changing the Goalposts
The Federal Circuit held that once the Board makes a factual finding that two types of services are highly similar, it must maintain that **same scope of similarity** when evaluating other factors, particularly Factor 6.

The court explained that Factor 6 does not require goods to be *identical*, only *similar*. Since the Board had already established as a matter of fact that general banking services and credit card services are highly similar under Factor 2, there was no legal basis to impose a "more stringent legal standard for similarity" under Factor 6. *Apex Bank*, slip op. at 7.

By narrowing the definition only for Factor 6, the Board artificially limited the evidence it would consider regarding the strength of CC Serve's mark. If the goods are similar enough to cause confusion (Factor 2), they are similar enough to count toward a crowded field analysis (Factor 6). Excluding general banking marks from the Factor 6 analysis contradicted the Board's own earlier finding that banking and credit card services are essentially overlapping markets.

### Conclusion
The error was not that the Board looked at different *types* of evidence for different factors, but that it changed the **definition of the relevant market** mid-analysis without justification. The court vacated the Factor 6 finding (and consequently the Factor 1 analysis, which depends on mark strength) and remanded with instructions to reconsider third-party marks using the **broad definition of similarity** already established in the Factor 2 analysis.

### How does the presence of third-party marks ('crowded field') impact the strength of a trademark and the likelihood of confusion analysis?
Question:
The opinion discusses Factor 6 extensively, noting that a 'crowded field' of similar marks makes consumers more adept at distinguishing between them, thereby weakening the senior mark's scope of protection. The Board found nine third-party uses insufficient to show weakness, citing cases where 14 or 26 uses were required. Is there a magic number of third-party marks needed to prove a field is crowded? How does proving a mark is 'weak' change the outcome of the overall likelihood of confusion test?
Answer:
There is no "magic number" of third-party marks required to prove a trademark field is crowded. As illustrated in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the inquiry is qualitative rather than purely quantitative; the critical issue is whether the evidence demonstrates that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks in the relevant market, thereby rendering the senior mark "weak" and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.

In *Apex*, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) erred not because nine third-party uses were numerically insufficient compared to precedents involving 14 or 26 marks, but because it artificially restricted the universe of comparable marks. The Board had previously found under the second *DuPont* factor that credit card services and banking/financing services were "highly similar," even "legally identical, in part." Yet, when analyzing the sixth *DuPont* factor (the number and nature of similar marks), the Board excluded third-party marks containing "Aspire" that were used for general banking services, considering only those specific to credit cards.

The Federal Circuit vacated this finding, holding that the scope of similarity must remain consistent across factors. If services are deemed similar for the purpose of establishing a risk of confusion, they must also be deemed similar when assessing whether a crowded field dilutes that risk. By excluding relevant banking marks, the Board failed to properly evaluate whether the term "Aspire" was weak in the broader financial services landscape.

Proving a mark is "weak" fundamentally alters the likelihood of confusion analysis in two key ways:

1.  **Narrow Scope of Protection:** A weak mark receives a narrower scope of protection than a strong, arbitrary, or fanciful mark. As noted in *Palm Bay Imports* and cited in *Apex*, when a field is crowded, consumers become more adept at identifying minor differences between marks. Consequently, a senior user cannot prevent others from using similar terms unless those terms are nearly identical.
2.  **Impact on Commercial Impression:** The strength of a mark directly influences the first *DuPont* factor (similarity of the marks). In *Apex*, the Federal Circuit explained that if the sixth factor establishes weakness due to a crowded field, this changes the "overall commercial impression" of the senior mark. A weak mark creates a different commercial impression than a strong one, often making confusion less likely even if the marks share common elements (like the word "Aspire").

Therefore, while prior cases mentioning 14 or 26 marks provide context for what constitutes "voluminous" evidence, there is no statutory threshold. The determinative factor is whether the proffered evidence—properly scoped to include all similar goods and services—proves that the marketplace conditions have educated consumers to distinguish among competitors using similar terminology.

### If Factor 6 (commercial strength) is recalculated on remand, why must Factor 1 (similarity of marks) also be vacated and reconsidered?
Question:
In Section III.C, the court vacates the Board's analysis of Factor 1 (appearance, sound, connotation) simply because Factor 6 is being remanded. The court states that commercial strength affects 'overall commercial impression.' I thought Factor 1 was purely about comparing the two marks side-by-side visually and phonetically. How does knowing whether a market is crowded or a mark is weak change the actual comparison of the words 'ASPIRE' vs. 'ASPIRE BANK'? Doesn't that conflate two separate factors?
Answer:
You are correct that Factor 1 (similarity of marks) traditionally involves a side-by-side comparison of the marks' appearance, sound, and connotation. However, under Federal Circuit precedent, the ultimate inquiry for Factor 1 is not merely a mechanical comparison of these elements, but an assessment of the **"overall commercial impression"** created by the marks.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court vacates Factor 1 alongside Factor 6 precisely because **commercial strength** (determined in Factor 6) fundamentally alters that "overall commercial impression."

Here is the breakdown of the court's reasoning based on Section III.C of the opinion:

### 1. Commercial Strength Defines Context
Factor 6 analyzes the number and nature of similar marks in the marketplace to determine if a mark is "weak" or "strong."
*   If a market is crowded with similar marks (e.g., many banks using "Aspire"), consumers are trained to look for minute differences to distinguish sources. In this context, small differences between "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" carry significant weight.
*   If the market is uncrowded and the mark is strong, consumers rely more on the dominant shared term ("ASPIRE"), making minor additions like "BANK" less effective at distinguishing the source.

### 2. The Interdependence of Factors
The court explicitly states: *"Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board's analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."*

The logic is that you cannot accurately gauge the "commercial impression" (Factor 1) without knowing the commercial context (Factor 6). The same two words can create entirely different impressions depending on whether they exist in a vacuum or amidst a sea of similar competitors.

### Conclusion
The court did not conflate the factors; rather, it recognized that they are analytically linked. Since the Board erred in defining the scope of relevant third-party marks for Factor 6 (Section III.B), its conclusion regarding commercial strength is unreliable. Without a valid determination of commercial strength, any finding regarding the "overall commercial impression" under Factor 1 is necessarily flawed. Therefore, both must be reconsidered together on remand to ensure the similarity analysis reflects the correct market reality.

### What is the significance of an Intent-to-Use application in a Section 2(d) opposition when the applicant has not yet launched their service?
Question:
Apex Bank filed an intent-to-use application; they don't actually offer credit cards yet. CC Serve opposes based on likelihood of confusion. Since Apex isn't currently using the mark in commerce for these specific services, how does the Board evaluate 'concurrent use'? Are they hypothesizing how Apex *would* use it? Does Apex's status as a future entrant affect how strictly they are held to compared to an existing competitor?
Answer:
In a Section 2(d) opposition involving an Intent-to-Use (ITU) application, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) does not evaluate "concurrent use" based on the applicant's current market activities, nor does it speculate on how the applicant *might* hypothetically use the mark in the future. Instead, the analysis presumes that the ITU applicant will use the mark exactly as described in their application identification of goods or services.

As illustrated in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the fact that Apex Bank had not yet launched credit card services or issued cards under the "ASPIRE BANK" mark did not shield it from a likelihood of confusion finding, nor did it alter the fundamental standard of comparison. The court reaffirmed that under Section 2(d), the inquiry turns on whether confusion is likely due to the **concurrent use** of the marks on their **respective goods**. For an ITU applicant, those "respective goods" are defined strictly by the scope of the application itself.

In *Apex*, although Apex was merely a retail bank offering checking and mortgage services at the time, its ITU application sought registration for "[b]anking and financing services." The Board (and the Federal Circuit on appeal) analyzed the similarity of services by comparing CC Serve's registered "credit card services" against the full breadth of services listed in Apex's application. The court affirmed the Board's finding that these services were "highly similar" and even "legally identical, in part," because the ordinary meaning of "banking" and "finance" encompasses issuing credit cards. 

Crucially, the court did not engage in a hypothetical exercise about how Apex *would* enter the market; rather, it treated the application's identification as a definitive statement of what Apex *will* do upon registration. Consequently:

1.  **No Hypothetical Usage Required:** The Board does not guess at marketing strategies or specific product features not contained in the ID manual. It assumes the applicant will offer all services listed in the application in a normal commercial manner.
2.  **Strict Comparison Based on Application Scope:** An ITU applicant is held to the same strictures as an existing user regarding the scope of their applied-for goods. Because Apex applied for broad "financing services," it could not argue that its actual current operations (which excluded credit cards) made the services dissimilar. The potential for overlap inherent in the application language was sufficient to satisfy the relatedness prong of the *DuPont* analysis.
3.  **Future Entrant Status:** Being a future entrant does not lower the bar for likelihood of confusion. To the contrary, Section 2(d) is designed precisely to prevent registration where confusion is likely *upon* introduction into commerce. If the applied-for services overlap with a registrant's existing services—as they did here regarding credit-related financing—the opposition can succeed even before the applicant sells a single unit.

Therefore, in an ITU context, "concurrent use" is a legal construct comparingthe opposer's actual usage againstthe applicant's *proposed* usage as codified in their application specifications. The absence of current sales affects neither the validity of the opposition nor the rigor of the *DuPont* factor analysis regarding service relatedness.
