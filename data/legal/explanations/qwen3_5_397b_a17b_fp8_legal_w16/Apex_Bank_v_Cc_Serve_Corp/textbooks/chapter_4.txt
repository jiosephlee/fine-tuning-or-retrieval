Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # Interdependence of Factors and Remand Instructions

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit illustrates a critical principle in trademark litigation: the thirteen *DuPont* factors used to determine likelihood of confusion are not isolated silos of analysis but an interconnected web where an error in one strand can unravel the entire fabric of the decision. While the court affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) finding regarding the similarity of the parties' services, it identified a fundamental flaw in how the Board assessed the strength of CC Serve's "ASPIRE" mark. This error did not remain contained; rather, it cascaded into the analysis of mark similarity, necessitating a partial vacatur and remand. This chapter examines how the court navigated the interdependence of commercial strength and commercial impression, demonstrating why a correct understanding of the marketplace is essential before comparing two marks side-by-side.

## The First DuPont Factor: Appearance, Sound, Connotation, and Commercial Impression

The first *DuPont* factor requires an assessment of the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties, considering their appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. As established in precedent cited by the court, such as *In re I.AM.Symbolic*, this inquiry is not a mechanical dissection of individual letters or syllables but a holistic evaluation of the overall commercial impression created by the marks. The central question is whether the marks are sufficiently similar that consumers are likely to believe they emanate from the same source. In *Apex Bank*, the comparison was between CC Serve's standard character mark "ASPIRE," used for credit card services, and Apex Bank's proposed "ASPIRE BANK" word and design marks for banking and financing services.

On its face, the shared root word "Aspire" suggests a degree of similarity. However, the court emphasizes that the weight given to this shared element depends heavily on context. If "Aspire" is a unique, arbitrary term in the financial sector, its presence in both marks carries significant weight toward finding confusion. Conversely, if "Aspire" is merely one of many common terms used by various entities in finance, its probative value diminishes. The Board initially concluded that the first factor weighed in favor of CC Serve, implying that the shared term created a strong likelihood of confusion. Yet, as the appellate court revealed, this conclusion rested on an incomplete factual foundation regarding how consumers actually encounter these terms in the marketplace.

## The Interplay Between Commercial Strength and Overall Commercial Impression

The sixth *DuPont* factor analyzes "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves as a proxy for determining the commercial strength or weakness of a plaintiff's mark. The legal theory underpinning this factor is straightforward: when a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become adept at distinguishing minor differences between them. Consequently, a mark existing in a crowded field is considered "weak" and is entitled to only a narrow scope of protection against others using similar terminology. As noted in *Juice Generation* and *Jack Wolfskin*, evidence that consumers are educated to distinguish between marks indicates a lack of exclusive commercial strength for any single user.

In *Apex Bank*, this dynamic creates a direct feedback loop with the first factor. The "commercial impression" analyzed under factor one cannot be accurately gauged without knowing whether the dominant feature of the mark (here, "Aspire") is distinctive or commonplace. If third-party usage reveals that dozens of financial institutions use "Aspire," then seeing "Aspire Bank" next to "Aspire Credit Cards" creates a different commercial impression than if CC Serve were the sole user of that term in finance. In a crowded market, consumers expect variation and look to secondary features—such as house names like "Bank" or specific logos—to differentiate sources. Therefore, an erroneous finding on factor six inevitably distorts the analysis under factor one by inflating perceived similarities that would otherwise be viewed as insignificant distinctions in a busy marketplace.

## Why Vacating Factor Six Necessitates Vacating Factor One

The core holding of this portion of *Apex Bank* rests on recognizing that you cannot separate market reality from visual comparison. The TTAB committed legal error by artificially restricting its analysis under factor six while simultaneously relying on broad findings under factor two (relatedness of goods). Specifically, having determined under factor two that credit card services and banking/financing services were "highly similar"—even partially legally identical—the Board paradoxically narrowed its lens for factor six to include only third-party marks used strictly for "credit card services."
