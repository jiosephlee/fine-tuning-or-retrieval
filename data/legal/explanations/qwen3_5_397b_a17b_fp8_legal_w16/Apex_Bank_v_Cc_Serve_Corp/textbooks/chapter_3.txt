Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 3: # The Error in Defining Relevant Markets for Third-Party Marks (DuPont Factor Six)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit addressed a critical methodological error committed by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) during its likelihood-of-confusion analysis. While the Board correctly identified a high degree of similarity between the parties' services under the second *DuPont* factor, it subsequently undermined its own analysis by artificially narrowing the scope of relevant third-party marks when evaluating the sixth *DuPont* factor. This chapter examines the court's reasoning regarding this inconsistency, explaining how the definition of "similar goods" must remain constant across all factors of the *DuPont* test and why excluding marks from closely related financial sectors constituted reversible legal error. By understanding this distinction, students can appreciate how the "crowded field" doctrine operates to adjust the scope of trademark protection based on the actual marketplace reality rather than rigid categorical distinctions.

## Purpose of Factor Six: Assessing Commercial Strength and Consumer Sophistication

The sixth factor in the *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* analysis evaluates "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." Unlike the first factor, which looks at the marks themselves, or the second, which compares the goods, the sixth factor serves as a thermometer for the commercial environment in which the marks operate. Its primary purpose is to determine the commercial strength of the senior user's mark. When a market is saturated with similar marks used by various entities on similar products, it suggests that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing between brands based on subtle differences. In such a "crowded field," individual marks are generally considered conceptually and commercially weaker, entitling them to a narrower scope of protection against potential infringers. Conversely, if a mark stands alone in a sparse marketplace, even minor similarities with a new entrant might trigger a finding of likely confusion. Therefore, Factor Six is not merely a headcount of competitors; it is an inquiry into consumer sophistication and the distinctiveness of the senior mark within its specific trade sector.

## The 'Crowded Field' Doctrine: How Numerous Similar Marks Narrow Protection Scope

The legal theory underpinning the "crowded field" doctrine is rooted in consumer behavior. As established in precedents like *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC* and *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports*, evidence that many third parties use similar marks on similar goods educates consumers to look beyond common elements to identify the source of a product. If numerous banks and financial institutions utilize variations of the word "Aspire," a reasonable consumer encountering "ASPIRE BANK" would be less likely to assume it originates from the same source as "ASPIRE" credit card services than if CC Serve were the sole user of that term in finance. Consequently, a showing of extensive third-party use tends to negate a finding of likelihood of confusion because it demonstrates that the shared element (in this case, "Aspire") has low discriminatory power. For an applicant like Apex Bank, proving a crowded field is often essential to overcoming an opposition based on a seemingly similar senior mark, as it effectively argues that the senior mark's monopoly over the term should be limited to its exact usage rather than extended to all variations.

## The Board's Erroneous Limitation: Restricting Analysis Only to 'Credit Card Services'

In *Apex Bank*, the TTAB committed a fundamental error by inconsistently defining the universe of comparable goods between Factor Two and Factor Six. When analyzing Factor Two (similarity of goods), the Board adopted a broad interpretation. It recognized that while Apex offered "banking and financing services" and CC Serve offered "credit card services," these categories overlapped significantly. The Board noted that banking inherently involves extending credit and that CC Serve's activities included managing accounts and authorizing transactions functions central to banking. Based on this functional overlap, the Board concluded the services were "legally identical, in part," weighing this factor heavily in favor of finding confusion. However, when shifting to Factor Six to evaluate third-party marks, the Board abruptly narrowed its focus. It restricted its review exclusively to third-party marks used specifically on "credit card services," dismissing Apex's evidence of forty-two other marks containing "Aspire" used in broader banking and financial contexts as "essentially irrelevant." By doing so, the Board ignored evidence that could have demonstrated a crowded field in the broader financial sector where both parties actually compete.

## Inconsistency in Legal Standards: Aligning Service Similarity Across All Factors

The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's decision on Factor Six precisely because an administrative agency cannot apply contradictory legal standards to identical factual predicates within the same proceeding. The court emphasized that once the Board makes a factual finding that two types of services are highly similar—or even partially legally identical—that finding must govern subsequent analyses involving those services. It is logically inconsistent to assert that banking and credit card services are close enough to cause consumer confusion (Factor Two) yet distant enough that third-party marks in general banking are irrelevant to assessing consumer sophistication (Factor Six).
