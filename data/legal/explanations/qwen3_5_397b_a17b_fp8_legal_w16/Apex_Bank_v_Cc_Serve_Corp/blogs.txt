Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Consistency Imperative: Why the *DuPont* Factors Cannot Operate in Silos

In the complex calculus of trademark law, the thirteen *DuPont* factors are rarely treated as a monolithic block; rather, they are distinct lenses through which courts examine the probability of consumer confusion. However, a recent decision from the Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* serves as a sharp reminder that while these factors analyze different aspects of the marketplace, they cannot operate under mutually exclusive definitions of reality. In vacating and remanding the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) refusal to register Apex Bank's "ASPIRE BANK" marks, the court identified a critical logical fracture in the Board's reasoning: the impermissibility of defining services as "legally identical" for one factor while simultaneously narrowing that same universe to exclude relevant third-party marks for another. This ruling does more than correct a specific evidentiary oversight; it establishes a procedural mandate for consistency, preventing agencies from engaging in definitional sleight-of-hand that artificially inflates the risk of confusion.

The underlying dispute centers on CC Serve Corp.'s ownership of the "ASPIRE" mark for credit card services and Apex Bank's attempt to register "ASPIRE BANK" for broader banking and financing services. Although Apex does not currently issue credit cards, it plans to offer internet banking under the new mark. The TTAB sustained CC Serve's opposition, largely driven by its finding under the second *DuPont* factor—that the similarity of goods weighed heavily in favor of confusion. The Board reasoned that because "banking" and "finance" inherently encompass extending credit, the parties' services were "legally identical, in part." This was a robust finding, suggesting a high degree of overlap between a credit card servicer and a retail bank. Yet, when the analysis shifted to the sixth *DuPont* factor—which assesses the strength of a mark based on the number and nature of similar marks in use—the Board abruptly contracted its worldview. Ignoring dozens of third-party uses of "Aspire" in the broader financial sector, the Board limited its inquiry strictly to "credit card services," concluding that only nine such marks existed and therefore the field was not crowded enough to weaken CC Serve's mark.

Judge Hughes's opinion dismantles this bifurcated approach with surgical precision. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's finding on the second factor, agreeing that substantial evidence supported the conclusion that banking and credit card services are highly related. However, the court found legal error in how that very finding was discarded during the sixth factor analysis. The logic is compelling: if the services are so similar that they could emanate from a single source (Factor 2), then third-party marks operating in that same sphere of "similar goods" must be relevant to determining whether consumers can distinguish between them (Factor 6). By restricting the sixth factor analysis to a subset of services narrower than what was established in the second factor, the Board created an internal contradiction. As the court noted, there is no justification for imposing a more stringent legal standard for similarity when evaluating market crowding than when evaluating direct competition. Once a factual finding regarding the scope of relatedness is made, that definition must remain constant throughout the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

This insistence on doctrinal consistency carries significant implications for future trademark litigation before both the TTAB and federal courts. Practitioners have long observed that administrative bodies sometimes engage in "factor siloing," where favorable findings on one element are isolated to avoid undermining unfavorable conclusions on another. *Apex Bank* suggests that such compartmentalization is legally fragile. If an agency determines that two industries are functionally overlapping for the purpose of establishing relatedness, it cannot later pretend those industries are hermetically sealed when assessing mark strength or commercial impression. This prevents agencies from cherry-picking narrow definitions to negate evidence of a crowded market—a tactic that often proves fatal to applicants arguing that their mark deserves only a narrow scope of protection due to widespread third-party use.

Furthermore, the decision highlights the cascading effect of the sixth factor on the first *DuPont* factor: the similarity of the marks themselves. The strength of a mark dictates its scope of protection; a weak mark residing in a crowded field requires a higher degree of similarity between competing marks to find a likelihood of confusion. Because the Board erred in excluding relevant third-party marks from its crowded-field analysis, it inevitably skewed its assessment of CC Serve's commercial strength and, by extension, its evaluation of whether "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" create confusingly similar commercial impressions. By vacating both findings, the Federal Circuit acknowledged that these factors are interlocking gears; if one gear turns on false premises, the entire mechanism fails. On remand, if considering broader financial service uses reveals a truly saturated "Aspire" landscape, CC Serve's mark may be deemed conceptually weak, potentially alteringthe outcome entirely despite unchanged facts about Apex's specific business plan.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* reinforces that trademark analysis is an exercise in holistic realism, not selective abstraction. The marketplace does not shift its boundaries depending on which legal test is being applied; consumers encounter brands within a consistent ecosystem of goods and services. By demanding that the TTAB maintain a uniform definition of "similarity" across all *DuPont* factors, the Federal Circuit ensures that likelihood-of-confusion determinations reflect actual market conditions rather than analytical artifacts. For applicants facing opposition based on crowded fields, this decision provides a powerful tool to challenge decisions where agencies narrowly define relevance only when it suits a finding of infringement, ensuring that evidence of widespread industry usage receives its full due weight.



# The Blurred Lines of Fintech: Why *Apex Bank* Demands a Holistic View of Consumer Confusion

In the rapidly converging worlds of traditional banking and specialized fintech services, the legal boundaries defining consumer confusion are being tested. The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* offers a critical correction to how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) analyzes likelihood of confusion in these hybrid markets. While the court affirmed that "banking" and "credit card services" are legally identical for the purposes of service similarity, it simultaneously rebuked the Board for adopting an artificially narrow lens when evaluating the strength of the marks involved. By vacating and remanding the Board's analysis of third-party usage, *Apex Bank* signals that courts must look at the financial ecosystem holistically; if services are similar enough to cause confusion, the market context used to evaluate mark strength must be equally broad.

The dispute arose when Apex Bank sought to register "ASPIRE BANK" for internet banking services, facing opposition from CC Serve Corp., which holds a longstanding registration for "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services. CC Serve does not issue cards directly but partners with banks to manage credit card programs. The TTAB sustained the opposition, largely driven by its finding that the parties' services were "legally identical, in part." The Board reasoned that because banking inherently involves extending credit, Apex's proposed services overlapped significantly with CC Serve's credit card management. Consequently, the Board concluded that the second *DuPont* factor—the similarity of goods and services—heavily favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit agreed with this assessment, noting that substantial evidence supported the conclusion that these services are related enough that consumers could mistakenly believe they emanate from a single source.

However, the Federal Circuit hit a doctrinal snag when reviewing the Board's treatment of the sixth *DuPont* factor: the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. This factor is crucial because a crowded marketplace suggests that consumers are sophisticated and accustomed to distinguishing between similar marks, thereby weakening the scope of protection afforded to any single registrant. Apex presented evidence of over forty third-party marks containing "Aspire" across the broader financial sector. The TTAB dismissed most of this evidence, restricting its view strictly to marks used on "credit card services" and ignoring those used in general banking or financing. The Board reasoned that since it had defined the relevant public narrowly as consumers of credit card services, only marks in that specific silo mattered. It found nine such marks insufficient to prove weakness, leaving CC Serve's mark with a "normal scope of protection."

Judge Hughes's opinion dismantles this compartmentalized approach as logically inconsistent. The court pointed out a fatal flaw in the Board's reasoning: having already established under the second *DuPont* factor that banking and credit card services are highly similar—indeed, partially identical—it was erroneous to revert to a hyper-specific definition of similarity when analyzing market crowding under the sixth factor. You cannot have it both ways; if the services are close enough to confuse a consumer about their source, they are close enough to inform what that consumer expects regarding branding diversity. By excluding third-party "Aspire" marks used in general banking, the Board failed to account for the reality that consumers encounter these brands within a unified financial landscape. The Federal Circuit rightly noted that imposing a more stringent standard for similarity under factor six than under factor two creates a distorted view of commercial reality.

This inconsistency forced the court to vacate not only the sixth factor analysis but also the first *DuPont* factor regarding the similarity of the marks themselves. The commercial impression of a mark is inextricably linked to its strength; if reconsideration reveals that "Aspire" is a weak term saturated across various financial verticals, the overall impression left on consumers changes fundamentally. A consumer seeing "ASPIRE BANK" alongside an existing "ASPIRE" credit card program may be far less likely to assume common ownership if they are already habituated to seeing multiple "Aspire" entities in finance. By remanding both factors, the Federal Circuit ensures that the Board re-evaluates whether CC Serve's mark truly commands broad protection or if it occupies merely a narrow niche in a crowded field.

The downstream implications of *Apex Bank* extend well beyond this specific dispute over credit cards and checking accounts. As fintech continues to blur the lines between distinct financial instruments—where neobanks offer lending, traditional banks launch digital wallets, and servicers partner with issuers—the temptation for adjudicators to slice markets into granular sub-sectors will persist. This decision serves as a judicial check against such fragmentation. It reinforces the principle that trademark law must reflect actual consumer perception rather than administrative convenience. In modern finance, where products are increasingly bundled and branded under umbrella identities, courts must define the "relevant public" and their exposure to similar marks with sufficient breadth to capture the true competitive environment. For financial institutions navigating this space, *Apex Bank* suggests that proving mark weakness requires demonstrating saturation across functionally related sectors, not just identical service codes.



# Commercial Strength Revisited: The Interplay Between Factor Six and Factor One

In the intricate calculus of trademark likelihood-of-confusion, practitioners often treat the thirteen *DuPont* factors as discrete silos, weighing each independently before summing the total. However, in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided September 25, 2025, the Federal Circuit reminded us that these factors are deeply entangled, particularly when it comes to the relationship between market crowding and commercial impression. The court's decision to vacate both the sixth factor (third-party uses) and the first factor (similarity of marks) underscores a critical doctrinal point: commercial strength is not a static attribute of a mark, but a variable entirely dependent on the marketplace context in which it operates. By forcing the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to reconsider how a "crowded field" reshapes the very perception of a mark, the Federal Circuit has signaled that evidence of third-party usage is not merely a defensive shield; it is a tool that can fundamentally alter the offensive scope of protection afforded to a senior user.

The dispute arose when Apex Bank sought to register "ASPIRE BANK" for banking and financing services, facing opposition from CC Serve Corp., the owner of the "ASPIRE" mark for credit card services. The TTAB initially sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion. Central to the Board's reasoning was its conclusion that while "credit card services" and general "banking services" were not identical in name, they were "legally identical, in part" because banking inherently involves extending credit. Consequently, the Board found the second *DuPont* factor—heavily weighted toward similarity of goods—decisive. Yet, when Apex presented evidence of forty-two third-party marks containing "Aspire" across the financial sector to argue that the field was crowded and CC Serve's mark was weak, the Board balked. It limited its sixth-factor analysis strictly to marks used for "credit card services," dismissing broader financial uses as irrelevant, and ultimately concluded that nine such marks did not constitute a crowded field sufficient to narrow CC Serve's scope of protection.

The Federal Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Hughes, identified a fatal inconsistency in the Board's logic. Having already established under the second factor that banking and credit card services are highly similar—indeed, partially legally identical—the Board could not logically pivot to a hyper-narrow definition of "similar goods" when evaluating third-party marks under the sixth factor. The court rightly noted that imposing a more stringent standard for similarity in the sixth factor than in the second creates an analytical disconnect. If the services are close enough to cause confusion between Apex and CC Serve, they are necessarily close enough for third-party uses in general banking to inform consumers about the distinctiveness of the term "Aspire" in that same sector. By excluding thirty-three relevant third-party marks simply because they covered broader financing rather than strict credit card issuance, the Board failed to account for the reality that consumers encounter these marks in a unified financial ecosystem.

What makes *Apex Bank* particularly significant for trademark practitioners is the court's treatment of the ripple effect from Factor Six to Factor One. The first *DuPont* factor analyzes the similarity of marks in their entirety, focusing on appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Crucially, this analysis does not occur in a vacuum; it is informed by the commercial strength of the senior mark. A mark that appears strong in isolation may project a vastly different commercial impression when viewed against a backdrop of ubiquitous similar terms. The Federal Circuit recognized that if the Board had properly considered all forty-two third-party uses, it might have found "Aspire" to be conceptually weak within the financial space. Such a finding would inevitably change how an adjudicator perceives differences between "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK." In a crowded field, minor distinctions carry more weight because consumers are conditioned to look for them; conversely, shared generic or suggestive elements carry less weight because they are common currency.

This causal link highlights a strategic imperative for litigants: third-party evidence should never be treated as merely one weighted factor among many to be tallied at the end of an analysis. Instead, it serves as foundational context that recalibrates every subsequent step of the inquiry. By vacating both factors simultaneously rather than just remanding on Factor Six alone (*Apex Bank*, slip op.), Judge Hughes emphasized that you cannot fix broken math without fixing variables throughout equation; if you redefine what constitutes similar goods due to existing market conditions ("crowdedness"), you must also redefine how those conditions influence consumer perception ("commercial impression"). This approach prevents courts from engaging in compartmentalized thinking where they acknowledge market saturation yet still apply broad scopes of protection inconsistent with those markets—a phenomenon sometimes referred to as granting rights stronger than actual market realities justify ("phantom strength").
