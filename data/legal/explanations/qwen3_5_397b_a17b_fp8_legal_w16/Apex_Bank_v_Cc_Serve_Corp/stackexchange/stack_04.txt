Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How does the presence of third-party marks ('crowded field') impact the strength of a trademark and the likelihood of confusion analysis?
Question:
The opinion discusses Factor 6 extensively, noting that a 'crowded field' of similar marks makes consumers more adept at distinguishing between them, thereby weakening the senior mark's scope of protection. The Board found nine third-party uses insufficient to show weakness, citing cases where 14 or 26 uses were required. Is there a magic number of third-party marks needed to prove a field is crowded? How does proving a mark is 'weak' change the outcome of the overall likelihood of confusion test?
Answer:
There is no "magic number" of third-party marks required to prove a trademark field is crowded. As illustrated in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the inquiry is qualitative rather than purely quantitative; the critical issue is whether the evidence demonstrates that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks in the relevant market, thereby rendering the senior mark "weak" and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.

In *Apex*, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) erred not because nine third-party uses were numerically insufficient compared to precedents involving 14 or 26 marks, but because it artificially restricted the universe of comparable marks. The Board had previously found under the second *DuPont* factor that credit card services and banking/financing services were "highly similar," even "legally identical, in part." Yet, when analyzing the sixth *DuPont* factor (the number and nature of similar marks), the Board excluded third-party marks containing "Aspire" that were used for general banking services, considering only those specific to credit cards.

The Federal Circuit vacated this finding, holding that the scope of similarity must remain consistent across factors. If services are deemed similar for the purpose of establishing a risk of confusion, they must also be deemed similar when assessing whether a crowded field dilutes that risk. By excluding relevant banking marks, the Board failed to properly evaluate whether the term "Aspire" was weak in the broader financial services landscape.

Proving a mark is "weak" fundamentally alters the likelihood of confusion analysis in two key ways:

1.  **Narrow Scope of Protection:** A weak mark receives a narrower scope of protection than a strong, arbitrary, or fanciful mark. As noted in *Palm Bay Imports* and cited in *Apex*, when a field is crowded, consumers become more adept at identifying minor differences between marks. Consequently, a senior user cannot prevent others from using similar terms unless those terms are nearly identical.
2.  **Impact on Commercial Impression:** The strength of a mark directly influences the first *DuPont* factor (similarity of the marks). In *Apex*, the Federal Circuit explained that if the sixth factor establishes weakness due to a crowded field, this changes the "overall commercial impression" of the senior mark. A weak mark creates a different commercial impression than a strong one, often making confusion less likely even if the marks share common elements (like the word "Aspire").

Therefore, while prior cases mentioning 14 or 26 marks provide context for what constitutes "voluminous" evidence, there is no statutory threshold. The determinative factor is whether the proffered evidence—properly scoped to include all similar goods and services—proves that the marketplace conditions have educated consumers to distinguish among competitors using similar terminology.
