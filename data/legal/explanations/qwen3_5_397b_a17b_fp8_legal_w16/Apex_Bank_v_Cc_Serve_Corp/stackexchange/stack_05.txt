Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### If Factor 6 (commercial strength) is recalculated on remand, why must Factor 1 (similarity of marks) also be vacated and reconsidered?
Question:
In Section III.C, the court vacates the Board's analysis of Factor 1 (appearance, sound, connotation) simply because Factor 6 is being remanded. The court states that commercial strength affects 'overall commercial impression.' I thought Factor 1 was purely about comparing the two marks side-by-side visually and phonetically. How does knowing whether a market is crowded or a mark is weak change the actual comparison of the words 'ASPIRE' vs. 'ASPIRE BANK'? Doesn't that conflate two separate factors?
Answer:
You are correct that Factor 1 (similarity of marks) traditionally involves a side-by-side comparison of the marks' appearance, sound, and connotation. However, under Federal Circuit precedent, the ultimate inquiry for Factor 1 is not merely a mechanical comparison of these elements, but an assessment of the **"overall commercial impression"** created by the marks.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court vacates Factor 1 alongside Factor 6 precisely because **commercial strength** (determined in Factor 6) fundamentally alters that "overall commercial impression."

Here is the breakdown of the court's reasoning based on Section III.C of the opinion:

### 1. Commercial Strength Defines Context
Factor 6 analyzes the number and nature of similar marks in the marketplace to determine if a mark is "weak" or "strong."
*   If a market is crowded with similar marks (e.g., many banks using "Aspire"), consumers are trained to look for minute differences to distinguish sources. In this context, small differences between "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" carry significant weight.
*   If the market is uncrowded and the mark is strong, consumers rely more on the dominant shared term ("ASPIRE"), making minor additions like "BANK" less effective at distinguishing the source.

### 2. The Interdependence of Factors
The court explicitly states: *"Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board's analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."*

The logic is that you cannot accurately gauge the "commercial impression" (Factor 1) without knowing the commercial context (Factor 6). The same two words can create entirely different impressions depending on whether they exist in a vacuum or amidst a sea of similar competitors.

### Conclusion
The court did not conflate the factors; rather, it recognized that they are analytically linked. Since the Board erred in defining the scope of relevant third-party marks for Factor 6 (Section III.B), its conclusion regarding commercial strength is unreliable. Without a valid determination of commercial strength, any finding regarding the "overall commercial impression" under Factor 1 is necessarily flawed. Therefore, both must be reconsidered together on remand to ensure the similarity analysis reflects the correct market reality.
