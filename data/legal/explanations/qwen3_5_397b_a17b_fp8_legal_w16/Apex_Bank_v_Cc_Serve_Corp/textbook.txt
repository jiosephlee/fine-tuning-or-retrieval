Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Procedural Posture and the Likelihood of Confusion Framework

To understand the Federal Circuit's decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, one must first grasp the distinct business models of the litigants and the specific procedural mechanism that brought them into conflict. At its core, this dispute concerns whether Apex Bank can register "ASPIRE BANK" marks for banking services when CC Serve Corp. already holds a registration for the "ASPIRE" mark used in connection with credit card services. CC Serve operates by partnering with banks to issue branded credit cards; it owns the "ASPIRE" registration, which dates back to 1998, and manages the accounts associated with these cards. In contrast, Apex Bank is a traditional retail bank chartered in Tennessee with physical branches offering checking, savings, mortgages, and loans. Crucially, at the time of the dispute, Apex did not offer credit cards but planned to launch an internet-only bank under the "ASPIRE BANK" name. The tension arises from Apex's intent to expand into financing services under a mark that shares the dominant term "ASPIRE" with CC Serve's established brand, despite the parties operating through slightly different channels within the broader financial sector.

The procedural journey of this case began not in a courtroom, but within the administrative machinery of the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications seeking registration for its word and design marks for "banking and financing services." Although an examining attorney initially approved the marks for publication after CC Serve filed a letter of protest, the publication triggered CC Serve's right to formally oppose the registration. In February 2020, CC Serve initiated an opposition proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB), alleging that Apex's proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with its own "ASPIRE" mark under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. The TTAB sustained the opposition, effectively blocking Apex's registration. Apex then appealed this administrative decision to the Federal Circuit, invoking the court's jurisdiction to review final decisions of the TTAB. Understanding this posture is vital: the Federal Circuit is not conducting a new trial but is reviewing the administrative record generated before the Board to determine if legal errors occurred or if factual findings lacked sufficient support.

The substantive legal battle turns on Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), which prohibits the registration of a mark that so resembles a previously registered mark as to be likely to cause confusion among consumers. To adjudicate such claims, courts and the TTAB utilize the framework established in *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, which outlines thirteen factors for assessing likelihood of confusion. It is important for students to note that while there are thirteen potential factors, neither the Board nor the appellate court is required to analyze every single one in every case. Instead, the decision-maker must focus only on those factors that are relevant to the specific facts of record. In *Apex Bank*, the Board focused heavily on three specific factors: the similarity of goods (factor two), similarity of marks (factor one), and third-party usage (factor six). # Analysis of Related Goods and Services (DuPont Factor Two)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit addressed a critical threshold issue in trademark opposition proceedings: whether the services offered by the applicant and the registrant are sufficiently related to support a finding of likelihood of confusion. Under the second factor of the *DuPont* analysis, the court examines the similarity of the parties' goods or services. This factor does not require that the services be identical; rather, it asks whether they are related in such a manner, or marketed under such circumstances, that consumers might mistakenly believe they emanate from the same source. In this case, the court affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's conclusion that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex Bank's proposed "banking and financing services" were highly similar, weighing heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. Understanding this portion of the opinion requires dissecting how the court interprets service descriptions, evaluates evidentiary support for relatedness, and rejects arguments based on strict competition.

## Defining the Scope of Registered Services: Interpreting 'Credit Card Services'

The analysis began with a necessary interpretive step regarding the scope of CC Serve's existing registration. CC Serve held a registration for the mark ASPIRE covering "credit card services," issued in 1998. However, during the pendency of Apex's application, the specific entry for "credit card services" had been deleted from the USPTO's Trademark ID Manual. Consequently, the Board could not rely on a standardized manual definition to determine the breadth of CC Serve's rights. Instead, it had to construe the ordinary meaning of the phrase as it existed at the time of registration and as understood in commerce.

The Board determined that "credit card services" is a broad term encompassing more than just the physical issuance of a plastic card. It includes counseling regarding credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorizing charges, monitoring accounts, sending alerts, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores. This expansive definition was crucial because Apex Bank argued it did not currently offer credit cards. However, Apex sought to register its ASPIRE BANK marks for "banking and financing services." By defining CC Serve's services broadly to include account management and financial transaction processing, the Board established a significant overlap with the traditional functions of banking institutions. The Federal Circuit upheld this interpretation, noting that substantial evidence supported the Board's construction of the registered services.

## The Legal Standard for Relatedness: Common Source Mistaken Belief

The core legal principle guiding Factor Two is that confusion arises not only from direct competition but from relatedness that suggests a common origin. As cited in *Apex Bank*, quoting *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, services are considered related if "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [if] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." This standard protects consumers who might reasonably assume that a company offering one type of financial service would naturally expand into another under the same brand name.

In applying this standard, the court looked beyond whether Apex and CC Serve were currently selling competing products side-by-side. Instead, it focused on whether a consumer encountering an "ASPIRE BANK" for banking services would logically assume it was connected to the existing "ASPIRE" credit card services. Given that banks frequently issue credit cards and manage credit accounts, and that non-bank entities often partner with banks to provide these very services (as CC Serve did), the commercial reality suggested a strong nexus between the two fields. The court reinforced that identity is not required; a high degree of similarity where consumers expect single-source provision is sufficient to satisfy this factor.

## Evidence of Overlap: Dictionary Definitions and Third-Party Registrations

To substantiate its finding that banking/financing services and credit card services are legally identical in part, both the Board and appellate court relied on objective evidence found within record citations J.A. 27–28 and J.A. 33–34 first through dictionary definitions then third-party trademark registrations then . The Board examined standard dictionary definitions for "bank," "banking," and "finance." These definitions revealed that extending credit and providing funds—often achieved through credit cards—are inherent components of banking activities therefore establishing conceptual overlap between Apex's proposed offerings . Furthermore , look at market behavior proved instructive ; numerous third - party registrations demonstrated single entities holding trademarks covering both credit card operations alongside general banking functions . Such registrations serve as probative evidence indicating industry practice recognizes these sectors as complementary rather than distinct silos thereby supporting inference consumers perceive potential unity source when encountering similar marks across these domains . This combination linguistic analysis empirical marketplace data provided robust foundation concluding second factor favored opposer significantly .

# The Error in Defining Relevant Markets for Third-Party Marks (DuPont Factor Six)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit addressed a critical methodological error committed by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) during its likelihood-of-confusion analysis. While the Board correctly identified a high degree of similarity between the parties' services under the second *DuPont* factor, it subsequently undermined its own analysis by artificially narrowing the scope of relevant third-party marks when evaluating the sixth *DuPont* factor. This chapter examines the court's reasoning regarding this inconsistency, explaining how the definition of "similar goods" must remain constant across all factors of the *DuPont* test and why excluding marks from closely related financial sectors constituted reversible legal error. By understanding this distinction, students can appreciate how the "crowded field" doctrine operates to adjust the scope of trademark protection based on the actual marketplace reality rather than rigid categorical distinctions.

## Purpose of Factor Six: Assessing Commercial Strength and Consumer Sophistication

The sixth factor in the *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* analysis evaluates "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." Unlike the first factor, which looks at the marks themselves, or the second, which compares the goods, the sixth factor serves as a thermometer for the commercial environment in which the marks operate. Its primary purpose is to determine the commercial strength of the senior user's mark. When a market is saturated with similar marks used by various entities on similar products, it suggests that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing between brands based on subtle differences. In such a "crowded field," individual marks are generally considered conceptually and commercially weaker, entitling them to a narrower scope of protection against potential infringers. Conversely, if a mark stands alone in a sparse marketplace, even minor similarities with a new entrant might trigger a finding of likely confusion. Therefore, Factor Six is not merely a headcount of competitors; it is an inquiry into consumer sophistication and the distinctiveness of the senior mark within its specific trade sector.

## The 'Crowded Field' Doctrine: How Numerous Similar Marks Narrow Protection Scope

The legal theory underpinning the "crowded field" doctrine is rooted in consumer behavior. As established in precedents like *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC* and *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports*, evidence that many third parties use similar marks on similar goods educates consumers to look beyond common elements to identify the source of a product. If numerous banks and financial institutions utilize variations of the word "Aspire," a reasonable consumer encountering "ASPIRE BANK" would be less likely to assume it originates from the same source as "ASPIRE" credit card services than if CC Serve were the sole user of that term in finance. Consequently, a showing of extensive third-party use tends to negate a finding of likelihood of confusion because it demonstrates that the shared element (in this case, "Aspire") has low discriminatory power. For an applicant like Apex Bank, proving a crowded field is often essential to overcoming an opposition based on a seemingly similar senior mark, as it effectively argues that the senior mark's monopoly over the term should be limited to its exact usage rather than extended to all variations.

## The Board's Erroneous Limitation: Restricting Analysis Only to 'Credit Card Services'

In *Apex Bank*, the TTAB committed a fundamental error by inconsistently defining the universe of comparable goods between Factor Two and Factor Six. When analyzing Factor Two (similarity of goods), the Board adopted a broad interpretation. It recognized that while Apex offered "banking and financing services" and CC Serve offered "credit card services," these categories overlapped significantly. The Board noted that banking inherently involves extending credit and that CC Serve's activities included managing accounts and authorizing transactions functions central to banking. Based on this functional overlap, the Board concluded the services were "legally identical, in part," weighing this factor heavily in favor of finding confusion. However, when shifting to Factor Six to evaluate third-party marks, the Board abruptly narrowed its focus. It restricted its review exclusively to third-party marks used specifically on "credit card services," dismissing Apex's evidence of forty-two other marks containing "Aspire" used in broader banking and financial contexts as "essentially irrelevant." By doing so, the Board ignored evidence that could have demonstrated a crowded field in the broader financial sector where both parties actually compete.

## Inconsistency in Legal Standards: Aligning Service Similarity Across All Factors

The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's decision on Factor Six precisely because an administrative agency cannot apply contradictory legal standards to identical factual predicates within the same proceeding. The court emphasized that once the Board makes a factual finding that two types of services are highly similar—or even partially legally identical—that finding must govern subsequent analyses involving those services. It is logically inconsistent to assert that banking and credit card services are close enough to cause consumer confusion (Factor Two) yet distant enough that third-party marks in general banking are irrelevant to assessing consumer sophistication (Factor Six). The appellate court reasoned that if consumers might confuse Apex's banking services with CC Serve's credit card services due to their similarity, then consumers must also be exposed to third-party banking marks when navigating this same marketplace segment Excluding those third-party banking marks from Factor Six created an artificial vacuum that misrepresented true market conditions and unfairly inflated perceived strength CC Serve mark The court held there was no justification for imposing stricter standard similarity under Factor Six than what had already been accepted under Factor Two requiring remand reconsider full range similar financial service markets

# Interdependence of Factors and Remand Instructions

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit illustrates a critical principle in trademark litigation: the thirteen *DuPont* factors used to determine likelihood of confusion are not isolated silos of analysis but an interconnected web where an error in one strand can unravel the entire fabric of the decision. While the court affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) finding regarding the similarity of the parties' services, it identified a fundamental flaw in how the Board assessed the strength of CC Serve's "ASPIRE" mark. This error did not remain contained; rather, it cascaded into the analysis of mark similarity, necessitating a partial vacatur and remand. This chapter examines how the court navigated the interdependence of commercial strength and commercial impression, demonstrating why a correct understanding of the marketplace is essential before comparing two marks side-by-side.

## The First DuPont Factor: Appearance, Sound, Connotation, and Commercial Impression

The first *DuPont* factor requires an assessment of the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties, considering their appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. As established in precedent cited by the court, such as *In re I.AM.Symbolic*, this inquiry is not a mechanical dissection of individual letters or syllables but a holistic evaluation of the overall commercial impression created by the marks. The central question is whether the marks are sufficiently similar that consumers are likely to believe they emanate from the same source. In *Apex Bank*, the comparison was between CC Serve's standard character mark "ASPIRE," used for credit card services, and Apex Bank's proposed "ASPIRE BANK" word and design marks for banking and financing services.

On its face, the shared root word "Aspire" suggests a degree of similarity. However, the court emphasizes that the weight given to this shared element depends heavily on context. If "Aspire" is a unique, arbitrary term in the financial sector, its presence in both marks carries significant weight toward finding confusion. Conversely, if "Aspire" is merely one of many common terms used by various entities in finance, its probative value diminishes. The Board initially concluded that the first factor weighed in favor of CC Serve, implying that the shared term created a strong likelihood of confusion. Yet, as the appellate court revealed, this conclusion rested on an incomplete factual foundation regarding how consumers actually encounter these terms in the marketplace.

## The Interplay Between Commercial Strength and Overall Commercial Impression

The sixth *DuPont* factor analyzes "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves as a proxy for determining the commercial strength or weakness of a plaintiff's mark. The legal theory underpinning this factor is straightforward: when a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become adept at distinguishing minor differences between them. Consequently, a mark existing in a crowded field is considered "weak" and is entitled to only a narrow scope of protection against others using similar terminology. As noted in *Juice Generation* and *Jack Wolfskin*, evidence that consumers are educated to distinguish between marks indicates a lack of exclusive commercial strength for any single user.

In *Apex Bank*, this dynamic creates a direct feedback loop with the first factor. The "commercial impression" analyzed under factor one cannot be accurately gauged without knowing whether the dominant feature of the mark (here, "Aspire") is distinctive or commonplace. If third-party usage reveals that dozens of financial institutions use "Aspire," then seeing "Aspire Bank" next to "Aspire Credit Cards" creates a different commercial impression than if CC Serve were the sole user of that term in finance. In a crowded market, consumers expect variation and look to secondary features—such as house names like "Bank" or specific logos—to differentiate sources. Therefore, an erroneous finding on factor six inevitably distorts the analysis under factor one by inflating perceived similarities that would otherwise be viewed as insignificant distinctions in a busy marketplace.

## Why Vacating Factor Six Necessitates Vacating Factor One

The core holding of this portion of *Apex Bank* rests on recognizing that you cannot separate market reality from visual comparison. The TTAB committed legal error by artificially restricting its analysis under factor six while simultaneously relying on broad findings under factor two (relatedness of goods). Specifically, having determined under factor two that credit card services and banking/financing services were "highly similar"—even partially legally identical—the Board paradoxically narrowed its lens for factor six to include only third-party marks used strictly for "credit card services."
