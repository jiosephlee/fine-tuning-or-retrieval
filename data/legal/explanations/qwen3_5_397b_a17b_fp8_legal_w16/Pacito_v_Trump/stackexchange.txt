Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How does the 'likelihood of success on the merits' factor differ between the § 1182(f) challenge and the APA challenge in this stay order?
Question:
I am confused by how the Ninth Circuit applied the *Nken* stay factors differently to the two distinct claims in this case. For the challenge to Executive Order 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the court says the government is 'likely to prevail' citing *Trump v. Hawaii* and the deference owed to the President's finding of detriment. However, for the APA challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services, the court says the government is 'not likely to prevail.' Can someone explain how a single panel can reach opposite conclusions on 'likelihood of success' within the same order? Does this mean the statutory interpretation of § 1522 overrides the broad discretion usually given to the executive in immigration matters?
Answer:
You have correctly identified the apparent tension in the panel's application of the *Nken* factors. However, the divergent outcomes on "likelihood of success" do not stem from a conflict between statutory interpretation and executive deference, nor does one statute "override" the other. Rather, the panel applied different legal standards to two distinct types of government action: a **proclamation suspending entry** versus an **agency failure to fund mandated services**.

Here is how the opinion distinguishes the two claims:

### 1. The § 1182(f) Challenge: Broad Discretion on Entry
Regarding the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, the panel found the government **likely to prevail** because 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) grants the President nearly plenary authority over the *entry* of aliens.

The opinion relies heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that this statute "exudes deference" to the President. The court explains that the "sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f) is a presidential finding that entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Because President Trump explicitly made this finding in the Executive Order, and because the text of § 1182(f) imposes no further substantive limits on that power, the court stated it could not engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Consequently, on the narrow question of whether the President can suspend *entry*, the law clearly favors the government.

### 2. The APA Challenge: Mandatory Duties Regarding Resettlement
Conversely, regarding the defunding of resettlement services for refugees *already admitted* or approved, the panel found the government **not likely to prevail** because 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes specific statutory duties that are not subject to the same boundless discretion found in § 1182(f).

The panel distinguishes this claim by focusing on the mandatory language ("shall") within § 1522 concerning domestic care. Unlike § 1182(f), which governs *who gets in*, § 1522 governs *what happens after they arrive*. The opinion highlights several provisions creating non-discretionary obligations:
*   **General Mandate:** Section 1522(a)(1)(A) directs the Director of ORR "to... make available sufficient resources," "provide... English language training," and "insure that cash assistance is made available."
*   **Unaccompanied Minors:** Section 1522(d)(2)(B) states the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for unaccompanied children during interim periods.
*   **Medical Support:** Section 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for medical and support services.

The panel reasoned that while Congress appropriates funds "to the extent of available appropriations," once those funds are appropriated (as they were for FY 2025), the statute commands their use for specific purposes. The government's total termination of cooperative agreements left it unable to fulfill these explicit statutory commands. As Judge Lee notes in his dissent, he views these provisions as mere "authorizations," but the majority interprets them as binding mandates that restrict executive discretion when it comes to caring for refugees already within U.S. jurisdiction or in transit under U.S. custody.

### Conclusion
The panel did not apply inconsistent standards; it applied context-specific statutes to distinct governmental functions.
*   Under **§ 1182(f)**, the statute delegates decision-making power entirely to the President regarding *exclusion*, leading to a high likelihood of success for the government.
*   Under **§ 1522**, Congress retained control by writing specific affirmative duties into law regarding *resettlement support*. Here, executive discretion is limited by clear congressional commandments ("shall"), leading to a low likelihood of success for a government attempt to ignore those duties entirely.

Thus, § 1522 does not override § 1182(f); rather, they operate in separate spheres—one governing admission thresholds and one governing post-admission welfare—with only latter containing judicially enforceable mandates relevant to this specific funding dispute.

### Is 8 U.S.C. § 1522 a mandatory spending statute or a discretionary authorization, and how does the 'available appropriations' clause affect this?
Question:
The majority opinion seems to treat 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a mandatory duty on the Director of ORR to provide reception and placement services, ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements. However, Judge Lee's dissent argues that the statute is merely an 'authorization,' pointing to the title of the section and the repeated use of 'authorized' in subsections (b) through (e). The majority relies on subsection (a)'s use of 'shall,' but qualifies it with 'to the extent of available appropriations.' Since Congress did appropriate funds (even if reduced by a rescission), does the presence of appropriated money automatically turn an 'authorized' program into a 'mandatory' one? How do courts typically distinguish between statutes that say an agency 'may' do something versus those that say it 'shall' do something 'to the extent of available appropriations'?
Answer:
Based on the panel order in *Pacito v. Trump*, the court treats 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a **mandatory duty** on the government to provide reception and placement services, provided that Congress has appropriated funds to support those services. The presence of appropriated money, even if reduced by a rescission, triggers the statutory obligation because the "available appropriations" clause functions as a limitation on the *scope* of the duty, not as a grant of discretion to eliminate the duty entirely.

The distinction between "may" (discretionary) and "shall" (mandatory) turns on how the court interprets the qualifying phrase "to the extent of available appropriations."

### The Majority's Interpretation: A Conditional Mandate
The majority opinion grounds its holding in the plain text of § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement **"shall"** provide specific resources (employment training, English language training, cash assistance, etc.) **"to the extent of available appropriations."**

The court reasons that:
1.  **"Shall" creates an obligation:** Unlike the permissive "authorized" language found in subsections (b) through (e) cited by the dissent, subsection (a) uses the imperative "shall." This indicates a congressional command rather than mere permission.
2.  **"Available Appropriations" is a cap, not a switch:** The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" limits *how much* service must be provided based on the funding level, but it does not give the Executive Branch the discretion to provide *zero* services when funds exist.
3.  **Funds were available:** The opinion explicitly notes that for FY 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion, which was subsequently reduced by an $800 million rescission. Because a significant sum remains appropriated ("available"), the condition precedent for the mandate is satisfied. Consequently, the government cannot terminate all cooperative agreements; it must reinstate those necessary to deliver services commensurate with the remaining funding.

As stated in the order: *"In light of the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements..."* The court views the statute as requiring action whenever funds are present, regardless of whether those funds are diminished.

### The Dissent's Counter-Argument: Authorization vs. Condition
Judge Lee's dissent argues that § 1522 is fundamentally an **authorization statute**, not a spending mandate. His reasoning highlights two key textual features:
*   **Statutory Title and Structure:** The section is titled "Authorization for programs..." and subsections (b) through (e) repeatedly state the Director is "**authorized**" to act. Lee argues these sections grant permission to spend if the Executive chooses to do so.
*   **Contextual Reading of "Shall":** Lee contends that subsection (a)'s use of "shall" applies only *if* the government elects to fund programs under subsections (b)–(e). He reads "In providing assistance under this section..." as a conditional trigger: *If* you choose to provide assistance, *then* you shall meet these conditions. Under this view, "available appropriations" simply means the program cannot exceed what Congress gave; it does not mean the program must continue as long as *any* money exists.

### Conclusion: How Courts Distinguish These Clauses
Under the *Pacito* majority's approach, courts distinguish between discretionary and mandatory statutes with "available appropriations" clauses by looking at whether Congress used **"shall"** regarding the core activity itself when funds are present.

*   If a statute says an agency **"may"** provide services using appropriated funds, it is discretionary; zero spending is permissible even if money exists.
*   If a statute says an agency **"shall"** provide services **"to the extent of available appropriations,"** it is mandatory; once Congress appropriates *any* amount greater than zero, the agency loses the discretion to cancel the program entirely and must utilize those funds to fulfill the statutory command to whatever degree possible.

In *Pacito*, because Congress did appropriate funds (despite the rescission), and because § 1522(a) commands that services "shall" be made available to that extent, the majority concludes Executive Order 14169's total defunding violates a clear statutory mandate.

### What is the procedural effect of vacating previous stays while simultaneously issuing a new 'stay pending appeal' with a specific carveout?
Question:
The operative paragraph of this order is procedurally dense. The court vacates its prior administrative stay (July 18) and its prior partial stay (March 25, as amended). It then states that the District Court's preliminary injunctions are 'stayed in their entirety, except as noted below.' The exception is a directive for the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted. Procedurally, does this mean the District Court's injunction blocking the refugee ban remains inactive (because it is stayed), while only the injunction regarding domestic services is active? How does this hybrid approach work practically? Is this effectively a modification of the stay rather than a simple grant or denial?
Answer:
Yes, your reading of the procedural effect is correct. The order creates a **hybrid stay** that functionally modifies the scope of the District Court's preliminary injunctions rather than simply granting or denying a stay in full.

Here is how the mechanism works based on the text of *Pacito v. Trump*:

### 1. The General Rule: A Broad Stay
The operative language states: "The district court's February 28... and March 24... preliminary injunctions are **stayed in their entirety**."
*   **Effect:** This suspends the legal force of the District Court's orders preventing the government from enforcing the refugee entry ban (Executive Order 14163). Consequently, the government may immediately resume suspending refugee entries and processing, as the injunction blocking those actions is now inactive.

### 2. The Exception: A Mandatory Carveout
The phrase "**except as noted below**" triggers a specific limitation on that stay. The "note below" directs the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."
*   **Effect:** This portion of the District Court's injunction (specifically the March 24 order regarding domestic services for already-admitted refugees) remains **active and enforceable**. The stay does not apply to this specific subset of relief.

### 3. Practical Application
Practically, this splits the litigation into two tracks pending the final opinion:
*   **Track A (Entry Ban):** The government prevails temporarily. They can halt new refugee admissions because the injunction stopping them is stayed.
*   **Track B (Domestic Services):** The plaintiffs prevail temporarily. The government must continue funding and operating resettlement services for refugees already inside the U.S. because the injunction requiring this is *not* stayed.

### Legal Characterization
You asked if this is effectively a modification of the stay. Precisely. By vacating its prior partial stay (which had a different carveout for conditionally approved individuals abroad) and issuing this new order, the Ninth Circuit is engaging in a **partial stay pending appeal**.

Under the *Nken v. Holder* standard cited in the opinion, a court balances four factors. Here, the panel found:
1.  **Likelihood of Success/Irreparable Harm:** Favored the government regarding the *entry suspension* (citing *Trump v. Hawaii* and § 1182(f)), justifying a stay on that front.
2.  **Likelihood of Success/Irreparable Harm:** Favored the plaintiffs regarding *domestic services*. The panel explicitly stated the government is "not likely to prevail" on the APA challenge regarding § 1522 mandates for admitted refugees, and highlighted the risk of leaving admitted refugees without statutory support.

Therefore, the court tailored the stay to match its preliminary assessment of the merits: staying the injunction where the government is likely to win, but preserving it where the plaintiffs are likely to win. This avoids rendering either party's potential victory on appeal moot due to irreversible changes during the pendency of the case.

### Does *Trump v. Hawaii*'s deference to presidential findings under § 1182(f) prevent judicial review of whether a suspension violates other statutes like the Refugee Act of 1980?
Question:
The majority cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to support staying the injunction against Executive Order 14163, noting that courts cannot engage in a 'searching inquiry' into the President's justification if he finds entry detrimental to US interests. However, Plaintiffs also alleged violations of the Refugee Act of 1980. If § 1182(f) gives such broad power to suspend entry, can that power be limited by later or more specific statutes like the Refugee Act? Why did the court seem to bypass any potential conflict between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act when analyzing likelihood of success for EO 14163, whereas they dug deep into statutory text for § 1522?
Answer:
**Answer**

No, *Trump v. Hawaii* does not prevent judicial review of whether a presidential suspension under § 1182(f) violates other statutes, such as the Refugee Act of 1980. The distinction in the *Pacito* panel order between the "suspension" of entry and the "defunding" of services rests on the difference between the **scope of presidential authority** to exclude aliens and the **statutory mandates** governing the treatment of refugees once admitted or in transit.

Here is how the opinion reconciles these issues:

### 1. *Hawaii* Governs the Suspension Power (§ 1182(f)), Not Conflicts with Other Statutes
The panel relies on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), specifically to address the plaintiffs' challenge to the **suspension of refugee admissions** under Executive Order 14163. Under § 1182(f), the President needs only to "find[]" that entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. As the panel notes, citing *Hawaii*, this provision "exudes deference," and courts cannot engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

Because the text of § 1182(f) grants the President broad discretion to suspend entry "for such period as he shall deem necessary," the panel found the government likely to succeed on the merits regarding the suspension itself. The court did not need to analyze a conflict with the Refugee Act here because § 1182(f) expressly allows the President to impose restrictions "in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA]." In this context, *Hawaii* confirms that the President's finding satisfies the sole prerequisite of § 1182(f), rendering a deeper statutory conflict analysis unnecessary for the *suspension* prong.

### 2. Separate Analysis for Statutory Mandates (§ 1522)
The court treated the challenge to the **termination of cooperative agreements and funding** differently because this issue turns on a separate statute: **8 U.S.C. § 1522**. While § 1182(f) governs *entry*, § 1522 governs *resettlement services* for refugees who are already admitted or in transit.

The panel distinguished this issue from the *Hawaii* deference analysis for two key reasons:
*   **Different Legal Question:** The question was not whether the President could suspend entry (where *Hawaii* applies), but whether the Executive Branch could cease providing services that Congress explicitly mandated via appropriation and statutory text.
*   **Mandatory Language:** Unlike the discretionary "may" in § 1182(f), the panel highlighted that § 1522 contains mandatory directives ("shall") regarding reception and placement services, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors, contingent only on "available appropriations." Since Congress had appropriated funds for FY 2025, the panel concluded the government is likely unable to prevail on its APA defense for terminating these specific services.

### Conclusion
The court did not "bypass" a potential conflict between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act; rather, it determined that **no conflict exists** regarding the suspension power because § 1182(f) independently authorizes the suspension regardless of other INA provisions (per *Hawaii*). However, that same deference does not extend to ignoring distinct statutory commands in § 1522 that require the provision of services using appropriated funds. As Judge Lee's dissent underscores, this turns on whether § 1522 is read as a mandate or an authorization, but the majority proceeded on the reading that it imposes obligations that survive a separate exercise of exclusionary power under § 1182(f).

### Can a court order an agency to reinstate specific contractors/cooperative agreements as a remedy for failing to provide statutorily required services?
Question:
The court orders: '[T]he government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary...' because they are unsure if new agencies can be found in time. I thought courts generally enjoin illegal actions or compel specific performance of duties, but they avoid micromanaging *how* an agency fulfills those duties (e.g., telling them which vendors to hire). By forcing State/HHS to reinstate *prior* agreements rather than just saying 'you must fund services,' is this order crossing into administrative management territory? Is this level of specificity common in preliminary relief stages involving complex procurement processes?
Answer:
This order does not cross the line into impermissible "micromanagement" because it is a targeted remedy designed to prevent a statutory violation where no other viable alternative exists to ensure immediate compliance. While courts generally avoid dictating the specific means by which an agency fulfills its duties, they retain the equitable power to order specific performance when an agency's chosen method (here, terminating all vendors without a replacement ready) results in a failure to perform a nondiscretionary statutory duty.

**The Legal Standard: Specific Performance vs. Administrative Discretion**
You are correct that under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and principles of equity, courts typically enjoin unlawful actions or compel an agency to perform a duty while leaving the *method* of performance to the agency's expertise. However, this presumption yields when the agency demonstrates an inability or unwillingness to fulfill a mandatory statutory obligation through any other means. When an agency terminates the *only* existing mechanism for delivering required services and admits uncertainty about establishing a new one, a generic order to "provide services" would be ineffective, rendering the plaintiffs' statutory rights illusory.

**Application to *Pacito v. Trump***
The Ninth Circuit's order is grounded directly in the tension between the government's admission of unpreparedness and the clear mandates of 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

1.  **Existence of a Mandatory Duty:** The majority explicitly rejects the notion that § 1522 is purely permissive in this context. Despite Judge Lee's dissent arguing that terms like "authorized" imply discretion, the majority points to specific subsections using mandatory language ("shall") regarding unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and the development of alternative projects (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)). Crucially, the majority notes that Congress appropriated billions for these purposes, reinforcing the expectation of service delivery. Once the court determines a statutory duty exists, it has the authority to fashion a remedy to enforce it.
2.  **Necessity of the Remedy:** The linchpin of the order is the government's own concession during oral argument. The opinion notes that while the government claimed in March to be preparing a "request for proposals," by September it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency." Because the government terminated *all* prior cooperative agreements and currently has no functional replacement, reinstating the prior agreements is not an act of micromanaging vendor selection; it is the *only* available mechanism to instantly cure the statutory violation.
3.  **Scope Limited to Statutory Compliance:** The order is narrowly tailored. It does not tell the agency how to run the programs indefinitely or dictate future procurement strategy. It directs reinstatement only of agreements "necessary to provide... services described in § 1522" and limits the scope to "usual and customary services." This ensures the remedy lasts only as long as needed to bridge the gap created by the agency's termination of its own capacity.

**Conclusion on Preliminary Relief**
Is this level of specificity common? Generally, no; courts prefer broader injunctive language. However, in preliminary relief stages involving urgent humanitarian statutes where an agency has dismantled its operational capacity, courts will adopt specific remedies if necessary to maintain the status quo and prevent irreparable harm. Here, because the government effectively removed its ability to comply with § 1522 by firing every contractor without a backup plan, the court ordered reinstatement not to manage administration, but to make compliance with the law factually possible. Without this specific directive, the statutory mandate would remain unfulfilled regardless of what general order was issued.

### How does Judge Lee reconcile his agreement on Presidential authority under § 1182(f) with his dissent regarding domestic service mandates under § 1522?
Question:
Judge Lee concurs that President Trump has vast discretion under § 1082(f) regarding border closure/suspension but dissents on whether domestic services are mandatory under § 1522. He argues that even if funds exist ('appropriations flow'), Congress used permissive language ('authorized') meaning no cause exists until affirmative steps are taken by Congress or Agency heads beyond mere appropriation availability? Is there precedent where appropriations alone create rights without explicit mandatory language elsewhere in statute.
Answer:
Judge Lee reconciles his positions by drawing a sharp distinction between **statutory grants of plenary executive power** (regarding entry) and **statutory authorizations conditioned on executive discretion** (regarding domestic spending). His analysis turns entirely on textualism: he reads the specific verbs and structural qualifiers in the statutes to determine whether Congress commanded an action or merely permitted it.

### 1. Agreement on Entry Suspension (§ 1182(f))
Judge Lee agrees with the majority that the President has the authority to suspend refugee entry because the text of **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** creates a mandatory duty for the courts to defer once the President makes a specific finding.

*   **The Legal Standard:** The statute states that whenever the President *finds* entry would be detrimental, he *may* suspend entry.
*   **Lee's Application:** Lee notes that the "sole prerequisite" is the President's finding of detriment. Because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contains this finding, the statutory condition is met. Once triggered, the statute vests "ample power" in the President. Here, the language ("may... suspend") operates as a broad grant of authority that, once exercised according to procedure, binds the judiciary to respect the separation of powers. There is no room for judicial second-guessing of the justification under *Trump v. Hawaii*.

### 2. Dissent on Domestic Services (§ 1522)
Conversely, Lee argues that **8 U.S.C. § 1522** does not create a similar binding obligation on the Executive Branch to fund or provide services, even if appropriations exist. He relies on three textual arguments to characterize the statute as permissive rather than mandatory:

*   **Statutory Title and Verbs:** Lee emphasizes that the title of § 1522 is "**Authorization** for programs..." He contrasts this with mandatory language, noting that Black's Law Dictionary defines "authorization" as permission, not command. Furthermore, he points out that the operative subsections (§§ 1522(b)–(e)) repeatedly state that the Director **"is authorized"** to provide assistance. In Lee's view, "authorized" signals discretion; Congress allowed the programs but did not compel them.
*   **The "Condition Precedent" Argument:** The majority relies on § 1522(a), which uses the word "shall," to argue services are mandatory "to the extent of available appropriations." Lee rejects this by analyzing the sentence structure. He argues that § 1522(a) only imposes conditions *"In providing assistance under this section."*
    *   **Logic:** If the Executive Branch chooses *not* to provide assistance (exercising its discretion under subsections b–e), then the conditional mandates in subsection (a) never trigger. The "shall" applies only to *how* services are delivered if they are delivered, not *whether* they must be delivered.
*   **Qualifiers in Specific Mandates:** Regarding subsections that seem more imperative (e.g., § 1522(d)(2)(B) regarding unaccompanied minors or § 1522(e)(7)(A) regarding medical support), Lee highlights limiting phrases like **"if necessary"** and **"as needed."** He argues these phrases return discretion to the agency head to decide if a situation warrants action, preventing these clauses from becoming absolute mandates independent of executive policy choices.

### Conclusion: Appropriations vs. Rights
To answer your specific question on precedent and appropriations: Judge Lee's opinion suggests that **appropriations alone do not create rights** absent explicit mandatory language elsewhere in the statute.

Lee posits that while Congress has appropriated funds ($3.178 billion), an appropriation is merely a ceiling on spending, not a floor requiring expenditure. Without a clear statutory command (using "shall" without qualifying conditions like "if necessary" or nested within an "if providing" clause), the existence of funds does not strip the Executive Branch of its discretion to decline to spend them. In his view, § 1522 creates a mechanism for spending *if* the President chooses, whereas § 1182(f) creates a mechanism for exclusion *that functions automatically* upon presidential finding.
