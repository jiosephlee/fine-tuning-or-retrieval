Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Presidential Authority Over Entry: Analyzing 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit faced an urgent procedural question: whether to stay the district court's preliminary injunctions that blocked the enforcement of Executive Order No. 14163. This executive order, issued by President Trump in January 2025, suspended the entry of refugees into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). While the appellate panel ultimately crafted a nuanced order that partially granted and partially denied the government's request for a stay, its analysis of the President's authority to suspend entry was unequivocal. The court held that the government demonstrated a strong likelihood of success on the merits regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. This conclusion rested on a strict reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the binding precedent of *Trump v. Hawaii*, which mandates significant judicial deference to presidential determinations regarding national security and foreign policy interests. Understanding this portion of the opinion requires dissecting how the court interpreted statutory text, applied the doctrine of deference, and weighed these factors against the rigorous standard required for obtaining a stay pending appeal.

## Statutory Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): The 'Detrimental to Interests' Prerequisite

The foundation of the government's argument, and the court's subsequent agreement, lay in the plain language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This statute grants the President broad authority to control the entry of aliens. Specifically, it provides that whenever the President "finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States," he may by proclamation suspend their entry or impose restrictions as deemed appropriate for such period as necessary. In *Pacito*, President Trump invoked this exact authority, issuing a formal finding that refugee entry under USRAP would be detrimental to U.S. interests and directing a suspension pending further review.

The Ninth Circuit's analysis focused heavily on the simplicity of this statutory prerequisite. As the panel noted, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, "[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The court emphasized that once this finding is made, the statute imposes no further substantive hurdles for the President to clear. In this case, because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contained such a finding, the textual requirement was satisfied. The panel reasoned that because "the language of § 1182(f) is clear," and because there were no express limitations in other parts of Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) negating this specific power in this context, "the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on th[e] President's authority." By anchoring its reasoning in this straightforward textualist approach, bypassed complex debates about legislative intent or historical practice regarding refugees specifically found in other statutes like *Negusie*. Instead, they looked strictly at whether Congress gave him power via section f - yes he did find detriments so therefore valid exercise unless contradicted somewhere else explicitly which wasn't shown here yet during interlocutory phase where only surface level scrutiny occurs typically anyway when dealing with stays rather than full trial records being reviewed de novo etcetera ad infinitum until final judgment rendered years later perhaps?
