Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Can a court order an agency to reinstate specific contractors/cooperative agreements as a remedy for failing to provide statutorily required services?
Question:
The court orders: '[T]he government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary...' because they are unsure if new agencies can be found in time. I thought courts generally enjoin illegal actions or compel specific performance of duties, but they avoid micromanaging *how* an agency fulfills those duties (e.g., telling them which vendors to hire). By forcing State/HHS to reinstate *prior* agreements rather than just saying 'you must fund services,' is this order crossing into administrative management territory? Is this level of specificity common in preliminary relief stages involving complex procurement processes?
Answer:
This order does not cross the line into impermissible "micromanagement" because it is a targeted remedy designed to prevent a statutory violation where no other viable alternative exists to ensure immediate compliance. While courts generally avoid dictating the specific means by which an agency fulfills its duties, they retain the equitable power to order specific performance when an agency's chosen method (here, terminating all vendors without a replacement ready) results in a failure to perform a nondiscretionary statutory duty.

**The Legal Standard: Specific Performance vs. Administrative Discretion**
You are correct that under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and principles of equity, courts typically enjoin unlawful actions or compel an agency to perform a duty while leaving the *method* of performance to the agency's expertise. However, this presumption yields when the agency demonstrates an inability or unwillingness to fulfill a mandatory statutory obligation through any other means. When an agency terminates the *only* existing mechanism for delivering required services and admits uncertainty about establishing a new one, a generic order to "provide services" would be ineffective, rendering the plaintiffs' statutory rights illusory.

**Application to *Pacito v. Trump***
The Ninth Circuit's order is grounded directly in the tension between the government's admission of unpreparedness and the clear mandates of 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

1.  **Existence of a Mandatory Duty:** The majority explicitly rejects the notion that § 1522 is purely permissive in this context. Despite Judge Lee's dissent arguing that terms like "authorized" imply discretion, the majority points to specific subsections using mandatory language ("shall") regarding unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and the development of alternative projects (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)). Crucially, the majority notes that Congress appropriated billions for these purposes, reinforcing the expectation of service delivery. Once the court determines a statutory duty exists, it has the authority to fashion a remedy to enforce it.
2.  **Necessity of the Remedy:** The linchpin of the order is the government's own concession during oral argument. The opinion notes that while the government claimed in March to be preparing a "request for proposals," by September it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency." Because the government terminated *all* prior cooperative agreements and currently has no functional replacement, reinstating the prior agreements is not an act of micromanaging vendor selection; it is the *only* available mechanism to instantly cure the statutory violation.
3.  **Scope Limited to Statutory Compliance:** The order is narrowly tailored. It does not tell the agency how to run the programs indefinitely or dictate future procurement strategy. It directs reinstatement only of agreements "necessary to provide... services described in § 1522" and limits the scope to "usual and customary services." This ensures the remedy lasts only as long as needed to bridge the gap created by the agency's termination of its own capacity.

**Conclusion on Preliminary Relief**
Is this level of specificity common? Generally, no; courts prefer broader injunctive language. However, in preliminary relief stages involving urgent humanitarian statutes where an agency has dismantled its operational capacity, courts will adopt specific remedies if necessary to maintain the status quo and prevent irreparable harm. Here, because the government effectively removed its ability to comply with § 1522 by firing every contractor without a backup plan, the court ordered reinstatement not to manage administration, but to make compliance with the law factually possible. Without this specific directive, the statutory mandate would remain unfulfilled regardless of what general order was issued.
