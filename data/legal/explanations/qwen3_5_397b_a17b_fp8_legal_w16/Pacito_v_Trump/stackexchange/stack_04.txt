Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does *Trump v. Hawaii*'s deference to presidential findings under § 1182(f) prevent judicial review of whether a suspension violates other statutes like the Refugee Act of 1980?
Question:
The majority cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to support staying the injunction against Executive Order 14163, noting that courts cannot engage in a 'searching inquiry' into the President's justification if he finds entry detrimental to US interests. However, Plaintiffs also alleged violations of the Refugee Act of 1980. If § 1182(f) gives such broad power to suspend entry, can that power be limited by later or more specific statutes like the Refugee Act? Why did the court seem to bypass any potential conflict between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act when analyzing likelihood of success for EO 14163, whereas they dug deep into statutory text for § 1522?
Answer:
**Answer**

No, *Trump v. Hawaii* does not prevent judicial review of whether a presidential suspension under § 1182(f) violates other statutes, such as the Refugee Act of 1980. The distinction in the *Pacito* panel order between the "suspension" of entry and the "defunding" of services rests on the difference between the **scope of presidential authority** to exclude aliens and the **statutory mandates** governing the treatment of refugees once admitted or in transit.

Here is how the opinion reconciles these issues:

### 1. *Hawaii* Governs the Suspension Power (§ 1182(f)), Not Conflicts with Other Statutes
The panel relies on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), specifically to address the plaintiffs' challenge to the **suspension of refugee admissions** under Executive Order 14163. Under § 1182(f), the President needs only to "find[]" that entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. As the panel notes, citing *Hawaii*, this provision "exudes deference," and courts cannot engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

Because the text of § 1182(f) grants the President broad discretion to suspend entry "for such period as he shall deem necessary," the panel found the government likely to succeed on the merits regarding the suspension itself. The court did not need to analyze a conflict with the Refugee Act here because § 1182(f) expressly allows the President to impose restrictions "in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA]." In this context, *Hawaii* confirms that the President's finding satisfies the sole prerequisite of § 1182(f), rendering a deeper statutory conflict analysis unnecessary for the *suspension* prong.

### 2. Separate Analysis for Statutory Mandates (§ 1522)
The court treated the challenge to the **termination of cooperative agreements and funding** differently because this issue turns on a separate statute: **8 U.S.C. § 1522**. While § 1182(f) governs *entry*, § 1522 governs *resettlement services* for refugees who are already admitted or in transit.

The panel distinguished this issue from the *Hawaii* deference analysis for two key reasons:
*   **Different Legal Question:** The question was not whether the President could suspend entry (where *Hawaii* applies), but whether the Executive Branch could cease providing services that Congress explicitly mandated via appropriation and statutory text.
*   **Mandatory Language:** Unlike the discretionary "may" in § 1182(f), the panel highlighted that § 1522 contains mandatory directives ("shall") regarding reception and placement services, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors, contingent only on "available appropriations." Since Congress had appropriated funds for FY 2025, the panel concluded the government is likely unable to prevail on its APA defense for terminating these specific services.

### Conclusion
The court did not "bypass" a potential conflict between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act; rather, it determined that **no conflict exists** regarding the suspension power because § 1182(f) independently authorizes the suspension regardless of other INA provisions (per *Hawaii*). However, that same deference does not extend to ignoring distinct statutory commands in § 1522 that require the provision of services using appropriated funds. As Judge Lee's dissent underscores, this turns on whether § 1522 is read as a mandate or an authorization, but the majority proceeded on the reading that it imposes obligations that survive a separate exercise of exclusionary power under § 1182(f).
