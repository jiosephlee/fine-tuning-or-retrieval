Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Is 8 U.S.C. § 1522 a mandatory spending statute or a discretionary authorization, and how does the 'available appropriations' clause affect this?
Question:
The majority opinion seems to treat 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a mandatory duty on the Director of ORR to provide reception and placement services, ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements. However, Judge Lee's dissent argues that the statute is merely an 'authorization,' pointing to the title of the section and the repeated use of 'authorized' in subsections (b) through (e). The majority relies on subsection (a)'s use of 'shall,' but qualifies it with 'to the extent of available appropriations.' Since Congress did appropriate funds (even if reduced by a rescission), does the presence of appropriated money automatically turn an 'authorized' program into a 'mandatory' one? How do courts typically distinguish between statutes that say an agency 'may' do something versus those that say it 'shall' do something 'to the extent of available appropriations'?
Answer:
Based on the panel order in *Pacito v. Trump*, the court treats 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a **mandatory duty** on the government to provide reception and placement services, provided that Congress has appropriated funds to support those services. The presence of appropriated money, even if reduced by a rescission, triggers the statutory obligation because the "available appropriations" clause functions as a limitation on the *scope* of the duty, not as a grant of discretion to eliminate the duty entirely.

The distinction between "may" (discretionary) and "shall" (mandatory) turns on how the court interprets the qualifying phrase "to the extent of available appropriations."

### The Majority's Interpretation: A Conditional Mandate
The majority opinion grounds its holding in the plain text of § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement **"shall"** provide specific resources (employment training, English language training, cash assistance, etc.) **"to the extent of available appropriations."**

The court reasons that:
1.  **"Shall" creates an obligation:** Unlike the permissive "authorized" language found in subsections (b) through (e) cited by the dissent, subsection (a) uses the imperative "shall." This indicates a congressional command rather than mere permission.
2.  **"Available Appropriations" is a cap, not a switch:** The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" limits *how much* service must be provided based on the funding level, but it does not give the Executive Branch the discretion to provide *zero* services when funds exist.
3.  **Funds were available:** The opinion explicitly notes that for FY 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion, which was subsequently reduced by an $800 million rescission. Because a significant sum remains appropriated ("available"), the condition precedent for the mandate is satisfied. Consequently, the government cannot terminate all cooperative agreements; it must reinstate those necessary to deliver services commensurate with the remaining funding.

As stated in the order: *"In light of the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements..."* The court views the statute as requiring action whenever funds are present, regardless of whether those funds are diminished.

### The Dissent's Counter-Argument: Authorization vs. Condition
Judge Lee's dissent argues that § 1522 is fundamentally an **authorization statute**, not a spending mandate. His reasoning highlights two key textual features:
*   **Statutory Title and Structure:** The section is titled "Authorization for programs..." and subsections (b) through (e) repeatedly state the Director is "**authorized**" to act. Lee argues these sections grant permission to spend if the Executive chooses to do so.
*   **Contextual Reading of "Shall":** Lee contends that subsection (a)'s use of "shall" applies only *if* the government elects to fund programs under subsections (b)–(e). He reads "In providing assistance under this section..." as a conditional trigger: *If* you choose to provide assistance, *then* you shall meet these conditions. Under this view, "available appropriations" simply means the program cannot exceed what Congress gave; it does not mean the program must continue as long as *any* money exists.

### Conclusion: How Courts Distinguish These Clauses
Under the *Pacito* majority's approach, courts distinguish between discretionary and mandatory statutes with "available appropriations" clauses by looking at whether Congress used **"shall"** regarding the core activity itself when funds are present.

*   If a statute says an agency **"may"** provide services using appropriated funds, it is discretionary; zero spending is permissible even if money exists.
*   If a statute says an agency **"shall"** provide services **"to the extent of available appropriations,"** it is mandatory; once Congress appropriates *any* amount greater than zero, the agency loses the discretion to cancel the program entirely and must utilize those funds to fulfill the statutory command to whatever degree possible.

In *Pacito*, because Congress did appropriate funds (despite the rescission), and because § 1522(a) commands that services "shall" be made available to that extent, the majority concludes Executive Order 14169's total defunding violates a clear statutory mandate.
