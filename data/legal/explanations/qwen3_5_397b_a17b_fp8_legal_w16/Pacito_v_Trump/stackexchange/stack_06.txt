Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How does Judge Lee reconcile his agreement on Presidential authority under § 1182(f) with his dissent regarding domestic service mandates under § 1522?
Question:
Judge Lee concurs that President Trump has vast discretion under § 1082(f) regarding border closure/suspension but dissents on whether domestic services are mandatory under § 1522. He argues that even if funds exist ('appropriations flow'), Congress used permissive language ('authorized') meaning no cause exists until affirmative steps are taken by Congress or Agency heads beyond mere appropriation availability? Is there precedent where appropriations alone create rights without explicit mandatory language elsewhere in statute.
Answer:
Judge Lee reconciles his positions by drawing a sharp distinction between **statutory grants of plenary executive power** (regarding entry) and **statutory authorizations conditioned on executive discretion** (regarding domestic spending). His analysis turns entirely on textualism: he reads the specific verbs and structural qualifiers in the statutes to determine whether Congress commanded an action or merely permitted it.

### 1. Agreement on Entry Suspension (§ 1182(f))
Judge Lee agrees with the majority that the President has the authority to suspend refugee entry because the text of **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** creates a mandatory duty for the courts to defer once the President makes a specific finding.

*   **The Legal Standard:** The statute states that whenever the President *finds* entry would be detrimental, he *may* suspend entry.
*   **Lee's Application:** Lee notes that the "sole prerequisite" is the President's finding of detriment. Because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contains this finding, the statutory condition is met. Once triggered, the statute vests "ample power" in the President. Here, the language ("may... suspend") operates as a broad grant of authority that, once exercised according to procedure, binds the judiciary to respect the separation of powers. There is no room for judicial second-guessing of the justification under *Trump v. Hawaii*.

### 2. Dissent on Domestic Services (§ 1522)
Conversely, Lee argues that **8 U.S.C. § 1522** does not create a similar binding obligation on the Executive Branch to fund or provide services, even if appropriations exist. He relies on three textual arguments to characterize the statute as permissive rather than mandatory:

*   **Statutory Title and Verbs:** Lee emphasizes that the title of § 1522 is "**Authorization** for programs..." He contrasts this with mandatory language, noting that Black's Law Dictionary defines "authorization" as permission, not command. Furthermore, he points out that the operative subsections (§§ 1522(b)–(e)) repeatedly state that the Director **"is authorized"** to provide assistance. In Lee's view, "authorized" signals discretion; Congress allowed the programs but did not compel them.
*   **The "Condition Precedent" Argument:** The majority relies on § 1522(a), which uses the word "shall," to argue services are mandatory "to the extent of available appropriations." Lee rejects this by analyzing the sentence structure. He argues that § 1522(a) only imposes conditions *"In providing assistance under this section."*
    *   **Logic:** If the Executive Branch chooses *not* to provide assistance (exercising its discretion under subsections b–e), then the conditional mandates in subsection (a) never trigger. The "shall" applies only to *how* services are delivered if they are delivered, not *whether* they must be delivered.
*   **Qualifiers in Specific Mandates:** Regarding subsections that seem more imperative (e.g., § 1522(d)(2)(B) regarding unaccompanied minors or § 1522(e)(7)(A) regarding medical support), Lee highlights limiting phrases like **"if necessary"** and **"as needed."** He argues these phrases return discretion to the agency head to decide if a situation warrants action, preventing these clauses from becoming absolute mandates independent of executive policy choices.

### Conclusion: Appropriations vs. Rights
To answer your specific question on precedent and appropriations: Judge Lee's opinion suggests that **appropriations alone do not create rights** absent explicit mandatory language elsewhere in the statute.

Lee posits that while Congress has appropriated funds ($3.178 billion), an appropriation is merely a ceiling on spending, not a floor requiring expenditure. Without a clear statutory command (using "shall" without qualifying conditions like "if necessary" or nested within an "if providing" clause), the existence of funds does not strip the Executive Branch of its discretion to decline to spend them. In his view, § 1522 creates a mechanism for spending *if* the President chooses, whereas § 1182(f) creates a mechanism for exclusion *that functions automatically* upon presidential finding.
