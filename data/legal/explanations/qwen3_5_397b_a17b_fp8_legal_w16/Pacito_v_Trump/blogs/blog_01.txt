# The Shadow of *Hawaii*: Ninth Circuit Grants Broad Stay on Refugee Suspension Under § 1182(f)

The legal landscape of presidential immigration authority shifted decisively again on September 12, 2025, as a Ninth Circuit panel issued a sweeping stay pending appeal in *Pacito v. Trump*. While the court carved out a narrow exception to preserve essential resettlement services for refugees already admitted to the United States, its handling of the core challenge to Executive Order No. 14163 offers a stark reminder of the enduring gravitational pull of *Trump v. Hawaii*. By staying the district court's injunction against the President's suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), the panel signaled that the "detrimental to the interests" finding required by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) remains a nearly impenetrable shield against judicial second-guessing, even when pitted against the structural mandates of the Refugee Act of 1980.

The procedural posture of *Pacito* is complex, reflecting a litigation history marked by rapid executive action and equally swift judicial responses throughout early 2025. Following President Trump's January 20 order suspending refugee entries and the subsequent defunding of resettlement services by the State Department, the Western District of Washington issued preliminary injunctions blocking both the entry ban and the termination of cooperative agreements with non-profit resettlement agencies. The government appealed, seeking a stay pending appeal. After a series of interim orders that preserved relief only for refugees conditionally approved before January 20, the full panel heard expedited oral argument earlier this month. In its September 12 order, the panel vacated its previous administrative stays and granted the government's motion in large part, halting the district court's interference with the suspension of new admissions while simultaneously ordering the reinstatement of funding for domestic reception and placement services.

On the question of suspension, the panel's reasoning is brief but potent, leaning almost entirely on the Supreme Court's 2018 precedent in *Trump v. Hawaii*. The court emphasized that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the Executive Branch, vesting the President with ample power to impose restrictions beyond those enumerated elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act. Citing *Hawaii*, the panel declared that it could not engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Because Executive Order No. 14163 contained an explicit finding that refugee entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests—a prerequisite explicitly set forth in the statute—the panel concluded the government is likely to succeed on the merits. This formulation effectively treats the President's subjective determination as a jurisdictional key; once turned, it unlocks broad authority that courts are ill-equipped to examine, regardless of whether critics argue such a blanket suspension undermines Congress's comprehensive scheme in the Refugee Act.

This application of *Hawaii* suggests that lower courts view challenges to § 1182(f) based on statutory conflict or policy inconsistency as largely foreclosed unless they can identify a clear textual limit within § 1182(f) itself—which none appears to exist on these facts. The plaintiffs' argument that a total suspension violates the ongoing treatment goals of the Refugee Act was seemingly dismissed as an impermissible attempt to substitute judicial policy preferences for presidential judgment. By refusing to weigh "domestic and foreign interests" independently, instead deferring to the "broad discretion" conferred on the President, the Ninth Circuit reinforces a doctrine where national security and foreign policy rationales render entry suspensions virtually unreviewable so long as facial statutory requirements are met.

However, where *Pacito* diverges significantly from a total victory for the administration is in its treatment of domestic resettlement obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Here, drawing a sharp line between exclusion and inclusion, three judges found that once refugees are admitted—or perhaps more accurately, once they are within statutory protections triggering specific duties—the Executive Branch loses its unfettered discretion to abandon them. The panel pointed to mandatory language ("shall") in § 1522 requiring specific services like employment training, English language acquisition, and cash assistance "to Refugees." Crucially, noting Congress had appropriated billions for these purposes despite recent rescissions attempts via legislation rather than executive fiat alone—the court held it unlikely that existing funds could simply be zeroed out by terminating cooperative agreements without violating statutory commands or arbitrary-and-capricious standards under Administrative Procedure Act principles similar grounds discussed implicitly through factual recitation about lack replacement vendors readiness plans presented during arguments..
