Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# 'Shall' vs. 'Authorized': The Statutory Interpretation Battle Over Mandatory Refugee Resettlement Funding

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit has drawn a sharp line between the President's plenary power to close the border and Congress's statutory command to care for those already inside it. Issued as an interlocutory order pending a full merits opinion, the panel's decision grants the government a sweeping stay regarding Executive Order 14163's suspension of new refugee admissions but simultaneously denies relief on the defunding of domestic resettlement services. The result is a fractured interim landscape: the door to the United States remains shut pursuant to the President's finding of detriment under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), yet the federal government is ordered to immediately reinstate cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies to serve refugees already admitted. While the majority grounds its holding in the imperative "shall" of the Refugee Act, Judge Lee's partial dissent exposes a profound fissure in administrative law jurisprudence regarding whether appropriations statutes create enforceable duties or merely permissive frameworks for executive discretion.

The majority's reasoning rests on a straightforward textualist reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, distinguishing sharply between the authority to exclude and the obligation to assist. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel readily conceded that the President possesses broad discretion to suspend entry when he finds it detrimental to national interests, satisfying the sole prerequisite of § 1182(f). However, the court refused to extend that deference to the termination of domestic support systems. The opinion meticulously catalogs the statutory language governing reception and placement, highlighting that § 1522(a)(1)(A) directs the Office of Refugee Resettlement to "make available" resources and "insure" cash assistance "to the extent of available appropriations." Crucially, the majority seized upon specific mandates within the statute, particularly § 1522(d)(2)(B), which states the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), commanding the Secretary to "develop and implement" alternative projects. For the panel, these commands, coupled with Congress's specific appropriation of billions for migration assistance despite recent rescissions, signaled a non-discretionary duty that survives a change in presidential policy priorities.

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a sophisticated counter-narrative that challenges the very premise of a mandatory duty. He argues that the majority ignores the forest for the trees by focusing on isolated instances of "shall" while disregarding the structural context of § 1522. As Judge Lee notes, the section's title is explicitly an "Authorization for programs," not a mandate. Throughout subsections (b) through (e), Congress repeatedly uses the phrase "is authorized" rather than "shall." In Judge Lee's view, § 1522(a)'s conditional mandates apply only if and when the Executive Branch chooses to exercise its authorized discretion to fund these programs. He characterizes provisions regarding unaccompanied minors and medical support as operating within a sphere of executive necessity ("if necessary," "as needed"), rendering them insufficient to override presidential control over foreign aid and domestic program implementation. This disagreement is not merely semantic; it strikes at the heart of how courts interpret spending clauses where Congress authorizes but does not explicitly compel continuous expenditure in the face of shifting executive strategies.

The doctrinal implications of this split are significant, particularly as lower courts increasingly grapple with cases involving impoundment-like tactics and regulatory defunding. The majority's approach suggests that once Congress appropriates funds and couples them with specific operational directives ("shall develop," "shall assume responsibility"), those directives crystallize into judicially enforceable rights regardless of broader authorizing language. This reading limits the President's ability to effectively nullify statutory schemes through inaction or contract termination without explicit congressional repeal. Conversely, Judge Lee's interpretation aligns with a more robust unitary executive theory, positing that unless Congress speaks with unmistakable clarity to strip away discretion, provisions labeled as "authorizations" leave room for policy recalibration. If adopted broadly, Lee's logic could empower future administrations to dismantle entire regulatory infrastructures simply by declining to issue new grants or renew contracts, even where underlying statutes contain pockets of mandatory-sounding language.

Downstream effects of *Pacito* will likely ripple beyond immigration law into debates over environmental protection, healthcare administration, and education funding, where similar tensions exist between general authorizations and specific programmatic requirements. The Supreme Court has shown renewed interest in major questions doctrine and non-delegation principles; this case presents a mirror image problem: can an agency decline to execute congressionally funded programs based on a reading of "authorization" versus "mandate"? The full opinion promised by Ninth Circuit will need to reconcile how "available appropriations" interact with executive refusal to spend. If Congress has spoken via specific dollar amounts and targeted service requirements, does silence on renewal mechanisms imply permanence? Or does every grant cycle reset presidential discretion?

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a stark reminder that victory in immigration litigation often depends on which side of the border one stands—literally and legally. For those seeking entry, *Hawaii* remains an almost insurmountable barrier against judicial second-guessing of presidential findings on national security and detriment. But for those already within jurisdictional reach, particularly vulnerable populations like unaccompanied minors, statutory text containing "shall" may still function as an anchor against political tides. As this case moves toward final disposition, watch closely how appellate courts navigate this dichotomy: treating exclusion as a prerogative of sovereignty while framing inclusion and integration as a statutory compact that binds even an unwilling Executive Branch.
