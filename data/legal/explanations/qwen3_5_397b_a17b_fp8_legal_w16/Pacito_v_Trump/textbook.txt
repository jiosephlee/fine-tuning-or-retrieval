# Procedural Posture and the Extraordinary Nature of Interim Relief

Understanding the procedural landscape of *Pacito v. Trump* is essential to grasping the significance of the Ninth Circuit's September 12, 2025, panel order. This case does not arrive at the appellate court as a routine appeal from a final judgment; rather, it involves a rapid succession of emergency motions, preliminary injunctions, and stays pending appeal concerning the suspension of the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). The document before us is not a final opinion resolving the merits of the plaintiffs' statutory and constitutional claims. Instead, it is an interlocutory order issued after expedited oral argument, designed to preserve the status quo while the full panel deliberates on the ultimate legality of Executive Order No. 14163 and the subsequent defunding of resettlement services. To navigate this opinion, one must distinguish between the district court's equitable remedies, the appellate court's prior administrative interventions, and the specific legal standard governing a "stay pending appeal" under *Nken v. Holder*. This chapter traces that complex history to explain why the court ruled as it did and what remains in effect during the pendency of the appeal.

## Chronology of District Court Preliminary Injunctions (February and March 2025)

The procedural journey began in the Western District of Washington shortly after President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163 on January 20, 2025, suspending refugee entries, followed by the State Department's suspension of USRAP funding on January 24. Plaintiffs, comprising individual refugees and non-profit resettlement organizations, filed suit alleging violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The district court moved swiftly. On February 25, 2025, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement of key sections of the Executive Order. Two days later, on February 26, the government responded by terminating nearly all cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies. Recognizing that enjoining only the Entry Ban was insufficient if funding for existing refugees remained cut off, the district court issued a written order on February 28 clarifying that its preliminary injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding.

The litigation escalated when the government failed to reinstate the terminated contracts despite the February 28 order. Consequently, on March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction specifically ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after February 25. These two district court orders—the February 28 order halting the ban and funding cuts, and the March 24 order mandating contract reinstatement—form the core subject matter of this appeal. The government filed notices of appeal immediately following each order, setting the stage for urgent appellate intervention. Understanding this timeline is crucial because the Ninth Circuit's current analysis turns on distinguishing between these two distinct forms of relief: stopping new executive action versus affirmatively compelling contractual performance.

# Presidential Authority Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): The Suspension of Entry

When a law student approaches an appellate order staying a preliminary injunction, the primary task is to discern how the court weighed the likelihood of success on the merits. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit faced a direct challenge to Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) based on a presidential finding that such entry would be detrimental to national interests. The central legal question driving the court's decision to stay the district court's injunction regarding the suspension of admissions was whether the President acted within the broad statutory authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The panel concluded that the government was "likely to prevail" on this specific issue, relying heavily on the textual clarity of the statute and the binding precedent of *Trump v. Hawaii*. This chapter unpacks the court's reasoning, illustrating how statutory text and judicial deference converge to create a high barrier for challengers seeking to enjoin presidential actions related to alien entry.

## Statutory Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the 'Detrimental to Interests' Finding

The foundation of the government's defense, and consequently the Ninth Circuit's analysis, rests on the plain language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This statute provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation... suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." The court in *Pacito* emphasized that the statutory prerequisite is singular and straightforward: the President must simply make a "finding" that entry would be detrimental. In this case, President Trump explicitly determined in Executive Order No. 14163 that refugee entry under USRAP met this standard.

For a student reading this opinion, it is crucial to note what the court did *not* require. The panel did not demand empirical evidence proving that refugees actually posed a threat, nor did it require a detailed cost-benefit analysis embedded within the executive order itself. Instead, the court focused strictly on whether the procedural requirement of making a finding had been satisfied. By stating that "the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here," the Ninth Circuit signaled that once the magic words reflecting a detrimental finding are present in a proclamation, the threshold condition of § 1182(f) is met. The statute does not prescribe *how* the President must arrive at that conclusion, only that he must arrive at it. Consequently, plaintiffs challenging the validity of the suspension face an uphill battle because they are attempting to dispute a subjective presidential determination that Congress expressly empowered him to make without further justification.

## The Doctrine of Deference in Presidential Entry Restrictions

Beyond the bare text of § 1182(f), the *Pacito* order relies on a robust doctrine of judicial deference regarding presidential control over borders. The panel quoted *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that § 1182(f) "'exudes deference' to the President." This phrasing is significant for understanding the standard of review applied by appellate courts in immigration cases involving national security or foreign policy implications. When Congress delegates authority using such expansive language—granting power to suspend "any class of aliens" for "such period as he shall deem necessary"—it signals an intent to vest "ample power" in the executive branch.

This deference shapes how a law student should interpret the court's refusal to second-guess the administration's policy choices. The opinion makes clear that questions regarding wisdom, efficacy, or moral propriety of suspending refugee admissions are political questions reserved for elected branches, not legal questions for Article III judges under this specific statute. # Mandatory Duties Under the Refugee Act: Interpreting 8 U.S.C. § 1522

The central tension in *Pacito v. Trump* regarding domestic resettlement services turns on the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a provision within the Refugee Act of 1980 that governs the assistance provided to refugees once they are admitted to the United States. While the parties and the court largely agree on the breadth of presidential authority to suspend future entries under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the dispute sharpens considerably when addressing the fate of refugees already inside the country or in transit. The majority opinion distinguishes between the President's power to halt admission and the Executive Branch's statutory obligation to care for those whom Congress has already authorized or who have already arrived. This distinction rests on a close reading of the text, structure, and specific operative verbs within § 1522, leading the court to conclude that certain resettlement duties are mandatory rather than discretionary.

## Statutory Structure of § 1522: Authorization vs. Mandate

To understand the court's reasoning, one must first navigate the structural architecture of § 1522, which presents a mix of permissive and imperative language. Judge Lee, in his partial dissent, argues that the statute is fundamentally an "authorization" scheme, pointing to the section's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—and the repeated use of the phrase "is authorized" in subsections (b) through (e). Under this view, Congress merely gave the Executive Branch permission to create programs if it chose to do so, leaving the actual implementation entirely to administrative discretion. If § 1522 were purely permissive, the government's decision to terminate all cooperative agreements and cease funding would be a lawful exercise of that discretion.

The majority, however, rejects a holistic reading of the statute as solely permissive. Instead, it isolates specific provisions where Congress shifted from granting authority to imposing duties. The court notes that while subsections (b) through (e) often speak in terms of what the Director is "authorized" to do, subsection (a) introduces a different regulatory regime titled "Conditions and consideration." By separating the creation of programs from the conditions under which they must operate, the statute creates a framework where the existence of a program might be discretionary, but the manner of its execution—and indeed, the execution of specific critical functions—is governed by mandatory commands. The majority's analysis hinges on identifying where Congress crossed the line from saying the government *can* act to saying it *must* act.

## The Significance of 'Shall' in Subsections (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The linchpin of the majority's holding is the presence of the word "shall" in key operative clauses. In legal drafting, "shall" typically denotes a mandatory obligation, whereas "may" indicates discretion. The majority points specifically to § 1522(a)(1)(A), which directs that "to the extent of available appropriations," the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall" make resources available for employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance designed to promote self-sufficiency. Furthermore, regarding unaccompanied minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states that during any interim period before placement, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility... and is authorized to make necessary decisions." Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for recent arrivals needing interim support and medical services.

Judge Lee contests this reading by arguing that these mandatory verbs are qualified by their context. He posits that § 1522(a)'s command to "shall make available" only triggers if the government first chooses to fund a program under subsections (b) through (e). In his view, phrases like "if necessary" in subsection (d) and "as needed" in subsection (e) return ultimate control to the Executive Branch's judgment. The majority implicitly rejects this circular logic in its interim order. # Balancing Equities: The Four-Factor Test for a Stay Pending Appeal

When a party appeals a district court's preliminary injunction, the appellate court must decide whether to pause the enforcement of that injunction while the appeal proceeds. This interim relief, known as a stay pending appeal, is an extraordinary remedy that requires a careful balancing of competing interests. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit utilized this procedural mechanism to navigate a complex dispute involving national security prerogatives and statutory welfare mandates. By applying the Supreme Court's framework from *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the panel demonstrated how a court can reach divergent conclusions on different aspects of the same case based on the distinct legal strengths and factual harms associated with each claim. The resulting order—a partial stay that halts the suspension of refugee entries but compels the reinstatement of resettlement services—illustrates the granular nature of equitable analysis in administrative law litigation.

## Overview of the *Nken* Four-Factor Standard

The legal architecture for granting a stay pending appeal rests on four discrete factors established by the Supreme Court in *Nken v. Holder*. As reiterated in *Pacito*, a moving party must demonstrate: (1) a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that they will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) that issuing the stay will not substantially injure other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) that the public interest favors the stay. The Ninth Circuit emphasized that while all four factors must be considered, the first two—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." This hierarchy means that if an appellant cannot show a substantial chance of winning their appeal or cannot prove that they face harm that cannot be undone by money damages or later judicial correction, the court need not linger extensively on the remaining equities. In *Pacito*, this structure allowed the court to bifurcate its analysis, treating the government's challenge to the entry suspension differently from its challenge to the defunding of resettlement programs.

The statute requires only that the President find entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States," a finding President Trump explicitly made in his order. Because the text of § 1182(f) grants broad discretion to suspend entry for any class of aliens, and because judicial review prohibits a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness" of the President's justification, plaintiffs faced a steep uphill battle proving invalidity under current precedent. # Judicial Interpretation in Conflict: The Majority View vs. Judge Lee's Dissent

The *Pacito v. Trump* panel order offers a striking illustration of how divergent methods of statutory construction can lead to opposite conclusions regarding the same text. While the majority and Judge Lee agree on the threshold issue—that the President possesses broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee entries—they sharply divide on the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. This statute governs the domestic resettlement of refugees already admitted to the United States. The core conflict turns on whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty on the executive branch to maintain specific service programs or merely authorizes discretionary spending. Understanding this disagreement requires a close examination of how each side weighs statutory titles, operative verbs, and qualifying phrases, revealing fundamental tensions between textualist rigor and purposive reading in the context of separation of powers.

## Judge Lee's Argument: The Permissive Nature of 'Authorization' Titles and Verbs

Judge Lee's dissent rests on a strict textualist approach, arguing that the plain language of § 1522 grants discretion rather than imposing mandates. He begins with the statute's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, Lee emphasizes that "authorization" denotes permission, not compulsion. For Judge Lee, the title sets the tonal framework for the entire section, signaling that Congress intended to allow, but not require, the executive branch to act. This initial observation colors his reading of the subsequent subsections.

Moving beyond the title, Judge Lee scrutinizes the operative verbs within the specific funding provisions of § 1522(b) through (e). He notes that these sections consistently state that the Director "is authorized" to provide various forms of assistance, such as initial resettlement, cash assistance, and medical aid. In legal drafting, the distinction between "may" or "is authorized" versus "shall" is often dispositive. Judge Lee argues that Congress's repeated choice of permissive language demonstrates an intent to leave the existence and scope of these programs to executive discretion, contingent upon the flow of appropriations. By focusing on these explicit grants of authority, he concludes that the statute creates a framework for optional action, leaving the decision to terminate cooperative agreements firmly within the President's policy-making domain.

## The Majority's Counter-Argument: Mandatory Conditions Attached to Discretionary Funding

The majority acknowledges the permissive language in certain subsections but rejects the conclusion that the entire statutory scheme is discretionary. Instead, they isolate § 1522(a), titled "Conditions and consideration," which employs mandatory language. This subsection dictates that "[i]n providing assistance under this section," the Director "shall" undertake specific actions, such as making resources available for employment training and ensuring women have equal opportunities in training programs. The majority interprets this structure differently than Judge Lee: while the decision to fund programs might be discretionary, once Congress appropriates funds and the executive elects to provide assistance (or has historically done so via cooperative agreements), statutory conditions attach that are non-negotiable.

Furthermore, the majority points to specific subsections that appear to impose direct obligations regardless of broader discretionary frameworks. They highlight § 1522(d)(2)(B), concerning unaccompanied minors, which states the Director "shall assume legal responsibility... if necessary." Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) commands that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for recent refugees. The majority reads these "shall" commands as creating affirmative duties that survive even if other parts of the statute offer flexibility. Their reasoning suggests that Congress did not intend for the executive branch to have unfettered discretion to dismantle statutorily contemplated safety nets entirely, particularly when doing so leaves vulnerable populations without the specific protections Congress explicitly commanded ("shall") be provided.

## Interpreting Qualifiers: 'If Necessary,' 'As Needed,' and 'To the Extent of Appropriations'

A pivotal point of contention between the opinions is how to interpret qualifying phrases that limit statutory commands. Both sides grapple with phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations," "if necessary," and "as needed." Judge Lee views these qualifiers as evidence of ultimate executive control. He argues that § 1522(a)'s command to provide services is prefaced by "In providing assistance under this section," meaning the mandatory conditions only trigger if the Executive chooses to provide assistance in the first place.
