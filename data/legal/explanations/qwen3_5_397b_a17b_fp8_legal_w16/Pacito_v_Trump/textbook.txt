# Procedural Posture and the Extraordinary Nature of Interim Relief

Understanding *Pacito v. Trump* requires navigating a dense thicket of procedural maneuvers that occurred over a compressed seven-month period in early 2025. This chapter focuses not on the ultimate resolution of the refugees' claims, but on the procedural vehicle the Ninth Circuit used to manage the case while it prepared for a final decision: the stay pending appeal. The court's September 12, 2025, order is an interlocutory directive, meaning it resolves neither the factual disputes nor the legal merits definitively. Instead, it serves as a temporary equilibrium, altering the status quo established by the district court without finally deciding whether the Executive Branch acted unlawfully. For the law student, this opinion offers a masterclass in how appellate courts utilize emergency powers to prevent irreparable harm during the pendency of litigation, specifically distinguishing between the President's authority to suspend entry and the statutory mandates governing domestic resettlement services.

## Timeline of Executive Orders 14163 and 14169 versus District Court Preliminary Injunctions

The procedural chaos in *Pacito* stems from the rapid succession of executive actions and judicial responses in January and February 2025. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, invoking 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of refugees into the United States, citing detriment to national interests. Four days later, relying on a separate order (No. 14169), the State Department halted all funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), including domestic resettlement services. The plaintiffs—a mix of individual refugees and non-profit organizations with cooperative agreements—responded by filing suit in the Western District of Washington. They alleged violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

The district court moved swiftly, issuing an oral preliminary injunction on February 25 blocking key sections of Executive Order 14163. When the government retaliated by terminating nearly all cooperative agreements for refugee services on February 26, the district court clarified its order on February 28 to explicitly prohibit the suspension of USRAP funding. A second preliminary injunction followed on March 24, ordering the reinstatement of terminated agreements. This created a fractured landscape: the Executive Branch was barred from suspending refugee admissions or cutting funding, yet it had already dismantled the infrastructure required to execute those programs. The government appealed both district court orders, setting the stage for appellate intervention. Understanding this timeline is crucial because the Ninth Circuit's subsequent analysis turns entirely on distinguishing between these two distinct phases of government action: the suspension of *entry* (governed by § 1182(f)) and the cessation of *domestic services* (governed by § 1522).

## The Evolution of Appellate Stays: From Partial Grants to Administrative Stays

Before issuing its September order, the Ninth Circuit engaged in a series of interim rulings that refined the scope of relief. Initially, on March 25, a motions panel granted a stay pending appeal regarding the suspension of refugee admissions but carved out an exception for individuals conditionally approved before January 20, 2025. This "limited carveout" recognized that completely halting admissions might cause irreparable harm to those already deep in the process. Over spring and summer, the panel issued clarifying orders in April and May to define this exception further.

Tensions escalated in July when the district court attempted to create an enforcement framework for this carveout, prompting the government to file an emergency motion. The Ninth Circuit responded on July 18 with an administrative stay freezing the district court's enforcement mechanisms pending expedited oral argument. By September, however, following briefing and argument, full panel concluded that their previous piecemeal approach was insufficient. The September 12 order represents a consolidation and revision of this history: it vacates both prior stays (the March partial stay and July administrative stay) and replaces them with a single, comprehensive stay pending appeal. # Presidential Authority Over Entry: Analyzing 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit faced an urgent procedural question: whether to stay the district court's preliminary injunctions that blocked the enforcement of Executive Order No. 14163. This executive order, issued by President Trump in January 2025, suspended the entry of refugees into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). While the appellate panel ultimately crafted a nuanced order that partially granted and partially denied the government's request for a stay, its analysis of the President's authority to suspend entry was unequivocal. The court held that the government demonstrated a strong likelihood of success on the merits regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. This conclusion rested on a strict reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the binding precedent of *Trump v. Hawaii*, which mandates significant judicial deference to presidential determinations regarding national security and foreign policy interests. Understanding this portion of the opinion requires dissecting how the court interpreted statutory text, applied the doctrine of deference, and weighed these factors against the rigorous standard required for obtaining a stay pending appeal.

## Statutory Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): The 'Detrimental to Interests' Prerequisite

The foundation of the government's argument, and the court's subsequent agreement, lay in the plain language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This statute grants the President broad authority to control the entry of aliens. Specifically, it provides that whenever the President "finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States," he may by proclamation suspend their entry or impose restrictions as deemed appropriate for such period as necessary. In *Pacito*, President Trump invoked this exact authority, issuing a formal finding that refugee entry under USRAP would be detrimental to U.S. interests and directing a suspension pending further review.

The Ninth Circuit's analysis focused heavily on the simplicity of this statutory prerequisite. As the panel noted, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, "[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The court emphasized that once this finding is made, the statute imposes no further substantive hurdles for the President to clear. In this case, because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contained such a finding, the textual requirement was satisfied. The panel reasoned that because "the language of § 1182(f) is clear," and because there were no express limitations in other parts of Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) negating this specific power in this context, "the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on th[e] President's authority." By anchoring its reasoning in this straightforward textualist approach, bypassed complex debates about legislative intent or historical practice regarding refugees specifically found in other statutes like *Negusie*. # Mandatory Duties in Refugee Resettlement: Interpreting 8 U.S.C. § 1522

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit draws a sharp distinction between the President's broad authority to suspend the entry of aliens and the Executive Branch's statutory obligations to care for refugees already within the United States or approved for admission. While the court readily acknowledges that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) grants the President sweeping discretion to halt refugee admissions based on a finding of detriment to national interests, it reaches a divergent conclusion regarding the defunding of domestic resettlement services. The pivotal moment in this order occurs when the panel determines that the government is unlikely to succeed on the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenges concerning the termination of reception and placement services. This determination rests on a specific reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the court interprets not merely as a grant of permissive authority, but as a source of mandatory duties triggered by congressional appropriations and specific statutory language. Understanding this distinction is essential to grasping why the court stayed the injunction against the entry suspension while simultaneously ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements for domestic services.

## Statutory Construction of § 1522: Distinguishing 'Authorization' from 'Mandate'

The core legal conflict in this portion of the opinion centers on whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 authorizes the Executive Branch to provide refugee services at its discretion or mandates that it do so once funds are available. The majority opinion rejects the government's implicit argument that the statute is purely permissive. Instead, the court focuses on the operative verb "shall" found in § 1522(a)(1)(A), which directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to perform specific actions "to the extent of available appropriations." The statute commands the Director to make resources available for employment training, ensure opportunities for English language acquisition, structure cash assistance to encourage self-sufficiency, and guarantee gender equity in training participation. By highlighting these directives, the court implies that while Congress limits the *scope* of these duties by the amount of money appropriated ("to the extent of available appropriations"), it does not leave the *decision* to provide them to executive whim. Once Congress appropriates funds, as it did for Fiscal Year 2025, the conditional limitation is satisfied, and the mandatory command ("shall") takes effect. This construction transforms § 1522 from a mere enabling statute into a binding obligation, distinguishing it fundamentally from § 1182(f), where the trigger for action is solely a presidential finding rather than a congressional funding mandate.

Judge Lee's partial dissent illuminates the counter-argument inherent in this statutory construction debate, providing necessary context for understanding the majority's interpretive choice. Judge Lee argues that because § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs..." and frequently uses phrases like "is authorized," the entire scheme should be read as discretionary. Under this view, subsequent uses of "shall" in subsection (a) merely establish conditions precedent—rules about *how* to act if the government *chooses* to act—rather than commands *to* act. The majority implicitly rejects this holistic reading in favor of a clause-specific analysis. By isolating subsection (a)'s directive that the Director "shall... make available sufficient resources," the court treats appropriations as an activating switch for mandatory duties rather than a ceiling on optional ones. This approach prevents the Executive Branch from nullifying congressional intent simply by refusing to execute funded programs, thereby preserving checks and balances between legislative funding powers and executive implementation responsibilities.

## The Significance of Congressional Appropriations in Creating Enforceable Duties

The existence of current congressional appropriations serves as a factual cornerstone for the court's conclusion that a mandatory duty exists under § 1522. The opinion explicitly notes that for Fiscal Year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance via Public Law No. 119-4, incorporating amounts from Division F of Public Law No. 118-147. # Balancing Equities and Crafting Tailored Remedial Relief

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit faced a complex procedural posture involving two distinct legal challenges: one targeting the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and another challenging the administration's defunding of domestic resettlement services under the Administrative Procedure Act and the Refugee Act of 1980. Rather than issuing a blanket stay or denying one entirely, the panel engaged in a nuanced application of the equitable standards for stays pending appeal. By dissecting the four factors established in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the court arrived at a split outcome. It granted a stay regarding the suspension of entry—a move favoring the government based on judicial deference to presidential authority in immigration control—while simultaneously refusing to stay the portion of the district court's order mandating the reinstatement of service agreements for refugees already within the United States. This chapter examines how the court balanced these competing equities to craft a remedy that preserved statutory mandates for admitted individuals without interfering with the Executive's foreign policy determinations regarding future entries.

## Applying the Four-Factor Test (*Nken v. Holder*) to Mixed Questions of Law

The threshold inquiry for any stay pending appeal requires the moving party to satisfy the four-factor test articulated in *Nken*: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury absent a stay, substantial injury to other parties, and the public interest. In *Pacito*, the panel treated these factors not as a monolithic block but as analytical tools to separate the plaintiffs' claims into two categories: those challenging the entry suspension and those challenging the cessation of resettlement funding. Regarding the challenge to Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended refugee admissions, the court found the government satisfied the first two critical *Nken* factors. Relying heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), the panel concluded the government was likely to succeed because § 1182(f) vests broad discretion in the President to suspend entry upon finding it detrimental to national interests. Since the President had made such a finding, and because courts generally avoid "searching inquiries" into the persuasiveness of such justifications, the government demonstrated a strong likelihood of prevailing on this specific issue. Consequently, on this limb of the case, equity favored a stay of the injunction preventing implementation of the travel ban.

However, this analysis shifted dramatically when applied to plaintiffs' claim that terminating cooperative agreements violated 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Here, the panel determined that unlike its prediction for § 1182(f), it did not believe "the government is likely to prevail." The distinction turned on statutory interpretation rather than constitutional deference or foreign policy discretion. While § 1182(f) invites deference regarding *who* enters, § 1522 outlines specific obligations regarding *how* admitted refugees are treated once inside borders. Because Congress explicitly appropriated funds ($3.178 billion for FY 2025) and used mandatory language ("shall") in key provisions governing reception and placement, legal precedent suggested these duties were binding regardless of policy shifts at higher levels of government against new arrivals (a point contested by Judge Lee in his partial dissent). Thus, applying *Nken*'s first factor yielded divergent results: high probability of success for staying relief related to entry bans, but low probability for staying relief related to cutting off existing support systems funded by current appropriations.

# Judicial Dissent on Statutory Interpretation: The Debate Over Discretionary Funding

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit's panel order presents a sharp divergence in judicial philosophy regarding the interpretation of the Refugee Act of 1980, specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522. While the majority opinion temporarily reinstates cooperative agreements to ensure the provision of resettlement services, viewing the statute as imposing a mandatory duty on the executive branch, Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a rigorous counter-argument rooted in textualism and separation of powers principles. For the law student, this dissent serves as a critical case study in how judges analyze statutory language to determine whether Congress has commanded an action or merely permitted it. Judge Lee argues that the majority misreads § 1522 by ignoring the permissive nature of its text, thereby improperly encroaching on the Executive's discretion to administer foreign aid and domestic resettlement programs. Understanding this dissent requires a close examination of the specific linguistic cues Judge Lee identifies and the structural constitutional concerns he raises regarding judicial management of executive functions.

## Textualist Arguments: The Significance of Section Titles and Permissive Language

Judge Lee's analysis begins with a foundational principle of statutory construction: the text itself is the primary source of legislative intent. He directs attention first to the title of § 1522, "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." In legal hermeneutics, while section titles are not dispositive, they provide important context for understanding the scope of a provision. Judge Lee emphasizes the word "Authorization," defining it via *Black's Law Dictionary* as "permission to do something" rather than a command. By contrasting "authorization" with "mandate," he establishes his core thesis: Congress intended to grant the Executive Branch the *power* to create resettlement programs if it chose to do so, not to strip the Executive of the choice entirely. This distinction is vital because if the statute is merely an authorization, the President retains the discretion to decline to exercise that power, whereas a mandate would legally compel action regardless of policy preferences.

Building on the section title, Judge Lee scrutinizes the operative verbs within the substantive subsections of § 1522. He notes that subsections (b) through (e), which detail specific types of aid such as initial resettlement, cash assistance, and medical support, uniformly state that the Director "is authorized" to provide such assistance. In contrast to mandatory language like "shall provide" or "must establish," the phrase "is authorized" signals discretion. Judge Lee critiques the majority for overlooking this consistent pattern of permissive language throughout the bulk of the statute. His argument suggests that when Congress wishes to impose a duty, it knows how to do so explicitly; conversely, when it uses "authorized," it intends to leave the decision-making process to the agency. This textualist approach demands that courts respect the plain meaning of these verbs rather than inferring obligations that the text does not expressly contain.

## Interpreting Qualifiers: 'If Necessary,' 'As Needed,' and 'Available Appropriations'

Beyond the binary distinction between "shall" and "may," Judge Lee delves into the qualifying phrases that modify even those parts of § 1522 that appear mandatory on their face. The majority relies heavily on § 1522(a), which states that in providing assistance, the Director "shall... make available sufficient resources." However, Judge Lee points out that this command is prefaced by two significant limitations: it applies only "[i]n providing assistance under this section" and is constrained "to the extent of available appropriations." He interprets the former phrase as conditional; essentially, *if* the government decides to fund a program under subsections (b) through (e), *then* it must adhere to certain conditions regarding how those funds are used (e.g., promoting economic self-sufficiency). The obligation attaches to the act of funding, not to the decision to fund in the first place. Furthermore, the appropriation qualifier reinforces fiscal discretion, implying that without specific congressional funding directives compelling expenditure, the executive retains control over whether to utilize available funds for these purposes.

Judge Lee extends this logic to other subsections cited by the majority as evidence of mandatory duties. For instance, § 1522(d)(2)(B) regarding unaccompanied minors states the Director "shall assume legal responsibility... if necessary." Judge Lee argues thatthe inclusion of "if necessary" injects a layer of executive judgment into what might otherwise look like a rigid command. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to develop projects for refugees "as needed." To Judge Lee, phrases like "if necessary" and "as needed" are not mere surplusage; they are deliberate statutory signals delegating authority to the Executive Branch to assess circumstances and determinethe appropriate response. By reading these qualifiers broadly, he contends that even sections containingthe word "shall" ultimately preserve executive discretion regardingthe existenceand scopeofthe programs themselves. Consequently,the majority'sreadingthatthese provisionscreatean unconditionaldutytomaintainspecificcooperativeagreementsignores thenuancedlimitationsembeddedinthetextbyCongress.
