Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does the 'relaxed' standing test for procedural injuries differ from standard standing, and why did AFL still fail?
Question:
The opinion mentions that when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest, courts 'relax' the requirements for imminence and redressability. However, the court says AFL failed even this relaxed standard. What exactly is the difference between proving injury/causation under the standard *Lujan* test versus this 'procedural injury' framework? Specifically, why wasn't the statutory mandate that OSC 'shall... conduct an investigation' enough to satisfy the injury element?
Answer:
The "relaxed" standing test for procedural injuries does not lower the bar for **injury-in-fact** or **causation**; rather, it only eases the requirements for **imminence** and **redressability**.

Under the standard *Lujan* test, a plaintiff must prove that their injury is actual or imminent and that a favorable court decision is likely to redress that injury. When a plaintiff alleges a violation of a **procedural right** granted by statute to protect a concrete interest, courts recognize that the injury occurs immediately upon the denial of the procedure itself. Consequently, the plaintiff does not need to prove that the ultimate harm they sought to avoid (e.g., environmental damage) is "imminent," nor do they need to prove with absolute certainty that following the procedure would have changed the outcome (*redressability*).

However, as the D.C. Circuit clarifies in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, this relaxation **"not wholly eliminat[es]"** the requirements of injury-in-fact or causation. The plaintiff must still demonstrate:
1.  A concrete, particularized interest that the procedure was designed to protect; and
2.  That the deprivation of the procedural right actually resulted in an injury to that concrete interest (causation).

### Why AFL Failed Even Under the Relaxed Standard

AFL failed because it could not bridge the gap between OSC's failure to investigate and any concrete injury to AFL. The court rejected both of AFL's theories of injury:

**1. Failure of Causation (The FOIA Records Theory)**
AFL argued that if OSC had investigated DOJ's FOIA compliance, DOJ might have eventually released the emails AFL wanted. The court found this causal chain too speculative to satisfy Article III, even under a relaxed standard.
*   **The Speculative Chain:** The court noted that establishing causation would require speculating on multiple independent actions: OSC choosing to pursue enforcement, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) choosing to sanction employees, and DOJ subsequently choosing to release records.
*   **Independent Actors:** Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the opinion emphasizes that causation cannot rely on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Because OSC's investigation does not bind DOJ or the MSPB to release records, AFL could not show that its inability to get the FOIA records was *caused* by OSC's inaction.

**2. Failure of Concrete Injury (The Informational Rights Theory)**
AFL alternatively argued it was injured by not receiving updates and notices regarding an OSC investigation.
*   **No Statutory Entitlement:** To have informational standing, a plaintiff must be denied information they are statutorily entitled to receive (*ASPCA v. Feld Ent.*).
*   **Discretionary Procedures:** While 5 U.S.C. § 1216 mandates an *investigation*, it does not mandate specific *notice procedures*. Those notice requirements exist under § 1214 for "prohibited personnel practices," but applying them to § 1216 FOIA investigations is discretionary (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*).
*   **Result:** Since AFL had no legal entitlement to receive notices during a § 1216 investigation, being denied those notices constituted no concrete injury. As the court stated, a "procedural right in vacuo"—without a concrete interest affected—is insufficient for standing (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*).

### Summary
The statutory mandate that OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" created a procedural duty, but it did not create a **right to a specific outcome** (release of documents) or a **right to specific information** (investigative updates). Without a concrete interest tied directly to the procedure itself, or a non-speculative link between the procedure and a tangible harm, AFL could not satisfy the irreducible minimums of injury and causation required by Article III.
