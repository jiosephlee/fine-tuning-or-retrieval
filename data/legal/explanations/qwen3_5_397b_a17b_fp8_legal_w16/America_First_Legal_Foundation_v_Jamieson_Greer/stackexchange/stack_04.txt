Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What distinguishes an 'informational injury' based on a statutory entitlement from a mere hope of receiving information during an investigation?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's second theory: that they were injured by not getting updates/notices from OSC. The opinion states that informational standing requires being denied information a statute 'entitled him to receive.' Since § 1216 mandates an investigation but doesn't explicitly mandate notice to the complainant (unlike § 1214), AFL had no entitlement. Is there any scenario where a plaintiff has standing to force an agency to investigate something just to get status updates, or must the underlying statute explicitly create a right to that specific information?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the distinction between a cognizable "informational injury" and a mere hope of receiving information turns entirely on whether the underlying statute creates a **specific legal entitlement** to that information.

### The Legal Standard: Statutory Entitlement vs. Discretion
To establish standing based on an informational injury, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they were denied information that a statute **"entitled [them] to receive."** *Am. Soc'y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoted in *AFL*).

The court clarifies that a statutory mandate for an agency to *act* (e.g., conduct an investigation) does not automatically create a statutory right for the complainant to *receive updates* about that action. If the statute grants the agency discretion regarding whether to provide notice or information, the plaintiff has no legally protected interest in receiving it, and therefore suffers no concrete injury when it is withheld.

### Application in *AFL v. Greer*
The court applied this distinction to reject AFL's second theory of standing, which claimed injury from not receiving notices or status updates from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

1.  **The Mandate to Investigate (§ 1216):** The court acknowledged that 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) might mandate OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, the text of § 1216 contains **no provision entitling a complainant to receive information** or notices regarding the progress of such an investigation.
2.  **The Contrast with § 1214:** The opinion explicitly contrasts § 1216 with § 1214 (which governs "prohibited personnel practices"). Section 1214 includes specific procedural provisions requiring OSC to notify the complaining party about the investigation's progress. See *AFL* slip op. at I.A ("Section 1214 sets forth procedures... including provisions for notifying the complaining party").
3.  **Discretionary Nature of Notice:** Because § 1216 lacks the explicit notice requirements found in § 1214, OSC's decision to provide updates during a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. The court notes that AFL itself acknowledged case law holding that OSC's decision to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary." *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011).

### Answering Your Specific Question
You asked if there is any scenario where a plaintiff has standing to force an investigation just to get status updates, or if the statute must explicitly create that right.

Under *AFL*, **the underlying statute must explicitly create (or clearly imply) a right to that specific information.**

*   **No Standing via Derivative Hope:** You cannot gain standing to force an investigation merely because, *if* the investigation occurred, the agency *might* choose to send you updates. As the court states, "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury."
*   **The Requirement:** To have standing based on an informational interest in the "fruits of the investigation," the plaintiff must point to a statutory clause that says the agency *must* provide that information to them. Without such an entitlement, the desire for updates remains a "mere hope," which is insufficient for Article III standing.

In short, a statutory command to *investigate* is not enough; there must be a separate statutory command (or clear implication) that the agency must *inform* the plaintiff about that investigation.
