Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) create a private right of action for citizens to compel OSC investigations into FOIA violations?
Question:
The text says OSC 'shall... conduct an investigation' of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Usually, mandatory language like 'shall' implies a duty. However, the court implies that even if this duty exists, a private citizen can't sue to enforce it without standing. If AFL lacks standing because they can't show redressability, does that mean no private citizen ever has standing to enforce § 1216(a)(3)? Is this effectively a commitment of enforcement discretion entirely to OSC, similar to prosecutorial discretion?
Answer:
No, the court's finding that *America First Legal Foundation* (AFL) lacked standing does not mean that **no** private citizen can ever have standing to enforce 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Rather, the holding indicates that a plaintiff cannot establish standing based solely on the statutory "shall" language if they cannot demonstrate that a court order compelling an investigation would concretely redress their specific injury.

The opinion clarifies that while § 1216(a)(3) uses mandatory language ("shall ... conduct an investigation"), this creates a duty owed to the public or the statute's scheme, not necessarily an individually enforceable right that bypasses Article III's causation and redressability requirements.

### The Barrier: Causation and Redressability, Not Just Discretion
Your intuition regarding prosecutorial discretion (*Heckler v. Chaney*) is partially correct, but the court's dismissal rests more heavily on the speculative nature of the causal chain required to show **redressability**.

Under *Lujan*, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision is likely to redress their injury. In this case, AFL argued that forcing OSC to investigate would eventually lead to DOJ releasing the withheld FOIA records. The court rejected this because the path from an OSC investigation to record release involves multiple independent actors making discretionary choices:
1.  OSC might investigate but choose **not** to seek corrective action.
2.  Even if OSC seeks action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might **not** sanction the employees.
3.  Even if sanctions occur, DOJ might **still not** release the records.

Judge Katsas writes that this chain relies on "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit" and "how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion." Therefore, AFL failed standing not because § 1216 grants OSC total immunity from suit, but because AFL could not prove that winning the lawsuit would actually get them their documents.

### Could Another Plaintiff Have Standing?
The opinion leaves open the possibility of standing for a different type of plaintiff or under a different theory of injury, provided they can meet the *Lujan* test:

*   **Different Injury:** If a plaintiff could identify an injury that is directly redressable by an OSC investigation (rather than the ultimate release of records), they might survive a motion to dismiss. However, the court also rejected AFL's second theory: that they had an informational interest in the *process* of the investigation itself (e.g., receiving status updates). The court noted that § 1216 does not entitle complainants to the specific notice procedures found in § 1214, and absent a statutory entitlement to receive information from OSC, there is no concrete "informational injury."
*   **Direct Causation:** If a scenario existed where an OSC investigation was the *sole* bottleneck preventing relief, and the outcome of that investigation mechanically triggered a remedy without further discretionary intervening steps, the redressability prong might be satisfied.

### Conclusion: Duty vs. Enforceability
The opinion distinguishes between a statutory duty and judicial enforceability by a private party. The phrase "shall conduct an investigation" imposes an obligation on OSC, but it does not automatically grant every citizen a private right of action enforceable in federal court if that citizen cannot link the breach of duty to a concrete, redressable harm.

Effectively, for most FOIA requesters, § 1216(a)(3) functions similarly to prosecutorial discretion because the mechanism it triggers (internal discipline/corrective action) is too attenuated from the requester's goal (document production) to satisfy Article III standing. However, this is a limitation of **this specific plaintiff's theory of harm**, not an absolute bar declaring that § 1216(a)(3) creates zero justiciable rights for all future litigants under all conceivable factual scenarios.
