Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Speculative Chain: D.C. Circuit Tightens Causation Standards in Enforcement Suits

In the increasingly crowded arena of administrative law litigation, public interest groups often attempt to use statutory enforcement mechanisms as a lever to pry open government records or force policy changes. The logic is seductive: if an agency fails to act, a watchdog group can sue to compel that action, hoping the resulting investigation will yield the substantive relief the group originally sought. On October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit firmly slammed the door on this strategy in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas held that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacked Article III standing to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations by the Department of Justice. While the underlying dispute concerned email searches and transparency, the opinion's true significance lies in its rigorous application of causation and redressability doctrines. By deploying recent Supreme Court precedent regarding "unfettered choices" by independent actors, the court signaled that the causal chains required to establish standing in third-party enforcement suits must be far more direct than previously assumed by many litigants.

The factual backdrop involves AFL's request for DOJ emails related to judicial recusals. When DOJ produced some records but maintained a policy of not searching individual employee emails without consent, AFL pivoted. Rather than continuing to litigate solely against DOJ under FOIA, AFL petitioned the OSC to investigate the agency's withholding policy as an "arbitrary or capricious" violation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The OSC declined, citing the specific triggers for mandatory investigation found in FOIA's own enforcement provision, which had not been met. The district court initially agreed with AFL that the OSC had misread its statutory authority but ultimately dismissed the case, viewing the decision to investigate as discretionary. On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the statutory interpretation question entirely, grounding its dismissal in the threshold requirement of Article III standing.

Judge Katsas's analysis dismantles AFL's standing theories by focusing intensely on the break in the causal chain between the requested judicial relief and the alleged injury. AFL argued that if the court ordered an OSC investigation, that investigation would likely lead to disciplinary proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which would in turn pressure DOJ into releasing the withheld emails. The court characterized this sequence as a series of speculations about the decisions of independent actors not before the court. Relying heavily on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024), Judge Katsas emphasized that causation cannot rest on predictions of how non-parties—here, both the OSC and the MSPB—might exercise their discretion. Even if a statute mandates an investigation, it rarely mandates a specific outcome of that investigation. Therefore, a court order compelling an inquiry does not guarantee, or even make "very likely," the production of documents by a separate executive branch component.

This reasoning marks a notable tightening of the "relaxed" standing standards often applied in procedural injury cases. Traditionally, courts have allowed plaintiffs alleging a violation of a procedural right to clear hurdles regarding imminence and redressability more easily, provided they could show a concrete interest threatened by the procedure's absence. However, *Greer* suggests that this relaxation has limits when the procedural right is merely a step removed from the substantive harm. The court rejected AFL's attempt to conflate a right to an investigation with a right to information. Because section 1216 creates no entitlement for complainants to receive specific data or notifications during an investigation—unlike distinct provisions governing prohibited personnel practices under section 1214—AFL could not demonstrate a concrete informational injury traceable to OSC's inaction. The opinion makes clear that a "procedural right in vacuo," divorced from a guaranteed concrete outcome, is insufficient to sustain jurisdiction.

The doctrinal implications of *Greer* extend well beyond FOIA disputes or the specific mechanics of Civil Service Reform Act enforcement. The decision serves as a potent warning to public interest litigants who view agency investigations as a proxy for obtaining underlying substantive benefits. In an era where plaintiffs frequently seek to compel regulatory action against third parties (such as banks, hospitals, or other agencies) to achieve their own policy goals, *Greer* reinforces that Article III demands a tight nexus between judicial intervention and injury redressal. If achieving relief requires speculating about how an investigator will weigh evidence, how an adjudicator will rule on sanctions, or how a regulated entity will react to potential penalties, the chain is too attenuated to support standing. This approach aligns with a broader trend in recent jurisprudence toward restricting access to federal courts for generalized grievances masquerading as specific legal injuries.

Furthermore, Judge Katsas's reliance on *Haaland v. Brackeen* underscores that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before it, not on whether its rationale might persuade outsiders. This distinction is crucial for separation-of-powers concerns; it prevents federal judges from issuing advisory opinions that effectively manage inter-agency dynamics without actual authority over all relevant players. By vacating the district court's judgment and ordering dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, the D.C. Circuit has raisedthe bar for future suits attempting to weaponize oversight statutes like section 1216 Litigants must now demonstrate not just that an agency failed to follow procedure, but that correcting that failure would directly and predictably cure their specific injury without relying on intervening acts of discretion by other branches or bureaus Of government .



# Procedural Rights in Vacuo: Why Statutory Mandates Alone Cannot Confer Standing

On October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit delivered a sharp reminder to administrative law litigants: Congress cannot legislate away the Constitution's case-or-controversy requirement. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, a unanimous panel authored by Judge Katsas vacated a district court judgment and dismissed a suit against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) for lack of Article III standing. The case arose from AFL's attempt to force OSC to investigate the Department of Justice's handling of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the underlying statute arguably commands OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, the court held that AFL lacked a concrete injury traceable to OSC's inaction. The decision reinforces a critical boundary in modern standing doctrine: a statutory "right" to a government procedure is meaningless for Article III purposes unless it protects a separate, concrete interest that is actually threatened.

## The Anatomy of the Dispute

The factual backdrop involves a classic transparency dispute layered with complex administrative enforcement mechanisms. AFL sought emails from the DOJ regarding judicial recusals. When the DOJ initially resisted searching employee emails without consent, AFL filed a FOIA suit against the DOJ—which remains pending and productive—and separately petitioned OSC to investigate the agency's alleged policy of arbitrary withholding. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" of allegations concerning arbitrary FOIA violations. OSC declined, citing the absence of specific judicial findings required by a different FOIA enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

The district court found that OSC had misinterpreted its statutory authority; Section 1216 did indeed empower OSC to investigate independent of the Section 552 triggers. However, the lower court ultimately dismissed the case, viewing the decision to pursue corrective action as discretionary even if the investigation itself was mandatory. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit bypassed this statutory interpretation entirely. Instead, Judge Katsas zeroed in on a threshold jurisdictional defect that neither party had initially briefed: AFL simply had no stake in the outcome sufficient to invoke federal judicial power.

## The Fallacy of Procedural Injury Without Concrete Harm

The core of Judge Katsas's analysis rests on the Supreme Court's admonition in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation... is insufficient" for standing. AFL attempted to navigate around this hurdle by invoking the "relaxed" standing standards often applied in procedural injury cases. Under precedents like *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, plaintiffs alleging a violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest face less stringent requirements regarding imminence and redressability. However, as the D.C. Circuit clarified, this relaxation does not eliminate the need for an underlying concrete interest or causation.

AFL proposed two theories of injury, both of which failed to clear the constitutional bar. First, they argued that OSC's failure to investigate prevented them from receiving the DOJ records they sought via FOIA. The court swiftly dismantled this causal chain as overly speculative. Any informational injury regarding the DOJ records was directly caused by the DOJ, not OSC. To link OSC's inaction to the non-production of records required assuming a multi-step domino effect: OSC would investigate, then choose to seek corrective action, then the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would sanction employees, and finally, DOJ would release the records. As Judge Katsas noted, relying on the "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts"—specifically OSC's enforcement discretion and MSPB's adjudicatory powers—renders causation too attenuated for Article III. A favorable ruling against OSC would not bind DOJ or MSPB, meaning it could not guarantee redress.

Second, AFL claimed an informational injury based on being denied updates about an OSC investigation itself. This argument collapsed because no statute entitled AFL to receive such information during a Section 1216 inquiry. While Section 1214 mandates notifications for "prohibited personnel practice" investigations, Section 1216 investigations into FOIA violations do not carry identical procedural guarantees Courts have previously recognized that applying Section 1214's notice requirements to Section 1216 investigations is within OSC's discretion. Because AFL had no legal entitlement to the information it claimed was withheld, it suffered no cognizable injury when OSC refused to provide it. You cannot be injured by being denied something you have no right to receive, regardless of whether an agency violated a duty to act internally.

# Statutory Silos: The Limits of Cross-Referencing Notice Provisions in FOIA Enforcement

On October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit issued a terse but significant opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, dismissing a challenge to the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) refusal to investigate an alleged Freedom of Information Act violation by the Department of Justice. While the underlying dispute concerned transparency and the scope of OSC's mandate under the Civil Service Reform Act, the court resolved the case on threshold grounds: Article III standing. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Katsas dismantled the plaintiff's attempt to bootstrap procedural rights from one statutory scheme into another, reinforcing a rigorous approach to informational standing that demands a clear statutory entitlement rather than a speculative hope of disclosure. The decision serves as a potent reminder that in administrative law, the mere existence of an investigatory duty does not automatically generate a private right to the fruits of that investigation.

The case arose from America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) FOIA request to the DOJ regarding emails between outside counsel and the Civil Division. When DOJ initially resisted searching employee emails without consent—a stance it later softened during parallel litigation—AFL petitioned OSC to investigate what it termed an arbitrary withholding policy. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation" of allegations concerning arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC declined, noting that the specific triggers for mandatory disciplinary proceedings under FOIA's own enforcement mechanism, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), had not been met. The district court agreed that OSC possessed broad authority under § 1216 but ultimately held that the decision to investigate remained discretionary. On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the merits of OSC's statutory authority entirely, focusing instead on whether AFL suffered a cognizable injury capable of supporting federal jurisdiction.

The core of AFL's standing argument rested on a creative reading of the interplay between two distinct sections of the Civil Service Reform Act. AFL contended that if OSC were compelled to investigate under § 1216, the agency would necessarily have to afford AFL the notice and progress updates required for "prohibited personnel practice" investigations under § 1214. By denying the investigation, OSC allegedly deprived AFL of this procedural information. Judge Katsas rejected this theory, drawing a sharp line between an agency's duty to act and a complainant's right to receive specific data. The court emphasized that while § 1216 mandates an investigation into FOIA violations, it creates no corresponding entitlement for the complainant to receive notices or updates akin to those in § 1214. Indeed, AFL conceded that existing precedent treats the application of § 1214's procedural safeguards to § 1216 investigations as discretionary. Without a statutory guarantee of receipt, the denial of such information cannot constitute a concrete "informational injury" sufficient for Article III standing.

This distinction highlights a critical doctrinal silo within administrative statutes: the separation between substantive enforcement mechanisms and procedural participation rights. Courts have long recognized that plaintiffs can suffer informational injury when denied data they are statutorily entitled to receive, as seen in cases like *FEC v. Akins*. However, *Greer* clarifies that this doctrine does not extend to situations where a plaintiff argues that triggering one statutory duty (§ 1216) should implicitly activate the procedural benefits of a different, non-applicable regime (§ 1214). The court refused to allow AFL to manufacture a concrete interest out of thin air by cross-referencing disjointed statutory provisions. In doing so, the panel reinforced the principle from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that a "procedural right in vacuo"—divorced from a concrete interest affected by the deprivation—is insufficient to confer standing.

Furthermore, the court systematically dismantled AFL's alternative theory: that OSC's failure to investigate caused AFL's inability to obtain the underlying DOJ records via FOIA. Here, the chain of causation proved too attenuated to satisfy constitutional requirements. Even if OSC had launched an investigation, any path leading to the release of documents would require a series of speculative steps: OSC choosing to pursue corrective action, the Merit Systems Protection Board sanctioning employees, and DOJ subsequently deciding to comply. As Judge Katsas noted, causation cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court, particularly when those actors include other executive branch entities exercising their own enforcement discretion. This analysis underscores a growing judicial skepticism toward theories of redressability that depend on complex, multi-agency hypotheticals rather than direct legal commands binding the defendant.

The implications of *Greer* extend well beyond this specific dispute over email searches. For transparency advocates and litigants seeking to leverage internal watchdog agencies like OSC to force agency compliance with FOIA, the decision raises a formidable barrier. It suggests that unless Congress explicitly grants complainants a right to specific information during an investigative process, courts will be reluctant to infer such rights through statutory interpolation. This limits the utility of suing watchdog agencies solely to compel investigations; without a guaranteed payoff in terms of participant access or document production, plaintiffs may struggle to demonstrate they have anything more than a generalized grievance about government opacity.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* reaffirms that Article III standing remains a gatekeeper that cannot be opened by clever statutory synthesis alone. By insisting on a direct link between statutory text and concrete injury, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that efforts to expand transparency through secondary enforcement channels must clear high hurdles regarding both causation and entitlements. While Congress retains the power to rewrite these statutes to create explicit private rights of action or notification requirements absent here, current jurisprudence leaves little room for courtsto bridge those gaps judicially. For now, the silos between investigatory mandates and procedural rewards remain firmly intact.
