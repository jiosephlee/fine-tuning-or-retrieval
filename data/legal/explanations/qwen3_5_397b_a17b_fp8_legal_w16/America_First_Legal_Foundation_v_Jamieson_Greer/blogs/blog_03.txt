Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Statutory Silos: The Limits of Cross-Referencing Notice Provisions in FOIA Enforcement

On October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit issued a terse but significant opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, dismissing a challenge to the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) refusal to investigate an alleged Freedom of Information Act violation by the Department of Justice. While the underlying dispute concerned transparency and the scope of OSC's mandate under the Civil Service Reform Act, the court resolved the case on threshold grounds: Article III standing. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Katsas dismantled the plaintiff's attempt to bootstrap procedural rights from one statutory scheme into another, reinforcing a rigorous approach to informational standing that demands a clear statutory entitlement rather than a speculative hope of disclosure. The decision serves as a potent reminder that in administrative law, the mere existence of an investigatory duty does not automatically generate a private right to the fruits of that investigation.

The case arose from America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) FOIA request to the DOJ regarding emails between outside counsel and the Civil Division. When DOJ initially resisted searching employee emails without consent—a stance it later softened during parallel litigation—AFL petitioned OSC to investigate what it termed an arbitrary withholding policy. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation" of allegations concerning arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC declined, noting that the specific triggers for mandatory disciplinary proceedings under FOIA's own enforcement mechanism, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), had not been met. The district court agreed that OSC possessed broad authority under § 1216 but ultimately held that the decision to investigate remained discretionary. On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the merits of OSC's statutory authority entirely, focusing instead on whether AFL suffered a cognizable injury capable of supporting federal jurisdiction.

The core of AFL's standing argument rested on a creative reading of the interplay between two distinct sections of the Civil Service Reform Act. AFL contended that if OSC were compelled to investigate under § 1216, the agency would necessarily have to afford AFL the notice and progress updates required for "prohibited personnel practice" investigations under § 1214. By denying the investigation, OSC allegedly deprived AFL of this procedural information. Judge Katsas rejected this theory, drawing a sharp line between an agency's duty to act and a complainant's right to receive specific data. The court emphasized that while § 1216 mandates an investigation into FOIA violations, it creates no corresponding entitlement for the complainant to receive notices or updates akin to those in § 1214. Indeed, AFL conceded that existing precedent treats the application of § 1214's procedural safeguards to § 1216 investigations as discretionary. Without a statutory guarantee of receipt, the denial of such information cannot constitute a concrete "informational injury" sufficient for Article III standing.

This distinction highlights a critical doctrinal silo within administrative statutes: the separation between substantive enforcement mechanisms and procedural participation rights. Courts have long recognized that plaintiffs can suffer informational injury when denied data they are statutorily entitled to receive, as seen in cases like *FEC v. Akins*. However, *Greer* clarifies that this doctrine does not extend to situations where a plaintiff argues that triggering one statutory duty (§ 1216) should implicitly activate the procedural benefits of a different, non-applicable regime (§ 1214). The court refused to allow AFL to manufacture a concrete interest out of thin air by cross-referencing disjointed statutory provisions. In doing so, the panel reinforced the principle from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that a "procedural right in vacuo"—divorced from a concrete interest affected by the deprivation—is insufficient to confer standing.

Furthermore, the court systematically dismantled AFL's alternative theory: that OSC's failure to investigate caused AFL's inability to obtain the underlying DOJ records via FOIA. Here, the chain of causation proved too attenuated to satisfy constitutional requirements. Even if OSC had launched an investigation, any path leading to the release of documents would require a series of speculative steps: OSC choosing to pursue corrective action, the Merit Systems Protection Board sanctioning employees, and DOJ subsequently deciding to comply. As Judge Katsas noted, causation cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court, particularly when those actors include other executive branch entities exercising their own enforcement discretion. This analysis underscores a growing judicial skepticism toward theories of redressability that depend on complex, multi-agency hypotheticals rather than direct legal commands binding the defendant.

The implications of *Greer* extend well beyond this specific dispute over email searches. For transparency advocates and litigants seeking to leverage internal watchdog agencies like OSC to force agency compliance with FOIA, the decision raises a formidable barrier. It suggests that unless Congress explicitly grants complainants a right to specific information during an investigative process, courts will be reluctant to infer such rights through statutory interpolation. This limits the utility of suing watchdog agencies solely to compel investigations; without a guaranteed payoff in terms of participant access or document production, plaintiffs may struggle to demonstrate they have anything more than a generalized grievance about government opacity.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* reaffirms that Article III standing remains a gatekeeper that cannot be opened by clever statutory synthesis alone. By insisting on a direct link between statutory text and concrete injury, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that efforts to expand transparency through secondary enforcement channels must clear high hurdles regarding both causation and entitlements. While Congress retains the power to rewrite these statutes to create explicit private rights of action or notification requirements absent here, current jurisprudence leaves little room for courtsto bridge those gaps judicially. For now, the silos between investigatory mandates and procedural rewards remain firmly intact.
