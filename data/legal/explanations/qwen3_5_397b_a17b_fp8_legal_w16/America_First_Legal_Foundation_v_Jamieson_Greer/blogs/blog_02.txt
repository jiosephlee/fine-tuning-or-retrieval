Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights in Vacuo: Why Statutory Mandates Alone Cannot Confer Standing

On October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit delivered a sharp reminder to administrative law litigants: Congress cannot legislate away the Constitution's case-or-controversy requirement. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, a unanimous panel authored by Judge Katsas vacated a district court judgment and dismissed a suit against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) for lack of Article III standing. The case arose from AFL's attempt to force OSC to investigate the Department of Justice's handling of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the underlying statute arguably commands OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, the court held that AFL lacked a concrete injury traceable to OSC's inaction. The decision reinforces a critical boundary in modern standing doctrine: a statutory "right" to a government procedure is meaningless for Article III purposes unless it protects a separate, concrete interest that is actually threatened.

## The Anatomy of the Dispute

The factual backdrop involves a classic transparency dispute layered with complex administrative enforcement mechanisms. AFL sought emails from the DOJ regarding judicial recusals. When the DOJ initially resisted searching employee emails without consent, AFL filed a FOIA suit against the DOJ—which remains pending and productive—and separately petitioned OSC to investigate the agency's alleged policy of arbitrary withholding. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" of allegations concerning arbitrary FOIA violations. OSC declined, citing the absence of specific judicial findings required by a different FOIA enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

The district court found that OSC had misinterpreted its statutory authority; Section 1216 did indeed empower OSC to investigate independent of the Section 552 triggers. However, the lower court ultimately dismissed the case, viewing the decision to pursue corrective action as discretionary even if the investigation itself was mandatory. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit bypassed this statutory interpretation entirely. Instead, Judge Katsas zeroed in on a threshold jurisdictional defect that neither party had initially briefed: AFL simply had no stake in the outcome sufficient to invoke federal judicial power.

## The Fallacy of Procedural Injury Without Concrete Harm

The core of Judge Katsas's analysis rests on the Supreme Court's admonition in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation... is insufficient" for standing. AFL attempted to navigate around this hurdle by invoking the "relaxed" standing standards often applied in procedural injury cases. Under precedents like *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, plaintiffs alleging a violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest face less stringent requirements regarding imminence and redressability. However, as the D.C. Circuit clarified, this relaxation does not eliminate the need for an underlying concrete interest or causation.

AFL proposed two theories of injury, both of which failed to clear the constitutional bar. First, they argued that OSC's failure to investigate prevented them from receiving the DOJ records they sought via FOIA. The court swiftly dismantled this causal chain as overly speculative. Any informational injury regarding the DOJ records was directly caused by the DOJ, not OSC. To link OSC's inaction to the non-production of records required assuming a multi-step domino effect: OSC would investigate, then choose to seek corrective action, then the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would sanction employees, and finally, DOJ would release the records. As Judge Katsas noted, relying on the "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts"—specifically OSC's enforcement discretion and MSPB's adjudicatory powers—renders causation too attenuated for Article III. A favorable ruling against OSC would not bind DOJ or MSPB, meaning it could not guarantee redress.

Second, AFL claimed an informational injury based on being denied updates about an OSC investigation itself. This argument collapsed because no statute entitled AFL to receive such information during a Section 1216 inquiry. While Section 1214 mandates notifications for "prohibited personnel practice" investigations, Section 1216 investigations into FOIA violations do not carry identical procedural guarantees Courts have previously recognized that applying Section 1214's notice requirements to Section 1216 investigations is within OSC's discretion. Because AFL had no legal entitlement to the information it claimed was withheld, it suffered no cognizable injury when OSC refused to provide it. You cannot be injured by being denied something you have no right to receive, regardless of whether an agency violated a duty to act internally.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* serves as a potent check on attempts to transform general statutory mandates into private rights of action through creative pleading strategies litigants increasingly argue that whenever Congress uses mandatory language like "shall investigate," it implicitly creates a procedural right enforceable by any interested party willing to file suit so long as they can frame their grievance as "informational."
