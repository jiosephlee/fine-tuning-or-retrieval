Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Speculative Chain: D.C. Circuit Tightens Causation Standards in Enforcement Suits

In the increasingly crowded arena of administrative law litigation, public interest groups often attempt to use statutory enforcement mechanisms as a lever to pry open government records or force policy changes. The logic is seductive: if an agency fails to act, a watchdog group can sue to compel that action, hoping the resulting investigation will yield the substantive relief the group originally sought. On October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit firmly slammed the door on this strategy in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas held that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacked Article III standing to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations by the Department of Justice. While the underlying dispute concerned email searches and transparency, the opinion's true significance lies in its rigorous application of causation and redressability doctrines. By deploying recent Supreme Court precedent regarding "unfettered choices" by independent actors, the court signaled that the causal chains required to establish standing in third-party enforcement suits must be far more direct than previously assumed by many litigants.

The factual backdrop involves AFL's request for DOJ emails related to judicial recusals. When DOJ produced some records but maintained a policy of not searching individual employee emails without consent, AFL pivoted. Rather than continuing to litigate solely against DOJ under FOIA, AFL petitioned the OSC to investigate the agency's withholding policy as an "arbitrary or capricious" violation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The OSC declined, citing the specific triggers for mandatory investigation found in FOIA's own enforcement provision, which had not been met. The district court initially agreed with AFL that the OSC had misread its statutory authority but ultimately dismissed the case, viewing the decision to investigate as discretionary. On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the statutory interpretation question entirely, grounding its dismissal in the threshold requirement of Article III standing.

Judge Katsas's analysis dismantles AFL's standing theories by focusing intensely on the break in the causal chain between the requested judicial relief and the alleged injury. AFL argued that if the court ordered an OSC investigation, that investigation would likely lead to disciplinary proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which would in turn pressure DOJ into releasing the withheld emails. The court characterized this sequence as a series of speculations about the decisions of independent actors not before the court. Relying heavily on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024), Judge Katsas emphasized that causation cannot rest on predictions of how non-parties—here, both the OSC and the MSPB—might exercise their discretion. Even if a statute mandates an investigation, it rarely mandates a specific outcome of that investigation. Therefore, a court order compelling an inquiry does not guarantee, or even make "very likely," the production of documents by a separate executive branch component.

This reasoning marks a notable tightening of the "relaxed" standing standards often applied in procedural injury cases. Traditionally, courts have allowed plaintiffs alleging a violation of a procedural right to clear hurdles regarding imminence and redressability more easily, provided they could show a concrete interest threatened by the procedure's absence. However, *Greer* suggests that this relaxation has limits when the procedural right is merely a step removed from the substantive harm. The court rejected AFL's attempt to conflate a right to an investigation with a right to information. Because section 1216 creates no entitlement for complainants to receive specific data or notifications during an investigation—unlike distinct provisions governing prohibited personnel practices under section 1214—AFL could not demonstrate a concrete informational injury traceable to OSC's inaction. The opinion makes clear that a "procedural right in vacuo," divorced from a guaranteed concrete outcome, is insufficient to sustain jurisdiction.

The doctrinal implications of *Greer* extend well beyond FOIA disputes or the specific mechanics of Civil Service Reform Act enforcement. The decision serves as a potent warning to public interest litigants who view agency investigations as a proxy for obtaining underlying substantive benefits. In an era where plaintiffs frequently seek to compel regulatory action against third parties (such as banks, hospitals, or other agencies) to achieve their own policy goals, *Greer* reinforces that Article III demands a tight nexus between judicial intervention and injury redressal. If achieving relief requires speculating about how an investigator will weigh evidence, how an adjudicator will rule on sanctions, or how a regulated entity will react to potential penalties, the chain is too attenuated to support standing. This approach aligns with a broader trend in recent jurisprudence toward restricting access to federal courts for generalized grievances masquerading as specific legal injuries.

Furthermore, Judge Katsas's reliance on *Haaland v. Brackeen* underscores that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before it, not on whether its rationale might persuade outsiders. This distinction is crucial for separation-of-powers concerns; it prevents federal judges from issuing advisory opinions that effectively manage inter-agency dynamics without actual authority over all relevant players. By vacating the district court's judgment and ordering dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, the D.C. Circuit has raisedthe bar for future suits attempting to weaponize oversight statutes like section 1216 Litigants must now demonstrate not just that an agency failed to follow procedure, but that correcting that failure would directly and predictably cure their specific injury without relying on intervening acts of discretion by other branches or bureaus Of government .
