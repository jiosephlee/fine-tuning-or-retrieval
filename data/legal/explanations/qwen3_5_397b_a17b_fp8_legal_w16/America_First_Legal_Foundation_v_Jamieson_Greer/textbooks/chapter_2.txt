Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # The Threshold Question: Article III Standing Doctrine

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals resolved the dispute without ever addressing whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) correctly interpreted its statutory duties under the Civil Service Reform Act or the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). Instead, the court focused entirely on a threshold question: whether the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation (AFL), possessed the constitutional authority to sue in federal court at all. This approach illustrates a fundamental principle of federal litigation: before a court can examine the merits of a legal claim, it must first confirm that it has subject-matter jurisdiction. In this case, the absence of Article III standing meant the district court lacked the power to hear the case, rendering any discussion of the underlying statutes moot. By vacating the district court's judgment on the merits and ordering a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction, the appellate court reinforced that standing is not merely a pleading formality but a constitutional limitation on the judicial branch.

## The Case-or-Controversy Requirement and Subject Matter Jurisdiction

The starting point for understanding this opinion is Article III of the Constitution, which limits the "judicial Power" of federal courts to resolving actual "Cases" and "Controversies." As Judge Katsas explains, standing doctrine flows directly from this restriction. It serves as a gatekeeping mechanism to ensure that federal courts do not issue advisory opinions or resolve abstract disagreements between citizens and the government. When a plaintiff files suit, they bear the burden of demonstrating that their case fits within this constitutional framework. If they cannot, the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, meaning it has no legal authority to proceed regardless of how strong the plaintiff's legal arguments might be. In *AFL v. Greer*, this jurisdictional defect was fatal; because AFL could not demonstrate standing, the appellate court was compelled to dismiss the case without reaching the substantive questions regarding OSC's investigative obligations.

## The Three Elements of Standing: Injury, Causation, and Redressability

To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must satisfy three irreducible elements derived from Supreme Court precedent, particularly *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*. First, the plaintiff must have suffered an "injury in fact," defined as an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent rather than conjectural. Second, there must be causation; the injury must be fairly traceable to the challenged action of the defendant. Third, there must be redressability; it must be likely, not merely speculative, that a favorable court decision will remedy the injury. The *AFL* opinion applies these elements rigorously to dismantle AFL's theories of harm. The court emphasized that at the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege specific facts plausibly supporting each of these three prongs. Failure to meet any single element destroys standing and deprives the court of jurisdiction.

## Procedural Injury vs. Substantive Injury: The 'Relaxed' Standard

A central tension in this case involves how standing requirements apply when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a procedural right granted by statute rather than a direct substantive harm. AFL argued that OSC violated its statutory right to an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In such scenarios, courts often apply a "relaxed" standard regarding imminence and redressability. As noted in *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, when a procedural right is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest, the strictness of proving immediate harm or guaranteed relief is somewhat reduced. However, Judge Katsas clarifies that this relaxation is not total elimination; even under this lenient framework, a plaintiff must still show that the procedural violation resulted in injury to some concrete, particularized interest. Furthermore, while imminence and redressability hurdles may be lower for procedural claims, causation remains stringent: there must still be sufficient proof linking defendants' actions to any resulting harm or future risk thereof due solely upon non-compliance with said procedure(s). Ultimately though—even applying relaxed standards—AFL failed both tests since neither claimed injury met basic thresholds necessary per established jurisprudence governing similar situations where plaintiffs assert purely technical breaches absent tangible consequences flowing immediately thereafter from those alleged infractions alone!
