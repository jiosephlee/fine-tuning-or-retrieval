Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 1: # Statutory Framework and Procedural History

To understand the ultimate disposition of *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, one must first grasp the complex statutory architecture governing the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and its intersection with the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The case does not turn on whether the Department of Justice (DOJ) actually violated FOIA, but rather on whether the OSC possessed a mandatory duty to investigate alleged violations and whether the plaintiff had standing to enforce that duty. The opinion begins by delineating the distinct powers granted to the OSC under the Civil Service Reform Act and contrasting them with the specific triggers required for OSC involvement under FOIA itself. This statutory landscape sets the stage for the procedural conflict: America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel an investigation based on one statutory provision, while the OSC relied on another to decline action. The district court's subsequent handling of this dispute—finding a legal error yet refusing to mandate relief—creates the unique procedural posture that led the Court of Appeals to pivot from the merits to the threshold question of Article III standing.

## The Dual Roles of the Office of Special Counsel: Sections 1214, 1215, and 1216

The Civil Service Reform Act establishes the OSC as an independent investigative and prosecutorial agency tasked with maintaining a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The opinion highlights two primary avenues through which the OSC exercises this authority. First, under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 and § 1215, the OSC investigates "prohibited personnel practices." When such practices are found, the OSC may seek corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) or pursue disciplinary action against individual employees. Crucially, Section 1214 includes specific procedural safeguards for complainants, mandating that the OSC notify the complaining party regarding the progress of an investigation. These notifications create a tangible flow of information between the agency and the whistleblower during the investigatory phase.

Second, Section 1216 expands the OSC's reach beyond traditional personnel practices to include other forms of misconduct. Specifically, Section 1216(a)(3) commands that the OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" into allegations concerning the "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under [FOIA]." Once an investigation is underway under this section, Section 1216(c) authorizes the OSC to seek corrective and disciplinary action using the same mechanisms found in Sections 1214 and 1215 "as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The tension in this case arises from interpreting whether Section 1216 operates as a standalone mandate requiring investigation upon mere allegation, or whether its execution is constrained by other statutory limitations regarding when such investigations are appropriate.

## The Intersection of FOIA and OSC Enforcement: Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

While Section 1216 grants broad investigatory power over FOIA violations, FOIA itself contains a narrower, specific provision governing OSC involvement found at 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This section outlines a strict three-part predicate that must be met before the OSC is required to initiate disciplinary proceedings. Under this provision, the OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding" only if a court has: (1) ordered the production of improperly withheld records; (2) awarded attorneys' fees against the agency; and (3) specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in its withholding.

The interplay between Section 1216's command to "conduct an investigation" and Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s specific triggers formed the crux of the legal disagreement between AFL and the OSC. The OSC interpreted its authority narrowly, arguing that because AFL had not secured a court order satisfying all three predicates of Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), there was no basis for intervention. Conversely, AFL argued that Section 1216 created an independent, mandatory duty to investigate arbitrary withholdings regardless of whether a court had already adjudicated those specific elements. Understanding this statutory friction is essential, as it defines exactly what AFL claimed it was denied: not necessarily immediate disclosure of documents from DOJ, but rather an official government inquiry into DOJ's conduct that might lead to such disclosure or employee discipline.

## Factual Background: AFL's FOIA Request Regarding DOJ Email Searches

The factual genesis of this litigation lies in AFL's efforts to investigate judicial recusals. To support this project, AFL submitted a FOIA request to DOJ seeking emails exchanged between an outside attorney and officials within DOJ's Civil Division. According to AFL's complaint in both administrative appeals and subsequent lawsuits, DOJ denied parts of this request based on an internal policy prohibiting searches of employee emails without prior consent from those employees. AFL characterized this policy as arbitrary and capricious under FOIA standards because it effectively immunized potentially responsive communications from discovery simply due to employee non-consent.
