Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the D.C. Circuit raise Article III standing sua sponte after the district court already ruled on the merits?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The district court dismissed AFL's claims on the merits (failure to state a claim), and neither party initially briefed standing in the appeal. Yet, the appellate court ordered supplemental briefing on standing and ultimately vacated the judgment for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Under what circumstances can an appellate court ignore the merits and decide standing on its own? Does *Steel Co.* require this even if the lower court already spent resources deciding the case?
Answer:
An appellate court not only *can* but **must** raise Article III standing *sua sponte* (on its own initiative) at any stage of the litigation, even after the district court has ruled on the merits. This is because standing is a component of **subject-matter jurisdiction**, which cannot be waived by the parties and cannot be conferred by their silence or agreement.

Here is the breakdown of why the D.C. Circuit acted as it did in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, grounded strictly in the text of the opinion you provided:

### 1. Standing is Jurisdictional, Not Merits-Based
The core reason the court ignored the merits analysis performed by the district court is found in Section II of the opinion. Judge Katsas writes: "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction."

Under federal law, if a plaintiff lacks standing, the federal courts lack the constitutional power ("judicial Power") to hear the case at all. As the opinion notes, citing *Steel Co.*, this restriction flows directly from Article III's "Case" or "Controversy" requirement. Because subject-matter jurisdiction is a threshold requirement, a judgment issued by a court without jurisdiction is void. Therefore, an appellate court has an independent obligation to ensure jurisdiction exists before it can validate any lower court ruling, regardless of whether the parties raised the issue.

### 2. The Parties Cannot Waive Jurisdictional Defects
You noted that neither party initially briefed standing. This is irrelevant to the appellate court's duty. As stated in the opinion, standing determines whether the district court had authority to act. Since parties cannot consent to federal jurisdiction where none exists, they cannot waive a standing defect by failing to raise it. Consequently, when the D.C. Circuit realizedstanding was unresolved, it was procedurally required to halt its review of the merits and order supplemental briefing to resolve the jurisdictional question first.

### 3. Application to *AFL v. Greer*
In this specific case, once the court analyzed the supplemental briefing, it determined that AFL failed to meet the *Lujan* test for standing (injury, causation, and redressability).
*   **Causation/Redressability:** The court found AFL's theory that an OSC investigation would lead to DOJ releasing records was too speculative. It relied on a chain of events involving independent actors (OSC, MSPB, DOJ) making discretionary choices. The opinion cites *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* to note that causation cannot rely on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."
*   **Informational Injury:** The court rejected AFL's claim to an informational injury because no statute entitled them to receive specific information from OSC during a § 1216 investigation; any such notice was discretionary under § 1214.

### Conclusion: The Procedural Result
Because AFL lacked standing, the district court never possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The proper remedy when a lack of jurisdiction is discovered on appeal is not to affirm or reverse based on the merits, but to **vacate** the lower court's judgment and remand with instructions to **dismiss** for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

This explains why the D.C. Circuit "ignored" the district court's extensive work on failure-to-state-a-claim: legally speaking, that work was undertaken without authority. As Judge Katsas concludes in Section III, "Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court's judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction."

### How does the 'relaxed' standing test for procedural injuries differ from standard standing, and why did AFL still fail?
Question:
The opinion mentions that when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest, courts 'relax' the requirements for imminence and redressability. However, the court says AFL failed even this relaxed standard. What exactly is the difference between proving injury/causation under the standard *Lujan* test versus this 'procedural injury' framework? Specifically, why wasn't the statutory mandate that OSC 'shall... conduct an investigation' enough to satisfy the injury element?
Answer:
The "relaxed" standing test for procedural injuries does not lower the bar for **injury-in-fact** or **causation**; rather, it only eases the requirements for **imminence** and **redressability**.

Under the standard *Lujan* test, a plaintiff must prove that their injury is actual or imminent and that a favorable court decision is likely to redress that injury. When a plaintiff alleges a violation of a **procedural right** granted by statute to protect a concrete interest, courts recognize that the injury occurs immediately upon the denial of the procedure itself. Consequently, the plaintiff does not need to prove that the ultimate harm they sought to avoid (e.g., environmental damage) is "imminent," nor do they need to prove with absolute certainty that following the procedure would have changed the outcome (*redressability*).

However, as the D.C. Circuit clarifies in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, this relaxation **"not wholly eliminat[es]"** the requirements of injury-in-fact or causation. The plaintiff must still demonstrate:
1.  A concrete, particularized interest that the procedure was designed to protect; and
2.  That the deprivation of the procedural right actually resulted in an injury to that concrete interest (causation).

### Why AFL Failed Even Under the Relaxed Standard

AFL failed because it could not bridge the gap between OSC's failure to investigate and any concrete injury to AFL. The court rejected both of AFL's theories of injury:

**1. Failure of Causation (The FOIA Records Theory)**
AFL argued that if OSC had investigated DOJ's FOIA compliance, DOJ might have eventually released the emails AFL wanted. The court found this causal chain too speculative to satisfy Article III, even under a relaxed standard.
*   **The Speculative Chain:** The court noted that establishing causation would require speculating on multiple independent actions: OSC choosing to pursue enforcement, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) choosing to sanction employees, and DOJ subsequently choosing to release records.
*   **Independent Actors:** Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the opinion emphasizes that causation cannot rely on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Because OSC's investigation does not bind DOJ or the MSPB to release records, AFL could not show that its inability to get the FOIA records was *caused* by OSC's inaction.

**2. Failure of Concrete Injury (The Informational Rights Theory)**
AFL alternatively argued it was injured by not receiving updates and notices regarding an OSC investigation.
*   **No Statutory Entitlement:** To have informational standing, a plaintiff must be denied information they are statutorily entitled to receive (*ASPCA v. Feld Ent.*).
*   **Discretionary Procedures:** While 5 U.S.C. § 1216 mandates an *investigation*, it does not mandate specific *notice procedures*. Those notice requirements exist under § 1214 for "prohibited personnel practices," but applying them to § 1216 FOIA investigations is discretionary (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*).
*   **Result:** Since AFL had no legal entitlement to receive notices during a § 1216 investigation, being denied those notices constituted no concrete injury. As the court stated, a "procedural right in vacuo"—without a concrete interest affected—is insufficient for standing (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*).

### Summary
The statutory mandate that OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" created a procedural duty, but it did not create a **right to a specific outcome** (release of documents) or a **right to specific information** (investigative updates). Without a concrete interest tied directly to the procedure itself, or a non-speculative link between the procedure and a tangible harm, AFL could not satisfy the irreducible minimums of injury and causation required by Article III.

### Why was the causal chain between OSC's inaction and DOJ's withholding considered too 'speculative' to support standing?
Question:
AFL argued that if OSC had investigated, they might have sanctioned DOJ employees, which might have compelled DOJ to release the records. The court rejected this as too attenuated because it relies on the choices of independent actors (MSPB and DOJ). How does this application of *Heckler v. Chaney* (agency discretion) and *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (independent actors) work in practice? At what point does a 'likely' outcome become mere 'speculation' in a FOIA enforcement context?
Answer:
The court in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* found the causal chain too speculative because establishing standing required the plaintiff (AFL) to rely on a series of hypothetical decisions by independent actors who were not parties to the lawsuit. Under Article III, a plaintiff must demonstrate that their injury is "fairly traceable" to the defendant's conduct and likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. Here, the chain of events necessary to cure AFL's injury (withholding of FOIA records) broke down at multiple points due to agency discretion and independent choice.

**The Speculative Causal Chain**
The opinion outlines a four-step sequence that AFL needed to prove was likely to occur if OSC had investigated:
1.  **OSC Discretion:** If OSC investigated, it *might* have chosen to pursue corrective or disciplinary action against DOJ employees. The court notes that courts generally cannot compel how an agency exercises its enforcement discretion, citing *Heckler v. Chaney*.
2.  **MSPB Independence:** If OSC pursued action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB)—an independent agency not before the court—*might* have decided to sanction the employees.
3.  **DOJ Choice:** If sanctions were imposed, DOJ *might* have decided to produce the records.
4.  **Redress:** Only then would AFL receive the records.

The court held that this chain relies on "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit (the MSPB and DOJ)." Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the opinion reinforces that causation cannot rest on the "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Because a judgment against OSC would not bind DOJ or the MSPB, there is no guarantee that compelling an investigation would result in the release of documents. As the court stated, redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not on whether it might "persuade" non-parties to act differently.

**Application of *Heckler* and *Alliance***
In practice, these doctrines create a high bar for standing when a plaintiff seeks to force one agency to regulate another:
*   ***Heckler v. Chaney*** applies because even if OSC were forced to investigate, the decision to actually bring enforcement proceedings remains within OSC's prosecutorial discretion. The court cannot assume OSC would take the specific step of seeking sanctions.
*   ***FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*** applies because the ultimate relief (document production) depends on the voluntary compliance of DOJ or an adjudicatory ruling by the MSPB. Since neither entity is bound by the litigation between AFL and OSC, any prediction that they would act in a specific way is constitutionally impermissible speculation.

**When "Likely" Becomes "Speculative"**
In this FOIA enforcement context, an outcome shifts from "likely" to "speculative" when the path to redress requires predicting how independent third parties will exercise their own statutory authority or discretion. Even if it is plausible that an investigation *could* lead to document production, it fails the standing test if it requires assuming that multiple distinct agencies will all make specific, favorable choices in sequence. Because AFL could not show that a victory against OSC would "very likely spur the next steps necessary" to get the records from DOJ, the causal link was severed.

### What distinguishes an 'informational injury' based on a statutory entitlement from a mere hope of receiving information during an investigation?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's second theory: that they were injured by not getting updates/notices from OSC. The opinion states that informational standing requires being denied information a statute 'entitled him to receive.' Since § 1216 mandates an investigation but doesn't explicitly mandate notice to the complainant (unlike § 1214), AFL had no entitlement. Is there any scenario where a plaintiff has standing to force an agency to investigate something just to get status updates, or must the underlying statute explicitly create a right to that specific information?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the distinction between a cognizable "informational injury" and a mere hope of receiving information turns entirely on whether the underlying statute creates a **specific legal entitlement** to that information.

### The Legal Standard: Statutory Entitlement vs. Discretion
To establish standing based on an informational injury, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they were denied information that a statute **"entitled [them] to receive."** *Am. Soc'y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoted in *AFL*).

The court clarifies that a statutory mandate for an agency to *act* (e.g., conduct an investigation) does not automatically create a statutory right for the complainant to *receive updates* about that action. If the statute grants the agency discretion regarding whether to provide notice or information, the plaintiff has no legally protected interest in receiving it, and therefore suffers no concrete injury when it is withheld.

### Application in *AFL v. Greer*
The court applied this distinction to reject AFL's second theory of standing, which claimed injury from not receiving notices or status updates from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

1.  **The Mandate to Investigate (§ 1216):** The court acknowledged that 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) might mandate OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, the text of § 1216 contains **no provision entitling a complainant to receive information** or notices regarding the progress of such an investigation.
2.  **The Contrast with § 1214:** The opinion explicitly contrasts § 1216 with § 1214 (which governs "prohibited personnel practices"). Section 1214 includes specific procedural provisions requiring OSC to notify the complaining party about the investigation's progress. See *AFL* slip op. at I.A ("Section 1214 sets forth procedures... including provisions for notifying the complaining party").
3.  **Discretionary Nature of Notice:** Because § 1216 lacks the explicit notice requirements found in § 1214, OSC's decision to provide updates during a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. The court notes that AFL itself acknowledged case law holding that OSC's decision to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary." *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011).

### Answering Your Specific Question
You asked if there is any scenario where a plaintiff has standing to force an investigation just to get status updates, or if the statute must explicitly create that right.

Under *AFL*, **the underlying statute must explicitly create (or clearly imply) a right to that specific information.**

*   **No Standing via Derivative Hope:** You cannot gain standing to force an investigation merely because, *if* the investigation occurred, the agency *might* choose to send you updates. As the court states, "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury."
*   **The Requirement:** To have standing based on an informational interest in the "fruits of the investigation," the plaintiff must point to a statutory clause that says the agency *must* provide that information to them. Without such an entitlement, the desire for updates remains a "mere hope," which is insufficient for Article III standing.

In short, a statutory command to *investigate* is not enough; there must be a separate statutory command (or clear implication) that the agency must *inform* the plaintiff about that investigation.

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) create a private right of action for citizens to compel OSC investigations into FOIA violations?
Question:
The text says OSC 'shall... conduct an investigation' of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Usually, mandatory language like 'shall' implies a duty. However, the court implies that even if this duty exists, a private citizen can't sue to enforce it without standing. If AFL lacks standing because they can't show redressability, does that mean no private citizen ever has standing to enforce § 1216(a)(3)? Is this effectively a commitment of enforcement discretion entirely to OSC, similar to prosecutorial discretion?
Answer:
No, the court's finding that *America First Legal Foundation* (AFL) lacked standing does not mean that **no** private citizen can ever have standing to enforce 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Rather, the holding indicates that a plaintiff cannot establish standing based solely on the statutory "shall" language if they cannot demonstrate that a court order compelling an investigation would concretely redress their specific injury.

The opinion clarifies that while § 1216(a)(3) uses mandatory language ("shall ... conduct an investigation"), this creates a duty owed to the public or the statute's scheme, not necessarily an individually enforceable right that bypasses Article III's causation and redressability requirements.

### The Barrier: Causation and Redressability, Not Just Discretion
Your intuition regarding prosecutorial discretion (*Heckler v. Chaney*) is partially correct, but the court's dismissal rests more heavily on the speculative nature of the causal chain required to show **redressability**.

Under *Lujan*, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision is likely to redress their injury. In this case, AFL argued that forcing OSC to investigate would eventually lead to DOJ releasing the withheld FOIA records. The court rejected this because the path from an OSC investigation to record release involves multiple independent actors making discretionary choices:
1.  OSC might investigate but choose **not** to seek corrective action.
2.  Even if OSC seeks action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might **not** sanction the employees.
3.  Even if sanctions occur, DOJ might **still not** release the records.

Judge Katsas writes that this chain relies on "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit" and "how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion." Therefore, AFL failed standing not because § 1216 grants OSC total immunity from suit, but because AFL could not prove that winning the lawsuit would actually get them their documents.

### Could Another Plaintiff Have Standing?
The opinion leaves open the possibility of standing for a different type of plaintiff or under a different theory of injury, provided they can meet the *Lujan* test:

*   **Different Injury:** If a plaintiff could identify an injury that is directly redressable by an OSC investigation (rather than the ultimate release of records), they might survive a motion to dismiss. However, the court also rejected AFL's second theory: that they had an informational interest in the *process* of the investigation itself (e.g., receiving status updates). The court noted that § 1216 does not entitle complainants to the specific notice procedures found in § 1214, and absent a statutory entitlement to receive information from OSC, there is no concrete "informational injury."
*   **Direct Causation:** If a scenario existed where an OSC investigation was the *sole* bottleneck preventing relief, and the outcome of that investigation mechanically triggered a remedy without further discretionary intervening steps, the redressability prong might be satisfied.

### Conclusion: Duty vs. Enforceability
The opinion distinguishes between a statutory duty and judicial enforceability by a private party. The phrase "shall conduct an investigation" imposes an obligation on OSC, but it does not automatically grant every citizen a private right of action enforceable in federal court if that citizen cannot link the breach of duty to a concrete, redressable harm.

Effectively, for most FOIA requesters, § 1216(a)(3) functions similarly to prosecutorial discretion because the mechanism it triggers (internal discipline/corrective action) is too attenuated from the requester's goal (document production) to satisfy Article III standing. However, this is a limitation of **this specific plaintiff's theory of harm**, not an absolute bar declaring that § 1216(a)(3) creates zero justiciable rights for all future litigants under all conceivable factual scenarios.

### Why didn't the existence of a separate FOIA lawsuit against DOJ cure the causation problem regarding OSC's failure to act?
Question:
The facts note that AFL already sued DOJ directly under FOIA and is receiving records. AFL tried to argue that OSC's failure to investigate caused them harm because DOJ hadn't produced everything yet. Why couldn't AFL argue that the OSC investigation was a necessary parallel track to ensure full compliance or prevent future violations? Does the fact that DOJ is already producing records undermine any claim of 'imminent' injury required for standing against OSC?
Answer:
The existence of a separate FOIA lawsuit against the Department of Justice (DOJ) did not cure the causation problem because, under Article III standing doctrine, the plaintiff's inability to obtain records is legally traceable to the agency withholding them (DOJ), not to the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) failure to investigate.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, the D.C. Circuit rejected AFL's argument that an OSC investigation was a necessary parallel track to ensure full compliance. The court held that any "informational injury" stemming from the non-production of records is "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." *Id.* at 10. Even if OSC had initiated an investigation, the chain of events required to actually force DOJ to produce the records was too speculative to satisfy the causation element of standing.

The court outlined why this causal chain fails:
1.  **Discretionary Enforcement:** Even if OSC investigated, it retains discretion over whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action. Courts generally cannot compel an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion in a specific way. *Id.* (citing *Heckler v. Chaney*).
2.  **Independent Actors:** If OSC sought action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would then have to decide whether to sanction employees.
3.  **Speculative Outcome:** Finally, DOJ would have to decide to produce the records based on those potential sanctions.

The opinion emphasizes that causation "cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts," specifically noting that both the MSPB and DOJ were not parties to the suit against OSC. *Id.* at 11 (quoting *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*). Because a favorable judgment against OSC would not bind DOJ or the MSPB, it could not guarantee the production of records. As the court stated, redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not on whether its logic might "persuade actors who are not before the court." *Id.* (quoting *Haaland v. Brackeen*).

Regarding your question about "imminent" injury: The fact that DOJ is already producing records in the separate lawsuit underscores that the mechanism for obtaining relief lies within that direct litigation, not in an OSC investigation. The court found that linking OSC's inaction to any remaining delay in production requires assuming a series of hypothetical decisions by third parties, rendering the injury conjectural rather than actual or imminent for purposes of suing OSC. Consequently, the parallel FOIA suit highlights that OSC is not the proper defendant to remedy the withholding, breaking the causal link required for standing.
