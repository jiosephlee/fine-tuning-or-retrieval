Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Legal Framework: Elements of Securities Fraud

To understand the appellate court's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, one must first grasp the specific statutory architecture governing securities fraud and how the Fifth Circuit interprets its core components. The dispute in this case did not turn on whether the defendants made false statements—they conceded that point—but rather on whether those statements constituted a crime under the specific legal definitions of a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud." The district court had dismissed the indictment based on a narrow reading of what constitutes property loss, specifically worrying that the defendants only deprived victims of information rather than tangible assets. The Court of Appeals reversed this dismissal by applying a rigorous framework derived from the text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and recent Supreme Court precedents clarifying the boundaries of federal fraud statutes. This chapter breaks down that framework, explaining how the court moves from statutory language to the ultimate conclusion that inducing investors to part with money through lies satisfies the elements of securities fraud, even if the investors receive securities of equivalent market value in return.

## Statutory Language of 18 U.S.C. § 1348

The foundation of the prosecution in *Constantinescu* rests on 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the primary federal criminal statute prohibiting securities fraud. The statute is broad in its scope but precise in its prohibitions. It criminalizes two distinct types of conduct executed knowingly and intentionally. First, it prohibits executing a "scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with" the purchase or sale of any security. Second, it prohibits obtaining money or property "by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises" in connection with such transactions. In *Constantinescu*, the government relied on both prongs, alleging that the defendants orchestrated a classic "pump and dump" operation where they purchased securities, inflated their prices through deceptive social media posts, and then sold them for a profit. The statutory language is critical because it ties the fraudulent conduct directly to the transaction itself; the deception must occur "in connection with" the purchase or sale. The district court's error stemmed from misinterpreting what kind of deprivation satisfies this statute, mistakenly believing that unless the victim lost net wealth, no "property" had been obtained in violation of the act. The appellate court's analysis begins by reaffirming that the statute protects against the deprivation of money or property through deception, regardless of the subsequent economic outcome for the victim.

## Integration of Mail and Wire Fraud Concepts into Securities Fraud

When interpreting § 1348, courts often face a scarcity of circuit-specific precedent directly addressing every nuance of securities fraud. As noted in *Constantinescu*, there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." To fill this gap, the Fifth Circuit looks to the lineage of federal fraud laws. Section 1348 explicitly borrows key concepts from the mail fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1341) and wire fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1342) statutes. Consequently, judicial interpretations of mail and wire fraud are highly persuasive and often controlling when analyzing securities fraud charges. This integration allows courts to apply decades of developed jurisprudence regarding what constitutes a "scheme" and what qualifies as an "intent to defraud." In *Constantinescu*, Judge Engelhardt relies heavily on this cross-statutory pollination, citing cases like *United States v. Greenlaw* and *United States v. Evans* which originally addressed mail or wire fraud but established principles now applicable to securities fraud. By treating these statutes as a cohesive family of laws, the court ensures consistency in how federal fraud is prosecuted across different mediums—whether via postal service, electronic transmission, or stock market manipulation. This approach prevents defendants from exploiting technical differences between statutes to escape liability for fundamentally similar deceptive conduct.

## Element One: Defining a 'Scheme to Defraud' via Material Misrepresentation

The first substantive hurdle in a securities fraud prosecution is establishing a "scheme to defraud." Under Fifth Circuit precedent adopted in *Constantinescu*, this element requires more than just telling a lie; it requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others for the purpose of obtaining something of value, typically money, from the deceived party. The concept hinges on causation: the deception must be designed to induce the victim to act in a way they otherwise would not have, specifically resulting in the transfer of assets. In *Constantinescu*, defendants argued that their scheme only deprived followers of accurate economic information—the ability to make informed trading decisions—rather than their money itself.
