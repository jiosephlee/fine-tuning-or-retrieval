Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 4: # The Intent Element: Profit Motive Versus Victim Economic Loss

The second pillar of the defendants' challenge in *United States v. Constantinescu* concerns the mental state required for securities fraud. Specifically, the defendants conceded that they engaged in deception but argued that the indictment failed to allege the requisite "intent to defraud." Their theory rested on a narrow interpretation of harm: they claimed that because their goal was self-enrichment through a "pump and dump" scheme, rather than the specific intention to leave their followers with a net financial loss, the element of intent was missing. They posited that since the followers received securities in exchange for their money—assets that presumably held some market value—the transaction lacked the harmful intent necessary for criminal fraud. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, relying heavily on recent Supreme Court precedent to clarify that the federal fraud statutes focus on the defendant's objective to obtain property through deception, not on whether the defendant specifically intended to impoverish the victim.

## Supreme Court Precedent: *Kousisis v. United States* and the Net Pecuniary Loss Rule

To resolve the dispute over intent, the court turned to *Kousisis v. United States*, a 2025 Supreme Court decision that directly addressed whether a defendant must intend to cause a victim's economic downfall to be convicted of fraud. In *Kousisis*, the defendant secured government contracts by lying about his compliance with subcontracting requirements for disadvantaged businesses. Although he fulfilled the contractual obligations using a "pass-through" entity, thereby providing the government with the services it paid for, he was still charged with fraud. The Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. Instead, liability attaches when a defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether the fraudster provides something of equal value in return. This ruling established that "net pecuniary loss" is not an element of the offense. In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit applied this principle directly, noting that the defendants' argument—that they intended only to profit, not to harm—mirrored the failed argument in *Kousisis*. The court made clear that under current law, a defendant's desire for personal gain satisfies the intent requirement even if the victim suffers no ultimate financial deficit.

## Decoupling Defendant Gain from Victim Loss in Fraud Analysis

The core legal lesson from *Constantinescu* is the decoupling of the defendant's gain from the victim's loss in the analysis of fraudulent intent. Historically, some defenses have attempted to link these two concepts, suggesting that if a victim receives fair market value, there can be no fraudulent intent because there is no corresponding injury. The Fifth Circuit dismantled this linkage by emphasizing that the statutory text focuses on the *method* of acquisition (deception) and the *object* of the scheme (money or property), not the final balance sheet of the victim. In the context of a "pump and dump" scheme, the defendants argued their intent was merely to inflate a stock price to sell their own shares, not to ensure their followers lost money on theirs. However, following *Kousisis*, the court reasoned that this distinction is legally irrelevant. The indictment alleged that defendants used false representations to induce followers to part with their money. Because obtaining that money was an "object" of their fraud, the intent element is satisfied. The law punishesthe deceptive means used to separate a victim from their property; it does not require prosecutors to prove that separating them from that property was done with malice toward their overall wealth.

## 'Something of Equal Value' Defense: Why Receiving Securities Does Not Negate Fraud

A critical component of this analysis involves addressing what might be called the "something of equal value" defense. Defendants often argue that because they delivered goods or services—in this case, shares of stock—together with taking money via lies (misrepresentations about trading positions), no fraud occurred because it wasn't theft; it was just bad business advice wrapped in hyperbole or falsity. The court explicitly rejected this notion by citing *Kousisis*, which confirmed that providing something in return does not negate fraud if inducement relied upon material falsehoods ("regardless").
