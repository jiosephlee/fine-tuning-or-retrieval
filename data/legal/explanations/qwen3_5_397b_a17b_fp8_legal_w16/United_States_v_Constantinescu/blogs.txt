# From 'Right-to-Control' to 'Fraudulent Inducement': How *Constantinescu* Navigates the Post-*Ciminelli* Landscape

The rise of "finfluencers"—social media personalities who command massive followings of retail investors—has created a fertile ground for securities manipulation, but it has also presented thorny legal questions about the boundaries of federal fraud statutes. Last week, in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit provided a crucial roadmap for prosecutors navigating this new terrain in the wake of the Supreme Court's recent contraction of fraud liability. Reversing a district court's dismissal of an indictment against eight defendants accused of a classic "pump and dump" scheme orchestrated via social media, the court clarified that while the deprivation of valuable economic information is no longer a standalone crime, the fraudulent inducement of voluntary financial transactions remains squarely within the scope of 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The decision serves as a vital corrective to lower courts that may have overread the Supreme Court's skepticism toward expansive fraud theories, reaffirming that tricking a victim into handing over money is still fraud, even if the victim receives something of market value in return.

The facts alleged in *Constantinescu* read like a cautionary tale for the TikTok and X (formerly Twitter) generation. The government accuses the defendants, who cultivated personas as expert traders, of purchasing low-volume securities and then flooding their social media channels with false and misleading representations about those assets' potential. This artificial "pump" induced followers to buy in, driving up the price, at which point the defendants quietly "dumped" their shares for substantial profits—allegedly totaling $114 million. The district court, however, threw out the indictment. Relying on a rigid interpretation of recent Supreme Court precedent, the lower court concluded that the indictment failed because it described a scheme to deprive followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. In essence, the district court viewed the followers' loss as merely informational—they made bad trades because they lacked accurate data—not proprietary.

The Fifth Circuit swiftly corrected this misapplication of law, drawing a sharp doctrinal line between the rejected "right-to-control" theory and the enduring "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The linchpin of the appellate court's analysis was *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023), where the Supreme Court struck down convictions based on the notion that defrauding someone of their right to control their assets through honest information constituted wire fraud. The *Ciminelli* Court held that the right to valuable economic information is not a traditional property interest protected by federal fraud statutes. However, as Judge Engelhardt emphasized for the unanimous panel, *Constantinescu* does not rely on this discredited theory. Instead, the indictment alleges that defendants used material misstatements to trick victims into entering contracts—specifically, buying securities—that required them to part with their money. This is the textbook definition of fraudulent inducement: using deception to cause a victim to voluntarily transfer property. By focusing on the transfer of funds triggered by lies, rather than the abstract loss of informational autonomy, the Fifth Circuit found the indictment comfortably situated within post-*Ciminelli* jurisprudence.

Perhaps more significant than the property interest analysis is the court's handling of the "intent to defraud" element regarding economic harm. The defendants argued that because they intended only to enrich themselves—and not necessarily to inflict a net pecuniary loss on their followers—they lacked the requisite intent to defraud. After all, when a follower buys a stock at an inflated price due to a pump-and-dump scheme, they technically receive a share certificate worth exactly what they paid for it at that moment; their loss is realized only later when the bubble bursts. The Fifth Circuit rejected this semantic gamesmanship by leaning heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, decided just months ago in April 2025. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court made clear that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off in a net sense. Rather, it is sufficient that obtaining money or property was an object of the scheme. As Judge Engelhardt noted, distinguishing between an intent to profit and an intent to harm is a "distinction without a difference" when the method employed is deception designed to extract money from another's pocket. The fraud is complete uponthe induced deprivation under false pretenses, regardless of whetherthe scammer provided goods or services of equivalent market value in exchange.

The doctrinal implications of *Constantinescu* extend well beyond social media trading schemes. By rigorously applying *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit has signaled that whilethe era of expanding fraud liability through novel theories like "right-to-control" is over,the core prohibitions against deceptive acquisition remain robust. # Profit Without Pecuniary Loss: The Decoupling of Victim Harm and Fraudulent Intent After *Kousisis*

In the high-stakes world of social media-driven "pump and dump" schemes, a familiar defense strategy has emerged: argue that because the victims received securities at fair market value, no fraud occurred. The logic suggests that if an influencer tricks followers into buying a stock, but those followers actually receive shares worth the price they paid (albeit temporarily inflated by the very deception), the transaction lacks the requisite harm for criminal liability. On October 2, 2025, the Fifth Circuit decisively rejected this arithmetic approach to fraud in *United States v. Constantinescu*. Relying on the Supreme Court's recent landmark decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, the appellate court clarified that federal fraud statutes focus on the deceptive *means* of acquiring property, not the net economic outcome for the victim. This ruling marks a significant doctrinal shift, effectively decoupling fraudulent intent from the requirement of proven pecuniary loss and closing a potential loophole for modern market manipulation.

The case arose from a sprawling indictment alleging that eight defendants, leveraging massive social media followings, orchestrated a classic pump-and-dump scheme. The government accused them of purchasing securities, posting false and misleading information to artificially inflate prices, and then quietly selling their holdings for substantial profits while their followers bought in at the peak. The district court dismissed the indictment, adopting a narrow view of property rights. It reasoned that because the defendants allegedly sought to deprive followers only of "potentially valuable economic information"—specifically, the truth about the defendants' trading positions—rather than traditional money or property, the indictment failed to state an offense. This reasoning echoed concerns raised in earlier Supreme Court jurisprudence regarding "right-to-control" theories of fraud. However, the Fifth Circuit's reversal underscores a critical distinction between withholding information and actively inducing a transfer of funds through lies.

The core of the Fifth Circuit's analysis rests on distinguishing between two competing theories of fraud: the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023), the Supreme Court struck down convictions based solely on the right-to-control theory, which posited that depriving someone of information needed to make economic decisions constituted fraud even without a direct loss of assets. The *Constantinescu* defendants hoped to ride this wave, arguing their scheme was merely about withholding information. Judge Engelhardt's opinion swiftly dispelled this notion. The court noted that the indictment did not rely on a abstract right to know; rather, it alleged a concrete scheme to use material misrepresentations to trick victims into parting with their money. By framing the charge as fraudulent inducement—where deception is the tool used to secure a contract or transfer—the court found the indictment squarely within the bounds of traditional property protection endorsed in *Ciminelli* and reaffirmed in *Kousisis*.

Where *Constantinescu* breaks new ground, however, is in its application of *Kousisis* to dismantle the "no net loss" defense. The defendants conceded they deceived their followers but argued they lacked "intent to defraud" because their goal was personal enrichment, not inflicting financial injury on their audience. They contended that since their followers received securities (property) in exchange for their cash, and because market forces arguably provided "fair value" at the moment of trade, there was no intent to cause harm. The Fifth Circuit characterized this as a distinction without a difference. Citing *Kousisis*, where the Supreme Court held that fraud is complete upon the deceptive acquisition of money regardless of whether the victim suffers a net pecuniary loss, the court ruled that providing something of equal value in return does not sanitize the deception. As long as obtaining the victim's money was an object of the scheme achieved through material falsehoods, the intent element is satisfied.

This reasoning represents a vital clarification for securities enforcement in the digital age. Prior to *Kousisis*, there was lingering ambiguity about whether prosecutors needed to prove that a defendant specifically intended to leave a victim poorer than before the transaction. In complex markets, especially those driven by hype cycles on platforms like X (formerly Twitter) or Reddit, proving exact dollar-for-dollar loss can be notoriously difficult due to volatility and timing issues. If defendants could escape liability by arguing that victims received "market value" stock—even if that value was artificially manufactured by their own lies—it would create an absurd incentive structure. A manipulator could claim immunity simply because asset prices hadn't crashed before prosecution began. By affirming that intent focuses on the deceptive procurement of funds rather than the final balance sheet of the victim, *Constantinescu* aligns statutory interpretation with economic reality: artificial inflation created by lies is itself a mechanism of theft, irrespective of subsequent market corrections.

The downstream effects of this decision are likely to be profound for both white-collar defense strategies and regulatory enforcement. Defense counsel can no longer credibly argue that a lack of specific intent to impoverish victims negates fraud charges under Sections 1348 or 1349 when fraudulent inducement is present. The focus shifts entirely to whether misrepresentations were made with knowledge of their falsity and whether those misrepresentations drove property transfers away from victims toward perpetrators (or third parties). # Regulating the Influencer Economy: The Expansion of Securities Fraud Liability in the Social Media Age

The line between financial advice, market manipulation, and protected speech has never been blurrier than in the age of the "finfluencer." As social media personalities amass millions of followers, their ability to move markets with a single post has outpaced regulatory clarity. On October 2, 2025, the Fifth Circuit stepped into this fray with *United States v. Constantinescu*, reversing a district court's dismissal of an indictment against a group of influencers accused of orchestrating a massive "pump and dump" scheme. The decision is significant not merely for its outcome, but for how it navigates the treacherous waters left by recent Supreme Court jurisprudence on fraud. By distinguishing between the deprivation of information and the fraudulent inducement of property transfers, the Fifth Circuit signals that traditional fraud doctrines are fully capable of adapting to digital marketplaces, treating follower trust as a mechanism for property transfer rather than a mere exchange of data.

The facts alleged in *Constantinescu* read like a modern cautionary tale for the retail investing boom. The government accuses eight defendants, including prominent social media figures, of purchasing securities, flooding their platforms with false representations about their positions and the assets' potential, and then secretly selling ("dumping") once follower demand artificially inflated the price. The scheme allegedly generated $114 million in profits. Yet, when charged with securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the district court dismissed the indictment. The lower court's reasoning hinged on a narrow view of property rights: it concluded that the defendants merely deprived followers of "potentially valuable economic information," which does not constitute a traditional property interest under current fraud precedents. This ruling threatened to create a safe harbor for digital market manipulators, suggesting that lying to induce a trade might not be criminal if the victim technically received the stock they paid for.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal rests on a precise doctrinal surgery separating two distinct theories of fraud: the rejected "right-to-control" theory and the enduring "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The district court's error lay in conflating these concepts in light of the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the High Court struck down convictions based on the idea that victims have a property right to control their assets through accurate information; depriving someone of that informational control is not enough for federal fraud liability. However, as the Fifth Circuit clarifies in *Constantinescu*, the indictment here did not rely on a right-to-control theory. Instead, it alleged classic fraudulent inducement: using material misstatements to trick victims into entering contracts that require handing over money or property. When an influencer lies about their holdings to convince a follower to buy a stock, they are not just withholding information; they are actively engineering a transfer of capital based on falsehoods. The court correctly identified that while information itself may not be "property" in this context, the money parted with due to deception certainly is.

Perhaps more critically, *Constantinescu* addresses the intent requirement in light of another recent Supreme Court blockbuster, *Kousisis v. United States* (2025). The defendants argued that they lacked an "intent to defraud" because their goal was self-enrichment, not necessarily causing net economic harm to their followers—a common defense in pump-and-dump scenarios where victims might arguably break even or sell before a crash. The Fifth Circuit swiftly dismantled this argument by leaning on *Kousisis*, which held that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. The essence of the crime is the scheme to obtain money or property through deception. Whether the victim receives fair market value in return is irrelevant to the completion of the fraud. By rejecting the notion that profit-seeking without explicit malice negates intent, the court ensures that the statutory focus remains on the integrity of the transaction itself, not a post-hoc accounting of the victim's portfolio performance.

From an analytical perspective, *Constantinescu* represents a necessary stabilization of securities law in the digital era without requiring new legislation. The district court's initial dismissal reflected a growing anxiety among some jurists that expanding fraud liability could chill legitimate speech or penalize hyperbolic marketing common on social media. However, such concerns misunderstand the mechanics of securities fraud. The Fifth Circuit's opinion rightly emphasizes that First Amendment protections do not extend to using speech as an instrumentality to execute a scheme to steal money. By grounding its reasoning firmly in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, panel author Judge Engelhardt avoids creating new law; instead, he applies established principles to a novel factual setting with rigor. The opinion effectively closes a loophole that would have allowed bad actors to rebrand traditional wire fraud as "informational asymmetry" simply because it occurred on Twitter or TikTok rather than via telephone or mail.

The downstream effects of this decision are likely to be profound for both regulators and content creators. For the Department of Justice and the SEC, *Constantinescu* provides a clear roadmap for prosecuting social media-driven market manipulation using existing statutes, reducing reliance on softer enforcement actions or cease-and-desist orders. It validates aggressive indictments so long as they clearly allege fraudulent inducement rather than vague deprivations of information control. For influencers and financial commentators, the ruling serves as a stark boundary marker: holding oneself out as a trader and soliciting investment creates fiduciary-adjacent risks under criminal law when coupled with deceptive practices. While honest analysis—even if wrong—remains protected, manufacturing artificial demand through lies about one's own position crosses squarely into criminal territory.

Ultimately, *United States v. Constantinescu* affirms that technology changesthe medium of fraud but not its nature. The Fifth Circuit recognized that behind every click, like, and share in a pump-and-dump scheme lies a tangible transfer of wealth procured by deceit. By refusing to let digital novelty obscure traditional property rights, the court has reinforcedthe principle that federal fraud statutes are flexible enough to police modern markets while remaining tethered to their historical roots protecting money and property from theft by trickery
