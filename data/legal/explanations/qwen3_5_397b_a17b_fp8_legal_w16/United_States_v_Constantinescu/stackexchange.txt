Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the precise legal distinction between the 'right-to-control' theory rejected in *Ciminelli* and the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory upheld in this case?
Question:
I am struggling to understand the line the Fifth Circuit draws here. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court said you can't convict someone just for depriving a victim of valuable economic information needed to make decisions. Here, the defendants allegedly deprived followers of accurate info about stock positions, causing them to buy. The court says this is okay because it's 'fraudulent inducement' to part with money, not just a loss of information control. Since both theories involve lying to influence an economic decision, what specific factual allegation in the indictment saves this from being a forbidden 'right-to-control' case? Is it simply that the victims actually handed over cash, or is there a deeper doctrinal difference regarding what constitutes 'property'?
Answer:
The distinction turns on **what the defendant intended to obtain** through the deception, not merely on the fact that deception influenced an economic decision.

Under the "right-to-control" theory rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), the government attempts to criminalize the deprivation of **informational interests**. In that framework, the "property" right at stake is defined as the victim's intangible right to make informed economic decisions using accurate data. The Supreme Court held this is insufficient because federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests (like money or tangible assets), not the abstract right to control one's assets via information. If the indictment in your case alleged only that the defendants lied to distort the market environment, thereby depriving followers of the *ability* to make good trades, it would fail under *Ciminelli*.

However, the Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* identifies a crucial factual allegation that saves this indictment: the scheme was designed to induce victims to **part with their money**.

The court relies on the "fraudulent-inducement" theory clarified in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025). Under this theory, the fraud is complete when a defendant uses material misrepresentations to trick a victim into entering a transaction that requires handing over money or property. The specific factual allegation in paragraph 13 of the indictment—that defendants posted false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security"—shifts the object of the fraud from *information* to *cash*.

The doctrinal difference is therefore grounded in the definition of "property":
*   **Right-to-Control (Forbidden):** The object of the fraud is the victim's **decision-making process**. The loss is purely informational (e.g., making a bad trade due to bad data).
*   **Fraudulent Inducement (Permitted):** The object of the fraud is the victim's **wallet**. The loss is the transfer of funds itself.

As *Constantinescu* notes, citing *Kousisis*, it does not matter if the victims received securities of equal market value in exchange for their cash, or if they ultimately suffered a net pecuniary loss. The crime lies in obtaining the money through deception. Because the indictment alleges the defendants' goal was to get followers to hand over purchase proceeds (which allowed defendants to "dump" their shares at inflated prices), it alleges a scheme to obtain traditional property (money), distinguishing it clearly from a mere deprivation of economic information.

### How does *Kousisis* redefine 'intent to defraud' when the defendant provides fair market value in exchange for the victim's money?
Question:
The defendants argued they didn't intend to harm anyone because the followers received securities at market value (even if inflated by the pump). The court cites *Kousisis* to say that actual economic loss isn't required. I'm confused about the mental state element here. If I trick you into buying a house for $500k that is objectively worth $500k at that exact moment (but I lied about why I'm selling), have I really 'intended to defraud' you if my only goal was my own commission, not your financial ruin? How does the court reconcile the common law intuition that fraud requires a victim's loss with this new rule that obtaining money via lies is enough, regardless of the exchange's fairness?
Answer:
The short answer to your confusion is that under the Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), the "intent to defraud" in federal fraud statutes focuses on the **method of acquisition** (deception) rather than the **economic outcome** for the victim.

Your hypothetical about selling a house at fair market value via a lie is precisely the scenario *Kousisis* addresses, and under current precedent, that conduct constitutes fraud. Here is how the court in *United States v. Constantinescu* reconciles this with common law intuitions:

### 1. The Shift from "Net Loss" to "Deprivation by Deceit"
Common law fraud often emphasizes damages because civil liability usually requires proof of loss. However, criminal fraud statutes (like 18 U.S.C. § 1348 discussed in *Constantinescu*) protect the integrity of transactions and property rights, not just the victim's balance sheet.

As the *Constantinescu* court explains, citing *Kousisis*:
> "A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he 'schem[es] to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.'"

The court explicitly rejects the argument that providing something of equal value negates the intent to defraud. In *Kousisis*, the defendant provided services (via a pass-through entity) that technically fulfilled the contract requirements, yet he was still liable because he obtained the government's money through material misrepresentations. Similarly, in your house example, even if the buyer gets a house worth $500k, you have still used a lie to induce them to part with their $500k. That deprivation of money via falsehood is the core injury the statute punishes.

### 2. Intent is Defined by the Object of the Scheme
You asked whether intending to make a commission, rather than intending to ruin the victim, satisfies the mental state element. The *Constantinescu* opinion clarifies that if your goal is to obtain money through lies, you possess the requisite intent.

The court notes:
> "Defendants argue they intended to profit—not harm any victim. But here, that is a distinction without a difference. As the indictment alleges, defendants' object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities."

The "intent to defraud" requires only:
1.  An intent to deceive; and
2.  An intent that obtaining the victim's money or property be an object of that deceit.

It does **not** require an specific intent to cause net pecuniary harm. As long as the defendant knows they are lying to get someone else's money (or property), and they succeed in getting it (or attempting to), the mental state is satisfied. The fact that the victim received securities at an inflated—but arguably "market"—price is irrelevant because the market price itself was artificial, created by the defendant's own lies ("pumping").

### 3. Reconciling with Common Law Intuition
The tension you feel stems from viewing fraud strictly as a tort requiring damages. *Constantinescu*, following *Kousisis*, treats fraud as a crime against property rights secured by honest dealings.

The court states:
> "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

In your house analogy: By lying about why you are selling, you have violated the buyer's right to make an informed decision about disposing of their property ($500k). The law views your lie as having stolen their autonomy over their funds, even if they ended up with an asset of equivalent monetary value. In securities cases like *Constantinescu*, this is even more pronounced because the "value" received by the followers was inherently tied to the fraudulent inflation; thus, they were deprived of both their money and honest market participation.

**Conclusion:** You do not need to intend for the victim to lose money overall; you only need to intend to use deception as the mechanism to transfer their money or property to yourself (or a third party). If you trick someone into handing over cash—even for a fair trade—you have intended to defraud them within the meaning of these statutes.

### Why did the District Court dismiss the indictment initially, and what standard of review allowed the Fifth Circuit to easily reverse?
Question:
The opinion mentions the District Court dismissed because the indictment failed to allege a deprivation of a 'traditional property interest.' Since we review indictments *de novo* and accept all allegations as true, wasn't it obvious to the lower court that 'information' isn't property? I'm trying to understand the procedural posture: Did the District Court interpret recent Supreme Court cases (*Ciminelli*) too broadly by assuming any scheme involving social media misinformation automatically falls under the banned 'right-to-control' theory? What specific language in the superseding indictment did the District Court seemingly ignore that made reversal so straightforward for the appellate panel?
Answer:
The District Court dismissed the indictment because it incorrectly conflated the "right-to-control" theory rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States* with the "fraudulent-inducement" theory alleged in this case. The Fifth Circuit's reversal was straightforward because, under the *de novo* standard of review, the appellate court was required to accept the indictment's allegations as true, which clearly established a scheme to obtain money rather than merely withhold information.

**The District Court's Error: Misapplying *Ciminelli***
The District Court erred by concluding that the indictment failed to allege a deprivation of a "traditional property interest." It appears the lower court interpreted recent Supreme Court precedent too broadly, assuming that any scheme involving misinformation on social media necessarily relies on the impermissible "right-to-control" theory. Under *Ciminelli*, the government cannot secure a fraud conviction based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information needed to make discretionary decisions (i.e., the right to control one's assets).

However, as the Fifth Circuit noted, the superseding indictment did not rely on this theory. Instead, it alleged a **fraudulent-inducement theory**. The indictment explicitly stated that defendants used false representations to trick followers into *purchasing* securities, thereby inducing them to "part with their money or property." By focusing on the act of inducing a transaction where victims handed over capital, the indictment targeted traditional property interests (money), distinguishing it from the abstract informational harm prohibited by *Ciminelli*.

**The Standard of Review: Why Reversal Was Clear**
The Fifth Circuit applied a **_de novo_** standard of review regarding the sufficiency of the indictment. As outlined in Section II of the opinion, this standard mandates two critical constraints on the reviewing court:
1.  The court must take all factual allegations in the indictment as **true**.
2.  The court need only determine if those accepted allegations state an offense.

Under this lens, the reversal was inevitable. The indictment alleged that defendants "pumped" stock prices via lies so that investors were "induced to purchase," allowing defendants to "dump" shares for a $114 million profit. When accepted as true, these facts describe a classic scheme to obtain money through deception. The District Court effectively ignored these specific allegations of financial transfer, mistakenly treating the case as one involving only informational deprivation. Because the text of the indictment plainly satisfied the elements of securities fraud under a fraudulent-inducement theory—specifically referencing 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and alleging an intent to obtain money—the Fifth Circuit had no discretion but to reverse.

Furthermore, regarding your point on intent: The District Court may have been swayed by arguments that defendants intended only to profit, not to harm victims financially (since victims received securities). However, citing *Kousisis v. United States*, the Fifth Circuit clarified that fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to cause net pecuniary loss or that the victim suffered an economic injury. The intent to deceive to obtain money is sufficient, rendering the defendants' claim that they "only wanted profit" legally irrelevant to the sufficiency of the charge.

### Does this ruling imply that any 'pump and dump' scheme automatically satisfies the 'scheme to defraud' element under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 post-*Ciminelli*?
Question:
The court seems to treat this as a classic fraudulent inducement case where investors were tricked into handing over money. However, in many pump-and-dump scenarios, early buyers might actually profit before the 'dump' occurs. If a follower bought during the 'pump,' saw their portfolio value rise temporarily, and sold before the crash, they suffered no loss and arguably got exactly what they wanted (a quick gain). Under this opinion's logic focusing on the *act* of inducement rather than net pecuniary loss, does every participant who bought based on a lie become a 'victim' for statutory purposes, even if they made money? Where is the limit on liability here?
Answer:
No, the ruling in *United States v. Constantinescu* does not imply that **any** "pump and dump" scheme automatically satisfies the "scheme to defraud" element, nor does it suggest that every participant who buys based on a lie is a statutory victim regardless of context. However, the opinion does clarify that under the **fraudulent-inducement theory**, the government need not prove that a specific investor suffered a net pecuniary loss or that the defendant specifically intended to harm the victim's bottom line.

The court's reasoning rests on two distinct but related points derived from *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*:

### 1. The Theory of Liability Matters
The Fifth Circuit distinguishes between a prohibited "right-to-control" theory and a permissible "fraudulent-inducement" theory.
*   **Right-to-Control (Prohibited):** As noted in *Constantinescu*, you cannot convict someone merely for depriving a victim of "potentially valuable economic information." If the indictment alleged only that followers lost the ability to make informed decisions, it would fail under *Ciminelli*.
*   **Fraudulent Inducement (Permitted):** The indictment in *Constantinescu* survived because it alleged the defendants used material misrepresentations to trick victims into **parting with their money**. The court states: "The indictment alleges a 'fraudulent-inducement theory,' under which a defendant 'uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.'"

Therefore, a "pump and dump" scheme is not automatically actionable; it must be charged and proven as a scheme where the object was to obtain money or property through deception, not merely to distort information flow.

### 2. Net Pecuniary Loss Is Not an Element
Your question regarding investors who profited touches directly on the court's application of *Kousisis*. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued they lacked intent to defraud because they sought profit, not victim harm, and because followers received securities (property) in exchange for their cash.

The court rejected this, holding that:
*   **Intent:** The intent to defraud is satisfied by the intent to deceive and cause *some* harm (the deprivation of money/property), not necessarily an overall financial loss. As the court writes, "a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he 'schem[es] to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.'"
*   **Victim Status:** The fact that a follower bought during the "pump," saw gains, and sold before the "dump" does not negate the fraud at the moment of inducement. The court explicitly notes that liability exists even when the fraudster "provides something... of equal value in return." The crime is complete upon the fraudulent inducement to hand over money.

### Where Is the Limit?
The limit on liability described in *Constantinescu$ is grounded in the **allegations of deceptive conduct aimed at obtaining property**.
1.  **Deception is Required:** There must be a material misrepresentation (e.g., lying about one's own trading position or the security's prospects). A mere failure to disclose or sharing optimistic but honest opinions would likely not satisfy the "deception" prong.
2.  **Object Must Be Property/Money:** The scheme's objective must be to obtain money or property from the victim. If the scheme only manipulated information without inducing a transfer of funds based on lies, it might fall back into the forbidden "right-to-control" category.
3.  **Inducement:** The lie must actually induce the transaction.

In summary, *Constantinescu* holds that if a defendant lies to induce an investor to part with money, the elements of securities fraud are met **even if** that investor momentarily profits or breaks even. The statute protects the integrity of the transaction and the victim's right to their money prior to deception, not just their final portfolio balance. However, this applies only where there is actual deceptive conduct designed to extract funds, not simply any volatile market activity labeled as a "pump and dump."

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 incorporate mail/wire fraud concepts after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, and does it create different standards for securities vs. general fraud?
Question:
The opinion states that § 1348 borrows key concepts from mail and wire fraud statutes. Given that *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* were technically wire/mail fraud cases (or interpreted general fraud statutes), does this mean securities fraud now has identical elements regarding property interests and intent? Or does the unique nature of securities markets (where information asymmetry is inherent) suggest that courts might eventually distinguish how these precedents apply specifically under § 1348 compared to § 1341/1343? The court says there is 'scant caselaw' in the 5th Circuit on § 1348 specifically—is this gap significant?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 incorporates the core elements of mail and wire fraud established in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* without creating a distinct standard for securities fraud regarding property interests or intent.

**Incorporation of Mail/Wire Fraud Concepts**
The court explicitly states that while there is "scant caselaw" construing § 1348 specifically within the Fifth Circuit, the statute "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Consequently, courts give the terms in § 1348 "similar treatment" to those in §§ 1341 and 1343. This borrowing means that the limitations and definitions articulated in recent Supreme Court precedent for general fraud apply equally to securities fraud. Specifically, § 1348 requires:
1.  A **scheme to defraud**, defined as a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value (money or property).
2.  An **intent to defraud**, defined as an intent to deceive and cause some harm resulting from that deceit.

**Application of *Ciminelli*: Property Interests**
The opinion confirms that § 1348 adheres strictly to the *Ciminelli* prohibition against the "right-to-control" theory. Under *Ciminelli*, depriving a victim of valuable economic information is insufficient for a fraud conviction because such information is not a "traditional property interest."

However, *Constantinescu* distinguishes between depriving a victim of *information* versus depriving them of *money* through fraudulent inducement. The court holds that the indictment survived dismissal because it alleged a "fraudulent-inducement theory," where defendants used misrepresentations to trick followers into parting with their money to purchase securities. The court notes that this theory "protects money and property" and is not a repackaging of the rejected right-to-control theory. Thus, the standard for what constitutes a protected property interest under § 1348 is identical to that under the general fraud statutes: it must be traditional money or property, not merely informational rights.

**Application of *Kousisis*: Intent and Economic Harm**
Regarding intent, the court applies *Kousisis* to reject the argument that securities fraud requires an intent to cause the victim a net economic loss. The defendants argued they intended only to profit, not to harm their followers (who received securities of arguably equal market value). Relying on *Kousisis*, the court held that the fraud statutes are satisfied when the defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim's money or property, "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

The opinion emphasizes that providing something of equal value in return (the securities) does not negate the fraud if the acquisition of the victim's money was achieved through materially false pretenses. Therefore, the specific nature of securities markets—where investors voluntarily exchange money for assets—does not create a loophole; if the exchange is induced by lies with the object of obtaining money, the intent element is met just as it would be in a non-securities context.

**Significance of the Caselaw Gap**
The court acknowledges the "scant caselaw" on § 1348 in the Fifth Circuit but treats this gap as procedural rather than substantive. Instead of developing a unique jurisprudence for securities fraud, the court immediately filled this void by importing existing mail and wire fraud precedents (*Greenlaw*, *Evans*, *Ciminelli*, and *Kousisis*). The lack of prior specific precedent did not lead to a differentiated standard; rather, it reinforced the reliance on the broader federal fraud framework.

In summary, *Constantinescu* establishes that § 1348 does not operate with different standards than §§ 1341/1343 post-*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. Securities fraud under § 1348 requires a scheme to deprive victims of traditional property interests (not just information) via fraudulent inducement, and it does not require proof that the defendant intended to cause net financial loss to the victim.

### What role does the concept of 'material misrepresentation' play in distinguishing criminal fraud from mere puffery in social media trading contexts?
Question:
The indictment relies on 'false and misleading information about trading positions.' In previous eras, influencers often used vague hype ('This stock is going to moon!') which might be considered non-actionable puffery. This opinion hinges on the idea that specific lies about *positions* induce people to part with money. At what point does a social media post cross from aggressive marketing or opinion into a 'material misrepresentation' sufficient for criminal liability under this new framework? Does the court's reliance on *Kousisis* lower bar for what counts as 'material' since actual harm isn't required?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the distinction between actionable criminal fraud and non-actionable "puffery" turns on the **specificity of the falsehood** regarding trading positions and the application of the **fraudulent-inducement theory** established in *Kousisis v. United States*.

Here is how the court distinguishes these concepts and addresses your question regarding the "materiality" bar:

### 1. Puffery vs. Material Misrepresentation
The opinion suggests that vague hype (e.g., "This stock is going to moon!") generally falls outside the scope of criminal fraud because it lacks the specific factual predicate required for a "scheme to defraud." In contrast, the indictment in *Constantinescu* survives dismissal because it alleges **"false and misleading information about [the defendants'] trading positions"** (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14).

The court treats statements about one's own ownership or trading activity as objective facts rather than subjective opinions or salesmanship. By allegedly lying about holding a position while simultaneously planning to "dump" it, the defendants made a material misrepresentation intended to deceive followers into parting with money. The court notes that a "scheme to defraud" requires a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value... from the [entity] to be deceived" (citing *United States v. Evans*). Therefore, the line is crossed when an influencer moves from expressing optimism about a stock's future performance to fabricating facts about their current financial stake in that stock to induce a transaction.

### 2. The Role of *Kousisis* and the Definition of "Material"
You asked if reliance on *Kousisis* lowers the bar for what counts as "material" because actual harm is not required. It is more accurate to say that *Kousisis* clarifies that **actual economic loss is not an element of the offense**, but it does not alter the definition of what makes a statement "material."

*   **No Requirement of Net Loss:** The defendants argued they intended to profit, not to harm their followers' bottom lines (since followers received securities of arguably equal market value at the time of purchase). Relying on *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit rejected this, holding that fraud statutes protect money and property regardless of whether the victim suffers a net pecuniary loss. As the court states, "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return (*Constantinescu*, Section III.B, citing *Kousisis*).
*   **Materiality Remains Tied to Inducement:** The "materiality" of the misrepresentation remains tied to its capacity to influence the victim's decision. The indictment sufficiently alleged materiality because the false statements about trading positions were designed specifically so that "other investors were induced to purchase the security" (Indictment ¶ 13).

### Conclusion
Under *Constantinescu*, a social media post crosses from aggressive marketing into criminal fraud when it contains **specific factual falsehoods** (such as lying about one's trading position) used to execute a **fraudulent-inducement scheme**.

The reliance on *Kousisis* does not lower the threshold for what constitutes a "lie"; rather, it removes the defense that no crime occurred simply because the victims technically received assets of equivalent value. The focus shifts entirely to whether the defendant used deception as an instrumentality to obtain money or property, irrespective of the ultimate economic outcome for the victim.
