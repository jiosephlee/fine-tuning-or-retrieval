Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Does this ruling imply that any 'pump and dump' scheme automatically satisfies the 'scheme to defraud' element under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 post-*Ciminelli*?
Question:
The court seems to treat this as a classic fraudulent inducement case where investors were tricked into handing over money. However, in many pump-and-dump scenarios, early buyers might actually profit before the 'dump' occurs. If a follower bought during the 'pump,' saw their portfolio value rise temporarily, and sold before the crash, they suffered no loss and arguably got exactly what they wanted (a quick gain). Under this opinion's logic focusing on the *act* of inducement rather than net pecuniary loss, does every participant who bought based on a lie become a 'victim' for statutory purposes, even if they made money? Where is the limit on liability here?
Answer:
No, the ruling in *United States v. Constantinescu* does not imply that **any** "pump and dump" scheme automatically satisfies the "scheme to defraud" element, nor does it suggest that every participant who buys based on a lie is a statutory victim regardless of context. However, the opinion does clarify that under the **fraudulent-inducement theory**, the government need not prove that a specific investor suffered a net pecuniary loss or that the defendant specifically intended to harm the victim's bottom line.

The court's reasoning rests on two distinct but related points derived from *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*:

### 1. The Theory of Liability Matters
The Fifth Circuit distinguishes between a prohibited "right-to-control" theory and a permissible "fraudulent-inducement" theory.
*   **Right-to-Control (Prohibited):** As noted in *Constantinescu*, you cannot convict someone merely for depriving a victim of "potentially valuable economic information." If the indictment alleged only that followers lost the ability to make informed decisions, it would fail under *Ciminelli*.
*   **Fraudulent Inducement (Permitted):** The indictment in *Constantinescu* survived because it alleged the defendants used material misrepresentations to trick victims into **parting with their money**. The court states: "The indictment alleges a 'fraudulent-inducement theory,' under which a defendant 'uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.'"

Therefore, a "pump and dump" scheme is not automatically actionable; it must be charged and proven as a scheme where the object was to obtain money or property through deception, not merely to distort information flow.

### 2. Net Pecuniary Loss Is Not an Element
Your question regarding investors who profited touches directly on the court's application of *Kousisis*. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued they lacked intent to defraud because they sought profit, not victim harm, and because followers received securities (property) in exchange for their cash.

The court rejected this, holding that:
*   **Intent:** The intent to defraud is satisfied by the intent to deceive and cause *some* harm (the deprivation of money/property), not necessarily an overall financial loss. As the court writes, "a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he 'schem[es] to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.'"
*   **Victim Status:** The fact that a follower bought during the "pump," saw gains, and sold before the "dump" does not negate the fraud at the moment of inducement. The court explicitly notes that liability exists even when the fraudster "provides something... of equal value in return." The crime is complete upon the fraudulent inducement to hand over money.

### Where Is the Limit?
The limit on liability described in *Constantinescu$ is grounded in the **allegations of deceptive conduct aimed at obtaining property**.
1.  **Deception is Required:** There must be a material misrepresentation (e.g., lying about one's own trading position or the security's prospects). A mere failure to disclose or sharing optimistic but honest opinions would likely not satisfy the "deception" prong.
2.  **Object Must Be Property/Money:** The scheme's objective must be to obtain money or property from the victim. If the scheme only manipulated information without inducing a transfer of funds based on lies, it might fall back into the forbidden "right-to-control" category.
3.  **Inducement:** The lie must actually induce the transaction.

In summary, *Constantinescu* holds that if a defendant lies to induce an investor to part with money, the elements of securities fraud are met **even if** that investor momentarily profits or breaks even. The statute protects the integrity of the transaction and the victim's right to their money prior to deception, not just their final portfolio balance. However, this applies only where there is actual deceptive conduct designed to extract funds, not simply any volatile market activity labeled as a "pump and dump."
