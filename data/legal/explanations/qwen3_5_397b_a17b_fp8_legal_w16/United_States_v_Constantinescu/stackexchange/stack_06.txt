Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What role does the concept of 'material misrepresentation' play in distinguishing criminal fraud from mere puffery in social media trading contexts?
Question:
The indictment relies on 'false and misleading information about trading positions.' In previous eras, influencers often used vague hype ('This stock is going to moon!') which might be considered non-actionable puffery. This opinion hinges on the idea that specific lies about *positions* induce people to part with money. At what point does a social media post cross from aggressive marketing or opinion into a 'material misrepresentation' sufficient for criminal liability under this new framework? Does the court's reliance on *Kousisis* lower bar for what counts as 'material' since actual harm isn't required?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the distinction between actionable criminal fraud and non-actionable "puffery" turns on the **specificity of the falsehood** regarding trading positions and the application of the **fraudulent-inducement theory** established in *Kousisis v. United States*.

Here is how the court distinguishes these concepts and addresses your question regarding the "materiality" bar:

### 1. Puffery vs. Material Misrepresentation
The opinion suggests that vague hype (e.g., "This stock is going to moon!") generally falls outside the scope of criminal fraud because it lacks the specific factual predicate required for a "scheme to defraud." In contrast, the indictment in *Constantinescu* survives dismissal because it alleges **"false and misleading information about [the defendants'] trading positions"** (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14).

The court treats statements about one's own ownership or trading activity as objective facts rather than subjective opinions or salesmanship. By allegedly lying about holding a position while simultaneously planning to "dump" it, the defendants made a material misrepresentation intended to deceive followers into parting with money. The court notes that a "scheme to defraud" requires a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value... from the [entity] to be deceived" (citing *United States v. Evans*). Therefore, the line is crossed when an influencer moves from expressing optimism about a stock's future performance to fabricating facts about their current financial stake in that stock to induce a transaction.

### 2. The Role of *Kousisis* and the Definition of "Material"
You asked if reliance on *Kousisis* lowers the bar for what counts as "material" because actual harm is not required. It is more accurate to say that *Kousisis* clarifies that **actual economic loss is not an element of the offense**, but it does not alter the definition of what makes a statement "material."

*   **No Requirement of Net Loss:** The defendants argued they intended to profit, not to harm their followers' bottom lines (since followers received securities of arguably equal market value at the time of purchase). Relying on *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit rejected this, holding that fraud statutes protect money and property regardless of whether the victim suffers a net pecuniary loss. As the court states, "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return (*Constantinescu*, Section III.B, citing *Kousisis*).
*   **Materiality Remains Tied to Inducement:** The "materiality" of the misrepresentation remains tied to its capacity to influence the victim's decision. The indictment sufficiently alleged materiality because the false statements about trading positions were designed specifically so that "other investors were induced to purchase the security" (Indictment ¶ 13).

### Conclusion
Under *Constantinescu*, a social media post crosses from aggressive marketing into criminal fraud when it contains **specific factual falsehoods** (such as lying about one's trading position) used to execute a **fraudulent-inducement scheme**.

The reliance on *Kousisis* does not lower the threshold for what constitutes a "lie"; rather, it removes the defense that no crime occurred simply because the victims technically received assets of equivalent value. The focus shifts entirely to whether the defendant used deception as an instrumentality to obtain money or property, irrespective of the ultimate economic outcome for the victim.
