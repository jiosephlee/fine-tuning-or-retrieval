Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the precise legal distinction between the 'right-to-control' theory rejected in *Ciminelli* and the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory upheld in this case?
Question:
I am struggling to understand the line the Fifth Circuit draws here. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court said you can't convict someone just for depriving a victim of valuable economic information needed to make decisions. Here, the defendants allegedly deprived followers of accurate info about stock positions, causing them to buy. The court says this is okay because it's 'fraudulent inducement' to part with money, not just a loss of information control. Since both theories involve lying to influence an economic decision, what specific factual allegation in the indictment saves this from being a forbidden 'right-to-control' case? Is it simply that the victims actually handed over cash, or is there a deeper doctrinal difference regarding what constitutes 'property'?
Answer:
The distinction turns on **what the defendant intended to obtain** through the deception, not merely on the fact that deception influenced an economic decision.

Under the "right-to-control" theory rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), the government attempts to criminalize the deprivation of **informational interests**. In that framework, the "property" right at stake is defined as the victim's intangible right to make informed economic decisions using accurate data. The Supreme Court held this is insufficient because federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests (like money or tangible assets), not the abstract right to control one's assets via information. If the indictment in your case alleged only that the defendants lied to distort the market environment, thereby depriving followers of the *ability* to make good trades, it would fail under *Ciminelli*.

However, the Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* identifies a crucial factual allegation that saves this indictment: the scheme was designed to induce victims to **part with their money**.

The court relies on the "fraudulent-inducement" theory clarified in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025). Under this theory, the fraud is complete when a defendant uses material misrepresentations to trick a victim into entering a transaction that requires handing over money or property. The specific factual allegation in paragraph 13 of the indictment—that defendants posted false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security"—shifts the object of the fraud from *information* to *cash*.

The doctrinal difference is therefore grounded in the definition of "property":
*   **Right-to-Control (Forbidden):** The object of the fraud is the victim's **decision-making process**. The loss is purely informational (e.g., making a bad trade due to bad data).
*   **Fraudulent Inducement (Permitted):** The object of the fraud is the victim's **wallet**. The loss is the transfer of funds itself.

As *Constantinescu* notes, citing *Kousisis*, it does not matter if the victims received securities of equal market value in exchange for their cash, or if they ultimately suffered a net pecuniary loss. The crime lies in obtaining the money through deception. Because the indictment alleges the defendants' goal was to get followers to hand over purchase proceeds (which allowed defendants to "dump" their shares at inflated prices), it alleges a scheme to obtain traditional property (money), distinguishing it clearly from a mere deprivation of economic information.
