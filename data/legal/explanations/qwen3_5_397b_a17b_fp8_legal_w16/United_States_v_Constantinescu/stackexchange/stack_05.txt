Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 incorporate mail/wire fraud concepts after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, and does it create different standards for securities vs. general fraud?
Question:
The opinion states that § 1348 borrows key concepts from mail and wire fraud statutes. Given that *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* were technically wire/mail fraud cases (or interpreted general fraud statutes), does this mean securities fraud now has identical elements regarding property interests and intent? Or does the unique nature of securities markets (where information asymmetry is inherent) suggest that courts might eventually distinguish how these precedents apply specifically under § 1348 compared to § 1341/1343? The court says there is 'scant caselaw' in the 5th Circuit on § 1348 specifically—is this gap significant?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 incorporates the core elements of mail and wire fraud established in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* without creating a distinct standard for securities fraud regarding property interests or intent.

**Incorporation of Mail/Wire Fraud Concepts**
The court explicitly states that while there is "scant caselaw" construing § 1348 specifically within the Fifth Circuit, the statute "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Consequently, courts give the terms in § 1348 "similar treatment" to those in §§ 1341 and 1343. This borrowing means that the limitations and definitions articulated in recent Supreme Court precedent for general fraud apply equally to securities fraud. Specifically, § 1348 requires:
1.  A **scheme to defraud**, defined as a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value (money or property).
2.  An **intent to defraud**, defined as an intent to deceive and cause some harm resulting from that deceit.

**Application of *Ciminelli*: Property Interests**
The opinion confirms that § 1348 adheres strictly to the *Ciminelli* prohibition against the "right-to-control" theory. Under *Ciminelli*, depriving a victim of valuable economic information is insufficient for a fraud conviction because such information is not a "traditional property interest."

However, *Constantinescu* distinguishes between depriving a victim of *information* versus depriving them of *money* through fraudulent inducement. The court holds that the indictment survived dismissal because it alleged a "fraudulent-inducement theory," where defendants used misrepresentations to trick followers into parting with their money to purchase securities. The court notes that this theory "protects money and property" and is not a repackaging of the rejected right-to-control theory. Thus, the standard for what constitutes a protected property interest under § 1348 is identical to that under the general fraud statutes: it must be traditional money or property, not merely informational rights.

**Application of *Kousisis*: Intent and Economic Harm**
Regarding intent, the court applies *Kousisis* to reject the argument that securities fraud requires an intent to cause the victim a net economic loss. The defendants argued they intended only to profit, not to harm their followers (who received securities of arguably equal market value). Relying on *Kousisis*, the court held that the fraud statutes are satisfied when the defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim's money or property, "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

The opinion emphasizes that providing something of equal value in return (the securities) does not negate the fraud if the acquisition of the victim's money was achieved through materially false pretenses. Therefore, the specific nature of securities markets—where investors voluntarily exchange money for assets—does not create a loophole; if the exchange is induced by lies with the object of obtaining money, the intent element is met just as it would be in a non-securities context.

**Significance of the Caselaw Gap**
The court acknowledges the "scant caselaw" on § 1348 in the Fifth Circuit but treats this gap as procedural rather than substantive. Instead of developing a unique jurisprudence for securities fraud, the court immediately filled this void by importing existing mail and wire fraud precedents (*Greenlaw*, *Evans*, *Ciminelli*, and *Kousisis*). The lack of prior specific precedent did not lead to a differentiated standard; rather, it reinforced the reliance on the broader federal fraud framework.

In summary, *Constantinescu* establishes that § 1348 does not operate with different standards than §§ 1341/1343 post-*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. Securities fraud under § 1348 requires a scheme to deprive victims of traditional property interests (not just information) via fraudulent inducement, and it does not require proof that the defendant intended to cause net financial loss to the victim.
