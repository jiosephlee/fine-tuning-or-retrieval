Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

Understanding a judicial opinion begins with a clear grasp of the underlying facts and the procedural journey the case has taken to reach the appellate court. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addresses a critical intersection between modern digital commerce and traditional federal fraud statutes. The case centers on an alleged "pump and dump" scheme executed through social media, raising novel questions about what constitutes a protected property interest in the age of information-driven markets. Before analyzing the court's legal reasoning regarding the scope of securities fraud, one must first understand the specific mechanics of the alleged conduct, the statutory framework invoked by the government, and the precise legal error identified by the district court that necessitated this appeal.

## The Mechanics of the Alleged 'Pump and Dump' Scheme

The factual core of this case involves a coordinated effort to manipulate stock prices for illicit profit, a practice commonly known as a "pump and dump." According to the superseding indictment, the defendants engaged in a three-step process designed to artificially inflate the value of specific securities before liquidating their holdings. First, the defendants would purchase a target security. Second, they would "pump" the price of that security by disseminating false and misleading information across various social media platforms. These posts were crafted to induce other investors to purchase the same security, thereby driving up demand and artificially increasing the market price. Finally, once the price reached a desired level due to this induced buying pressure, the defendants would "dump" their shares, secretly selling them for a substantial profit while leaving the unsuspecting investors with devalued assets. The government alleges that this scheme generated approximately $114 million in profits for the participants. This sequence of events forms the factual basis for the charge that the defendants engaged in a scheme to defraud persons in connection with the purchase or sale of securities.

## Defendants' Role as Social Media Influencers and Traders

A distinguishing feature of this case is the method by which the defendants allegedly effected their fraud: through their status as influential figures on social media. The indictment identifies eight individuals, including Edward Constantinescu and Perry "PJ" Matlock, who possessed large followings across multiple digital platforms. Crucially, these defendants did not merely post random content; they held themselves out to be skilled stock traders, cultivating an audience that trusted their expertise and trading acumen. By frequently posting about their trading activities, they established a relationship of reliance with their followers. The government contends that the defendants leveraged this trust to give weight to their misrepresentations regarding their own trading positions and the potential future price of specific securities. The allegation is not simply that false information was published, but that it was published by trusted sources whose apparent endorsement was essential to inducing followers to part with their money. This dynamic underscores why the followers were induced to act; they believed they were acting on valuable insider knowledge or expert analysis provided by individuals they viewed as successful market participants.

## Charges Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and Related Statutes

Based on these allegations, the grand jury returned an indictment charging the defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, alongside multiple substantive counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Section 1348 specifically prohibits schemes to "defraud any person in connection with... any security" or schemes to "obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security." One defendant, Daniel Knight, pleaded guilty to these charges. However, the remaining defendants challenged the legal sufficiency of the indictment itself, moving to dismiss it before trial. Their motion did not dispute the factual accuracy of the allegations but rather argued that even if true, those facts did not constitute a crime under the relevant statutes. This procedural move shifted focus from what happened to whether what happened fits withinthe statutory definition of securities fraud as interpreted by recent Supreme Court precedent.

# Legal Framework: Elements of Securities Fraud

To understand the appellate court's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, one must first grasp the specific statutory architecture governing securities fraud and how the Fifth Circuit interprets its core components. The dispute in this case did not turn on whether the defendants made false statements—they conceded that point—but rather on whether those statements constituted a crime under the specific legal definitions of a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud." The district court had dismissed the indictment based on a narrow reading of what constitutes property loss, specifically worrying that the defendants only deprived victims of information rather than tangible assets. The Court of Appeals reversed this dismissal by applying a rigorous framework derived from the text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and recent Supreme Court precedents clarifying the boundaries of federal fraud statutes. This chapter breaks down that framework, explaining how the court moves from statutory language to the ultimate conclusion that inducing investors to part with money through lies satisfies the elements of securities fraud, even if the investors receive securities of equivalent market value in return.

## Statutory Language of 18 U.S.C. § 1348

The foundation of the prosecution in *Constantinescu* rests on 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the primary federal criminal statute prohibiting securities fraud. The statute is broad in its scope but precise in its prohibitions. It criminalizes two distinct types of conduct executed knowingly and intentionally. First, it prohibits executing a "scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with" the purchase or sale of any security. Second, it prohibits obtaining money or property "by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises" in connection with such transactions. In *Constantinescu*, the government relied on both prongs, alleging that the defendants orchestrated a classic "pump and dump" operation where they purchased securities, inflated their prices through deceptive social media posts, and then sold them for a profit. The statutory language is critical because it ties the fraudulent conduct directly to the transaction itself; the deception must occur "in connection with" the purchase or sale. The district court's error stemmed from misinterpreting what kind of deprivation satisfies this statute, mistakenly believing that unless the victim lost net wealth, no "property" had been obtained in violation of the act. The appellate court's analysis begins by reaffirming that the statute protects against the deprivation of money or property through deception, regardless of the subsequent economic outcome for the victim.

## Integration of Mail and Wire Fraud Concepts into Securities Fraud

When interpreting § 1348, courts often face a scarcity of circuit-specific precedent directly addressing every nuance of securities fraud. As noted in *Constantinescu*, there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." To fill this gap, the Fifth Circuit looks to the lineage of federal fraud laws. Section 1348 explicitly borrows key concepts from the mail fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1341) and wire fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1342) statutes. Consequently, judicial interpretations of mail and wire fraud are highly persuasive and often controlling when analyzing securities fraud charges. This integration allows courts to apply decades of developed jurisprudence regarding what constitutes a "scheme" and what qualifies as an "intent to defraud." In *Constantinescu*, Judge Engelhardt relies heavily on this cross-statutory pollination, citing cases like *United States v. Greenlaw* and *United States v. Evans* which originally addressed mail or wire fraud but established principles now applicable to securities fraud. By treating these statutes as a cohesive family of laws, the court ensures consistency in how federal fraud is prosecuted across different mediums—whether via postal service, electronic transmission, or stock market manipulation. This approach prevents defendants from exploiting technical differences between statutes to escape liability for fundamentally similar deceptive conduct.

## Element One: Defining a 'Scheme to Defraud' via Material Misrepresentation

The first substantive hurdle in a securities fraud prosecution is establishing a "scheme to defraud." Under Fifth Circuit precedent adopted in *Constantinescu*, this element requires more than just telling a lie; it requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others for the purpose of obtaining something of value, typically money, from the deceived party. The concept hinges on causation: the deception must be designed to induce the victim to act in a way they otherwise would not have, specifically resulting in the transfer of assets. In *Constantinescu*, defendants argued that their scheme only deprived followers of accurate economic information—the ability to make informed trading decisions—rather than their money itself. # The Scheme Element: Rejecting the Right-to-Control Theory in Favor of Fraudulent Inducement

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the central legal battleground concerns the "scheme to defraud" element of the federal securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The defendants argued that the indictment was legally insufficient because it alleged a scheme to deprive victims of valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. This argument relied heavily on recent Supreme Court precedent limiting the scope of fraud statutes. However, the Fifth Circuit rejected this characterization, drawing a sharp distinction between the impermissible "right-to-control" theory and the permissible "fraudulent-inducement" theory. Understanding this distinction is critical for reading the opinion, as it determines whether the defendants' conduct falls within the statutory prohibition against obtaining money or property by false pretenses. The court's analysis clarifies that while one cannot be convicted for merely withholding information, one can be convicted for using lies to trick a victim into voluntarily handing over money.

## Supreme Court Precedent: *Ciminelli v. United States* and the Limits of Information Rights

To understand the defendants' motion to dismiss, one must first grasp the holding in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), which serves as the foundational precedent for this case. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court addressed the "right-to-control" theory of fraud. Under this theory, prosecutors previously argued that a defendant committed fraud by depriving a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. Essentially, the victim's "property" was defined as their right to control their assets based on accurate information. The Supreme Court firmly rejected this approach, holding that federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. The Court reasoned that a right to valuable economic information is not itself a traditional property interest. Therefore, a conviction cannot stand if it is based solely on the deprivation of information, even if that information would have influenced the victim's financial choices. In *Constantinescu*, the district court initially agreed with the defendants that the indictment fell into this trap, viewing the scheme as one that deprived followers of honest market data rather than their capital.

## Distinguishing Between Deprivation of Information and Deprivation of Money

The pivotal issue in *Constantinescu* is distinguishing between a scheme that deprives victims of information (which *Ciminelli* forbids as a sole basis for liability) and a scheme that uses misinformation to deprive victims of money (which remains unlawful). The defendants contended that their actions amounted to the former: they withheld truthful information about their own trading positions and intentions, thereby leaving their followers without the data needed to make informed investment decisions. They argued this was precisely the type of "information rights" violation *Ciminelli* excluded from criminal liability.

The Fifth Circuit, however, looked beyond the mere presence of missing information to identify what was actually taken from the victims. The court noted that while depriving someone of information alone is insufficient, using deception to induce a transfer of assets is distinct. The indictment did not merely allege that the defendants kept secrets; it alleged that they actively posted false and misleading statements to manipulate market prices. The crucial difference lies in the object of the fraud. In a pure right-to-control case, the harm is the distortion of decision-making capacity. In contrast, where fraudulent inducement is present, the harm is the actual separation of the victim from their funds or property. The court emphasized that following investors did not just lose access to truth; they lost their money when they purchased inflated securities based on lies. Thus, the indictment survived because it targeted the acquisition of money, not merely the corruption of informational flow.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory: Trickery Leading to Contractual Transfer

Having distinguished their case from *Ciminelli*, the court anchored its reasoning in the "fraudulent-inducement" theory, which was recently reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States*. This theory posits that a defendant commits fraud when they use a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a contract or transaction that requires handing over money or property. Unlike the right-to-control theory, which focuses on what knowledge was withheld before a decision was made, fraudulent inducement focuses on how specific lies caused a specific transfer of wealth.

# The Intent Element: Profit Motive Versus Victim Economic Loss

The second pillar of the defendants' challenge in *United States v. Constantinescu* concerns the mental state required for securities fraud. Specifically, the defendants conceded that they engaged in deception but argued that the indictment failed to allege the requisite "intent to defraud." Their theory rested on a narrow interpretation of harm: they claimed that because their goal was self-enrichment through a "pump and dump" scheme, rather than the specific intention to leave their followers with a net financial loss, the element of intent was missing. They posited that since the followers received securities in exchange for their money—assets that presumably held some market value—the transaction lacked the harmful intent necessary for criminal fraud. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, relying heavily on recent Supreme Court precedent to clarify that the federal fraud statutes focus on the defendant's objective to obtain property through deception, not on whether the defendant specifically intended to impoverish the victim.

## Supreme Court Precedent: *Kousisis v. United States* and the Net Pecuniary Loss Rule

To resolve the dispute over intent, the court turned to *Kousisis v. United States*, a 2025 Supreme Court decision that directly addressed whether a defendant must intend to cause a victim's economic downfall to be convicted of fraud. In *Kousisis*, the defendant secured government contracts by lying about his compliance with subcontracting requirements for disadvantaged businesses. Although he fulfilled the contractual obligations using a "pass-through" entity, thereby providing the government with the services it paid for, he was still charged with fraud. The Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. Instead, liability attaches when a defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether the fraudster provides something of equal value in return. This ruling established that "net pecuniary loss" is not an element of the offense. In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit applied this principle directly, noting that the defendants' argument—that they intended only to profit, not to harm—mirrored the failed argument in *Kousisis*. The court made clear that under current law, a defendant's desire for personal gain satisfies the intent requirement even if the victim suffers no ultimate financial deficit.

## Decoupling Defendant Gain from Victim Loss in Fraud Analysis

The core legal lesson from *Constantinescu* is the decoupling of the defendant's gain from the victim's loss in the analysis of fraudulent intent. Historically, some defenses have attempted to link these two concepts, suggesting that if a victim receives fair market value, there can be no fraudulent intent because there is no corresponding injury. The Fifth Circuit dismantled this linkage by emphasizing that the statutory text focuses on the *method* of acquisition (deception) and the *object* of the scheme (money or property), not the final balance sheet of the victim. In the context of a "pump and dump" scheme, the defendants argued their intent was merely to inflate a stock price to sell their own shares, not to ensure their followers lost money on theirs. However, following *Kousisis*, the court reasoned that this distinction is legally irrelevant. The indictment alleged that defendants used false representations to induce followers to part with their money. Because obtaining that money was an "object" of their fraud, the intent element is satisfied. The law punishesthe deceptive means used to separate a victim from their property; it does not require prosecutors to prove that separating them from that property was done with malice toward their overall wealth.

## 'Something of Equal Value' Defense: Why Receiving Securities Does Not Negate Fraud

A critical component of this analysis involves addressing what might be called the "something of equal value" defense. Defendants often argue that because they delivered goods or services—in this case, shares of stock—together with taking money via lies (misrepresentations about trading positions), no fraud occurred because it wasn't theft; it was just bad business advice wrapped in hyperbole or falsity. The court explicitly rejected this notion by citing *Kousisis*, which confirmed that providing something in return does not negate fraud if inducement relied upon material falsehoods ("regardless"). # Disposition and Broader Implications for Digital Asset Fraud

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* culminates in a decisive reversal of the district court's order dismissing the indictment, sending the case back for trial proceedings. This disposition resolves the immediate procedural standoff but carries weightier consequences for how federal fraud statutes apply to the modern landscape of social media-driven investing. By rejecting the defendants' narrow interpretation of "property" and "harm," the appellate court reaffirmed that traditional common-law fraud principles remain robust enough to encompass sophisticated "pump and dump" schemes executed through digital platforms. The court's reasoning clarifies that the mechanism of delivery—whether a face-to-face meeting, a mailed letter, or a viral social media post—does not alter the fundamental nature of the crime when the objective is to induce victims to part with their money through deception. Consequently, this opinion serves as a critical boundary marker, distinguishing between lawful, albeit risky, market speculation and criminal manipulation where false representations are the engine of profit.

## Final Holding: Reversal of Dismissal and Remand for Trial

The operative holding of *Constantinescu* is straightforward yet procedurally significant: the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's dismissal of the superseding indictment and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The district court had initially erred by concluding that the indictment failed to state an offense because it allegedly sought only to deprive followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. The appellate court corrected this legal conclusion, determining that the indictment, taken as true under the standard of review, sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. By reversing the dismissal, the Fifth Circuit restored the government's ability to prosecute Edward Constantinescu and his co-defendants on charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud and substantive counts of securities fraud. This procedural outcome underscores that at the indictment stage, the government need not prove its case or demonstrate actual economic loss; it must only articulate a coherent theory of liability that fits within the statutory framework, which the court found it had done by alleging a fraudulent inducement to purchase securities.

## _Ratio Decidendi_: Sufficiency of Allegations for Scheme and Intent

The core legal reasoning, or _ratio decidendi_, driving the reversal rests on the court's application of recent Supreme Court precedents, specifically _Ciminelli v. United States_ and _Kousisis v. United States_, to distinguish between impermissible "right-to-control" theories and valid "fraudulent-inducement" theories. The defendants argued that their scheme merely deprived followers of information necessary to make informed trading decisions, a theory explicitly rejected in _Ciminelli_ as insufficient to support a fraud conviction because information alone is not traditional property. However, the Fifth Circuit reasoned that the indictment in _Constantinescu_ did not rely on this prohibited theory. Instead, it alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants made material misrepresentations about their trading positions and stock prospects to trick followers into entering transactions that required handing over money. Under this theory, the "property" obtained was the followers' capital used to purchase the inflated securities, not merely their access to data. Furthermore, regarding intent, the court relied on _Kousisis_ to reject the notion that prosecutors must allege an intent to cause net pecuniary loss to the victim. The court clarified that the intent to defraud is satisfied by an intent to deceive and an object to obtain money or property, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal market value in return or suffers a ultimate financial deficit. Thus, the allegation that defendants intended to profit by inducing purchases via lies was sufficient to establish criminal intent, even if those purchases technically occurred at prevailing market prices at the moment of transaction.

## _Implications_: Applying Traditional Fraud Doctrines to Social Media 'Finfluencers'

The broader implication of _Constantinescu_ is its firm rejection of any implicit exemption for "finfluencers"—financial influencers operating on social media—from established fraud doctrines. Before this decision, there was ambiguity regarding whether manipulating stock prices through tweets, videos, or posts constituted a distinct category of conduct outside traditional securities fraud, particularly when framed as sharing "opinions" or withholding full disclosure of positions. The Fifth Circuit's analysis dismantles this potential safe harbor by anchoring liability in age-old concepts of deception and property deprivation. The opinion signals that holding oneself out as a skilled trader while secretly coordinating buys and sells to manipulate price constitutes a scheme to defraud just as surely as boiler room operations of previous decades. By focusing on the act of inducing victims to part with money through false pretenses, the court ensures that digital platforms are treated as conduits for commerce subject to existing regulatory guardrails, not as lawless zones where misrepresentation is permissible so long as it is packaged as content creation. This reinforces the principle that technology changes the speed and scale of fraud but does not redefine its essential elements.
