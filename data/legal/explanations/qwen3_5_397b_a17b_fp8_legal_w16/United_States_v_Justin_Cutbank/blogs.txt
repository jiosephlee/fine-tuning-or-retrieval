# Intrinsic Acts vs. Propensity: The Eighth Circuit's Expansive View of 'Continuous Possession' in Felon-in-Possession Cases

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit faced a familiar tension in federal criminal trials: how much of a defendant's past conduct can the government introduce to prove present guilt without crossing the line into forbidden character assassination? The case involved Justin Cutbank, a felon with a volatile history who held his girlfriend at gunpoint during a drug-fueled episode before engaging in a standoff with police. While the facts of the underlying crime were gruesome and the evidence of guilt overwhelming, the appellate panel's handling of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) offers a troubling glimpse into how courts are increasingly blurring the line between "intrinsic" evidence and prohibited propensity proof. By relying on a broad theory of "continuous possession," the Eighth Circuit has effectively carved out a significant exception to the safeguards designed to keep prior bad acts out of the jury box, raising concerns about whether defendants in felon-in-possession cases can ever receive a trial untainted by their history with firearms.

The crux of Cutbank's evidentiary challenge centered on testimony from his girlfriend and a housemate, both of whom stated they had seen Cutbank with the specific sawed-off rifle used in the crime on multiple occasions prior to the night of the assault. Cutbank objected, arguing this was classic propensity evidence—testimony intended to show that because he owned and used guns before, he likely possessed one on the night in question. Under Rule 404(b), such evidence is generally inadmissible to prove character or action in conformity therewith. However, the district court, affirmed unanimously by Judges Loken and his colleagues, categorized these prior sightings not as extrinsic bad acts, but as "intrinsic" evidence necessary to prove ownership and control. Citing *United States v. Battle*, the court reasoned that prior possession of the same weapon is directly relevant to establishing later possession, thereby framing the testimony as proof of a continuous chain of custody rather than a discrete prior bad act.

This reasoning, while procedurally efficient for prosecutors, stretches the concept of intrinsic evidence to its breaking point. Traditionally, intrinsic evidence refers to acts that are part of the same transaction or series of transactions as the charged offense—essentially, what happened immediately before, during, or after the crime to complete the story. By contrast, witnessing a defendant with a gun days, weeks, or months earlier is arguably distinct from the charged instance of possession. Yet, by labeling this "continuous possession," the Eighth Circuit allows prosecutors to bypass the strict notice requirements and balancing tests of Rule 404(b). The practical effect is profound: in any case where a specific firearm is identified, witnesses can testify to every previous instance they saw the defendant with that gun, regardless of how far back those instances occurred or how prejudicial they might be. The jury hears not just about the crime charged, but about a lifestyle of gun ownership that reinforces the very stereotype Rule 404(b) seeks to exclude—that once a felon touches a gun, they never really let go.

The danger lies in the circularity of this logic. To convict under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove knowing possession at a specific time. If direct evidence of possession at that moment is ambiguous—for instance, if the gun was found nearby but not on the person—the government might be tempted to fill the gap with testimony about prior access. In *Cutbank*, there was DNA onthe weapon and eyewitnesses placing it in his hand duringthe kidnapping, so any error was undoubtedly harmless underthe high barof *DeMarce*. # The Hollow Shield: Why 'Harmless Error' Analysis Renders Evidentiary Objections Futile in High-Stakes ACCA Prosecutions

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided by the Eighth Circuit on October 7, 2025, the court affirmed a staggering 292-month sentence for a felon in possession of a firearm under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). On its face, the result is unsurprising: Justin Cutbank's conduct was undeniably horrific. After a night of drug use, he held his girlfriend at gunpoint, terrorized her children, fled to a neighbor's garage, and bit a police officer while claiming to possess a weapon. The physical evidence—a sawed-off rifle with his DNA—and the testimony of multiple witnesses painted a picture of guilt that seems impervious to legal technicality. Yet, buried within Section II(E) of Judge Loken's unanimous opinion lies a troubling jurisprudential signal for criminal defense practitioners. By applying the "harmless error" doctrine with such sweeping breadth, the Eighth Circuit suggests that in cases involving grotesque facts and overwhelming physical evidence, the Federal Rules of Evidence function less as rigid guardrails and more as suggestions that district courts can ignore with impunity.

The crux of the issue lies in how appellate courts weigh procedural missteps against the sheer weight of a defendant's culpability. Cutbank raised several evidentiary challenges on appeal, most notably regarding the admission of emotionally charged testimony and restrictions on cross-examination. Specifically, during cross-examination concerning inconsistencies in her story, the victim, D.F., volunteered that she disliked guns because her sister had been killed by one. Cutbank moved to strike this testimony as unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403. Similarly, the district court prevented defense counsel from reading portions of a police report into the record to impeach another witness, C.F., citing authentication issues under Rule 901. While these rulings present legitimate debatable questions regarding the boundaries of relevance and the right to confront witnesses through effective cross-examination, the Eighth Circuit declined to engage deeply with their merits. Instead, the court pivoted immediately to the harmless error standard, concluding that because the government's evidence was "substantial," any error could not have substantially swayed the jury.

This analytical shortcut exposes a structural flaw in modern appellate review of criminal convictions: the tendency for the "harmless error" doctrine to swallow the rule itself. In *Cutbank*, the court explicitly notes that it will not reverse unless the jury was "substantially swayed" by improper evidence. But this formulation creates a paradoxical incentive structure for trial judges and prosecutors. When a defendant is accused of acts as visceral as domestic terrorism involving sawed-off shotguns and child endangerment, almost any evidentiary error becomes retroactively "harmless" because the remaining proof of guilt is so overpowering. The logic is circular: the more egregious the crime and the stronger the physical evidence, the more latitude a district court has to bend evidentiary rules without fear of reversal. Consequently, the protective purpose of rules like 403 (balancing probative value against prejudice) and 613 (impeachment by prior inconsistent statement) is nullified precisely in those high-stakes cases where rigorous adherence to procedure is most critical to ensuring a fair trial process.

The implications for defense strategy are stark and disheartening. In Section II(E), Judge Loken dismisses Cutbank's concerns about being thwarted during cross-examination of C.F. regarding her prior statements to police. The court holds that even if this restriction was erroneous—which it implies might be possible given its cursory treatment—it was harmless given DNA matches and other corroborating testimony. This approach effectively tells defense counsel that objecting to evidentiary overreach is often a futile exercise if the client faces mountains of incriminating physical evidence. If an appellate court presumes that jurors are incapable of distinguishing between admissible facts and inflammatory character attacks once they know a defendant bit a police officer and held a family hostage, then meticulous trial preservation loses its utility. The doctrine assumes a rational actor model of juror deliberation that may not hold up when emotions run high; yet, it uses that assumption to shield convictions from scrutiny.

Furthermore, this application of harmless error risks eroding long-term institutional integrity within federal courts. The Federal Rules of Evidence are designed not merely to filter out unreliable data but to maintain public confidence in the fairness of judicial proceedings. When appellate courts routinely bless questionable rulings by pointing to DNA tests or confessions elsewhere in the record, they signal that procedural justice is secondary to outcome certainty in "slam dunk" cases. Over time, this encourages district courts to adopt a utilitarian approach to evidence: if it helps secure a conviction against a clearly guilty party, admit it and let appellate cleanup handle any fallout via harmless error analysis. This dynamic disproportionately impacts defendants facing mandatory minimums under statutes like ACCA, where every month counts and every procedural safeguard serves as a check on prosecutorial power and judicial bias.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* stands as a grim reminder that in our current legal landscape due process protections are elastic; they stretch furthest when society demands retribution most loudly while snapping back tightest when stakes are low or ambiguity exists elsewhere rather than here today!



# Double Counting Danger? Navigating the Intersection of ACCA Mandates and USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancements

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided by the Eighth Circuit on October 7, 2025, the court affirmed a staggering 292-month sentence for a felon in possession of a firearm, reinforcing a rigid approach to sentencing calculations that often leaves defendants feeling the weight of duplicative punitive mechanisms. While the headline holding concerns the affirmation of a conviction stemming from a harrowing night of domestic terror and a standoff with police, the true doctrinal friction lies in the sentencing phase. Defendant Justin Cutbank argued that stacking the Armed Career Criminal Act's (ACCA) mandatory minimum enhancements with the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines' "connection with another felony" enhancement resulted in an impermissible double counting of his violent conduct. The Eighth Circuit's dismissal of this argument offers a stark illustration of how appellate courts are increasingly insulating district court discretion against challenges to overlapping statutory and guideline provisions, effectively closing the door on substantive reasonableness claims that do not rise to the level of procedural error.

The facts underlying *Cutbank* are undeniably grave, setting a high bar for any sympathy regarding the length of incarceration. After consuming methamphetamine and alcohol, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint, threatened her life, and struck her with the weapon before fleeing and engaging in a violent resistance against law enforcement. A jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), finding he possessed a sawed-off rifle and had three prior violent felony convictions. This triggered ACCA's fifteen-year mandatory minimum floor. However, the district court went further, applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which adds four levels to the offense score if the firearm was possessed "in connection with another felony offense." Because the underlying conduct involved kidnapping and assault—felonies distinct from the mere possession charge—the Probation Service Report recommended, and the district court accepted, a total offense level of 35. This calculation pushed the advisory range well above the ACCA floor, resulting in the 292-month sentence.

Cutbank's appeal focused narrowly on this sentencing enhancement. His core contention was not that he lacked prior convictions or that he didn't possess the gun during other crimes, but rather that applying both the ACCA mandate and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement punished him twice for the same violent behavior. He argued this duality violated the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The Eighth Circuit, speaking through Judge Loken, sidestepped the philosophical debate on double counting by framing the issue strictly through the lens of procedural versus substantive review. The court noted that because Cutbank did not object to the factual findings in the Presentence Investigation Report at trial, there was no procedural error in calculating the guidelines range. Once the range is correctly calculated, appellate review shifts to substantive reasonableness, where within-guidelines sentences carry a presumption of validity.

The court's reasoning reveals a deliberate judicial strategy to contain sentencing disputes within the bounds of *Gall v. United States*. By characterizing Cutbank's complaint as a challenge to substantive reasonableness rather than a miscalculation of the guidelines, the Eighth Circuit insulated its decision from de novo review. The opinion emphasizes that district courts need not provide a "mechanical recitation" of § 3553(a) factors so long as it is clear they considered them. Since District Judge implicitly rejected Cutbank's leniency arguments by imposing a within-range sentence amidst egregious facts—including domestic violence in front of children and assaulting an officer—the appellate court found no abuse of discretion. This approach effectively neutralizes "double counting" arguments unless Congress explicitly forbids specific combinations of statutes and guidelines, which it has not done for ACCA and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* highlights a growing tension in federal sentencing jurisprudence:the divergence between mathematical precision in guideline calculations and holistic justice under § 3553(a). Critics might argue that when ACCA already imposes a severe mandatory floor based on past violence, layering an additional enhancement for current violence creates a compounding effect that distorts proportionality. However, the Eighth Circuit's stance suggests that such policy grievances are best aired at sentencing hearings, not on appeal. The court signaled that absent a specific prohibition in the Guidelines Manual or statute, overlapping enhancements are features of the system, not bugs. By deferring heavily to the district judge's assessment of § 3553(a) factors, circuit courts are essentially outsourcing moral judgments about "excessive" punishment to trial judges who witness firsthand brutality like that exhibited by Cutbank.

The downstream effects of *Cutbank* will likely be felt most acutely in cases where defendants have strong mitigation narratives but weak procedural records.
