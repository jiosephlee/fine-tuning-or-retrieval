# Intrinsic Acts vs. Propensity: The Eighth Circuit's Expansive View of 'Continuous Possession' in Felon-in-Possession Cases

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit faced a grim factual tableau: a methamphetamine-fueled domestic terrorization where the defendant held his girlfriend at gunpoint, struck her with the weapon, and later engaged in a violent standoff with police. While the brutality of the underlying conduct makes sympathy for Justin Cutbank scarce, the legal question lurking beneath the surface of his appeal strikes at a persistent tension in federal criminal procedure: where does the line fall between evidence necessary to prove an element of a crime and impermissible character evidence designed to paint the defendant as a bad person?

Cutbank, a convicted felon, challenged his conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) largely on evidentiary grounds. His primary contention was that the district court erred by allowing witnesses to testify that they had seen him possess the specific sawed-off rifle on occasions prior to the night of the assault. He argued this violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally prohibits introducing evidence of other acts to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in accordance with that character. The Eighth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Loken, swiftly affirmed the conviction, doubling down on a doctrine that treats prior possession of the same weapon as "intrinsic" evidence rather than extrinsic propensity evidence. While legally consistent with Eighth Circuit precedent, this approach risks blurring the safeguards Rule 404(b) was designed to erect, particularly in cases where the underlying facts are as inflammatory as those in *Cutbank*.

## The Doctrine of Continuous Possession

The crux of Cutbank's evidentiary challenge was the testimony of his girlfriend, D.F., and her housemate, C.F., who stated they had seen Cutbank with the Marlin .22 caliber rifle—a weapon with a sawed-off barrel and stock—on multiple occasions before the November 2020 incident. Cutbank moved to exclude this testimony, arguing it served only to demonstrate his propensity for violence and illegal gun ownership. The district court denied the motion, categorizing the evidence as intrinsic to the charged offense. The Eighth Circuit agreed, relying heavily on its 2014 decision in *United States v. Battle*.

The court's reasoning rests on a specific interpretation of what constitutes "possession" in a felon-in-possession prosecution. To secure a conviction, the government must prove that the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm. In scenarios where direct evidence of possession at the exact moment of arrest might be contested—or where constructive possession is alleged—the government often seeks to bridge temporal gaps by showing a pattern of control over the weapon. As Judge Loken wrote, "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control." By framing prior sightings as proof of "continuous possession," the court effectively removes these instances from the ambit of Rule 404(b). If evidence is intrinsic to the charged crime, Rule 404(b)'s strictures do not apply; the evidence is admissible so long as it satisfies Rule 403's balancing test regarding unfair prejudice.

This classification allows prosecutors to introduce a narrative of ongoing criminality without clearing the high hurdles typically associated with "other acts" evidence. Under *Battle* and now *Cutbank*, if the gun is the same, prior handling of it is not an "other act" used to infer guilt; it is direct proof of the continuity required to sustain the possession element itself.

## The Analytical Tension: Element Proof or Character Assassination?

While the Eighth Circuit's logic follows a straight line from *Battle*, it invites critical scrutiny regarding how easily the "intrinsic" label can swallow Rule 404(b)'s protections whole. The theoretical distinction is clean: evidence offered to prove an element (possession) is intrinsic; evidence offered to suggest "he's a violent guy who likes guns" is extrinsic propensity evidence. However, in practice, these purposes are functionally identical in the minds of jurors. When a jury hears that Cutbank held this specific sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before terrifying his partner, they are undoubtedly learning both that he controlled the gun (the element) and that he has a history of arming himself (the character trait).

The danger here is not necessarily that innocent defendants will be convicted—the DNA match and eyewitness identification in *Cutbank* made reversal unlikely regardless—but that the doctrinal shortcut undermines the rigorous vetting process Rule 404(b) demands. Normally, before admitting prior bad acts, a court must engage in a detailed analysis ensuring probative value outweighs prejudicial impact and often must issue limiting instructions telling jurors exactly how they may (and may not) use the evidence. By declaring such evidence "intrinsic," courts bypass this gatekeeping mechanism entirely.

# The Limits of Appellate Advocacy: Waiver, Forfeiture, and the Court's Rebuke of 'Expanded' Issues

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit affirmed a felon-in-possession conviction and a lengthy Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) sentence. While the outcome was predictable given the overwhelming evidence of guilt and the defendant's extensive criminal history, the opinion offers a sharp, albeit brief, masterclass in appellate etiquette and the nuanced distinction between waiver and forfeiture. Specifically, in addressing an evidentiary dispute regarding cross-examination limits, the panel took the unusual step of publicly chastising the government for attempting to broaden the scope of the appeal. Yet, in a move that underscores the pragmatic reality of federal appellate practice, the court proceeded to resolve the very issue it scolded the government for raising. This tension reveals a circuit that demands strict adherence to preservation rules from counsel but reserves for itself the discretion to sweep away unpreserved errors when the record leaves no doubt as to the result.

The controversy arose during Cutbank's trial concerning the testimony of witness C.F., who stated she had seen the defendant with the firearm prior to the charged incident. Defense counsel sought to impeach C.F. using a police report containing her prior inconsistent statements. The district court permitted questioning about what C.F. told the police but sustained an objection when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the report into the record without proper authentication, as C.F. did not author the document. Crucially, Cutbank's opening brief on appeal failed to challenge this specific ruling. It was only in the government's response brief that the appellee flagged this as a potential error by the defense, framing it as an attempt by Cutbank to "thwart" cross-examination efforts.

Judge Loken's reaction to this tactical maneuver was immediate and stinging. Writing for a unanimous panel, he noted, "We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal." This language serves as a potent reminder of the adversarial system's boundaries. Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a), an appellant's brief must contain a statement of issues presented for review; issues not raised are typically deemed waived or forfeited. By injecting a new argument in its response brief, the government violated the spirit—if not always the letter—of efficient briefing, forcing the court to address a matter neither party had properly framed as a central contention. The Eighth Circuit's irritation signals a growing impatience with briefing strategies that treat reply mechanisms as opportunities to sandbag opponents with fresh theories rather than responding to arguments actually made.

However, despite this procedural rebuke, the court did not simply ignore the issue or declare it waived. Instead, it pivoted to address the merits under a plain error standard, concluding that even if preserved, Cutbank's claim lacked substance because extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be authenticated. This creates a fascinating doctrinal snapshot: The court criticized the expansion of issues yet engaged in a *sua sponte* review of an unpreserved claim to confirm its lack of merit. This approach suggests that while the Eighth Circuit views failure to preserve issues as a serious breach of advocacy discipline worthy of judicial censure, it stops short of treating such failures as absolute jurisdictional bars when innocence or fundamental fairness is not at stake. The court appears willing to overlook procedural defaults by parties if doing so allows it to definitively close off avenues for future litigation and affirm judgments supported by substantial evidence.

For practitioners in the Eighth Circuit, *Cutbank* offers a dual warning. First, do not expect leniency if you fail to preserve your client's arguments; while judges may dig into unpreserved issues to ensure justice is served, they will likely do so with visible annoyance and apply rigorous standards like plain error review rather than de novo scrutiny. Second, and perhaps more surprisingly for government attorneys, expandingthe scope of an appeal in a response brief is a risky strategy that can alienate the bench without yielding strategic gain. # Double Counting or Statutory Mandate? Navigating the Intersection of ACCA Mandatory Minimums and USSG § 2K2.1 Enhancements

On October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit issued a terse but significant reaffirmation of the mechanical rigidity inherent in the federal sentencing guidelines when they collide with statutory mandatory minimums. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court affirmed the 292-month sentence of a felon who argued that layering a specific offense-level enhancement atop an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) finding violated the fundamental proportionality principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). While the underlying facts of the case—a terrifying episode of domestic violence involving a sawed-off rifle and a standoff with police—are undeniably aggravating, Cutbank's legal argument touched on a persistent tension in federal sentencing: does the Guidelines' "greater of" rule mandate a form of impermissible double counting that renders a sentence substantively unreasonable? The Eighth Circuit's answer was a definitive no, signaling that in the hierarchy of sentencing calculations, the arithmetic of the Guidelines trumps abstract appeals to proportionality when the defendant is a violent recidivist.

The crux of Cutbank's appeal lay not in his conviction, which rested on overwhelming evidence including DNA matches and eyewitness testimony regarding his possession of a modified Marlin .22 caliber rifle, but in the mathematical architecture of his punishment. After a jury found Cutbank subject to ACCA penalties due to three prior violent felonies, the district court calculated his advisory guidelines range. Under USSG § 4B1.4, which governs armed career criminals, the court must apply whichever calculation yields the highest offense level: the standard career offender provision, the ACCA statutory minimum-based calculation, or the ordinary offense level adjusted for specific characteristics. Here, the Probation Office applied a four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because Cutbank possessed the firearm "in connection with another felony"—specifically, the kidnapping and assault of his girlfriend. This bumped his total offense level to 35, resulting in a range that exceeded what the bare ACCA floor would have dictated. Cutbank contended that this stacking of enhancements produced a sentence "greater than necessary," essentially punishing him twice for the same conduct that already triggered his status as an armed career criminal.

The Eighth Circuit, speaking through Judge Loken, dismantled this argument by adhering strictly to the procedural roadmap laid out by the Supreme Court in *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*. The court emphasized that appellate review of substantive reasonableness presupposes a procedurally sound calculation of the Guidelines range. Because § 4B1.4 explicitly commands courts to select the greatest applicable offense level, and because § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) plainly authorizes an enhancement for firearm possession connected to distinct felony conduct (regardless of whether charges were filed for that underlying felony), the district court committed no procedural error. The panel noted that the Guidelines are advisory, yet once calculated correctly, a within-Guidelines sentence carries a presumption of reasonableness. By pointing to Cutbank's extensive criminal history—nearly a dozen prior felonies—and the brutal nature of the instant offense, which included holding victims at gunpoint in their own home and assaulting law enforcement, the court concluded that any perceived "double counting" was merely Congress and the Sentencing Commission's deliberate choice to escalate penalties for dangerous combinations of conduct.

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* illustrates the diminishing returns of invoking § 3553(a)'s "parsimony principle" as a standalone shield against cumulative Guidelines enhancements in violent crime cases. The defendant's argument relied on a normative claim—that adding layers to an already severe statutory framework violates fairness—but failed to engage with the textual mandate of § 4B1.4(b). The Eighth Circuit's dismissal suggests that unless a defendant can demonstrate thatthe underlying conduct was already fully accounted for in a way that makes further enhancement logically impossible (a high bar given that ACCA focuses on criminal history while § 2K2.1 focuses on instantaneous conduct), courts will view these enhancements as complementary rather than redundant. The opinion implicitly rejects the notion that reaching an ACCA status creates a ceiling for judicial discretion; instead, it treats ACCA as a floor upon which additional aggravating factors can be built if supported by specific guideline provisions.

The doctrinal implications of this decision extend beyond Cutbank's specific circumstances. It reinforces a circuit split trend where courts increasingly treat Guidelines calculations as distinct silos: one for criminal history (ACCA/Career Offender) and one for offense conduct (Specific Offense Characteristics). By affirming that possession "in connection with another felony" remains valid even when that felony is part ofthe narrative leading to an ACCA designation, the Eighth Circuit ensures that defendants who commit new violent acts while possessing firearms face compounded consequences. This approach prioritizes deterrence and incapacitation over individualized mitigation in cases involving repeat violent offenders. For defense practitioners, *Cutbank* serves as a stark warning: challenging substantive reasonableness based solely onthe aggregate severity of overlapping enhancements is unlikely to succeed absent extraordinary circumstances or clear procedural miscalculations.

Cutbank* confirms that inthe Eighth Circuit, the machinery ofthe Sentencing Guidelines operates with little tolerance for equitable overrides when textually authorized enhancements align with egregious facts.
