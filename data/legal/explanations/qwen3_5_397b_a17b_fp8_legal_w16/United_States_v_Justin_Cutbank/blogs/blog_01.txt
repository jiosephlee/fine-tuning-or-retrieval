Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic Acts vs. Propensity: The Eighth Circuit's Expansive View of 'Continuous Possession' in Felon-in-Possession Cases

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit faced a familiar tension in federal criminal trials: how much of a defendant's past conduct can the government introduce to prove present guilt without crossing the line into forbidden character assassination? The case involved Justin Cutbank, a felon with a volatile history who held his girlfriend at gunpoint during a drug-fueled episode before engaging in a standoff with police. While the facts of the underlying crime were gruesome and the evidence of guilt overwhelming, the appellate panel's handling of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) offers a troubling glimpse into how courts are increasingly blurring the line between "intrinsic" evidence and prohibited propensity proof. By relying on a broad theory of "continuous possession," the Eighth Circuit has effectively carved out a significant exception to the safeguards designed to keep prior bad acts out of the jury box, raising concerns about whether defendants in felon-in-possession cases can ever receive a trial untainted by their history with firearms.

The crux of Cutbank's evidentiary challenge centered on testimony from his girlfriend and a housemate, both of whom stated they had seen Cutbank with the specific sawed-off rifle used in the crime on multiple occasions prior to the night of the assault. Cutbank objected, arguing this was classic propensity evidence—testimony intended to show that because he owned and used guns before, he likely possessed one on the night in question. Under Rule 404(b), such evidence is generally inadmissible to prove character or action in conformity therewith. However, the district court, affirmed unanimously by Judges Loken and his colleagues, categorized these prior sightings not as extrinsic bad acts, but as "intrinsic" evidence necessary to prove ownership and control. Citing *United States v. Battle*, the court reasoned that prior possession of the same weapon is directly relevant to establishing later possession, thereby framing the testimony as proof of a continuous chain of custody rather than a discrete prior bad act.

This reasoning, while procedurally efficient for prosecutors, stretches the concept of intrinsic evidence to its breaking point. Traditionally, intrinsic evidence refers to acts that are part of the same transaction or series of transactions as the charged offense—essentially, what happened immediately before, during, or after the crime to complete the story. By contrast, witnessing a defendant with a gun days, weeks, or months earlier is arguably distinct from the charged instance of possession. Yet, by labeling this "continuous possession," the Eighth Circuit allows prosecutors to bypass the strict notice requirements and balancing tests of Rule 404(b). The practical effect is profound: in any case where a specific firearm is identified, witnesses can testify to every previous instance they saw the defendant with that gun, regardless of how far back those instances occurred or how prejudicial they might be. The jury hears not just about the crime charged, but about a lifestyle of gun ownership that reinforces the very stereotype Rule 404(b) seeks to exclude—that once a felon touches a gun, they never really let go.

The danger lies in the circularity of this logic. To convict under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove knowing possession at a specific time. If direct evidence of possession at that moment is ambiguous—for instance, if the gun was found nearby but not on the person—the government might be tempted to fill the gap with testimony about prior access. In *Cutbank*, there was DNA onthe weapon and eyewitnesses placing it in his hand duringthe kidnapping, so any error was undoubtedly harmless underthe high barof *DeMarce*.
