Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# The Hollow Shield: Why 'Harmless Error' Analysis Renders Evidentiary Objections Futile in High-Stakes ACCA Prosecutions

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided by the Eighth Circuit on October 7, 2025, the court affirmed a staggering 292-month sentence for a felon in possession of a firearm under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). On its face, the result is unsurprising: Justin Cutbank's conduct was undeniably horrific. After a night of drug use, he held his girlfriend at gunpoint, terrorized her children, fled to a neighbor's garage, and bit a police officer while claiming to possess a weapon. The physical evidence—a sawed-off rifle with his DNA—and the testimony of multiple witnesses painted a picture of guilt that seems impervious to legal technicality. Yet, buried within Section II(E) of Judge Loken's unanimous opinion lies a troubling jurisprudential signal for criminal defense practitioners. By applying the "harmless error" doctrine with such sweeping breadth, the Eighth Circuit suggests that in cases involving grotesque facts and overwhelming physical evidence, the Federal Rules of Evidence function less as rigid guardrails and more as suggestions that district courts can ignore with impunity.

The crux of the issue lies in how appellate courts weigh procedural missteps against the sheer weight of a defendant's culpability. Cutbank raised several evidentiary challenges on appeal, most notably regarding the admission of emotionally charged testimony and restrictions on cross-examination. Specifically, during cross-examination concerning inconsistencies in her story, the victim, D.F., volunteered that she disliked guns because her sister had been killed by one. Cutbank moved to strike this testimony as unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403. Similarly, the district court prevented defense counsel from reading portions of a police report into the record to impeach another witness, C.F., citing authentication issues under Rule 901. While these rulings present legitimate debatable questions regarding the boundaries of relevance and the right to confront witnesses through effective cross-examination, the Eighth Circuit declined to engage deeply with their merits. Instead, the court pivoted immediately to the harmless error standard, concluding that because the government's evidence was "substantial," any error could not have substantially swayed the jury.

This analytical shortcut exposes a structural flaw in modern appellate review of criminal convictions: the tendency for the "harmless error" doctrine to swallow the rule itself. In *Cutbank*, the court explicitly notes that it will not reverse unless the jury was "substantially swayed" by improper evidence. But this formulation creates a paradoxical incentive structure for trial judges and prosecutors. When a defendant is accused of acts as visceral as domestic terrorism involving sawed-off shotguns and child endangerment, almost any evidentiary error becomes retroactively "harmless" because the remaining proof of guilt is so overpowering. The logic is circular: the more egregious the crime and the stronger the physical evidence, the more latitude a district court has to bend evidentiary rules without fear of reversal. Consequently, the protective purpose of rules like 403 (balancing probative value against prejudice) and 613 (impeachment by prior inconsistent statement) is nullified precisely in those high-stakes cases where rigorous adherence to procedure is most critical to ensuring a fair trial process.

The implications for defense strategy are stark and disheartening. In Section II(E), Judge Loken dismisses Cutbank's concerns about being thwarted during cross-examination of C.F. regarding her prior statements to police. The court holds that even if this restriction was erroneous—which it implies might be possible given its cursory treatment—it was harmless given DNA matches and other corroborating testimony. This approach effectively tells defense counsel that objecting to evidentiary overreach is often a futile exercise if the client faces mountains of incriminating physical evidence. If an appellate court presumes that jurors are incapable of distinguishing between admissible facts and inflammatory character attacks once they know a defendant bit a police officer and held a family hostage, then meticulous trial preservation loses its utility. The doctrine assumes a rational actor model of juror deliberation that may not hold up when emotions run high; yet, it uses that assumption to shield convictions from scrutiny.

Furthermore, this application of harmless error risks eroding long-term institutional integrity within federal courts. The Federal Rules of Evidence are designed not merely to filter out unreliable data but to maintain public confidence in the fairness of judicial proceedings. When appellate courts routinely bless questionable rulings by pointing to DNA tests or confessions elsewhere in the record, they signal that procedural justice is secondary to outcome certainty in "slam dunk" cases. Over time, this encourages district courts to adopt a utilitarian approach to evidence: if it helps secure a conviction against a clearly guilty party, admit it and let appellate cleanup handle any fallout via harmless error analysis. This dynamic disproportionately impacts defendants facing mandatory minimums under statutes like ACCA, where every month counts and every procedural safeguard serves as a check on prosecutorial power and judicial bias.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* stands as a grim reminder that in our current legal landscape due process protections are elastic; they stretch furthest when society demands retribution most loudly while snapping back tightest when stakes are low or ambiguity exists elsewhere rather than here today!
