Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Double Counting or Statutory Mandate? Navigating the Intersection of ACCA Mandatory Minimums and USSG § 2K2.1 Enhancements

On October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit issued a terse but significant reaffirmation of the mechanical rigidity inherent in the federal sentencing guidelines when they collide with statutory mandatory minimums. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court affirmed the 292-month sentence of a felon who argued that layering a specific offense-level enhancement atop an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) finding violated the fundamental proportionality principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). While the underlying facts of the case—a terrifying episode of domestic violence involving a sawed-off rifle and a standoff with police—are undeniably aggravating, Cutbank's legal argument touched on a persistent tension in federal sentencing: does the Guidelines' "greater of" rule mandate a form of impermissible double counting that renders a sentence substantively unreasonable? The Eighth Circuit's answer was a definitive no, signaling that in the hierarchy of sentencing calculations, the arithmetic of the Guidelines trumps abstract appeals to proportionality when the defendant is a violent recidivist.

The crux of Cutbank's appeal lay not in his conviction, which rested on overwhelming evidence including DNA matches and eyewitness testimony regarding his possession of a modified Marlin .22 caliber rifle, but in the mathematical architecture of his punishment. After a jury found Cutbank subject to ACCA penalties due to three prior violent felonies, the district court calculated his advisory guidelines range. Under USSG § 4B1.4, which governs armed career criminals, the court must apply whichever calculation yields the highest offense level: the standard career offender provision, the ACCA statutory minimum-based calculation, or the ordinary offense level adjusted for specific characteristics. Here, the Probation Office applied a four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because Cutbank possessed the firearm "in connection with another felony"—specifically, the kidnapping and assault of his girlfriend. This bumped his total offense level to 35, resulting in a range that exceeded what the bare ACCA floor would have dictated. Cutbank contended that this stacking of enhancements produced a sentence "greater than necessary," essentially punishing him twice for the same conduct that already triggered his status as an armed career criminal.

The Eighth Circuit, speaking through Judge Loken, dismantled this argument by adhering strictly to the procedural roadmap laid out by the Supreme Court in *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*. The court emphasized that appellate review of substantive reasonableness presupposes a procedurally sound calculation of the Guidelines range. Because § 4B1.4 explicitly commands courts to select the greatest applicable offense level, and because § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) plainly authorizes an enhancement for firearm possession connected to distinct felony conduct (regardless of whether charges were filed for that underlying felony), the district court committed no procedural error. The panel noted that the Guidelines are advisory, yet once calculated correctly, a within-Guidelines sentence carries a presumption of reasonableness. By pointing to Cutbank's extensive criminal history—nearly a dozen prior felonies—and the brutal nature of the instant offense, which included holding victims at gunpoint in their own home and assaulting law enforcement, the court concluded that any perceived "double counting" was merely Congress and the Sentencing Commission's deliberate choice to escalate penalties for dangerous combinations of conduct.

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* illustrates the diminishing returns of invoking § 3553(a)'s "parsimony principle" as a standalone shield against cumulative Guidelines enhancements in violent crime cases. The defendant's argument relied on a normative claim—that adding layers to an already severe statutory framework violates fairness—but failed to engage with the textual mandate of § 4B1.4(b). The Eighth Circuit's dismissal suggests that unless a defendant can demonstrate thatthe underlying conduct was already fully accounted for in a way that makes further enhancement logically impossible (a high bar given that ACCA focuses on criminal history while § 2K2.1 focuses on instantaneous conduct), courts will view these enhancements as complementary rather than redundant. The opinion implicitly rejects the notion that reaching an ACCA status creates a ceiling for judicial discretion; instead, it treats ACCA as a floor upon which additional aggravating factors can be built if supported by specific guideline provisions.

The doctrinal implications of this decision extend beyond Cutbank's specific circumstances. It reinforces a circuit split trend where courts increasingly treat Guidelines calculations as distinct silos: one for criminal history (ACCA/Career Offender) and one for offense conduct (Specific Offense Characteristics). By affirming that possession "in connection with another felony" remains valid even when that felony is part ofthe narrative leading to an ACCA designation, the Eighth Circuit ensures that defendants who commit new violent acts while possessing firearms face compounded consequences. This approach prioritizes deterrence and incapacitation over individualized mitigation in cases involving repeat violent offenders. For defense practitioners, *Cutbank* serves as a stark warning: challenging substantive reasonableness based solely onthe aggregate severity of overlapping enhancements is unlikely to succeed absent extraordinary circumstances or clear procedural miscalculations.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* confirms that inthe Eighth Circuit, the machinery ofthe Sentencing Guidelines operates with little tolerance for equitable overrides when textually authorized enhancements align with egregious facts.
