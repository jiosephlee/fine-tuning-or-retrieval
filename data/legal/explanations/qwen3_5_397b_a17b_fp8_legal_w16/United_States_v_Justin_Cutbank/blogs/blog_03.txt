Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Double Counting Danger? Navigating the Intersection of ACCA Mandates and USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancements

In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided by the Eighth Circuit on October 7, 2025, the court affirmed a staggering 292-month sentence for a felon in possession of a firearm, reinforcing a rigid approach to sentencing calculations that often leaves defendants feeling the weight of duplicative punitive mechanisms. While the headline holding concerns the affirmation of a conviction stemming from a harrowing night of domestic terror and a standoff with police, the true doctrinal friction lies in the sentencing phase. Defendant Justin Cutbank argued that stacking the Armed Career Criminal Act's (ACCA) mandatory minimum enhancements with the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines' "connection with another felony" enhancement resulted in an impermissible double counting of his violent conduct. The Eighth Circuit's dismissal of this argument offers a stark illustration of how appellate courts are increasingly insulating district court discretion against challenges to overlapping statutory and guideline provisions, effectively closing the door on substantive reasonableness claims that do not rise to the level of procedural error.

The facts underlying *Cutbank* are undeniably grave, setting a high bar for any sympathy regarding the length of incarceration. After consuming methamphetamine and alcohol, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint, threatened her life, and struck her with the weapon before fleeing and engaging in a violent resistance against law enforcement. A jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), finding he possessed a sawed-off rifle and had three prior violent felony convictions. This triggered ACCA's fifteen-year mandatory minimum floor. However, the district court went further, applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which adds four levels to the offense score if the firearm was possessed "in connection with another felony offense." Because the underlying conduct involved kidnapping and assault—felonies distinct from the mere possession charge—the Probation Service Report recommended, and the district court accepted, a total offense level of 35. This calculation pushed the advisory range well above the ACCA floor, resulting in the 292-month sentence.

Cutbank's appeal focused narrowly on this sentencing enhancement. His core contention was not that he lacked prior convictions or that he didn't possess the gun during other crimes, but rather that applying both the ACCA mandate and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement punished him twice for the same violent behavior. He argued this duality violated the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The Eighth Circuit, speaking through Judge Loken, sidestepped the philosophical debate on double counting by framing the issue strictly through the lens of procedural versus substantive review. The court noted that because Cutbank did not object to the factual findings in the Presentence Investigation Report at trial, there was no procedural error in calculating the guidelines range. Once the range is correctly calculated, appellate review shifts to substantive reasonableness, where within-guidelines sentences carry a presumption of validity.

The court's reasoning reveals a deliberate judicial strategy to contain sentencing disputes within the bounds of *Gall v. United States*. By characterizing Cutbank's complaint as a challenge to substantive reasonableness rather than a miscalculation of the guidelines, the Eighth Circuit insulated its decision from de novo review. The opinion emphasizes that district courts need not provide a "mechanical recitation" of § 3553(a) factors so long as it is clear they considered them. Since District Judge implicitly rejected Cutbank's leniency arguments by imposing a within-range sentence amidst egregious facts—including domestic violence in front of children and assaulting an officer—the appellate court found no abuse of discretion. This approach effectively neutralizes "double counting" arguments unless Congress explicitly forbids specific combinations of statutes and guidelines, which it has not done for ACCA and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* highlights a growing tension in federal sentencing jurisprudence:the divergence between mathematical precision in guideline calculations and holistic justice under § 3553(a). Critics might argue that when ACCA already imposes a severe mandatory floor based on past violence, layering an additional enhancement for current violence creates a compounding effect that distorts proportionality. However, the Eighth Circuit's stance suggests that such policy grievances are best aired at sentencing hearings, not on appeal. The court signaled that absent a specific prohibition in the Guidelines Manual or statute, overlapping enhancements are features of the system, not bugs. By deferring heavily to the district judge's assessment of § 3553(a) factors, circuit courts are essentially outsourcing moral judgments about "excessive" punishment to trial judges who witness firsthand brutality like that exhibited by Cutbank.

The downstream effects of *Cutbank* will likely be felt most acutely in cases where defendants have strong mitigation narratives but weak procedural records.
