Chapter 2: # Admissibility of Prior Possession and Intrinsic Evidence

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit confronted a critical evidentiary threshold common in felon-in-possession prosecutions: determining when evidence of a defendant's prior interactions with a specific firearm crosses the line from impermissible character evidence to admissible proof of an essential element of the crime. The central dispute arose from the testimony of two witnesses, D.F. and C.F., who stated they had seen the defendant holding the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on occasions preceding the night of the charged offense. The defense argued this testimony violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) by suggesting the defendant had a propensity to possess weapons. However, the district court admitted the testimony as "intrinsic" evidence, a classification vital to proving that Cutbank knowingly possessed the specific weapon recovered by police. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed this ruling, reinforcing the distinction between extrinsic bad acts used to show criminal disposition and intrinsic evidence necessary to complete the story of the charged crime.

## The Defense Argument: Propensity vs. Character Evidence under Rule 404(b)

At trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony regarding his prior handling of the rifle, framing his objection through the lens of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). This rule generally prohibits the introduction of evidence concerning other crimes, wrongs, or acts for the purpose of proving a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in accordance with that character. Cutbank's argument posited that allowing D.F. and C.F. to testify that they had seen him with the gun on previous nights was an attempt to paint him as someone who habitually carried weapons, thereby inviting the jury to convict him based on his perceived dangerousness rather than proof that he possessed the gun on November 19-20, 2020. By labeling this "propensity evidence," the defense sought to trigger the strict safeguards of Rule 404(b), which requires that such evidence serve a non-character purpose, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, or plan, and that its probative value not be substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. Had the court accepted this characterization, the testimony might have been excluded unless the government could demonstrate a specific permissible purpose distinct from showing Cutbank's general tendency to arm himself.

## The Legal Standard: Distinguishing 'Extrinsic' Bad Acts from 'Intrinsic' Evidence

The resolution of Cutbank's appeal hinged on a fundamental doctrinal distinction in federal evidence law: the difference between "extrinsic" bad acts governed by Rule 404(b) and "intrinsic" evidence that falls outside the rule's scope entirely. Extrinsic evidence refers to uncharged misconduct that is separate in time and place from the charged offense; such evidence is presumptively inadmissible for propensity purposes unless it fits within a specific exception. In contrast, intrinsic evidence consists of acts that are part of the same transaction or series of transactions as the charged offense, or evidence that is necessary to provide context to make sense of how or why an event occurred so quickly or naturally follows another event. Courts often describe intrinsic evidence as completing "the story" of what happened during or leading up to immediately before or after commission of an alleged crime so quickly thereafter so as reasonably be explained away otherwise would seem puzzling isolated occurrence admission does violate Fed.R.Evid..
