Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does FRE 613 interact with authentication requirements when impeaching a witness with a police report they didn't write?
Question:
During cross-examination of witness C.F., defense counsel tried to read from a police report containing C.F.'s prior inconsistent statements. The court stopped this because C.F. didn't author the report. I thought FRE 613 allowed extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statements to impeach a witness. Why did the court require 'authentication' of the report itself? Is there a difference between using a document to refresh recollection versus introducing it as extrinsic evidence of inconsistency, and why did the fact that a third party (the officer) wrote down C.F.'s words matter so much here?
Answer:
The court's ruling rests on a fundamental distinction in the Federal Rules of Evidence: **Rule 613 governs the *procedure* for impeaching a witness with prior inconsistent statements, but it does not suspend the *authentication* requirements of Article IX.**

While FRE 613(b) permits the introduction of extrinsic evidence (such as a document) to prove a prior inconsistent statement if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny it, the proponent of that evidence must still lay a proper foundation under **FRE 901**. Authentication requires evidence "sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is."

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the defense sought to read from a police report to impeach witness C.F. The critical flaw was that C.F. did not author the report; a police officer did. Therefore, the document was not self-authenticating as C.F.'s own statement. To admit the report as extrinsic evidence of what C.F. said, the defense needed to authenticate it by establishing that:
1.  The officer recorded C.F.'s statements accurately, and
2.  The document presented was indeed the record made by that officer at that time.

Because the defense attempted to introduce the contents of the report without calling the officer (the author) or otherwise providing testimony linking C.F.'s oral statements to the specific text in the document, they failed to meet their burden under FRE 901. As the Eighth Circuit succinctly noted, citing *United States v. Cumbie*: "Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated."

**Distinction Between Refreshing Recollection and Extrinsic Evidence**
Your question regarding refreshing recollection touches on a related but distinct doctrinal area. Under **FRE 612**, a witness may use a writing to refresh their memory while testifying. If used solely for refreshing, the document itself is generally not admitted into evidence as substantive proof or as an exhibit for the jury (unless justice requires), and strict authentication of the document's contents is often less rigorous because the witness is adopting the content through their refreshed testimony.

However, when counsel attempts to introduce the document itself as **extrinsic evidence** under FRE 613(b)—specifically to prove the inconsistency after the witness denies it or fails to recall—the document becomes independent evidence offered for its truth regarding what was previously said. At this stage, standard authentication rules apply fully. Since C.F. could not authenticate a document she did not write, and no other witness verified its accuracy, the court correctly excluded it. The fact that a third party wrote down C.F.'s words matters because it introduces potential issues of fabrication, misinterpretation, or selective recording by that third party, which authentication rules are designed to filter out before evidence reaches the jury.
