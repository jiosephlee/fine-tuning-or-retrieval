Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why did the court reject the argument that mentioning the victim's sister's death was unfairly prejudicial under FRE 403?
Question:
When D.F. explained why her memory of guns was fuzzy, she mentioned her sister was killed by a gun. The defense moved to strike this as unfairly prejudicial emotional testimony. The court denied it, citing FRE 403 and noting it responded to cross-examination about inconsistencies. I struggle with how courts balance probative value against unfair prejudice here. Doesn't mentioning a family murder inherently inflame the jury against the defendant more than it explains memory loss? At what point does an explanation for poor recollection become too emotionally charged to be admissible?
Answer:
The court rejected the defense's Rule 403 argument primarily because the testimony was **responsive to cross-examination** regarding the witness's credibility, and the defendant failed to demonstrate how the probative value was substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice.

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 403, evidence may be excluded only if its probative value is *substantially* outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. This is a high bar that favors admissibility. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed this specifically in Section II.D.

Here is the breakdown of the court's reasoning based on the opinion:

**1. The Testimony Was Probative of Credibility, Not Just Emotion**
The mention of the sister's death was not introduced gratuitously to inflame the jury. Instead, it arose directly during cross-examination when D.F. was confronted with prior inconsistent statements about whether she remembered seeing a gun. D.F. explained her lack of familiarity and fear of guns by stating, "as [her] sister was killed by a gun."
*   **Legal Significance:** This explanation provided a specific reason for her memory gaps or hesitation, directly rehabilitating her credibility against the defense's attack on her consistency. Without this context, the jury might have viewed her inconsistency as deception rather than trauma-induced avoidance. Therefore, the probative value regarding her truthfulness and memory was significant.

**2. Lack of Demonstrated Unfair Prejudice**
The appellate court noted that Cutbank "points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, and none is apparent."
*   **Legal Standard:** "Unfair prejudice" under Rule 403 refers to an undue tendency to suggest decision on an improper basis (such as emotion), rather than just evidence that damages the defendant's case. While hearing about a family member's murder is certainly emotional, the court found nothing in the record suggesting it confused the issues or caused the jury to ignore the evidence regarding the firearm possession charge.
*   **Context Matters:** The opinion further notes that another witness (C.F.) also mentioned the sister's death without objection, suggesting the information was part of the natural narrative context known to those present, rather than a shocking revelation inserted solely for dramatic effect.

**3. Broad Discretion and Harmless Error**
Even if there were a marginal error, the court emphasized two additional hurdles for reversal:
*   **Discretion:** Trial courts have "broad evidentiary discretion," particularly in managing cross-examination and assessing immediate courtroom dynamics (*Sprint/United Mgmt. Co.*).
*   **Harmless Error:** Citing *United States v. DeMarce*, the court reiterated that reversal requires showing the jury was "substantially swayed" by the evidence. Given the overwhelming physical evidence (DNA on the gun, recovery of the weapon) and testimonial evidence regarding Cutbank's actions that night, any potential prejudice from D.F.'s brief comment did not influence the verdict.

**Conclusion**
The court held that explaining *why* a witness has fuzzy memories of guns (due to past trauma) is highly relevant to assessing their reliability when attacked on inconsistency. Because Cutbank could not identify specific confusion or unfair prejudice that overwhelmed this relevance, and given the strength of the remaining evidence, the admission of the testimony fell well within the district court's discretion under Rule 403.
