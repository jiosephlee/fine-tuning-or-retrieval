Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the 'harmless error' standard apply cumulatively to multiple evidentiary rulings in a criminal trial?
Question:
The opinion lists several contested evidentiary rulings (prior possession testimony, timing of victim statements, impeachment limits, etc.) but ultimately affirms because any potential errors were 'harmless' given the overwhelming evidence. The court cites *DeMarce* saying reversal only happens if the jury was 'substantially swayed.' How do appellate courts analyze whether multiple small errors add up to substantial prejudice? If there were indeed five questionable rulings, doesn't their combined effect create more doubt than looking at them individually as harmless? What is the threshold for 'cumulative error' in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
In the Eighth Circuit, the "cumulative error" doctrine does not lower the threshold for reversal; rather, it requires the appellate court to assess whether the **combined effect** of multiple actual errors deprived the defendant of a fair trial. However, a critical prerequisite often overlooked by appellants is that there must be **multiple actual errors** to cumulate.

Based on *United States v. Cutbank*, here is how the court applies this standard:

### 1. The Threshold: Actual Errors Required
The doctrine of cumulative error cannot rescue a conviction if the individual rulings complained of were not erroneous in the first place. In *Cutbank*, the appellant argued that five distinct evidentiary rulings (regarding prior possession testimony, limiting instructions, impeachment limits, etc.) should be viewed together as prejudicial.

The Eighth Circuit explicitly rejected this approach because it found **no merit** in any of the underlying claims. As the court noted in Section II, "None of the contentions has merit." Because the district court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the intrinsic evidence of prior gun possession, properly handled hearsay/confrontation issues via live testimony, and correctly required authentication for extrinsic impeachment evidence, there were no errors to aggregate. Without at least two or more actual errors, a cumulative error analysis is unnecessary and inapplicable.

### 2. The Standard: "Substantial Prejudice"
Even if multiple errors had occurred, the standard for reversal remains high. Citing *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019), the *Cutbank* opinion reiterates that a conviction will only be overturned based on cumulative errors if there is **"substantial prejudice to the defendant."**

This aligns with the harmless error standard cited from *United States v. DeMarce*: reversal occurs only if the jury was **"substantially swayed"** by the improperly admitted evidence. The court looks at the totality of the record to determine if the errors, working in concert, influenced the verdict. If the evidence of guilt is overwhelming—as it was in *Cutbank* due to DNA matching, victim identification, and physical recovery of the weapon—the likelihood that minor or non-existent errors cumulatively swayed the jury is negligible.

### 3. Application to Your Hypothetical
You asked: *"If there were indeed five questionable rulings, doesn't their combined effect create more doubt than looking at them individually as harmless?"*

Theoretically, yes. If an appellate court finds that five separate rulings were indeed **erroneous** (not just "questionable"), it would then analyze whether their collective impact undermined confidence in the outcome. However, *Cutbank* illustrates that appellate courts frequently dispose of cumulative error arguments by first concluding that the individual rulings were correct applications of evidentiary rules (e.g., Rule 404(b) intrinsic evidence exceptions or Rule 613 authentication requirements).

In this specific opinion, the court affirmed because:
1.  Individually, none of the rulings constituted an abuse of discretion.
2.  Consequently, there was no pool of errors to accumulate.
3.  Even assuming arguendo that errors existed, the government's evidence was so "substantial" that any potential error would have had only a "slight influence on the verdict," failing to meet the *DeMarce* standard of substantially swaying the jury.

**Conclusion:** In the Eighth Circuit, you cannot bootstrap weak individual claims into a strong cumulative one. You must first demonstrate that multiple actual errors occurred; only then will the court weigh their aggregated weight against the strength of the prosecution's case to see if "substantial prejudice" resulted.
