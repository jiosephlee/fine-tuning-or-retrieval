Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What is the distinction between 'intrinsic' evidence and Rule 404(b) 'other acts' evidence in felon-in-possession cases?
Question:
In *United States v. Cutbank*, the defendant argued that testimony about him possessing the gun on previous occasions was improper propensity evidence under FRE 404(b). However, the Eighth Circuit ruled this was 'intrinsic' evidence. I am confused about the legal test for this distinction. Why does prior possession of the *same* weapon automatically become intrinsic to the charge of possession, rather than being treated as character evidence showing he is the type of person who owns guns? How does the court define the boundary where prior acts stop being part of the story and start being impermissible propensity proof?
Answer:
In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit draws the line between "intrinsic" evidence and Rule 404(b) "other acts" evidence based on **relevance to the specific element of possession** versus **propensity to commit crimes**.

The distinction turns on whether the prior act is necessary to complete the story of the charged crime or merely suggests the defendant has a bad character. In a felon-in-possession case under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g), the government must prove the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm. Possession is often a continuing state rather than a single instantaneous act. Therefore, evidence showing the defendant held the *same* weapon immediately before the charged incident is deemed "intrinsic" because it logically bridges the gap to prove he maintained control over that specific object at the time of the offense.

The court in *Cutbank* explicitly relies on *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), to articulate this test: **"Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon."**

Here is how the court applies this logic to your specific confusion:

1.  **Same Weapon, Continuous Control:** The witnesses (D.F. and C.F.) testified they had seen Cutbank with the specific sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle on previous occasions. The court viewed this not as evidence that Cutbank is "the type of person who owns guns" (which would be impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b)), but as factual proof establishing a chain of custody and control leading up to the night he fled with it. Without this context, it might be harder to prove he knowingly possessed *that specific* gun when he barricaded himself in the garage, especially given his erratic behavior and claims about listening devices.
2.  **Part of the Story vs. Character Proof:** The boundary you asked about is defined by whether the evidence explains *how* or *why* the charged possession occurred, or simply attacks the defendant's character. In *Cutbank*, the prior sightings were integral to demonstrating "continuous possession of the firearm before the incident." Because these acts involved the exact instrumentality of the crime and helped establish the timeline of control, they fell inside the boundary of intrinsic evidence.
3.  **No Rule 404(b) Analysis Required:** Because the evidence was classified as intrinsic, it did not trigger Rule 404(b)'s prohibition on character evidence. Rule 404(b) only applies to "other crimes, wrongs, or acts" offered to show propensity. If an act is intrinsic—meaning it is part of the same transaction or series of transactions as the charged offense—it is admissible so long as it satisfies Rule 403 (probative value outweighs prejudice). The court noted that Cutbank failed to cite any authority refuting this established precedent regarding continuous possession of the same weapon.

In short, prior possession becomes intrinsic in these cases when it serves as direct circumstantial evidence of **ownership or control** over the specific contraband at issue, rather than serving as a general indicator of criminal disposition. The Eighth Circuit treats prior handling of the *same* gun as a logical prerequisite to proving current possession, placing it firmly outside the scope of Rule 404(b).
