# Case Context: Facts, Procedure, and Statutory Framework

To fully grasp the Eighth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, a student must first reconstruct the narrative of the criminal conduct, the specific statutory violations charged, and the procedural mechanisms that led to the appellate review. This case serves as a practical illustration of how federal courts handle complex evidentiary disputes arising from volatile domestic incidents and how sentencing enhancements operate under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). The opinion affirms a conviction and a lengthy prison sentence, rejecting arguments that the trial court erred in admitting testimony about the defendant's prior relationship with the weapon or in calculating his sentencing guideline range. Understanding the gravity of the underlying facts is essential, as they inform both the jury's verdict on possession and the district court's application of sentencing enhancements for conducting a felony while armed.

## Factual Background: The Domestic Incident, Flight, and Confrontation with Law Enforcement

The factual predicate for this prosecution began late on the night of November 19, 2020, following Cutbank's consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine. The incident escalated rapidly within a private residence when Cutbank awakened his girlfriend, referred to in the records as D.F., and pointed a firearm directly at her face. Driven by paranoid delusions regarding "listening devices" and external threats, he forced D.F. into a closet at gunpoint. For a period D.F. described as feeling like hours, Cutbank held her captive, issuing death threats and physically assaulting her by striking her face with the weapon. The situation further deteriorated when he ordered D.F. to wake other occupants of the home, including her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F., thereby expanding the scope of terror within the household. Eventually, Cutbank fled the scene, taking both D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm.

Following his escape from the residence, Cutbank's interaction with law enforcement became increasingly confrontational. Police received reports of an intruder in a garage located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home. Upon arrival, officers found Cutbank barricaded inside. Over several hours, he made erratic statements and claimed to be armed. When officers finally moved to detain him, Cutbank resisted violently, biting an officer in the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer's service weapon. This sequence of events—from the domestic hostage situation to the violent resistance against police—forms the core factual matrix that justified both the initial charges and the subsequent sentencing enhancements related to reckless endangerment and felony commission.

## Physical Evidence: Recovery of the Sawed-Off Rifle, DNA Matching, and Witness Identification

A critical component of the government's case relied on physical evidence linking Cutbank to the specific weapon used during the crimes. During their investigation, officers searched the likely path Cutbank took between D.F.'s home and the garage where he was apprehended. Along this route, they recovered three key items: D.F.'s missing cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had been illegally modified with a sawed-off barrel and stock. The modification of the firearm is legally significant, as sawed-off rifles are heavily regulated under federal law and often trigger specific sentencing considerations.

Forensic analysis provided a direct link between Cutbank and the recovered weapon; DNA found on the rifle matched Cutbank's profile. However, physical evidence alone does not always prove continuous possession or intent without contextual testimony. Consequently, both D.F. and C.F. testified at trial that they had seen Cutbank in possession of this specific sawed-off rifle on multiple occasions prior to the night of the incident. This testimony was pivotal in establishing that Cutbank did not merely stumble upon the weapon but maintained dominion and control over it leading up to and duringthe criminal episode. The combination of forensic DNA matching and eyewitness identification created a robust evidentiary foundation for the possession charge.

## Procedural Posture: Jury Verdict on 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)

Cutbank was indicted and tried in federal district court on charges stemming from his status as a prohibited person possessing a firearm. Specifically, he was charged with violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), which prohibits any person previously convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for more than one year from shipping, transporting, receiving, or possessing firearms or ammunition in interstate commerce. Furthermore, due to his extensive criminal history, he was charged under 18 U.S.C.A § 924(e), known as thte Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA).

# Admissibility of Prior Possession as Intrinsic Evidence

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a critical evidentiary threshold issue common in prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1): the admissibility of testimony regarding a defendant's prior interactions with the specific firearm alleged to be the subject of the crime. The central dispute arose when the defendant moved *in limine* to exclude testimony from witnesses D.F. and C.F., who stated they had seen Cutbank possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on occasions preceding the night of the charged offense. Cutbank argued that such testimony constituted impermissible "propensity evidence" prohibited by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally bars evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts used to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in accordance with that character. The district court rejected this argument, classifying the testimony as "intrinsic evidence," and the appellate court affirmed, holding that prior possession of the specific weapon is directly relevant to proving ownership, control, and the continuity of possession required for conviction.

## The Defense Argument: Claims of Improper Propensity Evidence Under Rule 404(b)

Cutbank's pretrial motion framed the witnesses' accounts of seeing him with the rifle in the past as classic "bad acts" evidence designed to paint him as a dangerous individual likely to possess firearms illegally. Under Rule 404(b), evidence of prior misconduct is inadmissible if its sole purpose is to suggest that because the defendant committed similar acts before, he must have committed the act charged. The defense contended that allowing D.F. and C.F. to testify about seeing Cutbank with the gun on previous occasions would invite the jury to convict based on his perceived criminal propensity rather than on proof that he possessed the weapon on November 19–20, 2020. This argument relies on a protective function of the Federal Rules of Evidence: preventing jurors from making the logical leap that a person with a history of handling weapons is automatically guilty of the current charge, regardless of the specific facts surrounding the incident in question. By categorizing this testimony as extrinsic bad acts, Cutbank sought to limit the trial evidence strictly to the events of the night he was arrested, isolating the moment of discovery from any historical context of his relationship with the firearm.

## The Legal Standard: Distinguishing Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Evidence in Firearm Cases

The resolution of Cutbank's appeal hinged on the legal distinction between "extrinsic" evidence governed by Rule 404(b) and "intrinsic" evidence which falls outside the rule's scope entirely. Extrinsic evidence refers to uncharged conduct that is separate and distinct fromthe charged offense, often occurring at a different time or place, and offered primarily to show character or propensity. In contrast, intrinsic evidence consists of acts that are part of the same transaction or series of transactions as the charged offense, or evidence that completes the story ofthe crime by providing immediate context. In firearm possession cases, courts frequently recognize that proving unlawful possession often requires demonstrating more than just a snapshot moment where a gun is found; it may require showing a continuing courseof conduct or established control overthe weapon. When evidence explains howthe defendant came into possessionofthe firearm, establishes their familiarity withit, or demonstrates ongoing dominionand control leading up tothe seizure,it is deemed intrinsic tot he charged crime. Becauseintrinsic evidenceisnotofferedto provecharacterbutrathertoproveanelementoftheoffenseortoprovidenecessarycontext,itdoesnottriggerthebarsofRule404(b).

# Trial Management: Hearsay, Confrontation, and Impeachment Standards

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of evidentiary and procedural challenges arising from a volatile domestic incident that escalated into a standoff with law enforcement. While the primary legal battle often centers on the elements of the crime or the calculation of sentencing guidelines, appellate review frequently turns on the trial court's management of evidence. This chapter analyzes how the appellate court evaluated the district judge's handling of hearsay, Confrontation Clause concerns, witness credibility, and impeachment mechanics. The opinion illustrates that appellate courts grant significant deference to trial judges regarding the timing of jury instructions and the admissibility of background information necessary to explain inconsistent testimony. Furthermore, it reinforces the principle that even if individual rulings were erroneous, a conviction will stand if the remaining evidence is so substantial that the error was harmless.

## Hearsay and Limiting Instructions: Timing of Jury Guidance

A central procedural dispute in *Cutbank* concerned the timing of jury instructions regarding out-of-court statements. Before trial, Cutbank requested a specific limiting instruction to ensure the jury understood they could not consider his girlfriend D.F.'s initial statements to police as substantive evidence of his guilt until she testified personally. He argued this was necessary to avoid hearsay violations and protect his confrontation rights. The district court declined to give this instruction immediately upon the introduction of the officers' testimony, opting instead to rely on final instructions at the close of trial. On appeal, Cutbank argued this delay constituted an abuse of discretion.

The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument by clarifying the standard for reviewing a district court's decision not to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction. Citing *United States v. Bennett*, the court reiterated that a trial judge need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the precise moment it is admitted, provided an appropriate instruction is given at the conclusion of the trial. The logic underlying this rule is practical; interrupting the flow of testimony with complex legal caveats can sometimes confuse jurors more than it helps. As long as the final charge clearly delineates how the evidence may be used, the timing of the warning is within the trial court's sound discretion. In *Cutbank*, because the record showed no hearsay was improperly admitted as substantive evidence without eventual clarification, and because D.F. ultimately testified, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion in delaying the instruction.

## Confrontation Clause Satisfaction: The Impact of Declarants Testifying in Person

Closely linked to the hearsay issue was Cutbank's assertion that admitting D.F.'s prior statements violated his Sixth Amendment right to confront witnesses against him. The Confrontation Clause generally prohibits the admission of testimonial hearsay unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. However, *Cutbank* serves as a straightforward example of how this constitutional safeguard is satisfied when the declarant appears in court.

The court relied on established precedent, specifically *United States v. Charboneau*, to hold that the Confrontation Clause is fully satisfied when hearsay declarants actually appear in court and testify in person about their observations and experiences once again under oath subject to cross-examination by opposing counsel". # Sentencing Enhancements and Substantive Reasonableness

The sentencing phase of a criminal trial requires the district court to navigate a complex interplay between statutory mandates, such as the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), and the advisory framework of the United States Sentencing Guidelines. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed challenges to both the procedural calculation of the Guidelines range and the substantive reasonableness of the final sentence imposed. Understanding this portion of the opinion requires familiarity with how specific offense characteristics elevate a base offense level, how those calculations interact with mandatory minimum statutes, and the deferential standard appellate courts apply when reviewing a sentence within the properly calculated Guidelines range. The court's analysis reinforces that a sentence is procedurally sound if the Guidelines are calculated correctly and substantively reasonable if the district court meaningfully considers the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a).

## Guideline Calculation Base: Offense Levels for Sawed-Off Firearms and Altered Serial Numbers

The foundation of any federal sentence is the correct calculation of the advisory Guidelines range, which begins with determining the base offense level and applying specific adjustments based on the facts of the crime. In *Cutbank*, the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) established a base offense level of 26. This starting point was not arbitrary but derived directly from the nature of the weapon involved. Under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1, offenses involving a "sawed-off shotgun" or similar destructive devices carry higher base offense levels than standard firearms due to their inherent danger and lack of legitimate sporting utility. The opinion notes that the Marlin .22 caliber rifle recovered by police had a sawed-off barrel and stock, fitting the description under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and justifying the elevated baseline.

Beyond the base level, the Guidelines permit upward adjustments for specific aggravating circumstances present during the commission of the offense. The district court in *Cutbank* applied two such adjustments that contributed significantly to the final offense level. First, a three-level increase was applied because the firearm's serial number had been altered or obliterated. This enhancement reflects the policy judgment that removing identifying marks facilitates criminal activity and hinders law enforcement investigations. Second, a two-level increase was added because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police. These specific offense characteristics are additive; they stack upon the base level to reflect the totality of the defendant's conduct. By accurately identifying these features—the sawed-off nature ofthe gun, the missing serial number, and the dangerous flight—the district court ensured the initial mathematical framework of the sentence was procedurally correct, a prerequisite for appellate affirmation.

## The "In Connection With" Enhancement and Statutory Interplay

A central issue in *Cutbank* was the application of U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which mandates a four-level increase if a defendant possesses a firearm "in connection with another felony offense." The defendant argued that applying this enhancement resulted in a sentence conflicting with the principles of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), particularly because he was already subject to enhanced penalties under the ACCA. To resolve this, one must understand distinct roles played by statutory mandates versus guideline enhancements. The ACCA is a statute that imposes a mandatory minimum fifteen-year sentence on felons with three prior violent felony convictions who possess a firearm. While powerful, it operates independently ofthe Guidelines' specific offense characteristics.

The Eighth Circuit clarified that these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive but rather complementary layers of sentencing logic. The "in connection with" enhancement applies regardless of whether charges were brought or convictions obtained forthe underlying felony; it focuses strictly on whetherthe firearm facilitated or had some purpose or effect in relation to another felony. In Cutbank's case, his use ofthe gun during a domestic abduction and assault constituted such a felony nexus separate from his status as a prohibited possessor underthe ACCA.
