Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The Invisible Adversary: Procedural Fairness and the Normalization of Ex Parte Evidence in FOIA Litigation

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided yesterday by the D.C. Circuit, the court affirmed the FBI's refusal to release thousands of pages of records concerning an Iranian national allegedly linked to high-level terrorist networks. On its face, the opinion is a routine application of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A), which shields records that could interfere with pending law enforcement proceedings. Judge Katsas's unanimous panel rightly notes the gravity of the underlying allegations—connections to al-Qaeda, the Muslim Brotherhood, and convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla—to justify the withholding. Yet, beneath the substantive holding lies a profound procedural development that deserves closer scrutiny. By validating the government's heavy reliance on ex parte, under-seal declarations to resolve both exemption applicability and segregability, *Farahi* signals a deepening asymmetry in national security FOIA litigation. The decision illustrates how procedural defaults and judicial deference can effectively normalize a process where the plaintiff is forced to challenge invisible evidence with one hand tied behind their back.

The core tension in *Farahi* arises from the mechanism the government used to prove its case. To withhold the records, the FBI submitted public declarations alongside detailed ex parte submissions that neither Farahi nor his counsel were permitted to see. These sealed filings contained the specific facts establishing that investigations were ongoing and explaining why no non-exempt information could be reasonably segregated from the classified whole. When the district court initially balked at the age of these claims, ordering an update, the FBI responded with yet another set of public and ex parte declarations. On appeal, Farahi challenged the sufficiency of these updates and the segregability analysis, arguing essentially that the government's assertions were too conclusory given the passage of time. The D.C. Circuit, however, swept these challenges aside, leaning heavily on the fact that Farahi had failed to object to the use of ex parte procedures in the district court.

Judge Katsas's treatment of the ex parte issue is technically sound but procedurally stark. The opinion leans on *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, a recent precedent acknowledging that while ex parte declarations handicap plaintiffs, they are sometimes "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." Because Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of these sealed filings below, the appellate court deemed any challenge forfeited. This creates a dangerous feedback loop for FOIA litigants: if a plaintiff objects to ex parte procedures early on, they risk appearing obstructive in a national security case; if they remain silent to preserve goodwill or strategy, they waive their right to complain later about relying on unseen evidence. The result is a litigation environment where the government's word, delivered in a vacuum visible only to the judge, becomes self-proving absent a specific, timely objection that few practitioners may realize is necessary to preserve.

The doctrinal implications extend beyond waiver rules into the heart of adversarial testing. In standard civil litigation, summary judgment turns on whether there is a genuine dispute of material fact. That determination usually requires both sides to see the evidence. In *Farahi*, however, the "genuine dispute" regarding whether investigations are truly "pending or reasonably anticipated"—a requirement emphasized in cases like *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW)*—was resolved entirely within the black box of an ex parte filing. The court reasoned that because an earlier ex parte declaration had provided detail, a subsequent terse public update confirming "nothing had changed" was sufficient contextually. While logical within the closed system of sealed briefing, this approach strips the plaintiff of any meaningful ability to test whether an investigation has gone dormant or whether the "interference" rationale has grown stale over years of delay. The burden shifts subtly but significantly; instead of the government proving its case against scrutiny, it merely needs to avoid forfeiture by ensuring its secret filings are technically compliant while hoping for deference on substance.

Furthermore, *Farahi* reinforces a broad interpretation of "non-segregability" when national security is at stake. The court accepted that even if individual words or phrases might theoretically be redacted rather than entire documents withheld, such granular editing is not required if exempt and non-exempt material are "inextricably intertwined." Here, given that Farahi conceded his file involved sensitive counterterrorism probes involving named entities like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and charities financing terrorism ("Holy Land Foundation"), almost anything said about him risks revealing sources and methods or alerting co-conspirators about what remains unknown to them alone .
