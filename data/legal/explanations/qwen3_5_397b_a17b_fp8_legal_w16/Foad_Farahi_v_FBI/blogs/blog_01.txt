Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The Staleness Trap: How *Farahi* Resolves the Tension Between FOIA Delays and Ongoing Investigations

In the labyrinthine world of national security litigation, time is often the plaintiff's greatest ally and the government's most formidable adversary. Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), agencies cannot rest on laurels earned years prior; they must demonstrate that exemptions apply at the time of judicial review. This creates a "staleness trap" for the government: the very complexity of national security investigations that justifies withholding records often causes the multi-year delays that threaten to invalidate those withholdings. In *Farahi v. FBI*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit navigated this tension with a pragmatic ruling that prioritizes the totality of the record over rigid formalism. By affirming the lower court's reliance on a terse update declaration when viewed alongside a detailed, albeit older, ex parte submission, Judge Katsas's opinion signals that in high-stakes counterterrorism cases, courts may be willing to bridge temporal gaps through contextual inference rather than demanding fresh, granular public affidavits for every passing year.

The facts of *Farahi* illustrate the procedural quagmire common to complex FOIA disputes. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national with alleged ties to high-level terrorists including Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla, sought his FBI file. The FBI located over 10,000 pages but withheld the majority under Exemption 7(A), which protects records whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The agency moved for summary judgment in 2019 based on declarations from Section Chief David Hardy. However, by the time the district court ruled in late 2022, significant time had elapsed. Concerned that the 2019 assertions regarding ongoing investigations might have grown stale, the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice, ordering the FBI to update its assessment. The FBI responded with new declarations from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender in early 2023. While Bender confirmed investigations remained ongoing, his public declaration was notably brief, leading Farahi to argue on appeal that the government failed to meet its burden under *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*), which warns against vague assertions of ongoing probes.

Judge Katsas's opinion for the unanimous panel cuts through Farahi's argument by reframing how appellate courts should evaluate sequential declarations in protracted litigation. The core of the holding rests on the proposition that the Bender declaration was not intended to stand alone as a foundational proof of an ongoing investigation, but rather served as a temporal bridge confirming that the factual landscape described in the detailed 2019 Hardy declarations remained unchanged. The court acknowledged that viewed in isolation, Bender's statement that investigations "remain ongoing" was "surprisingly terse." Yet, reading the record holistically, the court found this sufficient. Because the initial Hardy submission—particularly the ex parte portion—had already established the nature and scope of a sprawling counterterrorism inquiry involving designated foreign terrorist organizations, the subsequent update merely needed to attest to continuity. The court reasoned that where an investigation involves deep-rooted networks like al-Qaeda and the Muslim Brotherhood, as publicly noted in Farahi's parallel immigration proceedings, it is entirely plausible that such an inquiry spans many years without resolution.

This approach represents a subtle but significant calibration of D.C. Circuit FOIA jurisprudence regarding national security. Historically, courts have been skeptical of "boilerplate" claims that investigations are perpetually open-ended precisely because such claims can immunize agencies from accountability indefinitely. *CREW* rightly demanded specificity to prevent agencies from invoking Exemption 7(A) as a permanent shield. However, *Farahi* recognizes a practical reality: requiring the government to re-litigate the entire scope and necessity of an exemption every time a case drags on due to voluminous records or classification reviews imposes an undue burden that could compromise sensitive sources and methods. By allowing a short-form update to piggyback on a robust prior submission, the court effectively rules that staleness is not an automatic disqualifier if the underlying investigative logic remains sound and unchallenged by new facts suggesting closure.

The implications for litigants and district courts are profound, particularly concerning the use of ex parte submissions. A notable feature of *Farahi* is its explicit endorsement of relying on sealed declarations to cure deficiencies in public filings when necessary to protect classified information. Farahi challenged his inability to rebutthe government's segregability arguments because they rested largely on Bender's ex parte filing. The court dismissed this concern, reiterating that while ex parte procedures handicap plaintiffs, they are sometimes "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." This reinforces a growing trend in national security FOIA cases where the "adversarial" nature of litigation is necessarily attenuated in favor of judicial trust in executive assurances regarding classification and operational sensitivity. For plaintiffs, this raisesthe bar significantly; challenging withholdings now requires more than pointing out gaps in public affidavits—it demands affirmative evidence that an investigation has closed or that circumstances have fundamentally shifted.
