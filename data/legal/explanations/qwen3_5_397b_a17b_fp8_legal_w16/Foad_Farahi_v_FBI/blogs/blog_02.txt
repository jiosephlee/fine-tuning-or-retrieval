Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Beyond Word Counts: The Expanding Scope of 'Reasonable Segregability' in Counterterrorism Files

In the high-stakes arena of national security litigation, the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) often collides with the imperative to protect sensitive investigative methods. While much attention is typically paid to the threshold question of whether an exemption applies, *Farahi v. FBI*, decided yesterday by the D.C. Circuit, offers a critical clarification on what happens after an exemption is invoked: the duty to segregate. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Katsas affirmed that the sheer volume of withheld records does not create a presumption of releasable content, nor does the presence of isolated non-exempt words mandate their disclosure if they lack independent informational value. By reinforcing the "inextricably intertwined" doctrine, the court has signaled that in complex counterterrorism files, context is king—and without it, redacted text is merely noise.

The case arose from Foad Farahi's request for his FBI file, a massive trove comprising over 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. The FBI withheld the majority of these records under Exemption 7(A), arguing that disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings related to Farahi's alleged ties to Al Qaeda and other terrorist entities. While Farahi challenged the pendency of these proceedings, his most structurally significant argument concerned segregability. He contended that given the astronomical size of the file, it was logically impossible for every single word to be exempt; surely, amidst thousands of pages, some non-sensitive information could be carved out and released. The D.C. Circuit rejected this arithmetic approach to transparency, holding that the statutory requirement to release "reasonably segregable" portions turns on the utility of the information, not the count of non-exempt words remaining after redaction.

Judge Katsas's reasoning rests on a pragmatic interpretation of what constitutes "information" under FOIA. The opinion leans heavily on precedent establishing that agencies need not release fragments of data that are so interspersed with exempt material that they convey no meaningful insight to the requester. As the court noted, citing *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, the focus of FOIA is on information, "not simply words which the Government has written down." If non-exempt phrases are scattered line-by-line throughout a document discussing sources, methods, and ongoing investigative leads, releasing those孤立的 snippets would not illuminate the subject matter for the public. Instead, it would provide a mosaic of disconnected facts that offer no substantive value while potentially revealing the scope or direction of an investigation through what little remains visible.

This distinction between "words" and "information" is vital for understanding the doctrinal trajectory of national security FOIA cases. Historically, plaintiffs have successfully argued that blanket withholdings violate the segregability mandate, forcing agencies to conduct granular reviews to release even small amounts of data. *Farahi* tightens this lane significantly for intelligence-heavy files. The court essentially validated the FBI's argument that in counterterrorism investigations, the context surrounding a fact is often as sensitive as the fact itself. A name mentioned in passing might be unclassified in isolation, but its placement within a specific chain of custody or alongside a particular surveillance technique could compromise an operation. By accepting that such material is "inextricably intertwined," the court allows agencies to treat entire documents—or large swaths thereof—as non-segregable units when the cost of extraction yields no intelligible product.

The implications of this ruling extend beyond Farahi's specific file to future challenges against wholesale redactions in complex investigations. Plaintiffs can no longer rely solely on the volume of withheld materials as a proxy for bad faith or over-classification. The burden shifts decisively toward demonstrating that specific categories of information can be extracted in a way that retains coherence and utility. Furthermore, the court's willingness to rely on *ex parte* declarations to verify this intertwining underscores the procedural hurdles facing challengers in national security cases. Because verifying segregability often requires examining how non-exempt bits relate to classified contexts, courts are increasingly deferring to agency assertions made behind closed doors, noting that this asymmetry is an "inherent" feature of litigating secrets without exposing them.

Ultimately, *Farahi* serves as a reminder that FOIA's promise of transparency is bounded by functional reality. In an era where intelligence gathering relies on connecting disparate dots across vast datasets, the D.C. Circuit has acknowledged that sometimes removing enough dots to satisfy a redaction requirement leaves a picture too fragmented to be seen at all. For advocates pushing for greater openness in counterterrorism files, this decision suggests that future victories will depend less on demanding page counts and more on developing sophisticated technical arguments about how specific data points can be decoupled from their sensitive operational frameworks without losing their meaning—a formidable task indeed.
