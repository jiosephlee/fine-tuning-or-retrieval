Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Did Farahi forfeit his argument regarding overbroad document categorization by failing to specify which categories were problematic?
Question:
In Section IV.C, Farahi argued generally that the FBI's document categories were too broad. The court dismissed this instantly, citing *Khine v. DHS*, saying he forfeited the argument because he didn't explain *why* specific categories were overbroad.

This seems like a harsh procedural trap. If the descriptions of the categories themselves are vague or generic in the public Vaughn index, how is a pro se or even represented plaintiff supposed to know which ones to attack without seeing the underlying documents? Is there a minimum level of descriptive detail the government must provide in their public declarations to prevent a forfeiture ruling like this?
Answer:
Yes, under the D.C. Circuit's precedent in *Farahi v. FBI*, Farahi did forfeit his argument regarding overbroad document categorization.

The court's reasoning in Section IV.C rests on a specific procedural requirement for FOIA litigants challenging an agency's Vaughn index. While it is true that an agency must define categories "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" (citing *Gallant v. NLRB*), the burden shifts to the plaintiff to identify precisely where the agency failed this standard.

The *Farahi* opinion explicitly states that Farahi "provide[d] no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." Because he argued only in general terms that the categories were too broad without pointing to specific categories and explaining *why* they lumped together exempt and non-exempt material or obscured the court's ability to review them, the court deemed the argument forfeited under *Khine v. DHS*.

To answer your concern about the "procedural trap": The D.C. Circuit does not require a plaintiff to see the underlying documents to challenge the *categories* themselves. Rather, the plaintiff must analyze the public descriptions of those categories in the Vaughn index or declarations. A sufficient challenge would argue, for example, that a category labeled "Investigative Reports" is overbroad because it ostensibly includes both raw intelligence (exempt) and purely administrative scheduling data (non-exempt) without distinction, making judicial review impossible. By failing to make any such specific contention against any specific category listed by the FBI, Farahi failed to preserve the issue for appeal.

The court did not address whether the government's descriptions were minimally sufficient because Farahi never articulated a defect in them; he merely issued a blanket objection. Consequently, regardless of how vague the public index might appear, the failure to specify the defect results in forfeiture.
