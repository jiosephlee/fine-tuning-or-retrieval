Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### To what extent does an applicant's prior concession in separate immigration proceedings waive their right to challenge the 'law enforcement purpose' nexus in a FOIA suit?
Question:
Section IV.A of the opinion states that Farahi conceded in his immigration case that he was investigated for ties to terrorist organizations and specific individuals like Khaled Sheikh Mohamed. The appellate court uses this external concession to immediately satisfy the 'compiled for law enforcement purposes' prong of Exemption 7(A), noting the FBI gets deference anyway.

Does this mean that if a requester has ever admitted to being under investigation in any other forum (civil, administrative, criminal), they are essentially precluded from arguing that the records weren't compiled for law enforcement purposes in a subsequent FOIA lawsuit? How does this interact with the requirement that the agency itself must establish the rational nexus?
Answer:
No, the court's reliance on Farahi's prior concessions does not create a blanket rule that any admission of an investigation in a separate forum automatically waives a FOIA plaintiff's right to challenge the "law enforcement purpose" nexus. Rather, the *Farahi* opinion illustrates how a plaintiff's judicial admissions in parallel proceedings can serve as powerful **evidentiary support** that helps the agency meet its burden of proof, particularly when combined with the deference owed to the FBI.

Here is how the opinion reconciles the applicant's concession with the agency's independent burden:

### 1. The Agency Retains the Burden of Proof
The opinion reaffirms the foundational principle that "[t]he government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies." To satisfy Exemption 7(A)'s threshold requirement, the FBI must still establish a "rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties'" and a connection between the individual and a "possible security risk or violation of federal law" (citing *Campbell* and *Pratt*).

The court did not hold that Farahi's concession *replaced* the FBI's obligation to submit declarations establishing this nexus. Instead, the FBI submitted detailed public and *ex parte* declarations (from Agents Hardy and Bender) explicitly stating that the records were compiled during investigations into national security violations and terrorism.

### 2. Concessions as Corroborative Context
The court used Farahi's concessions in his immigration proceedings not as a procedural waiver, but as factual corroboration that rendered the FBI's assertions indisputable.

In Section IV.A, the court notes that while the FBI's declarations established the investigative nature of the file, "**broader context confirms that assertion.**" The court points out that Farahi himself had previously conceded in district court filings that he was investigated for ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations and specific individuals like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed.

Because Farahi had already admitted these facts in another judicial forum, he could not credibly argue in the FOIA suit that there was no rational nexus between the FBI's files and a law enforcement duty regarding terrorism. The concession removed any genuine issue of material fact regarding the *purpose* of the compilation, allowing the court to conclude: "We have no doubt that the FBI carried its burden."

### 3. The Role of Deference
The interaction between the concession and the agency's burden is mediated by the standard of review. The opinion emphasizes that "because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference," especially in national security contexts (*CNSS*).

When an agency provides a plausible declaration asserting a law enforcement purpose (which receives deference), and the plaintiff has simultaneously admitted facts in another proceeding that align perfectly with that assertion, the plaintiff effectively undermines their own ability to contest the nexus. The concession does not shift the legal burden to the plaintiff; rather, it makes it impossible for the plaintiff to rebut the agency's prima facie case.

### Conclusion
An applicant's prior admission does not procedurally waive their right to challenge Exemption 7(A). A plaintiff remains free to argue that records were not compiled for law enforcement purposes even if they admitted to an investigation elsewhere (for example, if they argued they were investigating civil misconduct rather than federal crimes).

However, as *Farahi* demonstrates, such concessions will likely be treated as binding judicial admissions that validate the agency's claim of a rational nexus. If a plaintiff admits to being investigated for terrorism in immigration court, they cannot logically argue in a subsequent FOIA suit that their FBI file lacks a connection to federal law enforcement duties. The concession satisfies the factual predicate necessary for the court to accept the agency's deferred determination.
