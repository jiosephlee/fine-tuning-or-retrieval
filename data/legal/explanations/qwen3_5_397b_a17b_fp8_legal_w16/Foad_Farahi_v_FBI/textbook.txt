# Case Background and Procedural History

To fully grasp the legal reasoning in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, one must first navigate the complex factual backdrop involving national security investigations and the protracted procedural history that shaped the appellate court's review. This case is not merely a static dispute over document production; it is a dynamic litigation saga where the passage of time fundamentally altered the burdens of proof. The central tension arises from the intersection of Farahi's long-standing immigration battles, the FBI's counterterrorism inquiries, and the rigorous requirements of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) when exemptions are invoked years after an initial request. Understanding how the district court managed the "staleness" of government assertions and how the FBI responded with updated public and sealed declarations is essential to appreciating why the D.C. Circuit ultimately deferred to the agency's conclusions.

## Farahi's Immigration Proceedings and Alleged Terrorist Ties

The factual foundation of this FOIA dispute rests on Foad Farahi's status as an Iranian national living in the United States since 1993 and his subsequent entanglement with federal immigration and law enforcement authorities. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against Farahi due to a visa overstay. These proceedings became the arena for serious allegations regarding Farahi's associations with extremist groups. As detailed in prior litigation before the Eleventh Circuit, DHS opposed Farahi's applications for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture by presenting evidence of his connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, an FBI agent testified that Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, maintained connections to Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and other Al Qaeda members, and was linked to convicted terrorists such as Jose Padilla. Furthermore, Farahi was accused of supporting charities designated as financing terrorism, including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. These allegations are critical because they establish the "law enforcement purpose" behind the FBI's compilation of Farahi's file, a prerequisite for invoking FOIA Exemption 7(A). In the district court, Farahi effectively conceded these investigative premises, acknowledging that the FBI had investigated him for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and individuals later convicted of terrorism-related charges.

## The Initial FOIA Request and FBI Response Timeline

Farahi's quest for transparency began with a formal FOIA request seeking his entire FBI file. The scale of this request was immense; the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive information. However, the agency failed to respond within the statutory deadline mandated by FOIA, prompting Farahi to file suit. Following the initiation of litigation, the FBI engaged in a multi-year review process, during which it disclosed some records but withheld the majority. By October 2019, having completed its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment to justify its withholdings. In support of this motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy's declaration asserted two key points required for Exemption 7(A): first, that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, and second, that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were either pending or reasonably anticipated at that time. Notably, recognizing the sensitivity of certain details regarding ongoing investigations into terrorism, the FBI also moved to file a supplemental declaration from Hardy under seal and *ex parte*—meaning solely for the judge's eyes without disclosure to Farahi or his counsel. Farahi did not oppose this procedural mechanism, and the district court granted leave for these sealed submissions in October 2020.

# Governing Legal Framework for Exemption 7(A)

To understand the court's decision in *Farahi v. FBI*, one must first master the specific statutory architecture of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the judicial interpretation of Exemption 7(A). FOIA operates on a presumption of disclosure, mandating that federal agencies release records to the public upon request unless a specific statutory exemption applies. Exemption 7(A) serves as a critical shield for law enforcement activities, allowing agencies to withhold records when their release would harm active or future investigations. In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit applied this framework to uphold the FBI's refusal to release files related to an individual suspected of terrorist ties. The court's analysis hinged entirely on whether the FBI successfully navigated the two distinct prongs required by the statute and whether it met its evidentiary burden through adequate affidavits.

## Statutory Text of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A)

The starting point for any Exemption 7(A) analysis is the text of the statute itself, found at 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The exemption permits an agency to withhold "records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes" but only if producing them "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." This language establishes a conditional privilege; the mere fact that records are law enforcement-related is insufficient to trigger the exemption. The agency must additionally demonstrate a causal link between disclosure and a specific harm: interference with a proceeding. As noted in *Farahi*, this statutory requirement creates a two-part test that the government must satisfy to prevail on a motion for summary judgment. The court emphasized that both conditions are mandatory prerequisites; failure to prove either prong results in the loss of the exemption's protection.

## Prong One: The 'Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes' Standard

The first hurdle an agency must clear is proving that the withheld records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." In *Farahi*, the court reiterated that because the FBI is inherently a law enforcement agency, its determination on this issue is entitled to a "fair measure of deference." However, this deference is not blind. Citing precedent such as *Campbell v. DOJ*, the court explained that agency declarations must establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's lawful duties. Furthermore, there must be a connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.

In applying this standard to Farahi's case, the court looked beyond generic assertions. The FBI's public declaration stated that the records were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism, specifically noting Farahi's association with individuals involved in such violations. The court found this sufficient, particularly because Farahi himself had conceded in prior litigation that he was investigated for ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists. Thus, under Prong One, the "compiled for" requirement is satisfied when the agency demonstrates that the records were generated during an inquiry rooted in a legitimate law enforcement objective, supported by specific factual allegations connecting the subject to potential federal crimes or security threats.

## Prong Two: The 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Proceeding Requirement

Satisfying the first prong is only half the battle; the government must also prove that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The *Farahi* opinion clarifies that for Exemption 7(A) to apply, these proceedings must be either "pending" or "reasonably anticipated." The court affirmed that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings" under this standard. Crucially, the government does not need to prove that interference is certain; it need only show that such interference is reasonably expected. A classic example cited by the court is the risk of "tipping off" targets about the scope of an investigation, which could allow them to destroy evidence or flee before charges are filed.

A central tension in *Farahi* involved the passage of time. Farahi argued that because years had passed since the initial declarations were filed, vague assertions that an investigation remained "ongoing" were insufficient under precedents like *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*). While acknowledging that isolated, conclusory statements can be inadequate, the court held that declarations must be viewed in context. In this instance, the initial detailed declarations established the existence and nature of the investigation, while subsequent filings merely confirmed that circumstances had not changed. Because the underlying investigation involved complex terrorism probes connected to high-profile figures like Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla, the court found it plausible that such broad inquiries would remain open for extended periods. Therefore, Prong Two is met when the agency provides specific assurances—backed by contextual plausibility—that releasing information would jeopardize a concrete, prospective law enforcement action.

## Judicial Deference to the FBI in National Security Contexts

Underpinning the application of both prongs is the principle of judicial deference, which plays an amplified role when national security is implicated. The *Farahi* court explicitly stated that because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decisions to invoke Exemption 7 are entitled to deference. This deference becomes even more pronounced when records touch upon counterterrorism and national security concerns. Citing *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*,the opinion notes that courts consistently defer to executive branch judgments in these areas because judges lack specialized expertise in intelligence gathering and threat assessment. Consequently, when evaluating whether disclosure could interfere with proceedings involving alleged terrorist recruitment or financing—as was alleged against Farahi—the court accepts reasonable predictive judgments made by agency officials regarding potential harms, rather than demanding empirical proof of injury that might itself compromise sensitive methods.

# Analysis of Law Enforcement Purpose and Interference

In *Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit's affirmation of the district court's summary judgment turns on a precise application of Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The core legal inquiry requires the government to satisfy two distinct prongs: first, demonstrating that the withheld records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes," and second, proving that their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The appellate court's analysis provides a critical lesson in how courts evaluate agency declarations, particularly when national security is implicated and when litigation spans several years. Rather than demanding new evidence for every procedural step, the court examines the totality of the record, weighing initial detailed submissions against subsequent updates and considering the specific factual context of the plaintiff's alleged conduct. This section dissects how the court navigated Farahi's challenges regarding the age of the investigation and the perceived thinness of the government's updated justifications.

## Establishing the Nexus Between Farahi and National Security Investigations

The threshold requirement for invoking Exemption 7(A) is that the records must be compiled for law enforcement purposes. While the FBI enjoys a presumption of deference as a specialized law enforcement agency, this deference is not unconditional. The agency must establish a rational nexus between its investigation and one of its statutory duties, as well as a connection between the individual or incident in question and a potential violation of federal law or security risk. In *Farahi*, the court found this nexus overwhelmingly supported by both agency declarations and the plaintiff's own admissions.

The FBI's initial declaration by Section Chief David Hardy explicitly tied the withheld records to investigations of national security violations and international terrorism, noting Farahi's association with individuals directly involved in such activities. Crucially, the court reinforced this administrative assertion with facts Farahi himself had conceded in prior litigation. Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for ties to Foreign Terrorist Organizations designated by the State Department, connections to an enemy combatant convicted in federal court, and associations with high-profile figures like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla. By anchoring the "law enforcement purpose" finding in these undisputed facts regarding Farahi's alleged role as a recruiter and financier for terrorist entities, the court eliminated any ambiguity about whether the file was generated for legitimate investigative ends rather than arbitrary surveillance. This illustrates that even when an agency receives deference, a strong factual record linking the subject to specific criminal predicates solidifies the first prong of the Exemption 7(A) test.

## Defining 'Enforcement Proceedings' in Ongoing Counterterrorism Cases

Once the law enforcement purpose is established, the government must demonstrate that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either pending or reasonably anticipated. A common point of confusion for students is whether a formal charging document or indictment is necessary to constitute a "proceeding." The *Farahi* opinion clarifies that under D.C. Circuit precedent, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as enforcement proceedings. The standard does not require the government to prove that an indictment is imminent, only that it is gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case and that premature release would jeopardize that case.

In this context, the court accepted the FBI's assertion that its investigation into Farahi remained active. The definition of "proceedings" here is broad enough to encompass the pre-indictment phase of a counterterrorism investigation. The logic follows that if an investigation is actively gathering intelligence to build a potential prosecution, it constitutes a pending proceeding for FOIA purposes. The court relied on the principle that so long as the investigative machinery is turning—collecting evidence, interviewing witnesses, or analyzing data—the proceeding exists. Consequently, Farahi could not defeat Exemption 7(A) by arguing that no charges had yet been filed; the existence of an open investigation into serious terrorism offenses was sufficient to meet the statutory definition of an enforcement proceeding.

# Segregability Obligations and Ex Parte Procedures

While the primary hurdle in Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation often involves establishing that a specific exemption applies to withheld records, a secondary but critical layer of analysis concerns the agency's duty to release any non-exempt portions of those records and the procedural mechanisms used to adjudicate such disputes without compromising sensitive information. In *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit addressed these ancillary issues by reaffirming the limits of the statutory segregability requirement and validating the use of ex parte, under-seal declarations in cases involving national security investigations. The court's reasoning illustrates how procedural safeguards balance a requester's right to access against the government's imperative to protect ongoing law enforcement operations.

## The Statutory Duty to Segregate Non-Exempt Information

The FOIA statute imposes a clear affirmative duty on federal agencies: even when an exemption justifies withholding certain parts of a record, the agency must disclose "any reasonably segregable portion" after redacting the exempt material. As noted in *Farahi*, this obligation is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b), requiring agencies to take "reasonable steps" to separate disclosable information from protected content. In practice, this means an agency cannot withhold an entire document simply because it contains some exempt information; it must scrutinize the record line-by-line to isolate and release non-exempt facts. However, the court emphasized that this duty is not absolute. The standard is one of reasonableness, not perfection. An agency satisfies its burden by providing a detailed justification explaining why further segregation is not feasible, a standard the district court found the FBI met through its supplemental declarations in this case.

## 'Inextricably Intertwined' Doctrine: Words vs. Meaningful Information

A central concept limiting the segregability requirement is the doctrine that non-exempt portions need not be released if they are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions. In *Farahi*, the appellant argued that given the vast volume of responsive records—over 10,000 pages—it was statistically improbable that no meaningful information could be segregated. The court rejected this argument by distinguishing between isolated words or phrases and "meaningful information." Citing *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of the Air Force*, the opinion clarifies that if non-exempt material is randomly interspersed throughout a document such that releasing it would reveal exempt contexts or patterns, segregation is not required. The court reasoned that while individual words might technically be non-exempt, releasing them would not provide Farahi with useful intelligence ("information") but rather risks revealing the scope and direction of the investigation. Thus, when exempt and non-exempt materials are so tightly woven that separating them would leave only disjointed fragments devoid of context, the agency may withhold the record in its entirety.

## Challenges to Document Categorization and Waiver Issues

Agencies often organize their withholding justifications by categorizing documents based on the type of information they contain and the specific exemptions invoked. For these categories to be valid, they must be sufficiently distinct to allow a reviewing court to determine whether the claimed exemptions are properly applied to each group. In *Farahi*, the appellant briefly objected that the FBI's document categories were overly broad, potentially masking improper withholdings. However, the court disposed of this argument on procedural grounds rather than substantive merit. Because Farahi failed to provide any specific explanation as to why the identified categories were defective or overbroad, he forfeited the argument. This highlights a crucial aspect of appellate advocacy in FOIA cases: generalized objections to an agency's indexing system are insufficient to overcome a detailed agency declaration. Without a targeted challenge demonstrating how specific categories fail legal standards, a plaintiff waives the right to contest them on appeal.

## The Necessity and Legality of Ex Parte, Under-Seal Declarations

Perhaps the most procedurally significant aspect of *Farahi* is its endorsement of ex parte, under-seal submissions in sensitive FOIA litigation. Normally, adversarial proceedings require both parties to see and respond to all evidence submitted to the court. However, in cases involving national security or ongoing counterterrorism investigations—as evidenced by Farahi's links to designated terrorist organizations and enemy combatants—public disclosure of the government's justification could itself cause harm equivalent to releasing the actual records. The district court accepted public declarations alongside detailed ex parte submissions from FBI officials David Hardy and Joseph Bender. These sealed declarations allowed the government to describe the nature of ongoing investigations and specific risks of interference without tipping off subjects or associates. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach, noting that such procedures are sometimes "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." By reviewing these submissions privately, courts can verify that exemptions are legitimately invoked while maintaining operational secrecy.
