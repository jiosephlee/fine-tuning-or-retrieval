Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # Segregability Obligations and Ex Parte Procedures

While the primary hurdle in Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation often involves establishing that a specific exemption applies to withheld records, a secondary but critical layer of analysis concerns the agency's duty to release any non-exempt portions of those records and the procedural mechanisms used to adjudicate such disputes without compromising sensitive information. In *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit addressed these ancillary issues by reaffirming the limits of the statutory segregability requirement and validating the use of ex parte, under-seal declarations in cases involving national security investigations. The court's reasoning illustrates how procedural safeguards balance a requester's right to access against the government's imperative to protect ongoing law enforcement operations.

## The Statutory Duty to Segregate Non-Exempt Information

The FOIA statute imposes a clear affirmative duty on federal agencies: even when an exemption justifies withholding certain parts of a record, the agency must disclose "any reasonably segregable portion" after redacting the exempt material. As noted in *Farahi*, this obligation is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b), requiring agencies to take "reasonable steps" to separate disclosable information from protected content. In practice, this means an agency cannot withhold an entire document simply because it contains some exempt information; it must scrutinize the record line-by-line to isolate and release non-exempt facts. However, the court emphasized that this duty is not absolute. The standard is one of reasonableness, not perfection. An agency satisfies its burden by providing a detailed justification explaining why further segregation is not feasible, a standard the district court found the FBI met through its supplemental declarations in this case.

## 'Inextricably Intertwined' Doctrine: Words vs. Meaningful Information

A central concept limiting the segregability requirement is the doctrine that non-exempt portions need not be released if they are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions. In *Farahi*, the appellant argued that given the vast volume of responsive records—over 10,000 pages—it was statistically improbable that no meaningful information could be segregated. The court rejected this argument by distinguishing between isolated words or phrases and "meaningful information." Citing *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of the Air Force*, the opinion clarifies that if non-exempt material is randomly interspersed throughout a document such that releasing it would reveal exempt contexts or patterns, segregation is not required. The court reasoned that while individual words might technically be non-exempt, releasing them would not provide Farahi with useful intelligence ("information") but rather risks revealing the scope and direction of the investigation. Thus, when exempt and non-exempt materials are so tightly woven that separating them would leave only disjointed fragments devoid of context, the agency may withhold the record in its entirety.

## Challenges to Document Categorization and Waiver Issues

Agencies often organize their withholding justifications by categorizing documents based on the type of information they contain and the specific exemptions invoked. For these categories to be valid, they must be sufficiently distinct to allow a reviewing court to determine whether the claimed exemptions are properly applied to each group. In *Farahi*, the appellant briefly objected that the FBI's document categories were overly broad, potentially masking improper withholdings. However, the court disposed of this argument on procedural grounds rather than substantive merit. Because Farahi failed to provide any specific explanation as to why the identified categories were defective or overbroad, he forfeited the argument. This highlights a crucial aspect of appellate advocacy in FOIA cases: generalized objections to an agency's indexing system are insufficient to overcome a detailed agency declaration. Without a targeted challenge demonstrating how specific categories fail legal standards, a plaintiff waives the right to contest them on appeal.

## The Necessity and Legality of Ex Parte, Under-Seal Declarations

Perhaps the most procedurally significant aspect of *Farahi* is its endorsement of ex parte, under-seal submissions in sensitive FOIA litigation. Normally, adversarial proceedings require both parties to see and respond to all evidence submitted to the court. However, in cases involving national security or ongoing counterterrorism investigations—as evidenced by Farahi's links to designated terrorist organizations and enemy combatants—public disclosure of the government's justification could itself cause harm equivalent to releasing the actual records. The district court accepted public declarations alongside detailed ex parte submissions from FBI officials David Hardy and Joseph Bender. These sealed declarations allowed the government to describe the nature of ongoing investigations and specific risks of interference without tipping off subjects or associates. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach, noting that such procedures are sometimes "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." By reviewing these submissions privately, courts can verify that exemptions are legitimately invoked while maintaining operational secrecy.
