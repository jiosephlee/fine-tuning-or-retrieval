# The Staleness Trap: How *Farahi* Resolves the Tension Between Time and National Security in FOIA Litigation

In the world of Freedom of Information Act litigation, time is often the plaintiff's greatest ally. The logic is intuitive: as months turn into years, the government's claim that an investigation is "ongoing" or that disclosure would interfere with "pending" proceedings grows increasingly suspect. A declaration drafted in 2019 asserting active probes may ring hollow by 2024 absent fresh corroboration. Yet, in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit navigated a delicate path between this natural skepticism and the unique temporal realities of counterterrorism. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Katsas affirmed the FBI's withholding of thousands of pages concerning an Iranian national linked to high-level terror networks, establishing a pragmatic framework for how courts should treat "stale" declarations in long-running national security cases. The decision suggests that while the passage of time demands scrutiny, it does not necessarily require the government to reinvent the wheel with every new filing; rather, a terse update can suffice if it rests upon a previously detailed, albeit older, factual foundation.

The case centers on Foad Farahi, whose immigration battles have long been shadowed by allegations of deep ties to Al Qaeda, the Muslim Brotherhood, and convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla and Khaled Sheikh Mohammed. When Farahi sought his FBI file under FOIA, the bureau invoked Exemption 7(A), which shields records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The procedural history reveals the friction point: after the FBI submitted its initial justifications in 2019 and 2020, the district court—sensing the decay of certainty over time—denied summary judgment without prejudice. The lower court ordered the FBI to update its assessment to confirm that proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. The FBI complied with a new declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender in 2023, but Bender's public statement was notably brief, essentially confirming that investigations remained open without re-litigating the underlying facts. Farahi appealed, arguing that this brevity violated the standard set in *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*), which warns against vague assertions of ongoing activity.

Judge Katsas's opinion deftly resolves this tension by refusing to view the 2023 Bender declaration in a vacuum. The court acknowledged Farahi's premise—that a standalone claim that an investigation is "ongoing" can indeed be too conclusory to survive judicial review under *CREW*. However, the panel emphasized context over isolation. The Bender declaration was not an attempt to establish the existence of an investigation from scratch; it was a supplemental update designed to bridge the gap between the original, highly detailed ex parte submissions made by Section Chief David Hardy years earlier and the present day. Because Hardy's earlier sealed declaration had already provided considerable detail regarding the nature and scope of the counterterrorism probe, and because the district court had previously found those initial showings sufficient (albeit dated), Bender's role was merely to attest that nothing had materially changed. By treating the filings as a cumulative record rather than discrete events, the court validated a "supplemental update" approach that respects both the need for current verification and the logistical burdens of re-documenting complex, classified investigations in every renewed motion.

This reasoning carries significant doctrinal weight for future FOIA litigation involving national security. For years, plaintiffs have successfully leveraged the "staleness" argument to force agencies into difficult positions: either reveal sensitive operational details to prove an investigation is alive or risk losing Exemption 7(A) protection due to outdated paperwork. *Farahi* signals that appellate courts may be less willing to accept formalistic challenges when the underlying factual predicate remains robust within sealed records. The decision effectively creates a safe harbor for agencies dealing with decades-long counterterrorism inquiries, acknowledging that these investigations do not operate on standard criminal timetables. Where a subject is linked to transnational terror networks with enduring reach, as Farahi allegedly was, the presumption of continued investigative relevance is stronger, and the burden on the agency to refresh its public explanations is correspondingly lighter—provided they have laid a granular groundwork initially.

Furthermore, the opinion reinforces the high bar for challenging segregability in cases where exempt and non-exempt information are intimately entwined. Farahi argued that amidst some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of data, surely something could be released. The court rejected this quantitative intuition in favor of a qualitative analysis rooted in *Mead Data Central*. If non-exempt fragments are interspersed line-by-line with classified intelligence such that releasing them would reveal patterns or sources protected by Exemption 7(A), then no meaningful segregation is possible. The panel noted that FOIA guarantees access to information, not merely isolated words stripped of context. By upholding the district court's reliance on ex parte declarations to make this determination without forcing public justification that would itself compromise security interests, *Farahi* solidifies the procedural mechanisms available to protect sensitive sources while adjudicating transparency disputes.

# Information vs. Words: The Evolving Standard for Segregability in High-Volume Intelligence Files

In the contentious arena of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, few tensions are as acute as the clash between a requester's demand for transparency and the government's need to protect sensitive intelligence. Requesters often operate under a statistical assumption: if an agency produces 10,750 pages of documents, surely *something* within that mountain of paper can be safely released. They argue that the sheer volume of responsive records mathematically guarantees the existence of segregable, non-exempt information. On September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit pushed back firmly against this logic in *Foad Farahi v. FBI*. While the court affirmed the withholding of Farahi's file under Exemption 7(A) due to ongoing counterterrorism investigations, its most enduring contribution lies in Part V of the opinion. There, Judge Katsas reinvigorated a foundational but often overlooked principle: FOIA mandates the release of "information," not merely isolated "words." In doing so, the court provided critical guidance for agencies grappling with massive, interwoven intelligence files where redaction risks rendering the remaining text meaningless or dangerously misleading.

The factual backdrop of *Farahi* underscores the complexity facing modern FOIA adjudicators. Farahi, an Iranian national with alleged ties to high-level terrorist figures including Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla, sought his entire FBI file. The FBI located over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs but withheld the vast majority, citing interference with pending enforcement proceedings. After remanding the case for updated declarations regarding the status of these proceedings and the possibility of segregation, the district court ultimately sided with the Bureau. On appeal, Farahi contended that it was implausible for not a single meaningful fragment of information to be releasable from such a voluminous collection. The D.C. Circuit rejected this argument, grounding its reasoning in the seminal *Mead Data Central* doctrine that agencies are not required to perform redactions that leave behind disjointed sentences or phrases devoid of context.

The court's analytical pivot in Part V is subtle but profound. It distinguishes between the mechanical act of cutting out exempt words and the substantive goal of releasing useful information. Judge Katsas noted that while individual words or short phrases might technically be unclassified, their isolation from the surrounding exempt material strips them of informational value. If non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," as the FBI argued here, excising the exempt portions would leave a patchwork of syntax that conveys no coherent message to the reader. More importantly, in the context of counterterrorism intelligence, releasing fragmented data could be affirmatively harmful. A disconnected name, date, or location, stripped of the investigative narrative explaining its significance, could mislead the public or inadvertently signal investigative priorities to adversaries. By affirming that no "meaningful information" was reasonably segregable, the court signaled that the utility of the residual text is a prerequisite for release, not just its technical classification status.

This reasoning carries significant doctrinal implications for future litigation involving high-volume intelligence dumps. Plaintiffs have increasingly relied on a "volume-based" theory of segregability—the notion that large datasets must inherently contain releasable nuggets simply by virtue of their size. *Farahi* dismantles this presumption by shifting the focus from quantity to quality and coherence. The decision effectively grants agencies greater latitude to withhold entire documents or large swaths thereof when the exempt and non-exempt materials are inextricably intertwined. This is particularly vital in national security cases where context is everything; knowing *who* met *whom* is useless—and potentially dangerous—without knowing *why*, *when*, and under *what operational constraints* the meeting occurred. If those contextual elements are exempt, the bare facts cannot stand alone as "reasonably segregable" information.

Furthermore, *Farahi* reinforces the judiciary's willingness to rely on ex parte declarations to make these granular determinations. The court dismissed Farahi's complaint that he was handicapped by his inability to see the specific justifications for non-segregability, noting that such secrecy is "inherent" in cases where revealing the rationale would expose the very secrets at issue. This creates a challenging dynamic for FOIA plaintiffs: they must overcome detailed, sealed government affidavits describing how information is woven together without being able to see the tapestry themselves. While critics may argue this tilts the scale too heavily toward secrecy, the D.C. Circuit views it as a necessary mechanism to prevent litigants from reverse-engineering classified methodologies through adversarial testing of redaction logs.

Ultimately, *Farahi* serves as a reminder that FOIA is an instrument for illuminating government activity, not a tool for extracting raw data points that lack independent meaning. By reiterating that "the focus of FOIA is information… not simply words which the Government has written down," Judge Katsas has set a robust standard for agencies managing complex intelligence archives. For future requesters challenging withholdings in massive files, mere volume will no longer suffice as proof of error; they must demonstrate that coherent, meaningful information exists apart from the exempt context. In an era of big data and intricate surveillance networks, this distinction ensures that transparency does not come atthe costof clarityor security



# The Adversarial Deficit: Procedural Fairness and the Reliance on Ex Parte Declarations in National Security FOIA

In *Farahi v. FBI*, decided yesterday by the D.C. Circuit, the court affirmed the withholding of thousands of pages of records concerning an Iranian national suspected of ties to al-Qaeda and the Muslim Brotherhood. On its face, the opinion by Judge Katsas is a routine application of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A), which shields records that could interfere with pending law enforcement proceedings. The government demonstrated a nexus between the files and counterterrorism investigations, and the court deferred to the Executive's assertion that disclosure would tip off targets. Yet, beneath this standard doctrinal analysis lies a profound procedural tension that merits closer scrutiny. The *Farahi* decision exemplifies a growing trend in national security litigation: the effective dissolution of the adversarial process through the heavy reliance on *ex parte*, under-seal declarations. While the court frames this as an unavoidable necessity to protect sensitive intelligence, the result is a judicial review mechanism where the government acts as prosecutor, witness, and judge, leaving the requester with little recourse but to trust in the integrity of unseen submissions.

The procedural history of *Farahi* illustrates how easily the scales can tip in these cases. When the district court initially found the FBI's declarations potentially stale due to the passage of time, it ordered an update. The FBI complied not with a robust public briefing, but with a terse public declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, supplemented by a detailed *ex parte* filing visible only to the judge. Farahi challenged the sufficiency of these updates on appeal, arguing that the public declaration was conclusory. Judge Katsas acknowledged that Bender's public statement was "surprisingly terse" when viewed in isolation. However, he cured this deficiency by integrating it with prior *ex parte* submissions from years earlier and relying on the fact that Farahi had not opposed the use of *ex parte* procedures at the district level. By treating the sealed record as a continuous, cumulative body of evidence, the appellate court bypassed the need for contemporaneous public justification, effectively insulating the government's current assertions from direct adversarial testing.

This approach raises difficult questions about the forfeiture doctrine in the context of asymmetric information access. The court noted that because Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of *ex parte* declarations initially, he could not challenge their use on appeal regarding segregability. This creates a catch-22 for FOIA plaintiffs: how does one meaningfully object to a procedure designed to hide the very evidence necessary to formulate an objection? In traditional civil litigation, parties can test affidavits through discovery or cross-examination; in national security FOIA cases involving Exemption 7(A), the plaintiff is often left arguing against shadows. The *Farahi* opinion suggests that silence or failure to object early in the process constitutes waiver, even when that silence stems from an inability to see what one is supposedly contesting. This dynamic risks transforming FOIA litigation from an adversarial check on executive power into a unilateral certification process where courts rubber-stamp agency claims based solely on private assurances.

Furthermore, the court's handling of segregability underscores the erosion of transparency inherent in this model. Statutorily, agencies must release any "reasonably segregable" non-exempt portions of a record. Here, facing over 10,000 pages and dozens of CDs, farahi argued that surely something could be released. The district court rejected this based entirely on Bender's *ex parte* explanation that exempt and non-exempt material were "inextricably intertwined." Judge Katsas upheld this finding without independent verification accessible to public scrutiny beyond vague analogies to interwoven text ("line-by-line"). While precedent like *Mead Data Central* supports non-segregability when redaction renders documents meaningless fragments ("words which... [do] not convey[] information"), applying this standard purely via secret affidavit removes any external validation. It allows agencies to define categories so broadly or describe entanglements so opaquely that total withholding becomes functionally unreviewable by anyone other than Article III judges acting alone in chambers.
