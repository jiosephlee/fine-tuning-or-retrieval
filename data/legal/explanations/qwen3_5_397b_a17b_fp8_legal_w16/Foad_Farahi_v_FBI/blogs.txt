# The Staleness Trap: How *Farahi* Resolves the Tension Between FOIA Delays and Ongoing Investigations

In the labyrinthine world of national security litigation, time is often the plaintiff's greatest ally and the government's most formidable adversary. Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), agencies cannot rest on laurels earned years prior; they must demonstrate that exemptions apply at the time of judicial review. This creates a "staleness trap" for the government: the very complexity of national security investigations that justifies withholding records often causes the multi-year delays that threaten to invalidate those withholdings. In *Farahi v. FBI*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit navigated this tension with a pragmatic ruling that prioritizes the totality of the record over rigid formalism. By affirming the lower court's reliance on a terse update declaration when viewed alongside a detailed, albeit older, ex parte submission, Judge Katsas's opinion signals that in high-stakes counterterrorism cases, courts may be willing to bridge temporal gaps through contextual inference rather than demanding fresh, granular public affidavits for every passing year.

The facts of *Farahi* illustrate the procedural quagmire common to complex FOIA disputes. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national with alleged ties to high-level terrorists including Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla, sought his FBI file. The FBI located over 10,000 pages but withheld the majority under Exemption 7(A), which protects records whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The agency moved for summary judgment in 2019 based on declarations from Section Chief David Hardy. However, by the time the district court ruled in late 2022, significant time had elapsed. Concerned that the 2019 assertions regarding ongoing investigations might have grown stale, the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice, ordering the FBI to update its assessment. The FBI responded with new declarations from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender in early 2023. While Bender confirmed investigations remained ongoing, his public declaration was notably brief, leading Farahi to argue on appeal that the government failed to meet its burden under *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*), which warns against vague assertions of ongoing probes.

Judge Katsas's opinion for the unanimous panel cuts through Farahi's argument by reframing how appellate courts should evaluate sequential declarations in protracted litigation. The core of the holding rests on the proposition that the Bender declaration was not intended to stand alone as a foundational proof of an ongoing investigation, but rather served as a temporal bridge confirming that the factual landscape described in the detailed 2019 Hardy declarations remained unchanged. The court acknowledged that viewed in isolation, Bender's statement that investigations "remain ongoing" was "surprisingly terse." Yet, reading the record holistically, the court found this sufficient. Because the initial Hardy submission—particularly the ex parte portion—had already established the nature and scope of a sprawling counterterrorism inquiry involving designated foreign terrorist organizations, the subsequent update merely needed to attest to continuity. The court reasoned that where an investigation involves deep-rooted networks like al-Qaeda and the Muslim Brotherhood, as publicly noted in Farahi's parallel immigration proceedings, it is entirely plausible that such an inquiry spans many years without resolution.

This approach represents a subtle but significant calibration of D.C. Circuit FOIA jurisprudence regarding national security. Historically, courts have been skeptical of "boilerplate" claims that investigations are perpetually open-ended precisely because such claims can immunize agencies from accountability indefinitely. *CREW* rightly demanded specificity to prevent agencies from invoking Exemption 7(A) as a permanent shield. However, *Farahi* recognizes a practical reality: requiring the government to re-litigate the entire scope and necessity of an exemption every time a case drags on due to voluminous records or classification reviews imposes an undue burden that could compromise sensitive sources and methods. By allowing a short-form update to piggyback on a robust prior submission, the court effectively rules that staleness is not an automatic disqualifier if the underlying investigative logic remains sound and unchallenged by new facts suggesting closure.

The implications for litigants and district courts are profound, particularly concerning the use of ex parte submissions. A notable feature of *Farahi* is its explicit endorsement of relying on sealed declarations to cure deficiencies in public filings when necessary to protect classified information. Farahi challenged his inability to rebutthe government's segregability arguments because they rested largely on Bender's ex parte filing. The court dismissed this concern, reiterating that while ex parte procedures handicap plaintiffs, they are sometimes "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." This reinforces a growing trend in national security FOIA cases where the "adversarial" nature of litigation is necessarily attenuated in favor of judicial trust in executive assurances regarding classification and operational sensitivity. For plaintiffs, this raisesthe bar significantly; challenging withholdings now requires more than pointing out gaps in public affidavits—it demands affirmative evidence that an investigation has closed or that circumstances have fundamentally shifted.

# Beyond Word Counts: The Expanding Scope of 'Reasonable Segregability' in Counterterrorism Files

In the high-stakes arena of national security litigation, the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) often collides with the imperative to protect sensitive investigative methods. While much attention is typically paid to the threshold question of whether an exemption applies, *Farahi v. FBI*, decided yesterday by the D.C. Circuit, offers a critical clarification on what happens after an exemption is invoked: the duty to segregate. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Katsas affirmed that the sheer volume of withheld records does not create a presumption of releasable content, nor does the presence of isolated non-exempt words mandate their disclosure if they lack independent informational value. By reinforcing the "inextricably intertwined" doctrine, the court has signaled that in complex counterterrorism files, context is king—and without it, redacted text is merely noise.

The case arose from Foad Farahi's request for his FBI file, a massive trove comprising over 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. The FBI withheld the majority of these records under Exemption 7(A), arguing that disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings related to Farahi's alleged ties to Al Qaeda and other terrorist entities. While Farahi challenged the pendency of these proceedings, his most structurally significant argument concerned segregability. He contended that given the astronomical size of the file, it was logically impossible for every single word to be exempt; surely, amidst thousands of pages, some non-sensitive information could be carved out and released. The D.C. Circuit rejected this arithmetic approach to transparency, holding that the statutory requirement to release "reasonably segregable" portions turns on the utility of the information, not the count of non-exempt words remaining after redaction.

Judge Katsas's reasoning rests on a pragmatic interpretation of what constitutes "information" under FOIA. The opinion leans heavily on precedent establishing that agencies need not release fragments of data that are so interspersed with exempt material that they convey no meaningful insight to the requester. As the court noted, citing *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, the focus of FOIA is on information, "not simply words which the Government has written down." If non-exempt phrases are scattered line-by-line throughout a document discussing sources, methods, and ongoing investigative leads, releasing those孤立的 snippets would not illuminate the subject matter for the public. Instead, it would provide a mosaic of disconnected facts that offer no substantive value while potentially revealing the scope or direction of an investigation through what little remains visible.

This distinction between "words" and "information" is vital for understanding the doctrinal trajectory of national security FOIA cases. Historically, plaintiffs have successfully argued that blanket withholdings violate the segregability mandate, forcing agencies to conduct granular reviews to release even small amounts of data. *Farahi* tightens this lane significantly for intelligence-heavy files. The court essentially validated the FBI's argument that in counterterrorism investigations, the context surrounding a fact is often as sensitive as the fact itself. A name mentioned in passing might be unclassified in isolation, but its placement within a specific chain of custody or alongside a particular surveillance technique could compromise an operation. By accepting that such material is "inextricably intertwined," the court allows agencies to treat entire documents—or large swaths thereof—as non-segregable units when the cost of extraction yields no intelligible product.

The implications of this ruling extend beyond Farahi's specific file to future challenges against wholesale redactions in complex investigations. Plaintiffs can no longer rely solely on the volume of withheld materials as a proxy for bad faith or over-classification. The burden shifts decisively toward demonstrating that specific categories of information can be extracted in a way that retains coherence and utility. Furthermore, the court's willingness to rely on *ex parte* declarations to verify this intertwining underscores the procedural hurdles facing challengers in national security cases. Because verifying segregability often requires examining how non-exempt bits relate to classified contexts, courts are increasingly deferring to agency assertions made behind closed doors, noting that this asymmetry is an "inherent" feature of litigating secrets without exposing them.

Ultimately, *Farahi* serves as a reminder that FOIA's promise of transparency is bounded by functional reality. In an era where intelligence gathering relies on connecting disparate dots across vast datasets, the D.C. Circuit has acknowledged that sometimes removing enough dots to satisfy a redaction requirement leaves a picture too fragmented to be seen at all. For advocates pushing for greater openness in counterterrorism files, this decision suggests that future victories will depend less on demanding page counts and more on developing sophisticated technical arguments about how specific data points can be decoupled from their sensitive operational frameworks without losing their meaning—a formidable task indeed.



# The Invisible Adversary: Procedural Fairness and the Normalization of Ex Parte Evidence in FOIA Litigation

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided yesterday by the D.C. Circuit, the court affirmed the FBI's refusal to release thousands of pages of records concerning an Iranian national allegedly linked to high-level terrorist networks. On its face, the opinion is a routine application of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A), which shields records that could interfere with pending law enforcement proceedings. Judge Katsas's unanimous panel rightly notes the gravity of the underlying allegations—connections to al-Qaeda, the Muslim Brotherhood, and convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla—to justify the withholding. Yet, beneath the substantive holding lies a profound procedural development that deserves closer scrutiny. By validating the government's heavy reliance on ex parte, under-seal declarations to resolve both exemption applicability and segregability, *Farahi* signals a deepening asymmetry in national security FOIA litigation. The decision illustrates how procedural defaults and judicial deference can effectively normalize a process where the plaintiff is forced to challenge invisible evidence with one hand tied behind their back.

The core tension in *Farahi* arises from the mechanism the government used to prove its case. To withhold the records, the FBI submitted public declarations alongside detailed ex parte submissions that neither Farahi nor his counsel were permitted to see. These sealed filings contained the specific facts establishing that investigations were ongoing and explaining why no non-exempt information could be reasonably segregated from the classified whole. When the district court initially balked at the age of these claims, ordering an update, the FBI responded with yet another set of public and ex parte declarations. On appeal, Farahi challenged the sufficiency of these updates and the segregability analysis, arguing essentially that the government's assertions were too conclusory given the passage of time. The D.C. Circuit, however, swept these challenges aside, leaning heavily on the fact that Farahi had failed to object to the use of ex parte procedures in the district court.

Judge Katsas's treatment of the ex parte issue is technically sound but procedurally stark. The opinion leans on *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, a recent precedent acknowledging that while ex parte declarations handicap plaintiffs, they are sometimes "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." Because Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of these sealed filings below, the appellate court deemed any challenge forfeited. This creates a dangerous feedback loop for FOIA litigants: if a plaintiff objects to ex parte procedures early on, they risk appearing obstructive in a national security case; if they remain silent to preserve goodwill or strategy, they waive their right to complain later about relying on unseen evidence. The result is a litigation environment where the government's word, delivered in a vacuum visible only to the judge, becomes self-proving absent a specific, timely objection that few practitioners may realize is necessary to preserve.

The doctrinal implications extend beyond waiver rules into the heart of adversarial testing. In standard civil litigation, summary judgment turns on whether there is a genuine dispute of material fact. That determination usually requires both sides to see the evidence. In *Farahi*, however, the "genuine dispute" regarding whether investigations are truly "pending or reasonably anticipated"—a requirement emphasized in cases like *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW)*—was resolved entirely within the black box of an ex parte filing. The court reasoned that because an earlier ex parte declaration had provided detail, a subsequent terse public update confirming "nothing had changed" was sufficient contextually. While logical within the closed system of sealed briefing, this approach strips the plaintiff of any meaningful ability to test whether an investigation has gone dormant or whether the "interference" rationale has grown stale over years of delay. The burden shifts subtly but significantly; instead of the government proving its case against scrutiny, it merely needs to avoid forfeiture by ensuring its secret filings are technically compliant while hoping for deference on substance.

Furthermore, *Farahi* reinforces a broad interpretation of "non-segregability" when national security is at stake. The court accepted that even if individual words or phrases might theoretically be redacted rather than entire documents withheld, such granular editing is not required if exempt and non-exempt material are "inextricably intertwined." Here, given that Farahi conceded his file involved sensitive counterterrorism probes involving named entities like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and charities financing terrorism ("Holy Land Foundation"), almost anything said about him risks revealing sources and methods or alerting co-conspirators about what remains unknown to them alone .
