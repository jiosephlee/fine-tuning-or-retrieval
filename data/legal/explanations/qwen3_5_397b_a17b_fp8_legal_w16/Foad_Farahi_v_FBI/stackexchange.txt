Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the 'pending or reasonably anticipated' requirement for Exemption 7(A) apply when there is a multi-year gap between the initial declaration and the final judgment?
Question:
In *Farahi v. FBI*, the district court initially denied summary judgment because the original 2019 declarations were stale, ordering an update in 2022. The FBI then submitted a new declaration from Joseph Bender that was quite brief, simply stating investigations 'remain ongoing.' Farahi argued this was too conclusory under *CREW*. However, the appellate court accepted it as a valid 'update' rather than a standalone proof. 

My confusion lies in the temporal aspect: If an agency misses the statutory response deadline and litigation drags on for years, at what point does a simple assertion that an investigation is 'still open' become insufficient? Does the depth of detail required in the affidavit decay over time, or can an old, detailed ex parte declaration (like Hardy's from 2019) indefinitely prop up a newer, vague one (like Bender's)?
Answer:
In *Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit clarifies that the sufficiency of an agency's declaration under Exemption 7(A) does not necessarily "decay" over time, nor does a newer, briefer declaration need to stand entirely on its own if it serves as an **update** to a previously sufficient, detailed record.

The key distinction the court draws is between a declaration intended to **establish** the pendency of proceedings from scratch and one intended to **confirm** that circumstances have not changed since a prior finding.

### The "Update" vs. "Standalone" Distinction
You asked whether a vague assertion like "investigations remain ongoing" becomes insufficient as time passes. Under *CREW v. DOJ*, a purely conclusory statement is indeed inadequate to *initially* prove that proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated. However, the *Farahi* opinion explains that the 2023 Bender declaration was not evaluated in isolation.

Instead, the court viewed the procedural history as a continuum:
1.  **The Baseline:** The 2019 Hardy declaration provided "considerable detail" about the nature and scope of the counterterrorism investigation, establishing a rational nexus between the records and law enforcement duties.
2.  **The Judicial Finding:** In 2022, the district court explicitly found that the FBI had "**seemingly** met its burden" regarding pending proceedings but required an update solely due to the "significant passage of time."
3.  **The Update:** The subsequent Bender declaration was tasked only with confirming that the status quo persisted. Because the underlying factual basis (the detailed Hardy declaration) remained unrebutted and the district court had already tentatively validated it, Bender's terse confirmation that investigations "remain ongoing" was sufficient to bridge the temporal gap.

As Judge Katsas writes, "The government submitted the Bender declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending."

### Contextual Plausibility
Furthermore, the court noted that the "depth of detail" required is contextual. Even if treated more independently, the brevity of the update was bolstered by the external context of Farahi's case. The opinion highlights Farahi's admitted connections to high-level terrorists (such as Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla) and his role in immigration proceedings involving national security risks. Given the gravity and complexity of counterterrorism investigations involving such figures, the court found it "plausible" that an investigation initiated years prior would genuinely remain ongoing.

### Conclusion
To answer your specific questions:
*   **Does the detail required decay?** No. A detailed ex parte declaration (like Hardy's) does not expire automatically. It can continue to support a claim years later if supplemented by a credible update confirming the investigation's continued vitality.
*   **Can an old declaration prop up a new vague one?** Yes, provided the new declaration is framed as an **update** to cure staleness rather than a standalone proof, and where a court has already made a preliminary finding that the original showing was adequate.

The holding suggests that agencies are not required to re-litigate the entire factual basis of an investigation every few years; they must simply provide enough current assurance to satisfy the court that the earlier, detailed findings remain accurate.

### What is the precise procedural mechanism allowing courts to rely exclusively on ex parte, in camera declarations for both exemption and segregability determinations?
Question:
The opinion mentions that the FBI submitted declarations 'under seal and ex parte,' meaning Farahi and his counsel never saw them. The court relied heavily on these sealed documents to determine both that Exemption 7(A) applied and that no information was reasonably segregable. Farahi didn't oppose the use of ex parte filings initially, so he forfeited that argument, but I'm struggling with the due process implications. 

If the plaintiff cannot see the evidence used against them, how can they effectively argue that the categories are overbroad or that segregable facts exist? Is there a standard for when a court *must* require an unsealed Vaughn index versus when total secrecy via ex parte submission is permissible in national security FOIA cases?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Farahi v. FBI*, the procedural mechanism you are asking about is not a special statutory exception, but rather the application of standard **summary judgment practice** adapted to the unique constraints of national security litigation, coupled with the doctrine of **forfeiture**.

The D.C. Circuit clarifies that there is no absolute requirement for a public *Vaughn* index or adversarial testing when doing so would compromise the very secrets the exemption seeks to protect. Here is how the court justifies relying exclusively on *ex parte*, *in camera* declarations for both Exemption 7(A) and segregability determinations:

### 1. The "Catch-22" Justification
The primary legal rationale permitting total secrecy is pragmatic necessity. As Judge Katsas writes, the difficulty *ex parte* submissions create for plaintiffs is "inherent in those FOIA cases where... an *ex parte* declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

In this case, the court determined that providing Farahi with a detailed public justification for why specific information could not be segregated—or exactly what the ongoing investigation entailed—would itself reveal classified or sensitive law enforcement techniques and intelligence. Therefore, the court treated the *ex parte* submission not as an anomaly, but as the only viable method to adjudicate the government's burden under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b) without defeating the purpose of Exemption 7(A).

### 2. Procedural Forfeiture
Crucially, the court notes that Farahi's ability to challenge this mechanism was limited by his own litigation strategy. The opinion explicitly states: "Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of *ex parte* declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."

By failing to object when the FBI moved to file the Hardy and Bender declarations under seal, Farahi forfeited any argument that the procedure itself was impermissible. The appellate court implies that while due process generally favors adversarial testing, a plaintiff who consents to (or fails to oppose) an *in camera* review waives the right to later claim that the lack of access violated their rights.

### 3. The Standard for Sufficiency
You asked if there is a standard for when a court *must* require an unsealed index versus when secrecy is permissible. While *Farahi* does not draw a bright-line rule for all future cases, it reinforces existing D.C. Circuit precedent that allows courts to rely on government affidavits if they show **"reasonable specificity"** regarding why documents cannot be segregated (*Juarez v. DOJ*).

The standard applied here suggests that:
*   **Public Declarations:** Must establish the general nexus between the records and law enforcement duties (which Hardy did publicly).
*   **Ex Parte Declarations:** Are permitted to supply the granular details regarding the scope of ongoing investigations and the intertwining of exempt/non-exempt material when public disclosure would cause harm.

The court affirmed that because the district judge personally reviewed these sealed declarations and found them sufficiently detailed to confirm that non-exempt material was "inextricably intertwined" with exempt material, the summary judgment burden was met without needing a public *Vaughn* index for those specific segments.

### Conclusion
There is no separate statutory mechanism authorizing exclusive reliance on *ex parte* filings; rather, it is a judicial determination that **adversarial disclosure is impossible without compromising national security**, combined with the plaintiff's **failure to object** to the procedure at trial. As long as the agency provides "reasonable specificity" in its sealed submissions and the district court conducts a thorough *in camera* review, due process is satisfied even if the plaintiff never sees the evidence used against them.

### How does the 'inextricably intertwined' doctrine practically negate the statutory mandate to release 'reasonably segregable' portions of a record?
Question:
FOIA explicitly requires agencies to redact exempt info and release the rest (§ 552(b)). Yet, in this case involving 10,750 pages and 80 CDs, the court affirmed that *nothing* was reasonably segregable. The court cited *Mead Data Central*, saying that if non-exempt material is 'interspersed line-by-line,' it doesn't count as segregable because releasing isolated words isn't releasing 'information.'

I am having trouble distinguishing between a legitimate claim that documents are intertwined versus an agency using this as a blanket excuse to avoid redaction work. What factual showing must an agency make in their affidavit to prove that redaction is impossible versus just difficult? Does the sheer volume of records (10k+ pages) shift the burden of proof regarding segregability?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Farahi v. FBI*, the "inextricably intertwined" doctrine does not technically negate the statutory mandate to release segregable portions; rather, it defines the outer limit of what constitutes a "reasonably segregable portion" under the statute. The court's reasoning clarifies that the mandate applies only to **meaningful information**, not merely to non-exempt words or phrases standing alone.

Here is how the opinion addresses your specific concerns regarding the distinction between legitimate intertwining and agency overreach, the required factual showing, and the impact of volume.

### 1. The Legal Standard: Information vs. Words
The core tension you identify is resolved by the D.C. Circuit's adoption of the *Mead Data Central* standard, which the *Farahi* court reiterates: **"The focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down."**

Under this doctrine, if non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line" with exempt material such that releasing the non-exempt fragments would yield no coherent or useful intelligence, those fragments are not considered "reasonably segregable." In *Farahi*, the court acknowledged that while individual words or phrases might theoretically be disclosed without harming law enforcement interests, releasing them would not constitute releasing "information." Therefore, when exempt and non-exempt matter are so thoroughly mixed that separation destroys the utility of the remainder, the entire document (or section) may be withheld.

### 2. The Required Factual Showing
You asked what factual showing an agency must make to prove redaction is impossible versus just difficult. According to *Farahi*, the agency satisfies its burden by submitting affidavits that explain with **"reasonable specificity"** why segregation cannot occur.

In this case, the FBI met this standard through:
*   **Detailed Declarations:** The agency provided both public and *ex parte* declarations (from Section Chiefs Hardy and Bender).
*   **Judicial Verification:** Crucially, the district court reviewed the *ex parte* submission and found it contained a "detailed justification" for why no meaningful information could be segregated.
*   **Deference to Context:** The appellate court noted that because the case involved national security and counterterrorism investigations, the executive branch's assertions regarding the sensitivity of the data are entitled to significant deference.

The opinion suggests that an agency does not need to demonstrate physical impossibility (i.e., that they literally cannot cut out a sentence). Instead, they must demonstrate functional impossibility: that doing so would leave behind disjointed fragments that convey no intelligible information to the requester. The court relies heavily on the district judge's ability to review sensitive materials *in camera* (or via *ex parte* declarations) to verify that this "line-by-line" interspersion actually exists.

### 3. Does Volume Shift the Burden?
The sheer volume of records (10,750 pages and 80 CDs) did **not** shift the burden of proof or create a presumption against total withholding in *Farahi*.

While Farahi argued logically that "there must be some segregable... information in [such] a vast volume," the court rejected this probabilistic argument in favor of a qualitative one. The holding indicates that volume alone does not compel segregation if the nature of every page within that volume suffers from the same "inextricably intertwined" defect. If every page consists of classified investigative techniques or sources mixed with trivial non-exempt details line-by-line, then 10,000 pages of such documents yield zero reasonably segregable *information*, regardless of the page count.

### Summary for Practice
To distinguish a legitimate claim of intertwining from a blanket refusal to redact, look for:
1.  **Specificity in Affidavits:** Does the declaration explain *how* the exempt and non-exempt material are mixed (e.g., "sources names appear in every paragraph alongside operational details")? Vague assertions may fail (*CREW*), but detailed descriptions usually succeed.
2.  **Judicial Review:** Has a court actually reviewed an *ex parte* declaration confirming this mix? In *Farahi*, the affirmation rested largely on the fact that a judge had seen the unredacted files and agreed with the FBI's assessment.
3.  **Nature of Remaining Text:** Would releasing what remains provide actual insight ("information"), or merely isolated words? If it is only the latter, *Farahi* holds that segregation is not required by statute.

### To what extent does an applicant's prior concession in separate immigration proceedings waive their right to challenge the 'law enforcement purpose' nexus in a FOIA suit?
Question:
Section IV.A of the opinion states that Farahi conceded in his immigration case that he was investigated for ties to terrorist organizations and specific individuals like Khaled Sheikh Mohamed. The appellate court uses this external concession to immediately satisfy the 'compiled for law enforcement purposes' prong of Exemption 7(A), noting the FBI gets deference anyway.

Does this mean that if a requester has ever admitted to being under investigation in any other forum (civil, administrative, criminal), they are essentially precluded from arguing that the records weren't compiled for law enforcement purposes in a subsequent FOIA lawsuit? How does this interact with the requirement that the agency itself must establish the rational nexus?
Answer:
No, the court's reliance on Farahi's prior concessions does not create a blanket rule that any admission of an investigation in a separate forum automatically waives a FOIA plaintiff's right to challenge the "law enforcement purpose" nexus. Rather, the *Farahi* opinion illustrates how a plaintiff's judicial admissions in parallel proceedings can serve as powerful **evidentiary support** that helps the agency meet its burden of proof, particularly when combined with the deference owed to the FBI.

Here is how the opinion reconciles the applicant's concession with the agency's independent burden:

### 1. The Agency Retains the Burden of Proof
The opinion reaffirms the foundational principle that "[t]he government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies." To satisfy Exemption 7(A)'s threshold requirement, the FBI must still establish a "rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties'" and a connection between the individual and a "possible security risk or violation of federal law" (citing *Campbell* and *Pratt*).

The court did not hold that Farahi's concession *replaced* the FBI's obligation to submit declarations establishing this nexus. Instead, the FBI submitted detailed public and *ex parte* declarations (from Agents Hardy and Bender) explicitly stating that the records were compiled during investigations into national security violations and terrorism.

### 2. Concessions as Corroborative Context
The court used Farahi's concessions in his immigration proceedings not as a procedural waiver, but as factual corroboration that rendered the FBI's assertions indisputable.

In Section IV.A, the court notes that while the FBI's declarations established the investigative nature of the file, "**broader context confirms that assertion.**" The court points out that Farahi himself had previously conceded in district court filings that he was investigated for ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations and specific individuals like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed.

Because Farahi had already admitted these facts in another judicial forum, he could not credibly argue in the FOIA suit that there was no rational nexus between the FBI's files and a law enforcement duty regarding terrorism. The concession removed any genuine issue of material fact regarding the *purpose* of the compilation, allowing the court to conclude: "We have no doubt that the FBI carried its burden."

### 3. The Role of Deference
The interaction between the concession and the agency's burden is mediated by the standard of review. The opinion emphasizes that "because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference," especially in national security contexts (*CNSS*).

When an agency provides a plausible declaration asserting a law enforcement purpose (which receives deference), and the plaintiff has simultaneously admitted facts in another proceeding that align perfectly with that assertion, the plaintiff effectively undermines their own ability to contest the nexus. The concession does not shift the legal burden to the plaintiff; rather, it makes it impossible for the plaintiff to rebut the agency's prima facie case.

### Conclusion
An applicant's prior admission does not procedurally waive their right to challenge Exemption 7(A). A plaintiff remains free to argue that records were not compiled for law enforcement purposes even if they admitted to an investigation elsewhere (for example, if they argued they were investigating civil misconduct rather than federal crimes).

However, as *Farahi* demonstrates, such concessions will likely be treated as binding judicial admissions that validate the agency's claim of a rational nexus. If a plaintiff admits to being investigated for terrorism in immigration court, they cannot logically argue in a subsequent FOIA suit that their FBI file lacks a connection to federal law enforcement duties. The concession satisfies the factual predicate necessary for the court to accept the agency's deferred determination.

### Did Farahi forfeit his argument regarding overbroad document categorization by failing to specify which categories were problematic?
Question:
In Section IV.C, Farahi argued generally that the FBI's document categories were too broad. The court dismissed this instantly, citing *Khine v. DHS*, saying he forfeited the argument because he didn't explain *why* specific categories were overbroad.

This seems like a harsh procedural trap. If the descriptions of the categories themselves are vague or generic in the public Vaughn index, how is a pro se or even represented plaintiff supposed to know which ones to attack without seeing the underlying documents? Is there a minimum level of descriptive detail the government must provide in their public declarations to prevent a forfeiture ruling like this?
Answer:
Yes, under the D.C. Circuit's precedent in *Farahi v. FBI*, Farahi did forfeit his argument regarding overbroad document categorization.

The court's reasoning in Section IV.C rests on a specific procedural requirement for FOIA litigants challenging an agency's Vaughn index. While it is true that an agency must define categories "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" (citing *Gallant v. NLRB*), the burden shifts to the plaintiff to identify precisely where the agency failed this standard.

The *Farahi* opinion explicitly states that Farahi "provide[d] no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." Because he argued only in general terms that the categories were too broad without pointing to specific categories and explaining *why* they lumped together exempt and non-exempt material or obscured the court's ability to review them, the court deemed the argument forfeited under *Khine v. DHS*.

To answer your concern about the "procedural trap": The D.C. Circuit does not require a plaintiff to see the underlying documents to challenge the *categories* themselves. Rather, the plaintiff must analyze the public descriptions of those categories in the Vaughn index or declarations. A sufficient challenge would argue, for example, that a category labeled "Investigative Reports" is overbroad because it ostensibly includes both raw intelligence (exempt) and purely administrative scheduling data (non-exempt) without distinction, making judicial review impossible. By failing to make any such specific contention against any specific category listed by the FBI, Farahi failed to preserve the issue for appeal.

The court did not address whether the government's descriptions were minimally sufficient because Farahi never articulated a defect in them; he merely issued a blanket objection. Consequently, regardless of how vague the public index might appear, the failure to specify the defect results in forfeiture.

### How does the D.C. Circuit's definition of 'enforcement proceedings' encompass indefinite counterterrorism investigations without a specific target or imminent prosecution?
Question:
The court reiterates that 'ongoing criminal investigations' count as proceedings under Exemption 7(A). Here, there is no mention of an active grand jury, an indictment pending, or a specific trial date. The justification is purely that releasing info could 'tip off targets' about the scope of the investigation.

I am confused about the limits of this logic. If an investigation into historical terror financing (some events dating back decades) is technically 'open' but has had no active leads mentioned in years, does it still qualify as a 'concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding' under *CREW*? Where is the line drawn between a genuinely active investigation and one kept open solely to invoke FOIA exemptions?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit defines "enforcement proceedings" broadly enough to encompass indefinite counterterrorism investigations because the court treats **ongoing evidence-gathering** itself as a pending proceeding, provided the agency can demonstrate a rational nexus to a potential future criminal case.

Here is how the court draws the line between a genuinely active investigation and one kept open solely to invoke exemptions:

### 1. "Ongoing Investigations" Qualify as Proceedings
The court reaffirms the *CREW* standard that Exemption 7(A) applies to proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." Crucially, the opinion states that **"ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes."** 

You do not need an active grand jury, an indictment, or a specific trial date to satisfy this requirement. As the court notes, citing *Juarez*, "[s]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." Therefore, the "proceeding" is the investigative process itself, not just the subsequent litigation phase.

### 2. The Limit: A "Concrete Prospective" Proceeding
To prevent agencies from keeping files open indefinitely just to block FOIA requests, the court requires the existence of a **"concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding."** 

In *Farahi*, the court found this concreteness not merely through a bare assertion that the file was "open," but through two key factors grounded in the record:
*   **Specific Allegations of Threat:** The court looked to the factual background where Farahi was alleged to be a recruiter for the Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Al Qaeda members like Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, and linked to convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla. These specific ties to designated terrorist organizations and individuals created a plausible basis for believing a future criminal case was possible, distinguishing it from a stale file with no leads.
*   **Detailed Ex Parte Declarations:** While the public declaration by Agent Bender was "surprisingly terse," the court refused to view it in isolation. It relied on the earlier, detailed *ex parte* Hardy declaration which elaborated on the "nature and scope of the investigation." The combination of detailed initial proof plus a confirmation that "nothing had changed" satisfied the burden.

### 3. Addressing Your Concern About Stale Investigations
Your question asks where the line is drawn if an investigation has had "no active leads mentioned in years." Under *Farahi*, the passage of time alone does not invalidate an Exemption 7(A) claim if:
1.  The underlying subject matter involves serious national security threats (which warrants greater deference to the Executive); and
2.  The agency provides sufficient detail (often via *ex parte* submissions) showing that evidence gathering is still occurring or that releasing information would "tip off targets about the scope of the investigation."

The court explicitly rejected Farahi's argument that time cast doubt on the proceeding's status because the FBI successfully demonstrated that revealing information could compromise future actions against high-level terrorist networks. The logic suggests that in complex counterterrorism cases involving decades-old financing or recruitment, investigations remain "active" as long as they are gathering intelligence for a *possible* future prosecution, even if no immediate charges are planned.

**Summary:** The line is drawn at whether the agency can show a **rational nexus** between the investigation and a law enforcement duty (such as prosecuting terrorism violations) supported by specific facts about the suspect's dangerousness. If such a nexus exists, the investigation counts as a "pending proceeding" regardless of how much time has passed or whether an indictment is imminent.
