Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can a court grant a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal based on the affirmative defense of fair use at the pleading stage?
Question:
I understand that fair use is typically an affirmative defense that requires fact-intensive discovery and is usually reserved for summary judgment or trial. However, in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the copyright claims under Rule 12(b)(6), stating the defense was 'so clearly established by the complaint.' What is the procedural standard for deciding fair use on a motion to dismiss? Does the plaintiff essentially have to plead themselves out of court by admitting facts that make fair use undeniable?
Answer:
Yes, you have correctly identified the narrow procedural path the court took. While fair use is indeed an affirmative defense typically resolved after discovery, the Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* reaffirmed that a court may grant a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal when the defense is **"so clearly established by the complaint"** that no factual development could alter the outcome.

The procedural standard relies on the scope of materials a court may consider at the pleading stage. Under Second Circuit precedent cited in the opinion (*DiFolco* and *TCA Television Corp.*), the court reviews the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached as exhibits, and matters subject to judicial notice (such as Copyright Office registrations). If these sources alone demonstrate that the four statutory fair use factors overwhelmingly favor the defendant, the plaintiff has effectively "pleaded themselves out of court."

In *Santos*, the court found this high bar met because Santos's own allegations undermined his infringement claim across all four factors:

1.  **Purpose and Character (Transformative Use):** Santos's complaint admitted that his original videos were intended to convey "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." By contrasting this with his allegation that Kimmel used the clips to mock his "willingness... to say absurd things for money," Santos inadvertently pleaded facts showing the secondary use was highly transformative. The court noted that transformiveness is judged by what a "reasonable observer" would think based on these pleaded purposes, not by the subjective intent of the parties.
2.  **Nature of the Work:** The complaint and incorporated copyright registrations established that the videos were published immediately upon creation, weighing in favor of fair use.
3.  **Amount and Substantiality:** The court determined it could decide this factor simply by comparing the original videos to the JKL segments described in the complaint.
4.  **Market Effect:** Crucially, Santos's complaint portrayed the defendants' motive as "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary." The court reasoned that because creators generally do not license critical reviews or lampoons of their own work, such uses fall outside the notion of a "potential licensing market." Therefore, Santos failed to allege any cognizable market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor.

Furthermore, regarding Santos's argument that Kimmel's deception (using fictitious names) constituted bad faith that should nullify fair use, the court rejected this based on the complaint's internal logic. The opinion notes that while *Harper & Row* mentioned good faith, later jurisprudence has distanced itself from that requirement. More importantly, Santos's own complaint contradicted a claim of bad faith aimed at commercial supplantation, instead painting a picture of motivation driven by satire.

**Conclusion**
You do not need to prove fair use definitively at the motion to dismiss stage in every case; rather, dismissal is appropriate only when the **plaintiff's own factual allegations** render the fair use defense indisputable. In *Santos*, by explicitly detailing the divergent purposes of the original and secondary works and describing the secondary use as parody/criticism, Santos provided all necessary facts for the court to conclude as a matter of law that no reasonable jury could find infringement.

### Why did the court reject Santos's argument that Kimmel's deception regarding his identity negated the 'transformative' nature of the use?
Question:
Santos argued that because Kimmel lied to get the videos made, he couldn't claim the use was transformative (commentary vs. personal message). The court relied on *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* to say subjective intent doesn't matter, focusing instead on what a 'reasonable observer' perceives. How does this objective 'reasonable observer' test work when the context of creation involves fraud? If the creator didn't know they were being mocked, how can the resulting work be considered a commentary on their willingness to be mocked?
Answer:
The court rejected Santos's argument because, under current Supreme Court precedent, the **transformative nature of a work is determined objectively**, based on how a "reasonable observer" perceives the new work's purpose, rather than subjectively based on the creator's intent or the method of acquisition.

Here is how the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion applies this doctrine to your specific questions about fraud and context:

### 1. The Objective "Reasonable Observer" Standard
Santos argued that because Kimmel deceived him into making the videos, Kimmel could not claim the use was transformative commentary. The court squarely rejected this by relying on *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023). As Judge Lohier writes, "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."

The court distinguished between two different purposes:
*   **The Original Purpose:** What a reasonable observer perceives from the raw video alone. The opinion notes that Santos's own allegations admit a reasonable observer would see "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."
*   **The Secondary Purpose:** What a reasonable observer perceives when the video is aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* within the segment "Will Santos Say It?" In this context, the purpose shifts to commenting on "the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money."

Because these two purposes are distinct, the use is transformative. The fact that Kimmel privately intended to mock Santos *before* recording the video (subjective intent) is legally irrelevant; what matters is that the final broadcast conveys a different message to the audience than the original standalone video.

### 2. Why Fraud Does Not Negate Transformative Use
You asked how the test works when the creator didn't know they were being mocked. The court addresses this by separating the **acquisition** of the material from the **character** of the use.

Santos attempted to argue that Kimmel's deception constituted "bad faith," which historically (citing *Harper & Row*) could undermine a fair use defense. However, the *Santos* opinion clarifies that:
1.  Supreme Court jurisprudence has "distanced itself" from strict good-faith requirements in fair use analysis.
2.  Even if bad faith were relevant, Santos's own complaint undermined his argument. By alleging that Kimmel created a "mocking series of segments," Santos effectively admitted that Defendants were motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary," not merely an attempt to "supplant" Santos's commercial market for personalized greetings.

The court's logic implies that while fraud might give rise to other causes of action (like fraudulent inducement, which was dismissed here for lack of damages), it does not alter the semantic content of the final broadcast. A viewer watching *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* understands they are watching satire, regardless of how Jimmy Kimmel obtained footage. The "context of creation" involving fraud does not change the "context of consumption," which is where the transformative analysis resides.

### Summary
The court held that fairness in *how* the footage was obtained (the fraud argument) does not negate the transformation in *what* the footage communicates to the public. Since a reasonable observer sees a shift from a sincere personal message to public political/social satire, Factor One of fair use weighs heavily in favor of Kimmel, irrespective of his deceptive methods in procuring the raw material.

### Does the 'bad faith' requirement from *Harper & Row* still survive as a barrier to fair use after recent Supreme Court precedent?
Question:
The opinion mentions *Harper & Row*'s statement that 'fair use presupposes good faith,' but then immediately says the Supreme Court has 'distanced itself' from this. Given that Kimmel actively deceived Santos to procure the footage, why wasn't this considered bad faith sufficient to kill the fair use defense? Is 'bad faith' now irrelevant in Second Circuit fair use analysis unless it directly relates to market substitution?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the short answer is that while the Supreme Court has not explicitly overruled the "good faith" language from *Harper & Row*, the Second Circuit treats it as effectively moribund when the secondary use is transformative and does not act as a market substitute.

In *Santos*, the court directly addresses your concern regarding Kimmel's deception. You are correct that the opinion notes Kimmel "actively deceived Santos to procure the footage." However, the court rejects the argument that this deception constitutes "bad faith" sufficient to defeat a fair use defense. The panel explains this in two steps:

1.  **Supreme Court Retreat:** The opinion explicitly states that although *Harper & Row* declared that "[f]air use presupposes good faith," the Supreme Court has "since distanced itself from this statement." The court does not elaborate on which specific cases constitute this distancing, but it treats the proposition as weakened authority rather than a binding mandate.
2.  **Focus on Market Supplantation:** More importantly, the Second Circuit pivots the analysis away from the user's conduct (the deception) and toward the economic effect of the use. The court holds that even if bad faith were relevant, Santos's own complaint negated it because it failed to allege a purpose to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights. Instead, the complaint portrayed the defendants as motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary."

The opinion clarifies that under current Second Circuit precedent, "bad faith" in the form of deceptive procurement methods does not automatically kill a fair use defense if the resulting work is transformative and serves a different purpose than the original. The court emphasizes that the inquiry focuses on whether the secondary work acts as a "competing substitute" in the market. Since parody and criticism are unlikely to be licensed by the original creator (citing *Campbell*), there is no cognizable market harm, rendering the defendant's method of obtaining the material largely irrelevant to the statutory analysis.

Therefore, based on *Santos*, 'bad faith' is not entirely irrelevant, but it is no longer an independent bar to fair use. It appears to survive only insofar as it evidences an intent to create a market substitute. If the use is transformative (as Kimmel's mockery was found to be) and causes no market substitution, deceptive conduct in procuring the source material will not defeat the defense.

### How does the court distinguish between market harm caused by criticism versus market harm caused by substitution in Factor Four?
Question:
Regarding the fourth fair use factor, Santos seemed to argue that airing these videos damaged his brand and future earning potential. The court rejected this, citing *Campbell* and *Hachette*, saying we don't care if criticism devalues the work, only if it acts as a substitute. Can someone explain the line between 'devaluing through parody' (which is allowed) and 'usurping the market'? If no one hires Santos anymore because of this segment, isn't that effectively destroying his market for personalized videos?
Answer:
The distinction you are asking about lies at the heart of the fourth fair use factor: the difference between **market substitution** (which weighs against fair use) and **market devaluation through criticism** (which does not).

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit clarifies this line by relying heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* and its own recent precedent in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*. The court explains that the fourth factor asks "not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

Here is how the court draws that line in this specific context:

**1. Substitution vs. Criticism**
Market harm under Factor Four occurs only when the secondary work serves as a **substitute** for the original. This means the secondary work fulfills the same demand as the original, causing consumers to choose the copy over the licensed original.
*   **Substitution:** If Kimmel had aired Santos's videos simply so viewers could watch Santos's message without paying him, that would be substitution. Viewers would say, "I don't need to hire Santos; I can just watch Kimmel."
*   **Criticism/Parody:** Here, the court found Kimmel's use was transformative because it changed the purpose of the videos from "feelings of hope... and positivity" to a commentary on Santos's "willingness... to say absurd things for money." The audience watches the segment *for* the mockery, not for the original inspirational content. Therefore, the segment does not compete with Santos's Cameo business; it critiques it.

**2. Why Lost Future Earnings Don't Count as "Market Harm"**
You asked: *"If no one hires Santos anymore because of this segment, isn't that effectively destroying his market?"*

Under the doctrine articulated in *Santos*, the answer is **no**. The court explicitly notes that copyright law does not protect an author from losing income due to unfavorable criticism. As cited in the opinion, *Campbell* establishes that creators generally will not license critical reviews or lampoons of their own work; therefore, such uses fall outside the scope of any "potential licensing market" protected by copyright.

If Santos loses future clients because Kimmel exposed him as willing to say "absurd things for money," that is reputational damage resulting from protected speech (criticism/parody), not copyright infringement. The copyright monopoly grants control over reproduction and distribution, not control over public perception or reputation. To rule otherwise would grant copyright holders a veto power over negative reviews or satirical takes, which would stifle the very creativity and free expression fair use is designed to promote.

**Conclusion**
The line is drawn based on **function**, not financial outcome.
*   If the new work functions as a **replacement** for the old one, it causes cognizable market harm.
*   If the new work functions as **commentary** that happens to lower the value or demand for the original by exposing its flaws or mocking its message, that is not market harm under Factor Four.

In *Santos*, because Kimmel's segments did not offer a substitute for personalized congratulatory videos but rather offered a comedic critique of Santos's conduct, any loss of future earnings was deemed irrelevant to the fair use analysis.

### Why did Santos lack standing to sue for breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service if he was the content creator?
Question:
The court dismissed the breach of contract claim because Santos wasn't a party to the Terms of Service agreement between Kimmel (the user) and Cameo. This seems counterintuitive since Santos provided the service. Under Illinois law, why is there such a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties' here? Since other parts of the ToS explicitly mentioned third-party beneficiaries, does that imply that where it is silent, creators like Santos intentionally waived their right to enforce user obligations?
Answer:
The court's dismissal of Santos's breach of contract claim rests on the fundamental principle of **privity of contract** and the specific rules governing **third-party beneficiaries** under Illinois law, which governed the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS).

Although Santos was the content creator who performed the service, he was not a signatory to the agreement between the user (Kimmel) and the platform (Cameo). Under Illinois law, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). To overcome this presumption, the intent to benefit a third party cannot be merely implied; it must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration." *Id.*

The *Santos* opinion highlights a critical piece of textual evidence within the ToS itself to demonstrate that this high bar was not met: **the presence of express third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere in the contract.** The court reasoned that because other provisions of the ToS explicitly contained language allowing enforcement by third parties, the drafters knew how to create such rights when intended. Consequently, the silence regarding creators in the specific provisions Kimmel allegedly breached indicates a deliberate choice *not* to confer enforceable rights upon them. As the court noted, "when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly."

Therefore, Santos lacked standing not because he didn't provide value to the transaction, but because the contract he sought to enforce was strictly between Kimmel and Cameo. Without an "express declaration" extending enforcement rights to creators, Illinois law precludes him from suing as a third-party beneficiary for breaches of user obligations.

### What specific elements prevented an 'implied-in-fact' contract from existing between Santos and Kimmel despite the transaction occurring?
Question:
Even if there was no written contract between Santos and Kimmel directly, why didn't the act of requesting and paying for a video create an implied-in-fact contract under New York law? The court said Santos failed to allege a 'meeting of minds' regarding specific terms. Isn't there an implicit understanding in every Cameo transaction that the buyer won't misuse the video? What specific factual allegations was Santos missing in his complaint to prove this implied agreement?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the absence of an implied-in-fact contract stems not from the lack of a transaction, but from Santos's failure to plead the **essential terms** of such an agreement and the lack of factual allegations demonstrating a **"meeting of the minds"** regarding those specific terms.

Under New York law, which governs this claim, the formation of an implied-in-fact contract requires proof of "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine both existence and the extent of the terms. *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep't 2002). Crucially, a plaintiff must allege these terms in "nonconclusory language." *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep't 1994).

The Second Circuit identified two specific deficiencies in Santos's complaint that prevented the contract claim from surviving a motion to dismiss:

1.  **Failure to Plead Essential Terms:** The court noted that Santos's complaint failed to identify "those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." While you suggest there is an "implicit understanding" that buyers won't misuse videos, the court requires that such understandings be pled as specific contractual terms. Santos did not articulate what specific restriction Kimmel allegedly agreed to beyond general conclusions.
2.  **No Alleged Meeting of Minds Regarding Platform Terms:** Santos attempted to argue that Kimmel was bound by the Cameo Terms of Service via an implied contract. However, the court found no plausible allegation that there was "an indication of a meeting of minds... that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service." *DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass'n Compliance & Legal Div.*, 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep't 2008). The mere act of purchasing a video was insufficient to imply an agreement by Kimmel to abide by platform rules vis-à-vis Santos, particularly where the Terms of Service themselves contained express third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere but not for the provisions Santos sought to enforce.

In short, while a transaction occurred, New York law requires more than the exchange of money for goods to find an implied-in-fact contract; it requires factual allegations showing both parties mutually understood and agreed to specific restrictive terms limiting how the buyer could use the purchased content. Santos's complaint lacked these necessary factual details.

### Why is 'out-of-pocket loss' a mandatory element for fraudulent inducement in New York, and how does it differ from other damages?
Question:
The fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed because Santos didn't allege 'out-of-pocket loss.' He got paid for every video he made, so he didn't lose money on the transactions themselves. But shouldn't reputational damage or lost future earnings count as damages for fraud? Why does New York law strictly require direct financial loss (money spent or lost in the deal) rather than consequential damages for this specific tort?
Answer:
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim specifically because the plaintiff failed to plead **out-of-pocket loss**, a mandatory element under New York law. While you correctly note that Santos received payment for every video and thus suffered no direct financial deficit in the transactions, New York courts distinguish sharply between the types of damages recoverable in fraud versus other torts.

Under New York law, a cause of action for fraudulent inducement requires five elements: (1) a material misrepresentation of fact, (2) knowledge of its falsity, (3) an intent to induce reliance, (4) justifiable reliance by the plaintiff, and (5) **damage**. As the court notes citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, this fifth element strictly demands "actual out-of-pocket loss."

The rationale for this strict requirement lies in the "benefit-of-the-bargain" rule versus the "out-of-pocket" rule. In many jurisdictions, fraud plaintiffs can recover the difference between what they received and what they were promised (benefit-of-the-bargain), which could theoretically encompass lost future earnings or value. However, New York adheres to the stricter **out-of-pocket loss** standard for common law fraud. This measures damages as the difference between the price paid and the actual value of what was received. 

In *Santos*, because Santos performed the services (making the videos) and was fully compensated for them via the Cameo platform, he suffered no monetary loss on the exchange itself. The value he received (payment) equaled or exceeded the cost he incurred (his time and effort in creating the videos). Consequently, even if Kimmel's deception caused reputational harm or precluded Santos from securing more lucrative future contracts (consequential damages), these do not satisfy the specific pleading requirement for fraudulent inducement in New York. As the opinion states, without an allegation of "actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud," the claim cannot survive a motion to dismiss.

Therefore, while reputational damage and lost future earnings are valid forms of injury in torts like defamation or perhaps breach of contract depending on foreseeability, they are legally insufficient to sustain a standalone claim for fraudulent inducement under New York's rigorous standing requirements regarding pecuniary loss.
