Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The Objective Observer in the Age of Satire: *Santos v. Kimmel* and the Irrelevance of Subjective Intent Post-*Warhol*

Late-night comedy has long operated in a legal gray zone where the line between theft and transformation is drawn by the sharpness of the joke rather than the length of the clip. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, decided yesterday by the Second Circuit, that line was clarified with striking precision, reinforcing a principle that is becoming increasingly central to modern copyright jurisprudence: the subjective intent of a copyright holder—or even the machinations of a satirist—matters far less than how a reasonable observer perceives the final work. Former Congressman George Santos's appeal, challenging Jimmy Kimmel's use of personalized Cameo videos in a mocking segment titled "Will Santos Say It?", offered the court a unique factual matrix. Unlike traditional cases where a defendant appropriates existing footage, here the defendant actively solicited the creation of the copyrighted material through deception. Yet, writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Lohier affirmed the dismissal of Santos's claims, holding that Kimmel's deceptive procurement did not strip the subsequent broadcast of its transformative character. The decision serves as a potent reminder that in the wake of *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, fair use is an objective inquiry into the meaning of the work, not a moral audit of how it came to be.

The crux of Santos's argument rested on a novel theory of authorship and purpose. He contended that because Kimmel instigated the creation of the videos by posing as various fictitious fans, Kimmel was effectively the "designer" of their original purpose. Under this logic, Santos argued, the videos were never truly intended to convey "hope, strength, [and] perseverance," as his complaint admitted they appeared to do, but were always destined to be tools of ridicule. Therefore, Santos reasoned, Kimmel's later use of the clips on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* merely superseded his own original design rather than adding something new. The Second Circuit swiftly dismantled this contention by leaning heavily on the Supreme Court's recent guidance in *Warhol*. As Judge Lohier noted, whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user. The court found that while Santos subjectively knew he was selling generic greetings, and Kimmel subjectively knew he was setting a trap, the objective reality perceived by an audience was distinct: one context offered personal encouragement, while the other provided social commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. By severing the link between Kimmel's deceptive setup and the legal analysis of transformativeness, the court ensured that satirists can engage subjects without forfeiting fair use protections simply because they orchestrated the encounter.

This rigorous adherence to an objective standard also allowed the court to sidestep potential pitfalls regarding "bad faith." Santos attempted to revive an older strand of fair use jurisprudence suggesting that dishonest conduct nullifies the defense, citing *Harper & Row*'s suggestion that fair use presupposes "good faith and fair dealing." However, the Second Circuit correctly identified that higher courts have largely distanced themselves from treating bad faith as a dispositive factor, particularly when doing so would chill protected speech. The opinion astutely observes that Santos's own complaint undermined his bad faith argument; by alleging that Kimmel's motive was sarcastic criticism rather than commercial substitution, Santos inadvertently confirmed the expressive nature of the use. If Kimmel had merely wanted to resell Santos's videos as generic greetings to compete with Santos in Cameo's marketplace, that would be infringement. But using them to highlight political absurdity falls squarely within the realm of commentary protected by *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*. The deception involved in acquiringthe videos went tothe mannerof acquisition—a potential tort issue separate from copyright—notthe characterofthe use itself.

Beyond copyright, the opinion offers a succinct but significant rebuke to expanding theories of contractual liability in digital marketplaces. Santos attempted to pivot to state law claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement, arguing that Kimmel violated Cameo's Terms of Service by using false identities.
