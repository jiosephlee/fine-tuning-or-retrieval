# The Objective Observer in the Age of Satire: *Santos v. Kimmel* and the Irrelevance of Subjective Intent Post-*Warhol*

Late-night comedy has long operated in a legal gray zone where the line between theft and transformation is drawn by the sharpness of the joke rather than the length of the clip. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, decided yesterday by the Second Circuit, that line was clarified with striking precision, reinforcing a principle that is becoming increasingly central to modern copyright jurisprudence: the subjective intent of a copyright holder—or even the machinations of a satirist—matters far less than how a reasonable observer perceives the final work. Former Congressman George Santos's appeal, challenging Jimmy Kimmel's use of personalized Cameo videos in a mocking segment titled "Will Santos Say It?", offered the court a unique factual matrix. Unlike traditional cases where a defendant appropriates existing footage, here the defendant actively solicited the creation of the copyrighted material through deception. Yet, writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Lohier affirmed the dismissal of Santos's claims, holding that Kimmel's deceptive procurement did not strip the subsequent broadcast of its transformative character. The decision serves as a potent reminder that in the wake of *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, fair use is an objective inquiry into the meaning of the work, not a moral audit of how it came to be.

The crux of Santos's argument rested on a novel theory of authorship and purpose. He contended that because Kimmel instigated the creation of the videos by posing as various fictitious fans, Kimmel was effectively the "designer" of their original purpose. Under this logic, Santos argued, the videos were never truly intended to convey "hope, strength, [and] perseverance," as his complaint admitted they appeared to do, but were always destined to be tools of ridicule. Therefore, Santos reasoned, Kimmel's later use of the clips on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* merely superseded his own original design rather than adding something new. The Second Circuit swiftly dismantled this contention by leaning heavily on the Supreme Court's recent guidance in *Warhol*. As Judge Lohier noted, whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user. The court found that while Santos subjectively knew he was selling generic greetings, and Kimmel subjectively knew he was setting a trap, the objective reality perceived by an audience was distinct: one context offered personal encouragement, while the other provided social commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. By severing the link between Kimmel's deceptive setup and the legal analysis of transformativeness, the court ensured that satirists can engage subjects without forfeiting fair use protections simply because they orchestrated the encounter.

This rigorous adherence to an objective standard also allowed the court to sidestep potential pitfalls regarding "bad faith." Santos attempted to revive an older strand of fair use jurisprudence suggesting that dishonest conduct nullifies the defense, citing *Harper & Row*'s suggestion that fair use presupposes "good faith and fair dealing." However, the Second Circuit correctly identified that higher courts have largely distanced themselves from treating bad faith as a dispositive factor, particularly when doing so would chill protected speech. The opinion astutely observes that Santos's own complaint undermined his bad faith argument; by alleging that Kimmel's motive was sarcastic criticism rather than commercial substitution, Santos inadvertently confirmed the expressive nature of the use. If Kimmel had merely wanted to resell Santos's videos as generic greetings to compete with Santos in Cameo's marketplace, that would be infringement. But using them to highlight political absurdity falls squarely within the realm of commentary protected by *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*. # Bad Faith No More? The Erosion of the 'Good Faith' Requirement in Fair Use Doctrine

The intersection of celebrity satire, digital platforms, and copyright law rarely produces opinions that reshape foundational doctrines, but the Second Circuit's recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a striking clarification on the role of "good faith" in fair use analysis. Former Congressman George Santos sued comedian Jimmy Kimmel and ABC after Kimmel, using fictitious identities on the Cameo platform, commissioned personalized videos from Santos only to air them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a segment mocking Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. While the outcome—a dismissal of Santos's claims—might seem predictable given the overtly parodic nature of the broadcast, the court's reasoning regarding the defendant's deceptive procurement of the source material signals a significant doctrinal shift. By explicitly rejecting the argument that Kimmel's subterfuge constituted "bad faith" sufficient to nullify a fair use defense, the Second Circuit appears to be finalizing the erosion of the ethical constraints once thought central to Section 107.

At the heart of Santos's appeal was the contention that Kimmel's use of fake names to commission the videos demonstrated bad faith, which should automatically disqualify the fair use defense. This argument leaned heavily on language from the Supreme Court's 1985 decision in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, which famously stated that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." For decades, lower courts have wrestled with how strictly to apply this dictum, particularly when a secondary user obtains material through deception or breach of confidence. However, Judge Lohier's opinion for the Second Circuit cuts through this ambiguity with notable brevity. The court acknowledged the *Harper & Row* quote but immediately noted that the Supreme Court has "since distanced itself from this statement." More importantly, the panel found that even if bad faith were relevant, Santos's own complaint undermined his position: the allegations painted a picture of defendants motivated by sarcastic criticism and commentary, not an intent to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights.

This distinction is crucial. The Second Circuit effectively decouples the morality of the acquisition method from the legality of the subsequent use, provided the ultimate purpose is transformative rather than substitutive. In *Santos*, Kimmel's deception was instrumental in creating the parody; without posing as a fan willing to pay for nonsensical requests, he could not have generated the raw footage necessary to critique Santos's commodification of his public persona. Had the court adopted a rigid view where any deceit in procurement tainted the entire fair use analysis, it would have created a perverse incentive structure. Creators engaging in investigative journalism or biting satire often rely on undercover methods or false pretenses to reveal truths or highlight hypocrisies. By refusing to let Kimmel's fibs about his identity defeat his First Amendment-inflected copyright defense, the Second Circuit prioritizes the expressive value of the secondary work over the cleanliness of the creator's hands during the gathering phase.

The doctrinal implications of this approach extend well beyond late-night television gags. The opinion suggests that "bad faith" in fair use is narrowing into a specific inquiry about market substitution rather than general ethical conduct. The court reinforced this by citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, noting that creators are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions; therefore, such uses fall outside the notion of a potential licensing market regardless of how they were obtained. If a secondary user's conduct does not aim to usurp the market for the original work by offering a competing substitute, the manner in which they acquired the underlying material becomes largely irrelevant to the statutory four-factor test. This aligns with modern trends in copyright jurisprudence that view fair use as a flexible safety valve for free expression rather than a reward for virtuous behavior.

Nevertheless, this ruling does not grant carte blanche for theft or fraud in all contexts. The court was careful to limit its holding to situations where there is no intent to supplant commercial rights. Had Kimmel used these videos to sell merchandise or simply rebroadcast them without commentary, deceptive acquisition might still serve as evidence of predatory intent affecting other factors like market harm or purpose and character (as seen in older cases involving stolen manuscripts). Yet, *Santos* makes clear that when transformation is evident and obvious—as it was when juxtaposing Santos's earnest messages against Kimmel's mocking context—the threshold for establishing bad faith rises precipitously high.

Ultimately, *Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a pragmatic update to fair use doctrine for an era defined by reactive media and viral content creation platforms like Cameo and TikTok. It confirms that while copyright law protects authors' economic interests, it does not function as a shield against embarrassment caused by one's own voluntary acts being repurposed for public critique—even if that critique was engineered through trickery. By affirming that fair use can survive deceptive procurement when used for genuine commentary, Second Circuit ensures that satirists need not seek permission (which would never be granted) nor reveal their hand prematurely lest their ability to criticize be chilled before it begins.



# The Platform Paradox: Why Third-Party Beneficiary Claims Fail in Digital Marketplace Disputes

While the media coverage of *Santos v. Kimmel* has understandably fixated on the Second Circuit's robust affirmation of fair use in the realm of political satire, the opinion's treatment of George Santos's state law contract claims reveals a far more structural and troubling reality for the modern digital economy. Beyond the copyright analysis lies a stark reminder of how archaic principles of contract privity collide with the architecture of two-sided digital marketplaces. By dismissing Santos's breach of contract claims because he was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service agreed to by Jimmy Kimmel, the court underscores a critical liability gap: individual content creators often lack standing to enforce platform rules against bad-faith users, leaving them exposed to exploitation while platforms remain insulated from direct enforcement duties.

The core of Santos's state law argument rested on the premise that Kimmel violated Cameo's Terms of Service by submitting requests under fictitious names, and that Santos, as a creator on the platform, should be able to enforce those terms as a third-party beneficiary. The Second Circuit, applying Illinois law to interpret the Terms of Service, swiftly dismantled this theory. The court reiterated the "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties," noting that for such status to attach, the intent must be so clear as to function as an "express declaration." The opinion highlights a crucial textual clue within the Cameo agreement itself: other provisions explicitly granted enforcement rights to third parties, while the provisions regarding user conduct did not. Under standard canons of construction, this silence was fatal to Santos's claim. The court reasoned that where the drafters knew how to create third-party rights and chose not to do so in specific sections, they could not be interpreted as having accidentally conferred them elsewhere.

This rigorous adherence to traditional contract doctrine exposes a significant vulnerability for gig-economy workers and independent creators. In physical commerce, a vendor might have direct contractual recourse against a customer who violates store policy through fraud or deception. However, in digital ecosystems like Cameo, Uber, or Upwork, the contractual web is triangular yet legally bifurcated. The platform contracts separately with the provider (Santos) and the consumer (Kimmel). Unless the platform explicitly drafts its Terms of Service to create a horizontal right of action between these two distinct groups—a rarity driven by fears of increased litigation exposure—the creator is left without a contractual shield. As Judge Lohier's opinion illustrates, even egregious conduct, such as inducing performance through deliberate deception about one's identity, does not automatically generate a cause of action for the performer if no "meeting of minds" ever occurred between them and the deceptive user.

The implications extend beyond the inability to sue for breach; they touch upon the very nature of reliance in digital transactions. Santos also attempted to argue for an implied-in-fact contract or fraudulent inducement, but these too foundered on the rocks of specificity and damages requirements under New York law. The court noted that Santos failed to plead essential terms of any implied agreement with Kimmel, nor could he demonstrate the requisite "out-of-pocket loss" for fraud, given that he was paid for his services regardless of Kimmel's ulterior motives. This creates a perverse incentive structure: bad actors can exploit technical loopholes in verification systems or anonymity features to manipulate creators into generating content for purposes antithetical to their brand or intent, secure in the knowledge that the creator has no direct contractual lever to pull. The platform retains discretion over whether to police its users, but the individual creator bears the reputational and professional cost when that policing fails.

Ultimately, *Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a cautionary tale for legislators and platform designers alike. While the Second Circuit correctly applied existing precedent regarding privity and third-party beneficiaries, the result highlights a doctrinal lag behind technological reality. Current contractual frameworks presume a bilateral relationship that simply does not exist in multi-sided markets. Until platforms choose to rewrite their Terms of Service to expressly empower creators as third-party beneficiaries—or until courts begin to recognize new theories of duty in algorithmic marketplaces—creators will remain legally isolated islands. They are bound by platform rules yet powerless to enforce them against the very users whose behavior those rules are designed to curb. For now, the remedy for such exploitation remains limited to public shaming or statutory claims like copyright fair use defenses, rather than direct contractual accountability.
