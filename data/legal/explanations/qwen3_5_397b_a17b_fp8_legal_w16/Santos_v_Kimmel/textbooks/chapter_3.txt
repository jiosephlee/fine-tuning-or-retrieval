Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # Preemption and State Law Contract Claims

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the plaintiff attempted to salvage his litigation strategy by pivoting from federal copyright claims to various state law causes of action, including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. While the district court initially dismissed the contract claims on the grounds that they were preempted by the federal Copyright Act, the Second Circuit took a more efficient path. The appellate court affirmed the dismissal not by resolving the complex preemption question, but by determining that Santos's complaints failed to state a valid claim under applicable state laws regardless of preemption. This section of the opinion serves as a critical lesson in civil procedure and contract law: even if a claim survives federal preemption hurdles, it must still satisfy the substantive elements of state contract formation and the rigorous pleading standards required at the motion-to-dismiss stage. By analyzing why Santos could not enforce the Cameo platform's Terms of Service as a non-signatory and why his allegations of an implied agreement were insufficient, students can understand how courts dissect contractual relationships in the digital age.

## Copyright Act Preemption Doctrine Overview

When a plaintiff brings state law claims that overlap with rights protected by the Copyright Act, defendants often argue that those state claims are "preempted." Under Section 301 of the Copyright Act, Congress intended to create a uniform federal system for protecting creative works, thereby displacing state laws that grant equivalent rights. Generally, a state law claim is preempted if the work at issue falls within the subject matter of copyright and if the rights granted under state law are equivalent to the exclusive rights provided by federal copyright law. In *Santos*, the district court found that Santos's breach of contract claims were indeed preempted because they essentially sought to prevent the unauthorized reproduction and display of his videos—rights already governed by federal statute.

However, an important exception exists for contracts. Courts have long recognized that private agreements can impose obligations that differ from default copyright rules without being preempted, provided the claim requires an "extra element" beyond mere copying. Typically, this extra element is a promise between parties that creates a duty distinct from the general public's duty not to infringe. In this case, Santos argued his claims were qualitatively different from copyright infringement because they relied on specific contractual promises made through the Cameo platform. Interestingly, the Second Circuit explicitly declined to resolve whether these claims were actually preempted. Instead, Judge Lohier noted that because the claims failed on their own merits under state law principles, there was no need to reach the constitutional or statutory preemption analysis. This judicial economy highlights a key strategic reality for litigants: defeating a claim on substantive state law grounds renders the often-thorny preemption debate moot.

## Direct Breach of Contract: Third-Party Beneficiary Status under Illinois Law

A central pillar of Santos's breach of contract theory was his attempt to enforce the Cameo Terms of Service directly against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC. Santos alleged that Kimmel violated these terms by using fictitious names to request videos, thereby breaching provisions regarding user conduct. The fatal flaw in this argument was privity of contract. Santos was not a signatory to the agreement between Kimmel (the user) and Cameo (the platform); he was merely the talent fulfilling a request generated through that platform. To sue for breach of a contract to which one is not a party, a plaintiff must qualify as an intended third-party beneficiary.

The court applied Illinois law to interpret the Cameo Terms of Service, as specified within the agreement itself. Under Illinois jurisprudence, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This presumption places a heavy burden on anyone claiming third-party status. The court explained that for a non-signatory to enforce a contract provision, the intent to benefit that third party must be so clear that it amounts to practically an express declaration within the text of the agreement. Ambiguity or incidental benefit is insufficient; the contracting parties must have specifically intended to grant enforcement rights to outsiders like Santos.

## The Presumption Against Non-Contracting Party Benefits

Applying this strict standard, the Second Circuit found Santos's direct breach of contract claim untenable. The opinion notes that Santos could identify no language in the Cameo Terms of Service stating that performers (who are not parties to the user agreement) were intended beneficiaries of the rules governing user registration or identity verification. The court engaged in a textual analysis of the Terms of Service and observed that other specific provisions within the same document *did* contain explicit third-party beneficiary clauses. For instance, certain sections expressly allowed third parties to enforce specific obligations.

This contrast proved decisive. Under standard principles of contract construction (*expressio unius est exclusio alterius*), when drafters include third-party beneficiary language in some sections but omit it in others, courts infer that they did not intend to create such rights where silence prevails. Because Cameo had demonstrated it knew how to confer third-party rights when desired, its failure to do so regarding user identity provisions indicated that performers like Santos were not intended enforcers of those terms. Consequently, Santos lacked standing to sue for Kimmel's alleged violation of user guidelines because he was legally invisible to that specific contractual relationship. Without standing as an intended beneficiary, his direct breach of contract claim collapsed immediately under Illinois law.
