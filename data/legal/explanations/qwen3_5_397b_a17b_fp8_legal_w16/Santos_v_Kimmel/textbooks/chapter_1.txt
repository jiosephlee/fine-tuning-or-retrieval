Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Procedural Posture and Standards of Review

To fully grasp the Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel*, one must first understand the specific procedural juncture at which the case was decided. This opinion does not represent the outcome of a full trial where witnesses testified and evidence was weighed; rather, it is a review of a dismissal granted at the very beginning of the litigation process. The former Congressman George Santos filed suit alleging copyright infringement and various state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney after Kimmel used fictitious names to commission personalized videos on the Cameo platform, only to air them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a segment mocking Santos's willingness to say "absurd things for money." The District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed Santos's complaint before the defendants were even required to file an answer or engage in discovery. Consequently, the appellate court's task is not to determine definitively whether Kimmel's actions were morally commendable or legally pristine based on a complete factual record, but solely to determine whether Santos's written complaint contained sufficient legal allegations to survive a motion to dismiss.

## Origins of the Dispute: The 'Will Santos Say It?' Segments

The factual foundation for this procedural analysis rests on the sequence of events described in Santos's complaint. Santos alleged that he created personalized videos in response to requests submitted through Cameo, believing he was fulfilling orders from individual fans. In reality, these requests were made by Jimmy Kimmel under pseudonyms. Kimmel subsequently broadcast these videos on his late-night talk show as part of a recurring satirical series titled "Will Santos Say It?" The core tension driving the lawsuit is the divergence between the purported purpose of the videos when created—conveying messages of "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity"—and the purpose for which they were ultimately used by the defendants: comedic commentary criticizing Santos's character. While these facts set the stage for a substantive debate over fair use and contractual obligations, at this procedural stage, they exist only as allegations accepted as true for the sake of argument. The court assumes these events occurred exactly as Santos described them to test whether, even under that most favorable reading, the law provides him a remedy.

## The District Court's Rule 12(b)(6) Dismissal

The mechanism by which this case was halted in the lower court was Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which allows a defendant to move for dismissal for "failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted." When the District Court granted this motion, it effectively ruled that even if every fact Santos alleged were proven true at trial, he still would not win as a matter of law. Specifically, Judge Cote concluded that Kimmel's use of the videos constituted "fair use" under the Copyright Act, serving as an absolute bar to Santos's federal copyright claims. Regarding the state law claims—including breach of contract and fraudulent inducement—the District Court found they were either preempted by federal copyright law or independently failed to meet the necessary legal elements. By dismissing the case at this early stage, the District Court signaled that no amount of additional evidence or discovery could cure the fundamental legal deficiencies in Santos's pleading. This sets a high bar for reversal on appeal; Santos cannot simply argue that he needs more time to find proof, but must demonstrate that his initial legal theory was sound.

## De Novo Review Standard for Motions to Dismiss

On appeal, the Second Circuit applied a *de novo* standard of review to the District Court's dismissal. As noted by Judge Lohier citing *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, reviewing a case *de novo* means the appellate court gives no deference to the lower court's legal conclusions. Instead, the Second Circuit steps into shoes of the district judge and analyzes the complaint anew, applying its own independent judgment regarding whether it states valid causes of action while accepting all well-pleaded factual allegations as true. However crucial this independence is regarding questions of law—such as interpreting statutory text like Section 107 (Fair Use) or analyzing preemption—it does not allow either court to make credibility determinations or weigh conflicting evidence. If there had been ambiguity requiring extrinsic evidence outside four corners of pleadings , summary judgment might have been appropriate procedure instead outright dismissal below; here though both courts agreed facts presented framed pure question applicable doctrines warranted rejection plaintiff theories threshold level litigation . This rigorous standard underscores why opinions affirming dismissals often hinge strictly on textual interpretation statutes precedents rather than equities particular situation litigants involved .
