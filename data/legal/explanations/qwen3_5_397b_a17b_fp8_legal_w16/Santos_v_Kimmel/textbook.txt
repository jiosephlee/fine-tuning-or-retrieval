# Procedural Posture and Standards of Review

To fully grasp the Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel*, one must first understand the specific procedural juncture at which the case was decided. This opinion does not represent the outcome of a full trial where witnesses testified and evidence was weighed; rather, it is a review of a dismissal granted at the very beginning of the litigation process. The former Congressman George Santos filed suit alleging copyright infringement and various state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney after Kimmel used fictitious names to commission personalized videos on the Cameo platform, only to air them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a segment mocking Santos's willingness to say "absurd things for money." The District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed Santos's complaint before the defendants were even required to file an answer or engage in discovery. Consequently, the appellate court's task is not to determine definitively whether Kimmel's actions were morally commendable or legally pristine based on a complete factual record, but solely to determine whether Santos's written complaint contained sufficient legal allegations to survive a motion to dismiss.

## Origins of the Dispute: The 'Will Santos Say It?' Segments

The factual foundation for this procedural analysis rests on the sequence of events described in Santos's complaint. Santos alleged that he created personalized videos in response to requests submitted through Cameo, believing he was fulfilling orders from individual fans. In reality, these requests were made by Jimmy Kimmel under pseudonyms. Kimmel subsequently broadcast these videos on his late-night talk show as part of a recurring satirical series titled "Will Santos Say It?" The core tension driving the lawsuit is the divergence between the purported purpose of the videos when created—conveying messages of "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity"—and the purpose for which they were ultimately used by the defendants: comedic commentary criticizing Santos's character. While these facts set the stage for a substantive debate over fair use and contractual obligations, at this procedural stage, they exist only as allegations accepted as true for the sake of argument. The court assumes these events occurred exactly as Santos described them to test whether, even under that most favorable reading, the law provides him a remedy.

## The District Court's Rule 12(b)(6) Dismissal

The mechanism by which this case was halted in the lower court was Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which allows a defendant to move for dismissal for "failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted." When the District Court granted this motion, it effectively ruled that even if every fact Santos alleged were proven true at trial, he still would not win as a matter of law. Specifically, Judge Cote concluded that Kimmel's use of the videos constituted "fair use" under the Copyright Act, serving as an absolute bar to Santos's federal copyright claims. Regarding the state law claims—including breach of contract and fraudulent inducement—the District Court found they were either preempted by federal copyright law or independently failed to meet the necessary legal elements. By dismissing the case at this early stage, the District Court signaled that no amount of additional evidence or discovery could cure the fundamental legal deficiencies in Santos's pleading. This sets a high bar for reversal on appeal; Santos cannot simply argue that he needs more time to find proof, but must demonstrate that his initial legal theory was sound.

## De Novo Review Standard for Motions to Dismiss

On appeal, the Second Circuit applied a *de novo* standard of review to the District Court's dismissal. As noted by Judge Lohier citing *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, reviewing a case *de novo* means the appellate court gives no deference to the lower court's legal conclusions. Instead, the Second Circuit steps into shoes of the district judge and analyzes the complaint anew, applying its own independent judgment regarding whether it states valid causes of action while accepting all well-pleaded factual allegations as true. However crucial this independence is regarding questions of law—such as interpreting statutory text like Section 107 (Fair Use) or analyzing preemption—it does not allow either court to make credibility determinations or weigh conflicting evidence. # Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Defense

The central legal conflict in *Santos v. Kimmel* revolves around whether Jimmy Kimmel's broadcast of personalized videos created by former Congressman George Santos constitutes copyright infringement or is protected under the doctrine of fair use. When Santos filed his complaint, he alleged that Kimmel, along with ABC and Disney, infringed his copyrights by airing videos Santos had created for the Cameo platform. These videos were solicited by Kimmel under fictitious names and subsequently edited into a recurring segment on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* titled "Will Santos Say It?" The District Court dismissed Santos's complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim, ruling that the fair use defense barred the copyright claims as a matter of law. On appeal, the Second Circuit affirmed this dismissal, providing a instructive example of how courts analyze fair use at the pleading stage. The court's reasoning underscores that fair use is not merely an excuse for infringement but a flexible mechanism designed to prevent the rigid application of copyright law from stifling the very creativity the statute aims to promote. To understand the court's holding, one must examine the four statutory factors codified in 17 U.S.C. § 107 and how they interact with the specific factual allegations regarding parody and commentary.

## The Four Statutory Factors of Fair Use (17 U.S.C. § 107)

The Copyright Act establishes a framework for evaluating fair use through four non-exclusive factors. As outlined in *Santos*, these factors are: (1) the purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. While all four factors must be weighed together, the Second Circuit emphasized that they are not applied mechanically. Instead, they serve as guideposts to determine whether a secondary use serves the public interest by adding something new, rather than merely superseding the object of the original creation. In *Santos*, the court found that the first factor—the purpose and character of the use—was particularly dispositive, though it also addressed why Santos's challenges to the remaining three factors failed based on the face of his own complaint.

## Factor One: Transformative Purpose vs. Original Intent

The primary battleground in *Santos* was the first fair use factor, specifically focusing on whether Kimmel's use was "transformative." A use is transformative if it adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, altering the original with new expression, meaning, or message. Santos argued that Kimmel's use was not transformative because Kimmel himself had instigated the creation of the videos by submitting requests through Cameo. Essentially, Santos contended that because Kimmel "designed" the original purpose of the videos by requesting them, he could not later claim a transformative purpose when broadcasting them.

The Second Circuit rejected this argument by distinguishing between who commissioned a work and how that work is ultimately used. # Preemption and State Law Contract Claims

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the plaintiff attempted to salvage his litigation strategy by pivoting from federal copyright claims to various state law causes of action, including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. While the district court initially dismissed the contract claims on the grounds that they were preempted by the federal Copyright Act, the Second Circuit took a more efficient path. The appellate court affirmed the dismissal not by resolving the complex preemption question, but by determining that Santos's complaints failed to state a valid claim under applicable state laws regardless of preemption. This section of the opinion serves as a critical lesson in civil procedure and contract law: even if a claim survives federal preemption hurdles, it must still satisfy the substantive elements of state contract formation and the rigorous pleading standards required at the motion-to-dismiss stage. By analyzing why Santos could not enforce the Cameo platform's Terms of Service as a non-signatory and why his allegations of an implied agreement were insufficient, students can understand how courts dissect contractual relationships in the digital age.

## Copyright Act Preemption Doctrine Overview

When a plaintiff brings state law claims that overlap with rights protected by the Copyright Act, defendants often argue that those state claims are "preempted." Under Section 301 of the Copyright Act, Congress intended to create a uniform federal system for protecting creative works, thereby displacing state laws that grant equivalent rights. Generally, a state law claim is preempted if the work at issue falls within the subject matter of copyright and if the rights granted under state law are equivalent to the exclusive rights provided by federal copyright law. In *Santos*, the district court found that Santos's breach of contract claims were indeed preempted because they essentially sought to prevent the unauthorized reproduction and display of his videos—rights already governed by federal statute.

However, an important exception exists for contracts. Courts have long recognized that private agreements can impose obligations that differ from default copyright rules without being preempted, provided the claim requires an "extra element" beyond mere copying. Typically, this extra element is a promise between parties that creates a duty distinct from the general public's duty not to infringe. In this case, Santos argued his claims were qualitatively different from copyright infringement because they relied on specific contractual promises made through the Cameo platform. Interestingly, the Second Circuit explicitly declined to resolve whether these claims were actually preempted. Instead, Judge Lohier noted that because the claims failed on their own merits under state law principles, there was no need to reach the constitutional or statutory preemption analysis. This judicial economy highlights a key strategic reality for litigants: defeating a claim on substantive state law grounds renders the often-thorny preemption debate moot.

## Direct Breach of Contract: Third-Party Beneficiary Status under Illinois Law

A central pillar of Santos's breach of contract theory was his attempt to enforce the Cameo Terms of Service directly against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC. Santos alleged that Kimmel violated these terms by using fictitious names to request videos, thereby breaching provisions regarding user conduct. The fatal flaw in this argument was privity of contract. Santos was not a signatory to the agreement between Kimmel (the user) and Cameo (the platform); he was merely the talent fulfilling a request generated through that platform. To sue for breach of a contract to which one is not a party, a plaintiff must qualify as an intended third-party beneficiary.

The court applied Illinois law to interpret the Cameo Terms of Service, as specified within the agreement itself. Under Illinois jurisprudence, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This presumption places a heavy burden on anyone claiming third-party status. The court explained that for a non-signatory to enforce a contract provision, the intent to benefit that third party must be so clear that it amounts to practically an express declaration within the text of the agreement. Ambiguity or incidental benefit is insufficient; the contracting parties must have specifically intended to grant enforcement rights to outsiders like Santos.

## The Presumption Against Non-Contracting Party Benefits

Applying this strict standard, the Second Circuit found Santos's direct breach of contract claim untenable. The opinion notes that Santos could identify no language in the Cameo Terms of Service stating that performers (who are not parties to the user agreement) were intended beneficiaries of the rules governing user registration or identity verification. The court engaged in a textual analysis of the Terms of Service and observed that other specific provisions within the same document *did* contain explicit third-party beneficiary clauses. For instance, certain sections expressly allowed third parties to enforce specific obligations.

This contrast proved decisive. Under standard principles of contract construction (*expressio unius est exclusio alterius*), when drafters include third-party beneficiary language in some sections but omit it in others, courts infer that they did not intend to create such rights where silence prevails. Because Cameo had demonstrated it knew how to confer third-party rights when desired, its failure to do so regarding user identity provisions indicated that performers like Santos were not intended enforcers of those terms. Consequently, Santos lacked standing to sue for Kimmel's alleged violation of user guidelines because he was legally invisible to that specific contractual relationship. Without standing as an intended beneficiary, his direct breach of contract claim collapsed immediately under Illinois law.

# Fraudulent Inducement and Damages Requirements

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the plaintiff's attempt to pursue state law remedies alongside his copyright claims culminated in a challenge to the dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim. While much of the litigation focused on the transformative nature of parody under copyright law, the disposition of the fraud claim turned on a fundamental principle of New York tort law: the necessity of proving actual economic injury. The Second Circuit's affirmation of the District Court's dismissal provides a clear lesson on the pleading standards for fraud, specifically illustrating that a cause of action for fraudulent inducement cannot survive without an allegation of out-of-pocket financial loss. This section examines why the receipt of payment for the induced act served as a fatal barrier to Santos's fraud claim, distinguishing between mere deception and legally cognizable damages.

## Elements of Fraudulent Inducement under New York Common Law

To understand the court's reasoning in *Santos*, one must first grasp the specific architecture of a fraudulent inducement claim under New York law. Unlike general negligence or breach of contract, fraud is an intentional tort that requires a heightened showing of both misconduct and resulting harm. As cited by the Second Circuit in *Santos*, referencing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, a plaintiff must typically allege that the defendant made a material misrepresentation of fact, known to be false, with the intent to induce reliance, and that the plaintiff justifiably relied on that misrepresentation to their detriment. However, the critical element that often determines the viability of such a claim at the motion-to-dismiss stage is the final component: damages.

The court in *Santos* did not dwell extensively on whether Kimmel's use of fictitious names constituted a misrepresentation or whether Santos relied on those identities; rather, it zeroed in on the absence of alleged injury. Under New York common law, reliance alone is insufficient to sustain a lawsuit. The reliance must result in a tangible loss. The appellate court noted that Santos challenged the dismissal specifically regarding his failure to allege "any out-of-pocket loss." This framing signals that even if every other element of fraud were present—even if Kimmel had acted with malicious deceit—the claim would still fail as a matter of law if Santos could not demonstrate that he lost money or property as a direct result of that deceit. This strict requirement serves as a gatekeeping mechanism, ensuring that courts do not adjudicate grievances based solely on hurt feelings, wounded pride, or reputational slights when no economic transaction has gone awry.

## The Out-of-Pocket Loss Requirement (*Lama Holding Co.*)

The cornerstone of the Second Circuit's analysis regarding the fraud claim is its adherence to the precedent set in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* In *Lama*, New York's highest court clarified that to recover for fraudulent inducement, a plaintiff must prove "actual loss," defined specifically as out-of-pocket pecuniary damages. This standard distinguishes fraud from other torts where nominal damages or emotional distress might suffice. The rule dictates that the plaintiff must show they parted with something of value—money, property, or a legal right—and received less in return than what was promised, or nothing at all due to the欺诈.

In *Santos*, the court applied this rigorous standard directly to the facts alleged in the complaint. Santos argued that he was tricked into creating videos because Kimmel concealed his identity behind pseudonyms on the Cameo platform. However, the court observed that Santos failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket expenditure caused by this deception. Under New York law, as reiterated in cases like *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, speculative damages or harm to one's reputation do not satisfy the damage element for fraudulent inducement. The logic is rooted in compensatory principles: tort law aims to make an injured party whole financially, not to punish bad behavior absent financial injury in this specific context. Because Santos did not spend money to create the videos nor lose existing assets due to Kimmel's ruse, he could not meet the threshold established by *Lama*. The absence of this specific type of loss rendered the claim legally deficient regardless of how egregious the underlying deception might appear.

## Distinguishing Economic Loss from Other Forms of Injury in Fraud Cases

A crucial pedagogical point arising from *Santos* is the sharp distinction New York law draws between economic loss and other forms of injury, such as reputational harm or emotional distress. Plaintiffs often conflate being "harmed" in a colloquial sense with being "damaged" in a legal sense. In this case, Santos undoubtedly suffered reputational consequences; the videos were broadcast nationally on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a segment mocking his willingness to say absurd things for money. One could argue that this exposure damaged his public standing and future earning potential in politics or media. Yet, for purposes of a fraudulent inducement claim premised on the transaction itself, these downstream effects are irrelevant if they do not stem from an immediate out-of-pocket loss tied to the induction.

The court's reasoning implies that while reputational harm might support other causes of action (such as defamation or certain privacy torts), it cannot bootstrap a failing fraud claim where no money was lost in the initial exchange. The "out-of-pocket" rule requires looking strictly at balance sheet realities at the moment of reliance: Did the plaintiff pay money they would not have paid? Did they transfer property they would have kept? If the answer is no, there is no actionable fraud under this specific theory, even if subsequent events cause significant non-economic suffering. By refusing to expand the definition of damages to include reputational injury within this specific tort framework, New York courts maintain a boundary between contract/tort disputes over transactions and broader personal injury claims involving dignity and reputation.
