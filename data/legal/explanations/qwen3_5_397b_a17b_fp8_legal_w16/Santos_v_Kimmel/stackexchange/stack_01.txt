Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can a court grant a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal based on the affirmative defense of fair use at the pleading stage?
Question:
I understand that fair use is typically an affirmative defense that requires fact-intensive discovery and is usually reserved for summary judgment or trial. However, in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the copyright claims under Rule 12(b)(6), stating the defense was 'so clearly established by the complaint.' What is the procedural standard for deciding fair use on a motion to dismiss? Does the plaintiff essentially have to plead themselves out of court by admitting facts that make fair use undeniable?
Answer:
Yes, you have correctly identified the narrow procedural path the court took. While fair use is indeed an affirmative defense typically resolved after discovery, the Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* reaffirmed that a court may grant a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal when the defense is **"so clearly established by the complaint"** that no factual development could alter the outcome.

The procedural standard relies on the scope of materials a court may consider at the pleading stage. Under Second Circuit precedent cited in the opinion (*DiFolco* and *TCA Television Corp.*), the court reviews the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached as exhibits, and matters subject to judicial notice (such as Copyright Office registrations). If these sources alone demonstrate that the four statutory fair use factors overwhelmingly favor the defendant, the plaintiff has effectively "pleaded themselves out of court."

In *Santos*, the court found this high bar met because Santos's own allegations undermined his infringement claim across all four factors:

1.  **Purpose and Character (Transformative Use):** Santos's complaint admitted that his original videos were intended to convey "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." By contrasting this with his allegation that Kimmel used the clips to mock his "willingness... to say absurd things for money," Santos inadvertently pleaded facts showing the secondary use was highly transformative. The court noted that transformiveness is judged by what a "reasonable observer" would think based on these pleaded purposes, not by the subjective intent of the parties.
2.  **Nature of the Work:** The complaint and incorporated copyright registrations established that the videos were published immediately upon creation, weighing in favor of fair use.
3.  **Amount and Substantiality:** The court determined it could decide this factor simply by comparing the original videos to the JKL segments described in the complaint.
4.  **Market Effect:** Crucially, Santos's complaint portrayed the defendants' motive as "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary." The court reasoned that because creators generally do not license critical reviews or lampoons of their own work, such uses fall outside the notion of a "potential licensing market." Therefore, Santos failed to allege any cognizable market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor.

Furthermore, regarding Santos's argument that Kimmel's deception (using fictitious names) constituted bad faith that should nullify fair use, the court rejected this based on the complaint's internal logic. The opinion notes that while *Harper & Row* mentioned good faith, later jurisprudence has distanced itself from that requirement. More importantly, Santos's own complaint contradicted a claim of bad faith aimed at commercial supplantation, instead painting a picture of motivation driven by satire.

**Conclusion**
You do not need to prove fair use definitively at the motion to dismiss stage in every case; rather, dismissal is appropriate only when the **plaintiff's own factual allegations** render the fair use defense indisputable. In *Santos*, by explicitly detailing the divergent purposes of the original and secondary works and describing the secondary use as parody/criticism, Santos provided all necessary facts for the court to conclude as a matter of law that no reasonable jury could find infringement.
