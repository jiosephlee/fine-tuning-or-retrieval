Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did Santos lack standing to sue for breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service if he was the content creator?
Question:
The court dismissed the breach of contract claim because Santos wasn't a party to the Terms of Service agreement between Kimmel (the user) and Cameo. This seems counterintuitive since Santos provided the service. Under Illinois law, why is there such a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties' here? Since other parts of the ToS explicitly mentioned third-party beneficiaries, does that imply that where it is silent, creators like Santos intentionally waived their right to enforce user obligations?
Answer:
The court's dismissal of Santos's breach of contract claim rests on the fundamental principle of **privity of contract** and the specific rules governing **third-party beneficiaries** under Illinois law, which governed the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS).

Although Santos was the content creator who performed the service, he was not a signatory to the agreement between the user (Kimmel) and the platform (Cameo). Under Illinois law, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). To overcome this presumption, the intent to benefit a third party cannot be merely implied; it must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration." *Id.*

The *Santos* opinion highlights a critical piece of textual evidence within the ToS itself to demonstrate that this high bar was not met: **the presence of express third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere in the contract.** The court reasoned that because other provisions of the ToS explicitly contained language allowing enforcement by third parties, the drafters knew how to create such rights when intended. Consequently, the silence regarding creators in the specific provisions Kimmel allegedly breached indicates a deliberate choice *not* to confer enforceable rights upon them. As the court noted, "when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly."

Therefore, Santos lacked standing not because he didn't provide value to the transaction, but because the contract he sought to enforce was strictly between Kimmel and Cameo. Without an "express declaration" extending enforcement rights to creators, Illinois law precludes him from suing as a third-party beneficiary for breaches of user obligations.
