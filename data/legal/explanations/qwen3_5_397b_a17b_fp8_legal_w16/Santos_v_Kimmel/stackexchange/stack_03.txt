Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does the 'bad faith' requirement from *Harper & Row* still survive as a barrier to fair use after recent Supreme Court precedent?
Question:
The opinion mentions *Harper & Row*'s statement that 'fair use presupposes good faith,' but then immediately says the Supreme Court has 'distanced itself' from this. Given that Kimmel actively deceived Santos to procure the footage, why wasn't this considered bad faith sufficient to kill the fair use defense? Is 'bad faith' now irrelevant in Second Circuit fair use analysis unless it directly relates to market substitution?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the short answer is that while the Supreme Court has not explicitly overruled the "good faith" language from *Harper & Row*, the Second Circuit treats it as effectively moribund when the secondary use is transformative and does not act as a market substitute.

In *Santos*, the court directly addresses your concern regarding Kimmel's deception. You are correct that the opinion notes Kimmel "actively deceived Santos to procure the footage." However, the court rejects the argument that this deception constitutes "bad faith" sufficient to defeat a fair use defense. The panel explains this in two steps:

1.  **Supreme Court Retreat:** The opinion explicitly states that although *Harper & Row* declared that "[f]air use presupposes good faith," the Supreme Court has "since distanced itself from this statement." The court does not elaborate on which specific cases constitute this distancing, but it treats the proposition as weakened authority rather than a binding mandate.
2.  **Focus on Market Supplantation:** More importantly, the Second Circuit pivots the analysis away from the user's conduct (the deception) and toward the economic effect of the use. The court holds that even if bad faith were relevant, Santos's own complaint negated it because it failed to allege a purpose to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights. Instead, the complaint portrayed the defendants as motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary."

The opinion clarifies that under current Second Circuit precedent, "bad faith" in the form of deceptive procurement methods does not automatically kill a fair use defense if the resulting work is transformative and serves a different purpose than the original. The court emphasizes that the inquiry focuses on whether the secondary work acts as a "competing substitute" in the market. Since parody and criticism are unlikely to be licensed by the original creator (citing *Campbell*), there is no cognizable market harm, rendering the defendant's method of obtaining the material largely irrelevant to the statutory analysis.

Therefore, based on *Santos*, 'bad faith' is not entirely irrelevant, but it is no longer an independent bar to fair use. It appears to survive only insofar as it evidences an intent to create a market substitute. If the use is transformative (as Kimmel's mockery was found to be) and causes no market substitution, deceptive conduct in procuring the source material will not defeat the defense.
