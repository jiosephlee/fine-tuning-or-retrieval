Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the court reject Santos's argument that Kimmel's deception regarding his identity negated the 'transformative' nature of the use?
Question:
Santos argued that because Kimmel lied to get the videos made, he couldn't claim the use was transformative (commentary vs. personal message). The court relied on *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* to say subjective intent doesn't matter, focusing instead on what a 'reasonable observer' perceives. How does this objective 'reasonable observer' test work when the context of creation involves fraud? If the creator didn't know they were being mocked, how can the resulting work be considered a commentary on their willingness to be mocked?
Answer:
The court rejected Santos's argument because, under current Supreme Court precedent, the **transformative nature of a work is determined objectively**, based on how a "reasonable observer" perceives the new work's purpose, rather than subjectively based on the creator's intent or the method of acquisition.

Here is how the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion applies this doctrine to your specific questions about fraud and context:

### 1. The Objective "Reasonable Observer" Standard
Santos argued that because Kimmel deceived him into making the videos, Kimmel could not claim the use was transformative commentary. The court squarely rejected this by relying on *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023). As Judge Lohier writes, "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."

The court distinguished between two different purposes:
*   **The Original Purpose:** What a reasonable observer perceives from the raw video alone. The opinion notes that Santos's own allegations admit a reasonable observer would see "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."
*   **The Secondary Purpose:** What a reasonable observer perceives when the video is aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* within the segment "Will Santos Say It?" In this context, the purpose shifts to commenting on "the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money."

Because these two purposes are distinct, the use is transformative. The fact that Kimmel privately intended to mock Santos *before* recording the video (subjective intent) is legally irrelevant; what matters is that the final broadcast conveys a different message to the audience than the original standalone video.

### 2. Why Fraud Does Not Negate Transformative Use
You asked how the test works when the creator didn't know they were being mocked. The court addresses this by separating the **acquisition** of the material from the **character** of the use.

Santos attempted to argue that Kimmel's deception constituted "bad faith," which historically (citing *Harper & Row*) could undermine a fair use defense. However, the *Santos* opinion clarifies that:
1.  Supreme Court jurisprudence has "distanced itself" from strict good-faith requirements in fair use analysis.
2.  Even if bad faith were relevant, Santos's own complaint undermined his argument. By alleging that Kimmel created a "mocking series of segments," Santos effectively admitted that Defendants were motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary," not merely an attempt to "supplant" Santos's commercial market for personalized greetings.

The court's logic implies that while fraud might give rise to other causes of action (like fraudulent inducement, which was dismissed here for lack of damages), it does not alter the semantic content of the final broadcast. A viewer watching *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* understands they are watching satire, regardless of how Jimmy Kimmel obtained footage. The "context of creation" involving fraud does not change the "context of consumption," which is where the transformative analysis resides.

### Summary
The court held that fairness in *how* the footage was obtained (the fraud argument) does not negate the transformation in *what* the footage communicates to the public. Since a reasonable observer sees a shift from a sincere personal message to public political/social satire, Factor One of fair use weighs heavily in favor of Kimmel, irrespective of his deceptive methods in procuring the raw material.
