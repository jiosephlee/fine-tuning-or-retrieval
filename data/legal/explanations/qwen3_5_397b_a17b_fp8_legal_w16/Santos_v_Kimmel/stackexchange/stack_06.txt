Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What specific elements prevented an 'implied-in-fact' contract from existing between Santos and Kimmel despite the transaction occurring?
Question:
Even if there was no written contract between Santos and Kimmel directly, why didn't the act of requesting and paying for a video create an implied-in-fact contract under New York law? The court said Santos failed to allege a 'meeting of minds' regarding specific terms. Isn't there an implicit understanding in every Cameo transaction that the buyer won't misuse the video? What specific factual allegations was Santos missing in his complaint to prove this implied agreement?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the absence of an implied-in-fact contract stems not from the lack of a transaction, but from Santos's failure to plead the **essential terms** of such an agreement and the lack of factual allegations demonstrating a **"meeting of the minds"** regarding those specific terms.

Under New York law, which governs this claim, the formation of an implied-in-fact contract requires proof of "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine both existence and the extent of the terms. *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep't 2002). Crucially, a plaintiff must allege these terms in "nonconclusory language." *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep't 1994).

The Second Circuit identified two specific deficiencies in Santos's complaint that prevented the contract claim from surviving a motion to dismiss:

1.  **Failure to Plead Essential Terms:** The court noted that Santos's complaint failed to identify "those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." While you suggest there is an "implicit understanding" that buyers won't misuse videos, the court requires that such understandings be pled as specific contractual terms. Santos did not articulate what specific restriction Kimmel allegedly agreed to beyond general conclusions.
2.  **No Alleged Meeting of Minds Regarding Platform Terms:** Santos attempted to argue that Kimmel was bound by the Cameo Terms of Service via an implied contract. However, the court found no plausible allegation that there was "an indication of a meeting of minds... that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service." *DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass'n Compliance & Legal Div.*, 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep't 2008). The mere act of purchasing a video was insufficient to imply an agreement by Kimmel to abide by platform rules vis-à-vis Santos, particularly where the Terms of Service themselves contained express third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere but not for the provisions Santos sought to enforce.

In short, while a transaction occurred, New York law requires more than the exchange of money for goods to find an implied-in-fact contract; it requires factual allegations showing both parties mutually understood and agreed to specific restrictive terms limiting how the buyer could use the purchased content. Santos's complaint lacked these necessary factual details.
