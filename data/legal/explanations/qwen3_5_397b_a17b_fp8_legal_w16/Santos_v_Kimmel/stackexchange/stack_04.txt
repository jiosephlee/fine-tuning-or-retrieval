Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the court distinguish between market harm caused by criticism versus market harm caused by substitution in Factor Four?
Question:
Regarding the fourth fair use factor, Santos seemed to argue that airing these videos damaged his brand and future earning potential. The court rejected this, citing *Campbell* and *Hachette*, saying we don't care if criticism devalues the work, only if it acts as a substitute. Can someone explain the line between 'devaluing through parody' (which is allowed) and 'usurping the market'? If no one hires Santos anymore because of this segment, isn't that effectively destroying his market for personalized videos?
Answer:
The distinction you are asking about lies at the heart of the fourth fair use factor: the difference between **market substitution** (which weighs against fair use) and **market devaluation through criticism** (which does not).

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit clarifies this line by relying heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* and its own recent precedent in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*. The court explains that the fourth factor asks "not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

Here is how the court draws that line in this specific context:

**1. Substitution vs. Criticism**
Market harm under Factor Four occurs only when the secondary work serves as a **substitute** for the original. This means the secondary work fulfills the same demand as the original, causing consumers to choose the copy over the licensed original.
*   **Substitution:** If Kimmel had aired Santos's videos simply so viewers could watch Santos's message without paying him, that would be substitution. Viewers would say, "I don't need to hire Santos; I can just watch Kimmel."
*   **Criticism/Parody:** Here, the court found Kimmel's use was transformative because it changed the purpose of the videos from "feelings of hope... and positivity" to a commentary on Santos's "willingness... to say absurd things for money." The audience watches the segment *for* the mockery, not for the original inspirational content. Therefore, the segment does not compete with Santos's Cameo business; it critiques it.

**2. Why Lost Future Earnings Don't Count as "Market Harm"**
You asked: *"If no one hires Santos anymore because of this segment, isn't that effectively destroying his market?"*

Under the doctrine articulated in *Santos*, the answer is **no**. The court explicitly notes that copyright law does not protect an author from losing income due to unfavorable criticism. As cited in the opinion, *Campbell* establishes that creators generally will not license critical reviews or lampoons of their own work; therefore, such uses fall outside the scope of any "potential licensing market" protected by copyright.

If Santos loses future clients because Kimmel exposed him as willing to say "absurd things for money," that is reputational damage resulting from protected speech (criticism/parody), not copyright infringement. The copyright monopoly grants control over reproduction and distribution, not control over public perception or reputation. To rule otherwise would grant copyright holders a veto power over negative reviews or satirical takes, which would stifle the very creativity and free expression fair use is designed to promote.

**Conclusion**
The line is drawn based on **function**, not financial outcome.
*   If the new work functions as a **replacement** for the old one, it causes cognizable market harm.
*   If the new work functions as **commentary** that happens to lower the value or demand for the original by exposing its flaws or mocking its message, that is not market harm under Factor Four.

In *Santos*, because Kimmel's segments did not offer a substitute for personalized congratulatory videos but rather offered a comedic critique of Santos's conduct, any loss of future earnings was deemed irrelevant to the fair use analysis.
