Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why is 'out-of-pocket loss' a mandatory element for fraudulent inducement in New York, and how does it differ from other damages?
Question:
The fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed because Santos didn't allege 'out-of-pocket loss.' He got paid for every video he made, so he didn't lose money on the transactions themselves. But shouldn't reputational damage or lost future earnings count as damages for fraud? Why does New York law strictly require direct financial loss (money spent or lost in the deal) rather than consequential damages for this specific tort?
Answer:
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim specifically because the plaintiff failed to plead **out-of-pocket loss**, a mandatory element under New York law. While you correctly note that Santos received payment for every video and thus suffered no direct financial deficit in the transactions, New York courts distinguish sharply between the types of damages recoverable in fraud versus other torts.

Under New York law, a cause of action for fraudulent inducement requires five elements: (1) a material misrepresentation of fact, (2) knowledge of its falsity, (3) an intent to induce reliance, (4) justifiable reliance by the plaintiff, and (5) **damage**. As the court notes citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, this fifth element strictly demands "actual out-of-pocket loss."

The rationale for this strict requirement lies in the "benefit-of-the-bargain" rule versus the "out-of-pocket" rule. In many jurisdictions, fraud plaintiffs can recover the difference between what they received and what they were promised (benefit-of-the-bargain), which could theoretically encompass lost future earnings or value. However, New York adheres to the stricter **out-of-pocket loss** standard for common law fraud. This measures damages as the difference between the price paid and the actual value of what was received. 

In *Santos*, because Santos performed the services (making the videos) and was fully compensated for them via the Cameo platform, he suffered no monetary loss on the exchange itself. The value he received (payment) equaled or exceeded the cost he incurred (his time and effort in creating the videos). Consequently, even if Kimmel's deception caused reputational harm or precluded Santos from securing more lucrative future contracts (consequential damages), these do not satisfy the specific pleading requirement for fraudulent inducement in New York. As the opinion states, without an allegation of "actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud," the claim cannot survive a motion to dismiss.

Therefore, while reputational damage and lost future earnings are valid forms of injury in torts like defamation or perhaps breach of contract depending on foreseeability, they are legally insufficient to sustain a standalone claim for fraudulent inducement under New York's rigorous standing requirements regarding pecuniary loss.
