Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # State Protection and Internal Relocation Alternatives

In the landscape of asylum law, establishing that an applicant has suffered persecution is only the first hurdle. Even if an applicant demonstrates past harm or a well-founded fear of future harm, relief may still be denied if the home country's government is willing and able to provide protection, or if the applicant can safely relocate to another part of their home country. *Jimenez v. Bondi* provides a critical examination of these two distinct but related bars to asylum: the "unable or unwilling" standard regarding state protection and the reasonableness of internal relocation. For the law student, this opinion illustrates how courts scrutinize the sufficiency of an applicant's efforts to seek state aid and evaluate the factual record regarding safety within specific regions of the country of origin. The Tenth Circuit's analysis emphasizes that generalized fears of government corruption must be supported by concrete evidence of systemic failure, and that temporary success in hiding within the home country can significantly undermine claims that nationwide relocation is impossible.

## 'Unable or Unwilling' Standard: Government Response Requirements

The core inquiry into state protection asks whether the government of the applicant's home country is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. As articulated in *Jimenez*, this analysis is not abstract; it relies heavily on evidence regarding the government's actual response to the applicant's specific situation. The court identifies two primary types of information that weigh heavily in this determination: first, whether the government took steps to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered direct protection to the applicant, such as placing them in protective custody. Crucially, this analysis is intertwined with the issue of notice. The court reasoned that without clear notice of the alleged persecution, it is impossible to determine how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have intervened. Therefore, a key factual predicate for claiming a lack of state protection is whether the applicant actually reported the threats or violence to authorities. In *Jimenez*, despite Jimenez's background as a law student and intern for the National Prosecutor's office, and his father's employment within the Colombian criminal justice system, neither utilized these connections to formally report the wrongdoing or corruption they alleged. The court viewed this silence as a significant gap in their claim, noting that without such reporting, there was no empirical basis to conclude the government would have failed them.

## Futility Exceptions: When Reporting Is Dangerous or Useless

Applicants often argue that they did not report crimes because doing so would have been futile or dangerous, particularly when they believe their persecutors are allied with the government. *Jimenez* clarifies that while a failure to report is not automatically fatal to a claim, the burden remains on the applicant to demonstrate that seeking help would indeed have been pointless or perilous. The petitioners in this case argued that contacting authorities was futile because their primary antagonist, "Don Rafa," was politically aligned with then-President Gustavo Petro and operated with impunity. However, the court found this assertion unsupported by the record. The opinion highlights contradictory evidence: law enforcement had previously attempted several searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property specifically to address criminal conduct occurring there. These prior governmental actions served as powerful rebuttal evidence, suggesting that the state was neither wholly captured by nor indifferent to Don Rafa's operations. Consequently, the court concluded that the petitioners could not meet their burden of showing that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them had they chosen to utilize available legal channels. The mere belief that persecutors have high-level ties is insufficient when objective evidence shows the government actively opposing those same actors.

## Assessing Evidence of Systemic vs. Individual Police Failure

A nuanced but vital distinction drawn in *Jimenez* is between systemic government failure and isolated instances of unhelpful behavior by individual officers. To prove that a government is "unwilling" to protect its citizens, an applicant generally must show evidence of systemic breakdown rather than relying on anecdotal interactions. In this case, Jimenez testified that he spoke with two individual police officers who advised him not to seek trouble and implied that police were part of the problem. The court rejected this as proof of statewide inability or unwillingness to protect. Citing precedent from other circuits adopted in its reasoning, the opinion notes that police apathy in a specific instance does not necessarily reflect official government policy or capacity. The fact that two officers gave dismissive advice does not equate to a constitutional or institutional refusal to enforce laws against narco-traffickers. Because Jimenez did not seek help outside his immediate neighborhood or through his established professional networks within the justice system, he failed to generate a record demonstrating that the entire apparatus of the Colombian state was compromised regarding his safety. This teaches students that asylum claims based on state failure require broad evidentiary support; isolated negative experiences with low-level officials are rarely sufficient to overturn findings of fact regarding state capability.

## Internal Relocation Burden: Safety and Reasonableness Test

Even if an applicant establishes a well-founded fear of persecution in their home region, asylum may be denied if they can reasonably relocate to another part of their country where they would be safe. Under federal regulations cited in *Jimenez*, this acts as a rebuttal mechanism for the government: if it shows by a preponderance of evidence that internal relocation is feasible, eligibility for asylum may be extinguished unless conditions have fundamentally changed elsewhere (which was not argued here). The test involves assessing both safety and reasonableness—not merely whether survival is possible underground but whether life can be resumed without undue hardship tied directly to identity-based persecution factors like gender roles affecting mobility (though economic burdens alone are typically insufficient).
