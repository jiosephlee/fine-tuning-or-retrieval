Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Analyzing the Severity of Harm: Why Threats Did Not Constitute Persecution

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit confronted a factual scenario that, while deeply troubling to the average observer, fell short of the rigorous legal threshold required for an asylum grant. The central hurdle for Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family was establishing that the harms they suffered in Colombia amounted to "persecution" under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Although the Immigration Judge found the Petitioners credible, credibility alone does not transform unpleasant or frightening experiences into legally cognizable persecution. This chapter dissects the court's reasoning regarding the severity of harm, illustrating how the Tenth Circuit distinguishes between general hardship and the specific statutory definition of persecution. By analyzing the court's treatment of threats, psychological distress, and isolated incidents of intimidation, students will learn how appellate courts apply the "substantial evidence" standard to reject claims where the inflicted suffering, however real to the applicant, does not meet the high bar set by circuit precedent.

## Defining Persecution: More Than Restrictions or Sporadic Threats

To understand the court's holding in *Jimenez*, one must first grasp the restrictive definition of "persecution" within the Tenth Circuit. The INA does not define the term, leaving its contours to be shaped by case law. As Judge Tymkovich reiterates, citing *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, a finding of persecution requires the "infliction of suffering or harm" upon individuals differing in race, religion, or political opinion in a manner regarded as "offensive." Crucially, this standard demands something more than mere "restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This distinction is vital for law students: not every violation of rights or every scary encounter constitutes persecution. The harm must be severe enough to cross a threshold from harassment or discrimination into systematic oppression or significant physical injury. In *Jimenez*, the court emphasizes that while the Petitioners faced a hostile environment, their experiences were categorized largely as restrictions on their liberty and threats to their safety, which, without escalation to actual violence or detention, remain insufficient under existing precedent.

## The Cumulative Effect Doctrine vs. Isolated Incidents

A common strategy in asylum litigation is to argue that while individual incidents may seem minor, their collective weight creates a pattern of persecution. The Tenth Circuit acknowledges this "cumulative effect" doctrine, noting in *Ritonga v. Holder* that courts must look at incidents collectively rather than in isolation. However, *Jimenez* serves as a critical boundary marker for this doctrine. The court clarifies that aggregation cannot cure a fundamental deficiency in the severity of the underlying acts. If the individual components of a petitioner's experience are merely threats or non-physical intimidation, adding them together does not magically transmute them into persecution. In this case, even when viewing the threatening phone calls, the brochure, and the confrontations as a whole, the court found no evidence of physical injury or sustained confinement. The cumulative effect doctrine allows a series of lesser harms to add up to persecution only if those harms, in total, rise to the level of severe suffering; it does not allow a collection of non-persecutory events to become persecutory simply by virtue of their number.

## Insufficiency of Threats Without Physical Harm or Detention

The most decisive factor in the court's analysis was the absence of physical harm. The Petitioners explicitly conceded during their merits hearing and in their briefing that they suffered no lasting physical injury. While the court notes that physical harm is not strictly *necessary* to prove persecution—as extreme deprivation or prolonged arbitrary detention could suffice—it is often a prerequisite when the primary allegation involves threats. Citing *Karki v. Holder*, the opinion reaffirms that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." The logic here is preventative; asylum is designed for those who have already suffered severe harm or face a high probability of such harm, not necessarily for those who have been threatened but escaped unscathed. In *Jimenez*, despite being held at gunpoint and told to leave their neighborhood, Petitioners were released without injury. The court viewed these episodes as terrifying but ultimately as contingent threats that were never executed to the point of causing bodily harm, thereby failing to meet the severity requirement established in cases like *Brandy v. Holder*.

## Psychological and Emotional Harm as Standalone Grounds for Persecution

Petitioners attempted to bridge the gap left by their lack of physical injuries by pointing to significant psychological and emotional trauma. Mrs. Duarte testified regarding her inability to sleep, and evidence suggested their young son suffered from panic attacks and stress following an incident at his school. While sympathetic, the court ruled that such psychological impacts, stemming directly from threats that did not result in physical harm or detention, are insufficient to establish persecution on their own. Referencing *Brandy*, where an applicant's ten-hour detention and threats were deemed inadequate despite being "psychologically unsettling," Judge Tymkovich makes clear that emotional distress is frequently a byproduct of any threatening situation but rarely qualifies as independent proof of persecution absent more egregious underlying conduct. The court noted that counsel conceded at oral argument that Tenth Circuit jurisprudence has consistently rejected asylum claims based on similar or even more extreme fact patterns involving only emotional harm and unexecuted threats. Thus, subjective feelings of fear and anxiety, however genuine, cannot substitute for objective evidence of severe suffering mandated by statute.

Garland*, brandishing a weapon coupled with verbal commands to leave an area constitutes a threat contingent on compliance rather than an act of completed persecution.
