Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Procedural Posture and Scope of Review

Understanding a federal appellate court's decision in an immigration case begins with mastering the procedural framework that constrains the judges. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit operates not as a fact-finder retrying the case, but as a reviewer of an existing administrative record. The court's task is narrowly defined: to determine whether the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) acted within the bounds of the law and whether its factual conclusions were supported by "substantial evidence." This chapter outlines the factual genesis of the dispute, traces the procedural steps from the issuance of a Notice to Appear to the final agency decision, identifies exactly which legal arguments survived to reach the appellate court, and explains the rigorous standard of review that ultimately dictates the outcome. By grasping these preliminary mechanics, you will see why the court focuses intensely on the sufficiency of the evidence rather than re-weighing the credibility of the witnesses or imagining alternative scenarios.

## Factual Summary: The Jimenez Family's Political Activity and Conflict with Narco-Traffickers

The narrative underlying this appeal centers on Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons, all natives and citizens of Colombia. Before fleeing to the United States in December 2022, the family resided in Bogotá, where they operated a furniture factory and engaged in political activism. Since 2012, they had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party, and during the 2022 presidential election cycle, they actively campaigned for Federico Gutiérrez, a rival to the eventual winner, Gustavo Petro. Their campaign work involved canvassing neighborhoods door-to-door, an activity that initially drew generic threatening phone calls targeting Liberal Party affiliates. These threats escalated in severity when a colleague was kidnapped and murdered, yet the petitioners continued their political efforts undeterred.

The nature of the danger facing the Jimenez family shifted significantly when Mr. Jimenez utilized his background as a provisional lawyer and former intern for the National Prosecutor's Office to assist a client in a property dispute. The tenant occupying his client's property turned out to be Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa," a leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro. Upon discovering that Don Rafa was using the property for illicit activities—including human trafficking and drug operations—and attempting to intervene legally, Mr. Jimenez became a specific target. Unlike earlier generalized political threats, subsequent attacks were direct and personalized: armed men confronted Mr. Jimenez at his factory accusing him of being a "snitch" for prosecutors; they assaulted an employee while searching for the family; and one assailant grabbed Mr. Jimenez's young son outside his school. Following an encounter with a local police officer who advised them to leave rather than offering protection, the family fled Colombia for the United States three months prior to being served with removal proceedings.

## Procedural History: From Notice to Appear to BIA Affirmance

The procedural trajectory of *Jimenez* follows the standard path for individuals arriving at the border without admission or parole. Three months after their arrival, Department of Homeland Security (DHS) officials served each petitioner with a Notice to Appear (NTA), charging them as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). In August 2023, before an Immigration Judge (IJ), petitioners admitted to being inadmissible aliens but simultaneously applied for asylum and withholding of removal based on their experiences in Colombia. # Defining Persecution: The Threshold of Harm

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed a fundamental principle of asylum law: not all hardship, danger, or unpleasantness rises to the level of "persecution" under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Before an applicant can succeed on a claim for asylum or withholding of removal, they must first clear the threshold of proving that they have suffered past persecution or possess a well-founded fear of future persecution. For Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, the tragedy of their experience in Colombia—including death threats, an armed confrontation, and the terrifying abduction attempt of their child—was undeniable. However, in the eyes of the law, the severity of these events did not cross the statutory line required for relief. This chapter examines how the court distinguishes between severe harassment and actionable persecution, focusing on why threats and psychological trauma, absent significant physical injury or total deprivation of liberty, often fail to satisfy this rigorous legal standard.

## Legal Definition of Persecution vs. Harassment or Discrimination

The INA provides no explicit statutory definition for the term "persecution," leaving its contours to be shaped by judicial interpretation. In *Jimenez*, the court relied on established Tenth Circuit precedent to define persecution as the "infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ... in a way regarded as offensive." Crucially, the court emphasized that this concept "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This distinction creates a high bar for applicants; while discrimination, harassment, and general societal violence are regrettable realities in many nations, they do not automatically constitute persecution for immigration purposes.

The court's analysis in *Jimenez* illustrates this dichotomy clearly. The petitioners faced a campaign of intimidation that included threatening phone calls, a menacing brochure labeling them "snitches," and an incident where two men held Jimenez at gunpoint inside his factory. Despite the visceral fear these events generated, the court categorized them largely as threats and restrictions rather than the infliction of actual suffering or harm requisite for persecution. The legal framework demands that the harm be concrete and severe. Because Jimenez conceded during his merits hearing that he suffered no physical harm and sustained no lasting physical injuries from these encounters, the court struggled to classify the events as anything more than severe harassment. The holding underscores that while the subjective terror felt by an applicant is real, the objective legal standard requires a demonstration of harm that exceeds mere threats or temporary constraints on movement.

## The Cumulative Effect Doctrine: Aggregating Incidents to Meet the Standard

Recognizing that persecutors rarely rely on a single act to terrorize their targets, courts employ the "cumulative effect doctrine." This principle dictates that adjudicators must not view incidents in isolation but rather assess whether the collective impact of multiple adverse events amounts to persecution. As cited in *Jimenez*, citing *Ritonga v. Holder*, even if individual incidents might seem insufficient on their own, their aggregation could theoretically reach the necessary threshold of severity. The petitioners in *Jimenez* leaned heavily on this doctrine, asking the court to view the totality of their ordeal—the weekly phone calls, the murder of a colleague, the armed confrontation at the factory, and near-abduction of their son—as a unified pattern of persecution.

However, the application of the cumulative effect doctrine has limits; it cannot transform a series of non-persecutory acts into persecution if none of the underlying components involve sufficient harm. In *Jimenez*, although the court agreed to evaluate the events collectively, it ultimately found that adding together various threats and instances of psychological distress did not alter their fundamental nature. The arithmetic of accumulation failed because zero plus zero still equals zero in terms of physical injury or prolonged detention. The court noted that while Petitioners endured "unfortunate circumstances," summing up threats that lacked follow-through physical violence and emotional distress did not create a composite picture meeting the statutory definition. Consequently, even under a holistic review, substantial evidence supported Board's conclusion that Petitioners' experiences fell short because they lacked an anchor event involving serious bodily harm or extensive deprivation of freedom around which lesser harms could accumulate into persecution.

# The Nexus Requirement: Political Opinion vs. Personal Motive

In the landscape of asylum law, establishing that an applicant has suffered harm is only the first step; the applicant must also prove that the harm occurred "on account of" a statutorily protected ground. This causal link, known as the "nexus" requirement, serves as a critical filter distinguishing refugees entitled to protection from individuals fleeing generalized violence, personal vendettas, or criminal retaliation. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit provides a instructive examination of how courts dissect mixed-motive scenarios where political activity and criminal enterprise intersect. The court's analysis underscores that even when an applicant holds genuine political views and suffers severe threats, asylum eligibility turns on whether those views were a central reason for the persecution, rather than merely incidental to a dispute over money, power, or property.

## Statutory Protected Grounds: Focus on Political Opinion

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines a refugee as someone unable or unwilling to return to their home country due to persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners anchored their claim specifically in "political opinion." They were active members of the Colombian Liberal Party and had campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a rival to the current president, Gustavo Petro. Their activism included door-to-door canvassing during a volatile election cycle,期间 which they received threatening phone calls and witnessed the murder of a colleague.

However, the existence of a political opinion and the occurrence of harmful events are not sufficient on their own. The statute demands a specific relationship between the two. As the court notes, the applicant bears the burden of proving that their political opinion was not just present in the background, but was actually the driver of the persecutor's actions. In *Jimenez*, while the petitioners undoubtedly possessed a political identity that subjected them to some level of risk common to many activists in Colombia, the court scrutinized whether the specific escalations in threats—the armed confrontations at their factory and the abduction attempt involving their child—were truly motivated by that political identity or by something else entirely.

## The 'One Central Reason' Standard in Mixed-Motive Cases

Persecutors often act with multiple motivations. A gang leader might target an individual both because of their political affiliation and because they refuse to pay extortion fees. To address these complex realities Congress enacted the "one central reason" standard. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), an applicant must demonstrate that race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the persecution.

The Tenth Circuit explains that this standard excludes situations where the protected ground is "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." If a protected characteristic plays only a minor role in the persecutor's calculus, the nexus requirement is not met. This standard prevents applicants from qualifying for asylum based on harms that are primarily driven by non-protected motives, such as personal grudges or profit-seeking criminal enterprises, simply because a protected trait happens to exist alongside those motives. In *Jimenez*, the court applied this rigorous test to determine whether the petitioners' political campaigning was a central engine of Don Rafa's aggression or merely a convenient label attached to a different conflict.

## Distinguishing Political Persecution from Criminal Retaliation

A recurring challenge in asylum jurisprudence is distinguishing between persecution based on ideology and retaliation arising from interference with criminal operations. Courts look closely at the timing and content of threats to identify the persecutor's primary intent. In *Jimenez*, the record revealed a clear shift in the nature ofthe danger facingthe family. # Government Protection and Internal Relocation

In the landscape of asylum law, establishing that an applicant has suffered past persecution or possesses a well-founded fear of future persecution is only half the battle. Even if an applicant successfully proves that they were harmed, relief may still be denied if the government of their home country is willing and able to protect them, or if the applicant could reasonably relocate to a safer region within that country. The opinion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* provides a critical illustration of how the Tenth Circuit applies these doctrines, specifically focusing on the evidentiary burdens placed on applicants to demonstrate state failure and the logical framework used to assess internal relocation options. For the law student reading this opinion, understanding the distinction between isolated instances of official indifference and systemic government collapse is essential, as is grasping why localized threats often fail to justify a finding that an entire nation is unsafe.

## State Ability and Willingness: The Duty to Report and Opportunity for Government Response

The core inquiry regarding government protection asks whether the home state is "unable or unwilling" to control the persecutors. As articulated in *Jimenez*, this analysis hinges heavily on whether the government had notice of the danger and a meaningful opportunity to respond. The court emphasizes that an applicant cannot simply assume futility; there must be evidence suggesting that seeking help would have been pointless or dangerous. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that the Colombian government was complicit because their persecutor, "Don Rafa," was politically aligned with the current administration. However, the court found this assertion unsupported by the record. Crucially, Jimenez—a provisional lawyer with connections to the National Prosecutor's office—and his father, who worked in the criminal justice system, never formally reported the specific threats or criminal activities to authorities beyond casual conversations with two local patrol officers.

The court's reasoning underscores a fundamental principle: without a formal report or a clear attempt to seek protection, an appellate court cannot speculate on how the government would have reacted. The opinion notes that while a failure to report is not automatically fatal to a claim, it significantly undermines an argument that the government is unwilling to act. In *Jimenez*, the lack of reporting meant there was no concrete evidence that the state refused protection. Furthermore, the record contained affirmative evidence contradicting the claim of state complicity; law enforcement had previously attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property to curb illegal activities like narcotics trafficking and prostitution. These prior governmental actions suggested an intent to enforce the law rather than an alignment with criminals. Consequently, the court concluded that the petitioners failed to meet their burden of proving that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, as they never gave the state a genuine chance to intervene against the specific threats they faced.

## Systemic Failure vs. Individual Officer Apathy: Evaluating Evidence of Corruption

A common pitfall for asylum seekers, illustrated vividly in *Jimenez*, is conflating the apathy of individual low-level officials with systemic government failure. Petitioners often cite interactions with unhelpful police officers as proof that the entire state apparatus is corrupt or incapable of providing safety. The Tenth Circuit explicitly rejects this equivalence. In *Jimenez*, the family testified that two police officers advised them not to "seek trouble" and implied that officers were part of the problem. While distressing, the court characterized these interactions as anecdotal and insufficient to prove a nationwide policy of non-protection. Relying on Seventh Circuit precedent cited favorably in the discussion, *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court clarified that asylum requires evidence of systemic failure rather than "one-off" conversations with unhelpful personnel.

This distinction protects the integrity of the asylum statute by ensuring it remains a remedy for those fleeing states that have collapsed or turned against their citizens, rather than a shield against isolated incidents of bureaucratic inefficiency or individual misconduct. In *Jimenez*, despite Jimenez's professional background in law and his family's ties to law enforcement, they presented no evidence of broader institutional corruption preventing justice in their case. The court reasoned that if high-level agencies like the National Prosecutor's office were truly compromised regarding Don Rafa, Jimenez—who had worked as an intern there—should have been able to produce documentation or testimony reflecting such systemic barriers. Instead, his reliance on informal chats with street-level patrols failed to bridgethe gap between individual rudeness and state-sponsored persecution. Thus, substantial evidence supportedthe BIA's findingthatthe Colombian state remained capableof protectingits citizens when properly alerted throughofficial channels .
