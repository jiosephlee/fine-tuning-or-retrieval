# Procedural Posture and Scope of Review

Understanding the procedural journey of an immigration case is essential to grasping the limits of a federal appellate court's power. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit does not act as a fact-finder retrying the case from scratch. Instead, it reviews a finalized administrative record created by the Executive Branch. This chapter outlines who the parties are, what relief they sought, the specific decisions made by the lower tribunals, and the rigorous legal standards that govern how the Tenth Circuit evaluates those decisions. By mastering the procedural posture and the scope of review, you will understand why the court focuses its analysis on whether the agency's findings were supported by "substantial evidence" rather than whether the judges would have decided the case differently themselves.

## Parties and Relief Sought: Asylum, Withholding of Removal, and CAT Claims

The petitioners in this case are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons, all natives and citizens of Colombia. Upon arriving in the United States without admission or parole in late 2022, they were placed in removal proceedings by the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). In response to the Notice to Appear charging them as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), the family applied for three distinct forms of humanitarian protection: asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT).

It is crucial to distinguish these forms of relief because they carry different legal standards and consequences. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief that, if granted, allows a noncitizen to remain in the United States and eventually apply for lawful permanent resident status. To qualify, an applicant must prove they are a "refugee"—someone unable or unwilling to return to their home country due to persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. Withholding of removal is a mandatory form of relief with a higher burden of proof; it prevents removal to a specific country where the applicant's life or freedom would be threatened but does not provide a path to citizenship. Finally, CAT protection prohibits removal to a country where it is more likely than not that the individual would be tortured by or with the acquiescence of public officials, regardless of whether the torture is linked to a protected ground like political opinion. While the petitioners initially sought all three protections based on threats from narco-traffickers and alleged government complicity, their appeal to the Tenth Circuit narrows significantly regarding which claims are actively contested.

## Administrative History: IJ Denial and BIA Affirmance

# The Legal Framework for Asylum Eligibility

Understanding the court's decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* requires a firm grasp of the statutory architecture governing asylum in the United States. The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not grant asylum simply because an individual has faced danger or hardship abroad; rather, it provides a specific legal pathway for those who meet the rigorous definition of a "refugee." In *Jimenez*, the Tenth Circuit's denial of relief turns entirely on the Petitioners' failure to satisfy these statutory elements. This chapter unpacks the legal framework applied by the court, detailing how the INA defines eligibility, the distinction between past and future harm, and the specific burdens an applicant must carry to prove their case.

## Statutory Definition of a Refugee (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42))

The gateway to asylum eligibility is the statutory definition of a "refugee" found in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). As cited in *Jimenez*, Congress defines a refugee as any person who is outside their country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return to, or avail themselves of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. Crucially, this fear or experience of persecution must be "on account of" one of five statutorily protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In *Jimenez*, the court emphasizes that asylum is a discretionary form of relief, but eligibility is mandatory only if the applicant first proves they are a refugee. The Petitioners in this case, natives of Colombia, bore the initial burden of fitting their experiences within this narrow definition. The statute creates a causal link requirement; general violence, civil unrest, or personal disputes do not suffice unless they are tied directly to one of the five protected characteristics. The court's analysis begins and ends with whether the Petitioners' suffering was caused by their political opinion or another protected ground, illustrating that the definition acts as a filter excluding those fleeing harm for purely personal or criminal reasons.

## Past Persecution vs. Well-Founded Fear of Future Persecution

An applicant may establish refugee status through two distinct pathways: by demonstrating that they have already suffered "past persecution," or by showing a "well-founded fear of future persecution." These are separate inquiries with different evidentiary focuses. Past persecution looks backward at incidents that have already occurred, asking whether the severity of those incidents rises to the level of legal persecution. Future persecution looks forward, requiring an assessment of probability and risk upon return.

In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners argued both theories, claiming they suffered past persecution through threats and psychological trauma, and that they feared future harm due to the ongoing power of their persecutors. The court addressed these arguments sequentially. First, it analyzed whether the threats, gunpoint confrontations, and harassment constituted "past persecution." Finding that they did not, the court then shifted to whether there was a well-founded fear of future harm. The distinction is vital because failing to prove past persecution removes certain procedural advantages for the applicant, forcing them to rely solely on predictive evidence regarding future risks—a often more difficult hurdle to clear without a history of severe harm.

## The Rebuttable Presumption Arising from Past Persecution

One of the most significant legal consequences of proving past persecution is the creation of a rebuttable presumption regarding future fear. As explained in *Jimenez*, if an applicant successfully demonstrates past persecution, the law presumes they have a well-founded fear of future persecution upon return. This shifts the burden of proof to the government. Once past persecution is established, the government must prove by a preponderance of evidence either that conditions in the country have fundamentally changed such that fear is no longer reasonable, or that the applicant could safely relocate internally within their home country.

However, this presumption is conditional. In *Jimenez*, because the court concluded that the Petitioners failed to establish past persecution—ruling that threats and emotional distress alone were insufficient—the presumption never attached. Consequently, the Petitioners retained the full burden of proving a well-founded fear of future persecution without any assistive presumptions. They had to affirmatively demonstrate that it was more likely than not that they would be persecuted upon return. The court's refusal to find past persecution meant the Petitioners could not leverage this procedural mechanism, leaving them vulnerable on all fronts: they had to prove severity, nexus, and lack of state protection entirely from scratch regarding future risks.

## The Three-Prong Test: Harm, Nexus, and State Control

To succeed on either a past or future persecution claim, an applicant must satisfy a three-prong test derived from circuit precedent and incorporated into the INA's framework. As articulated in *Jimenez*, citing *Niang v. Gonzales* and *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, an applicant must prove: (1) an incident or series of incidents rising to the level of persecution; (2) that such harm was inflicted on account of a statutorily protected ground (nexus); and (3) that the harm was committed by the government or by forces the government is unable or unwilling to control. Failure on any single prong results in denial.

First regarding **harm**, *Jimenez* clarifies that not all unpleasant experiences constitute persecution. The court reiterates that threats alone are generally insufficient and that while physical injury is not strictly required, the harm must be severe enough to be considered "offensive" beyond mere restrictions on liberty or sporadic intimidation. The Petitioners' experience with threatening phone calls and verbal warnings did not meet this high threshold in aggregation.

Second regarding **nexus**, even if harm is severe, it must be linked to a protected ground like political opinion. In *Jimenez*, although Petitioners were active in a political party opposed to current leadership interests ("political opinion"), evidence showed they were targeted specifically because Jimenez interfered with "Don Rafa's" criminal enterprise during his work as an intern lawyer ("personal/professional dispute"). The court ruled their political opinion was merely tangential or incidental rather than "one central reason" for targeting them under 8 U.S.C § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). # Analyzing the Severity of Harm: Why Threats Did Not Constitute Persecution

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit confronted a factual scenario that, while deeply troubling to the average observer, fell short of the rigorous legal threshold required for an asylum grant. The central hurdle for Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family was establishing that the harms they suffered in Colombia amounted to "persecution" under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Although the Immigration Judge found the Petitioners credible, credibility alone does not transform unpleasant or frightening experiences into legally cognizable persecution. This chapter dissects the court's reasoning regarding the severity of harm, illustrating how the Tenth Circuit distinguishes between general hardship and the specific statutory definition of persecution. By analyzing the court's treatment of threats, psychological distress, and isolated incidents of intimidation, students will learn how appellate courts apply the "substantial evidence" standard to reject claims where the inflicted suffering, however real to the applicant, does not meet the high bar set by circuit precedent.

## Defining Persecution: More Than Restrictions or Sporadic Threats

To understand the court's holding in *Jimenez*, one must first grasp the restrictive definition of "persecution" within the Tenth Circuit. The INA does not define the term, leaving its contours to be shaped by case law. As Judge Tymkovich reiterates, citing *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, a finding of persecution requires the "infliction of suffering or harm" upon individuals differing in race, religion, or political opinion in a manner regarded as "offensive." Crucially, this standard demands something more than mere "restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This distinction is vital for law students: not every violation of rights or every scary encounter constitutes persecution. The harm must be severe enough to cross a threshold from harassment or discrimination into systematic oppression or significant physical injury. In *Jimenez*, the court emphasizes that while the Petitioners faced a hostile environment, their experiences were categorized largely as restrictions on their liberty and threats to their safety, which, without escalation to actual violence or detention, remain insufficient under existing precedent.

## The Cumulative Effect Doctrine vs. Isolated Incidents

A common strategy in asylum litigation is to argue that while individual incidents may seem minor, their collective weight creates a pattern of persecution. The Tenth Circuit acknowledges this "cumulative effect" doctrine, noting in *Ritonga v. Holder* that courts must look at incidents collectively rather than in isolation. However, *Jimenez* serves as a critical boundary marker for this doctrine. The court clarifies that aggregation cannot cure a fundamental deficiency in the severity of the underlying acts. If the individual components of a petitioner's experience are merely threats or non-physical intimidation, adding them together does not magically transmute them into persecution. In this case, even when viewing the threatening phone calls, the brochure, and the confrontations as a whole, the court found no evidence of physical injury or sustained confinement. The cumulative effect doctrine allows a series of lesser harms to add up to persecution only if those harms, in total, rise to the level of severe suffering; it does not allow a collection of non-persecutory events to become persecutory simply by virtue of their number.

## Insufficiency of Threats Without Physical Harm or Detention

The most decisive factor in the court's analysis was the absence of physical harm. The Petitioners explicitly conceded during their merits hearing and in their briefing that they suffered no lasting physical injury. While the court notes that physical harm is not strictly *necessary* to prove persecution—as extreme deprivation or prolonged arbitrary detention could suffice—it is often a prerequisite when the primary allegation involves threats. Citing *Karki v. Holder*, the opinion reaffirms that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." The logic here is preventative; asylum is designed for those who have already suffered severe harm or face a high probability of such harm, not necessarily for those who have been threatened but escaped unscathed. In *Jimenez*, despite being held at gunpoint and told to leave their neighborhood, Petitioners were released without injury. The court viewed these episodes as terrifying but ultimately as contingent threats that were never executed to the point of causing bodily harm, thereby failing to meet the severity requirement established in cases like *Brandy v. Holder*.

## Psychological and Emotional Harm as Standalone Grounds for Persecution

Petitioners attempted to bridge the gap left by their lack of physical injuries by pointing to significant psychological and emotional trauma. Mrs. Duarte testified regarding her inability to sleep, and evidence suggested their young son suffered from panic attacks and stress following an incident at his school. While sympathetic, the court ruled that such psychological impacts, stemming directly from threats that did not result in physical harm or detention, are insufficient to establish persecution on their own. Referencing *Brandy*, where an applicant's ten-hour detention and threats were deemed inadequate despite being "psychologically unsettling," Judge Tymkovich makes clear that emotional distress is frequently a byproduct of any threatening situation but rarely qualifies as independent proof of persecution absent more egregious underlying conduct. The court noted that counsel conceded at oral argument that Tenth Circuit jurisprudence has consistently rejected asylum claims based on similar or even more extreme fact patterns involving only emotional harm and unexecuted threats. Thus, subjective feelings of fear and anxiety, however genuine, cannot substitute for objective evidence of severe suffering mandated by statute.

# The Nexus Requirement: Political Opinion vs. Personal Vendetta

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit's analysis of the "nexus" requirement serves as a critical lesson in distinguishing between harm suffered on account of a statutorily protected ground and harm resulting from personal disputes or criminal interference. While the petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, undoubtedly faced severe threats and intimidation, the court determined that these actions lacked the necessary causal connection to their political opinions to qualify for asylum. This chapter examines how the court applied the "one central reason" standard to separate genuine political persecution from a vendetta rooted in the protection of illicit revenue streams. Understanding this distinction is essential for law students, as establishing nexus often proves to be the most complex hurdle in asylum cases involving mixed motives.

## The 'One Central Reason' Statutory Standard

The foundation of the court's nexus analysis rests on the statutory language of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), which requires an applicant to demonstrate that race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. As Judge Tymkovich explains, this standard demands more than a showing that a protected ground played some role in the persecutor's motivation; it requires that the ground be significant enough that it cannot be characterized as incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for the harm. The court emphasizes that while a persecutor may act out of multiple motives simultaneously, the protected ground must hold a position of centrality in the decision to inflict harm. If the primary driver of the violence is a personal grievance or a desire to protect criminal interests, with political opinion serving merely as a backdrop or secondary factor, the statutory requirement for nexus remains unsatisfied.

## Distinguishing Protected Grounds from Incidental Motives

A pivotal aspect of the *Jimenez* opinion is its rigorous separation of protected political activity from unprotected personal conflicts. The petitioners argued that their affiliation with the Colombian Liberal Party and their support for a presidential candidate opposing the current administration constituted the basis for Don Rafa's threats. However, the court scrutinized the timeline and nature of the escalation in threats to determine whether political opinion was truly central. The record revealed that while Jimenez received initial threatening phone calls during his campaign work, these threats did not escalate into direct confrontations or specific death warrants until after he intervened in a property dispute involving Don Rafa's illegal operations. The court reasoned that if political opposition were the central motive, the severity of the persecution likely would have correlated directly with their campaign intensity regardless of their professional interactions with Don Rafa. Instead, the intensification of danger coincided precisely with Jimenez's attempt to evict a tenant who was facilitating narco-trafficking and prostitution, suggesting that the core motive was retaliatory rather than ideological.

## Mixed-Motive Analysis in Asylum Jurisprudence

The *Jimenez* case illustrates the complexities inherent in mixed-motive asylum claims, where persecutors may cite political labels while acting primarily out of self-interest. The court acknowledged that persecutors often use political rhetoric to justify their actions or intimidate victims, but reiterated that the adjudicator must look beyond surface-level accusations to identify the dominant motive. In this instance, although Jimenez was labeled a "snitch" linked to his political affiliations and internship with prosecutor's office, contextual evidence indicated these labels were tools used by criminals to silence an investigator threatening their business model. The court held that even if political animus existed alongside criminal retaliation, it remained subordinate to the financial and operational imperative of protecting Don Rafa's revenue stream from law enforcement scrutiny. Consequently, because the political opinion was not a central reason—but rather a convenient pretext or tangential element—the petitioners failed to meet their burden of proof under existing Tenth Circuit precedent regarding mixed motives.

## Fact Pattern Analysis: Campaign Activities vs. Property Dispute Interference

To arrive at its conclusion, the court engaged in a detailed factual reconstruction comparing two distinct phases of harassment faced by the petitioners: their public campaign activities and their private legal intervention. During the campaign phase, despite receiving weekly threatening calls and witnessing the murder of a colleague due to their Liberal Party activism, Jimenez and his wife continued their work without facing direct physical confrontation from organized groups tied specifically to those political views. The turning point occurred only after Jimenez utilized his provisional law license to investigate a property occupied by Don Rafa's organization. It was immediately following this investigation—where Jimenez uncovered evidence of human trafficking and drug operations—that armed men confronted him at gunpoint and issued explicit death orders attributed directly to Don Rafa. This chronological juxtaposition allowed the court to conclude that while political vulnerability existed generally in Colombia due to election tensions (as evidenced by threats against other party members), Petitioners were uniquely targeted because they posed an immediate threat to Don Rafa's criminal enterprise through civil litigation tactics disguised as legal aid rather than solely through partisan politics. Thus，the facts demonstrated causation flowing directly from interference with crime rather than pure ideology。

# State Protection and Internal Relocation Alternatives

In the landscape of asylum law, establishing that an applicant has suffered persecution is only the first hurdle. Even if an applicant demonstrates past harm or a well-founded fear of future harm, relief may still be denied if the home country's government is willing and able to provide protection, or if the applicant can safely relocate to another part of their home country. *Jimenez v. Bondi* provides a critical examination of these two distinct but related bars to asylum: the "unable or unwilling" standard regarding state protection and the reasonableness of internal relocation. For the law student, this opinion illustrates how courts scrutinize the sufficiency of an applicant's efforts to seek state aid and evaluate the factual record regarding safety within specific regions of the country of origin. The Tenth Circuit's analysis emphasizes that generalized fears of government corruption must be supported by concrete evidence of systemic failure, and that temporary success in hiding within the home country can significantly undermine claims that nationwide relocation is impossible.

## 'Unable or Unwilling' Standard: Government Response Requirements

The core inquiry into state protection asks whether the government of the applicant's home country is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. As articulated in *Jimenez*, this analysis is not abstract; it relies heavily on evidence regarding the government's actual response to the applicant's specific situation. The court identifies two primary types of information that weigh heavily in this determination: first, whether the government took steps to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered direct protection to the applicant, such as placing them in protective custody. Crucially, this analysis is intertwined with the issue of notice. The court reasoned that without clear notice of the alleged persecution, it is impossible to determine how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have intervened. Therefore, a key factual predicate for claiming a lack of state protection is whether the applicant actually reported the threats or violence to authorities. In *Jimenez*, despite Jimenez's background as a law student and intern for the National Prosecutor's office, and his father's employment within the Colombian criminal justice system, neither utilized these connections to formally report the wrongdoing or corruption they alleged. The court viewed this silence as a significant gap in their claim, noting that without such reporting, there was no empirical basis to conclude the government would have failed them.

## Futility Exceptions: When Reporting Is Dangerous or Useless

Applicants often argue that they did not report crimes because doing so would have been futile or dangerous, particularly when they believe their persecutors are allied with the government. *Jimenez* clarifies that while a failure to report is not automatically fatal to a claim, the burden remains on the applicant to demonstrate that seeking help would indeed have been pointless or perilous. The petitioners in this case argued that contacting authorities was futile because their primary antagonist, "Don Rafa," was politically aligned with then-President Gustavo Petro and operated with impunity. However, the court found this assertion unsupported by the record. The opinion highlights contradictory evidence: law enforcement had previously attempted several searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property specifically to address criminal conduct occurring there. These prior governmental actions served as powerful rebuttal evidence, suggesting that the state was neither wholly captured by nor indifferent to Don Rafa's operations. Consequently, the court concluded that the petitioners could not meet their burden of showing that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them had they chosen to utilize available legal channels. The mere belief that persecutors have high-level ties is insufficient when objective evidence shows the government actively opposing those same actors.

## Assessing Evidence of Systemic vs. Individual Police Failure

A nuanced but vital distinction drawn in *Jimenez* is between systemic government failure and isolated instances of unhelpful behavior by individual officers. To prove that a government is "unwilling" to protect its citizens, an applicant generally must show evidence of systemic breakdown rather than relying on anecdotal interactions. In this case, Jimenez testified that he spoke with two individual police officers who advised him not to seek trouble and implied that police were part of the problem. The court rejected this as proof of statewide inability or unwillingness to protect. Citing precedent from other circuits adopted in its reasoning, the opinion notes that police apathy in a specific instance does not necessarily reflect official government policy or capacity. The fact that two officers gave dismissive advice does not equate to a constitutional or institutional refusal to enforce laws against narco-traffickers. Because Jimenez did not seek help outside his immediate neighborhood or through his established professional networks within the justice system, he failed to generate a record demonstrating that the entire apparatus of the Colombian state was compromised regarding his safety. This teaches students that asylum claims based on state failure require broad evidentiary support; isolated negative experiences with low-level officials are rarely sufficient to overturn findings of fact regarding state capability.

## Internal Relocation Burden: Safety and Reasonableness Test

Even if an applicant establishes a well-founded fear of persecution in their home region, asylum may be denied if they can reasonably relocate to another part of their country where they would be safe. Under federal regulations cited in *Jimenez*, this acts as a rebuttal mechanism for the government: if it shows by a preponderance of evidence that internal relocation is feasible, eligibility for asylum may be extinguished unless conditions have fundamentally changed elsewhere (which was not argued here). The test involves assessing both safety and reasonableness—not merely whether survival is possible underground but whether life can be resumed without undue hardship tied directly to identity-based persecution factors like gender roles affecting mobility (though economic burdens alone are typically insufficient).
