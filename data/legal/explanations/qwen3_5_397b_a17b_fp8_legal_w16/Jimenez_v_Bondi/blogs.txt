Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The Threshold of Trauma: Why Threats and Psychological Harm Still Fall Short of 'Persecution' in the Tenth Circuit

In the high-stakes arena of asylum litigation, the line between a terrifying ordeal and legally cognizable "persecution" often determines whether a family remains in the United States or faces removal to danger. On October 7, 2025, the Tenth Circuit drew that line sharply in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, reaffirming a rigorous standard that continues to exclude applicants whose primary injuries are psychological rather than physical. The case involves Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, Colombian nationals who fled after a harrowing series of events involving death threats, a gunpoint confrontation, and an attempt to abduct their young son. Despite these facts, the court denied their petition for review, holding that the harms suffered did not rise to the level of past persecution under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).

*Jimenez* matters because it crystallizes the Tenth Circuit's enduring skepticism toward claims predicated on threats and emotional distress absent significant physical injury or prolonged detention. For practitioners and lower courts, the opinion serves as a stark reminder that the "cumulative effect" doctrine—often invoked to aggregate multiple incidents of harassment into a finding of persecution—has hard limits. When those incidents consist largely of intimidation tactics by non-state actors, even severe ones, the Tenth Circuit appears unwilling to stretch the statutory definition of persecution to cover them. This approach maintains a high barrier for entry, potentially leaving vulnerable individuals without recourse even when their subjective fear is profound and objectively reasonable.

## The Anatomy of Fear vs. The Definition of Persecution

The factual backdrop of *Jimenez* reads like a thriller novel, yet the legal outcome was a straightforward affirmation of denial. Jimenez, a law student and provisional attorney, became entangled with "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader, while attempting to resolve a property dispute for a client. What followed was an escalation of violence: anonymous phone calls, a threatening brochure labeling Jimenez a "snitch," a nighttime encounter where two men held him at gunpoint, an assault on his employee, and finally, an attempt by an armed man to grab Jimenez's young son outside his school. The family subsequently lived in hiding for three weeks before fleeing to the United States.

Despite conceding that the petitioners were credible, the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) found no past persecution. The Tenth Circuit agreed, anchoring its analysis in long-standing precedent that distinguishes between "restrictions or threats to life and liberty" and actual persecution. Writing for the court, Judge Tymkovich emphasized that while threats are considered in the cumulative assessment of harm, they are insufficient on their own to constitute persecution. Crucially, the petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical harm. Although Jimenez was held at gunpoint and his son was grabbed, these incidents were fleeting and resulted in no bodily injury.

The court's treatment of psychological harm was equally dismissive. The petitioners presented evidence of lasting trauma: Mrs. Duarte testified she could not sleep, and their son developed panic attacks. The court acknowledged these symptoms as "unfortunate circumstances" but categorically rejected them as a basis for asylum. Citing *Brandy v. Holder* and other precedents, the panel noted that it has "rarely, if ever" granted asylum based solely on threats and psychological harm. This creates a doctrinal hierarchy where physical pain or extended deprivation of liberty acts as a gatekeeper; without crossing this threshold, even severe emotional scarring remains legally invisible in the context of establishing past persecution.

## The Limits of Cumulative Harm and Mixed Motives

One might expect that aggregating these distinct terrors—the gunpoint threat, the child abduction attempt, the sustained campaign of intimidation—would tip the scales toward a finding of persecution under the "cumulative effect" doctrine recognized in *Ritonga v. Holder*. However, *Jimenez* illustrates how narrowly this doctrine is applied when individual incidents fail to meet the baseline severity required by circuit precedent. The court viewed each incident through a lens that demanded tangible physical consequence or confinement. Because none of the individual acts involved beating, torture, or lengthy imprisonment, their sum did not magically transform into persecution. This application suggests that in the Tenth Circuit, zero plus zero still equals zero; multiplying non-persecutory events does not necessarily create a persecutory whole if none involve physical violation or sustained loss of freedom.

Furthermore, even if the harm had cleared the severity hurdle, the petitioners faced a formidable obstacle regarding nexus—the requirement that persecution be "on account of" a protected ground like political opinion. The petitioners argued they were targeted due to their support for opposition politician Federico Gutierrez against current President Gustavo Petro's allies. The court found substantial evidence supporting the BIA's conclusion that political opinion was merely tangential. The record indicated that Don Rafa's ire was sparked not by Jimenez's campaigning—which had proceeded without major incident prior to June 2022—but by his professional interference in Don Rafa's illicit real estate operations and suspected cooperation with prosecutors regarding searches at trafficked properties.

This distinction highlights a critical vulnerability in many asylum claims involving criminal enterprises with political ties: mixed motives are common, but only protected grounds count if they are "at least one central reason." Here, economic retaliation for interfering with drug profits eclipsed political animus in the eyes of the court. By deferring to agency fact-finding on motive so readily ("It is not our prerogative to reweigh evidence"), judges effectively insulate decisions where criminal vendettas overshadow ideological ones from reversal on appeal regardless how intertwined those motivations appear on paper today!

# Untangling Mixed Motives: The 'Central Reason' Test in Cases of Political Activism vs. Criminal Retribution

In the complex landscape of asylum jurisprudence, few doctrines are as fact-intensive—and as unforgiving—as the "nexus" requirement. An applicant must prove not only that they suffered persecution, but that the harm was inflicted *on account of* a statutorily protected ground, such as political opinion. This becomes exponentially difficult when the persecutor operates with mixed motives, blending personal vendettas, criminal enterprise protection, and genuine political animus. On October 7, 2025, the Tenth Circuit issued a stark reminder of how high this bar remains in *Jimenez v. Bondi*. While the petitioners presented a harrowing narrative involving death squads, narco-traffickers, and a polarized Colombian election, the court ultimately concluded that their political activism was merely "tangential" to the true driver of the violence: the protection of a drug lord's illicit revenue stream.

The case centers on Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, Colombian nationals who fled to the United States after a terrifying escalation of threats in Bogotá. The factual backdrop reads like a thriller: Jimenez, a law student and provisional attorney, campaigned for a presidential candidate opposing the incumbent Gustavo Petro. Simultaneously, in his legal capacity, he intervened in a property dispute involving "Don Rafa," a notorious narco-trafficking guerrilla leader aligned with President Petro's party. After Jimenez discovered Don Rafa was using the disputed property for human trafficking and drug operations, the threats intensified. Men held Jimenez at gunpoint, assaulted his employees, and even grabbed his young son outside of school. Yet, despite these visceral dangers and the obvious overlap between Don Rafa's political alignment and Jimenez's opposition activity, the Tenth Circuit affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) denial of asylum.

The crux of the court's decision lies in its application of the "central reason" test. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), as amended by the REAL ID Act of 2005, a protected ground need not be the sole reason for persecution, but it must be "at least one central reason." It cannot be incidental, tangential, or subordinate to another motive. Writing for the panel, Judge Tymkovich meticulously dissected the timeline of events to separate political retribution from criminal self-interest. The court noted that while Jimenez faced low-level threats during his campaigning, those threats did not escalate into life-threatening violence until he directly challenged Don Rafa's control over the trafficking property. The men who attacked Jimenez explicitly accused him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for police raids on Don Rafa's operation.

This distinction is critical for practitioners and lower courts navigating similar mixed-motive cases. The Tenth Circuit reasoned that because the escalation of violence correlated precisely with Jimenez's interference in Don Rafa's business model—not merely his attendance at political rallies—the primary motive was economic and operational security for a criminal enterprise. The court viewed the petitioners' political affiliation as an aggravating factor that perhaps made them easier targets or provided convenient rhetoric ("death to snitches from Liberal Party"), but not as the *causal engine* of the persecution. By characterizing the political opinion as "subordinate" to the desire to silence a witness threatening a lucrative illegal operation, the court sidestepped what might otherwise look like a classic case of political suppression.

From an analytical perspective, *Jimenez* illustrates a rigorous, perhaps overly rigid, temporal causation test that asylum seekers must survive. The decision suggests that when a persecutor has a compelling non-political incentive to harm an applicant—such as protecting drug profits or silencing a whistleblower—the burden shifts dramatically to the applicant to prove that politics played an independent, central role. In this instance, the fact that Don Rafa was politically aligned with the current administration was deemed insufficient to transform a crime-control motivation into political persecution. The court effectively ruled that sharing a political enemy with your persecutor does not automatically satisfy the nexus requirement if the persecutor's immediate goal is preserving their criminal empire rather than suppressing dissent broadly construed.

The implications of this reasoning extend well beyond Colombia. In jurisdictions where state actors or quasi-state groups blur the lines between governance and organized crime, applicants will face an uphill battle distinguishing between being targeted for *who they oppose* versus *what they know*. If federal courts increasingly adopt this approach, we may see a narrowing of asylum eligibility for journalists, lawyers, and activists who expose corruption within politically connected criminal networks. These individuals are often targeted precisely because their exposure threatens both political power structures and illicit economies simultaneously. *Jimenez* signals that unless applicants can produce direct evidence linking the harm specifically to their ideological stance—distinct from their investigative or professional actions—their claims may fail at step one of nexus analysis regardless of how severe or politically charged context appears on paper?

# Corruption or Rogue Actors? Defining Government 'Unwillingness' When Local Police Advise Flight

In the complex landscape of asylum jurisprudence, few questions are as fraught as determining when a state has truly abdicated its duty to protect its citizens. Applicants fleeing nations plagued by organized crime often face a harrowing reality: the very institutions designed to safeguard them are infiltrated, indifferent, or outright hostile. Yet, under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), proving that a government is "unable or unwilling" to control persecutors requires more than a terrifying encounter with a corrupt official; it demands evidence of systemic failure. Last week, in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit provided a stark illustration of this high bar, rejecting an asylum claim where petitioners relied heavily on a single police officer's advice to flee rather than demonstrating a broader collapse of state authority.

The case centers on Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, Colombian nationals who fled after Jimenez, a law student and political activist, tangled with "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking leader with alleged ties to the current administration. The factual narrative is undeniably compelling: Jimenez faced death threats, his workplace was invaded by armed men, and his young son was grabbed outside of school. Crucially, when Jimenez sought help from a local patrol officer prior to departing Colombia, he was told that "police officers were contributing to the problem" and advised that the family should simply leave. For many observers, such an admission from a uniformed representative of the state would seem to seal the argument for government unwillingness. However, writing for the panel, Judge Tymkovich affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) denial, drawing a sharp distinction between individual apathy and institutional incapacity.

The court's reasoning rests on a rigorous application of the "substantial evidence" standard, which compels deference to agency fact-finding unless any reasonable adjudicator would be forced to conclude otherwise. While the Tenth Circuit acknowledged the severity of the threats against the Jimenez family, it found that the record contained significant countervailing evidence regarding the Colombian government's posture toward Don Rafa's criminal enterprise. Specifically, the opinion highlights that law enforcement had attempted multiple searches and seizures at the property used by the traffickers, even if those efforts were ultimately unsuccessful. In the eyes of the court, these active attempts to dismantle the operation undermined the petitioners' assertion that the state was wholly complicit or powerless. The panel reasoned that without evidence that higher authorities sanctioned Don Rafa's actions or systematically blocked investigations, the misconduct of a single officer—or even a localized unit—does not equate to state sponsorship of persecution.

This distinction between rogue actors and state policy is pivotal in *Jimenez*. The petitioners argued that because their persecutors were politically aligned with President Gustavo Petro and because one officer warned them away, seeking further protection would have been futile. The court rejected this leap in logic, noting that Jimenez, who had interned with the National Prosecutor's office and whose father worked within the justice system, never utilized those channels to report the specific threats against his family. Citing recent precedent like *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* (7th Cir.), Judge Tymkovich emphasized that "a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect." The ruling suggests that applicants bear an affirmative burden to exhaust available avenues of redress before claiming futility, unless they can demonstrate that doing so would inevitably lead to harm—a standard not met merely by citing general corruption or isolated warnings.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Jimenez* reinforces a trend in federal appellate courts toward requiring granular proof of systemic breakdown rather than accepting generalized assertions of state weakness. In nations where authority is fragmented—where central governments may oppose cartels while local precincts are compromised—this creates a difficult evidentiary tightrope for asylum seekers. The decision implies that anecdotal evidence of corruption, however chilling, must be contextualized against macro-level data showing whether the state is making good-faith efforts to combat the threat. If law enforcement raids occur elsewhere in the region or if other branches of government are actively prosecuting similar groups, courts may view local failures as aberrations rather than proof of total state collapse. This approach protects states from being labeled "unwilling" based on isolated incidents but risks overlooking realities where central directives never trickle down to street-level enforcement due to pervasive intimidation.

The downstream effects of this ruling will likely be felt most acutely in cases involving powerful transnational criminal organizations embedded within democratic frameworks. By signaling that unsuccessful police operations do not automatically prove inability—and that discouraging comments from low-level officers do not prove unwillingness—the Tenth Circuit has raised the stakes for future litigants. Attorneys representing clients from countries like Mexico, Colombia, or El Salvador will need to marshal more robust country condition reports and expert testimony linking specific threats to high-level policy decisions or widespread institutional paralysis. Mere testimony about a scary interaction with a cop will no longer suffice; applicants must paint a picture of a justice system that is structurally incapable of intervention across multiple vectors.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a sobering reminder of the gap between human suffering and legal sufficiency in asylum law. The Jimenez family undoubtedly experienced terror and felt abandoned by their local community's protectors. Yet, under current statutory interpretation and binding precedent, feeling unprotected is not synonymous with being legally unprotected by the state. Until Congress alters the INA's stringent requirements or until appellate consensus shifts on what constitutes "systemic" failure in fractured societies applicants facing hybrid threats from state-aligned criminals will continue to face an uphill battle in convincing federal courts that their government has truly turned its back on them.
