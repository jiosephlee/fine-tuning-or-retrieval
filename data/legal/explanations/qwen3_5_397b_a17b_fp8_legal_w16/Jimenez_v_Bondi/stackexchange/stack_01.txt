Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Can threats and psychological harm alone ever constitute 'past persecution' under 10th Circuit precedent?
Question:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court denied asylum because the petitioners suffered no physical harm, relying on cases like *Karki* and *Brandy* to say threats alone are insufficient. However, the petitioners did suffer significant psychological trauma (panic attacks, inability to sleep) and had their child grabbed at gunpoint. The opinion states that 'rarely, if ever' is asylum granted on threats/psychological harm alone. Is there a bright-line rule in the 10th Circuit that physical injury is effectively required for a finding of persecution, or is this a fact-specific determination where extreme psychological terror could theoretically suffice? How severe must the non-physical harm be to cross the threshold?
Answer:
No, the Tenth Circuit has not established a bright-line rule requiring physical injury for a finding of past persecution, but *Jimenez v. Bondi* makes clear that the bar for establishing persecution based solely on threats and psychological harm is exceptionally high—so high that the court notes it has "rarely, if ever" granted asylum in such cases.

While the opinion explicitly acknowledges that "physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong," it reinforces the longstanding precedent that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). To cross the threshold without physical violence, the cumulative effect of the mistreatment must entail "suffering or harm... in a way regarded as offensive" that goes beyond "mere restrictions or sporadic threats." *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004); *Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App'x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners presented a relatively strong factual predicate for psychological harm: repeated death threats, an armed confrontation at their workplace, and an incident where their young son was grabbed and threatened at gunpoint. The petitioners also documented specific psychological consequences, including panic attacks and insomnia. Despite these facts, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA's denial. The court reasoned that while the events were "physically stressful or psychologically unsettling," fears stemming from threats—even those involving firearms and children—are generally insufficient to rise to the level of persecution absent actual physical infliction of harm.

The opinion suggests that for non-physical harm to suffice, the severity must exceed the facts present in *Jimenez*. The court pointed to prior unpublished decisions where asylum was denied despite "more extreme fact patterns," including applicants who were detained for ten hours (*Brandy*), threatened with machetes and robbed multiple times (*Carias-Mejia*), or subjected to numerous direct threats (*Chhetri*). By citing these cases alongside the holding in *Jimenez*, the court indicates that purely psychological trauma and threats, even when credible and terrifying, typically fall short of the statutory definition of persecution unless accompanied by some form of realized physical violence or perhaps prolonged detention/torture not present here.

Therefore, while no absolute rule mandates physical injury, *Jimenez* confirms that in practice, the Tenth Circuit requires a showing of harm significantly more severe than credible threats and resulting anxiety to find past persecution.
