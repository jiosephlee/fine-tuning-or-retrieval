Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Can threats and psychological harm alone ever constitute 'past persecution' under 10th Circuit precedent?
Question:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court denied asylum because the petitioners suffered no physical harm, relying on cases like *Karki* and *Brandy* to say threats alone are insufficient. However, the petitioners did suffer significant psychological trauma (panic attacks, inability to sleep) and had their child grabbed at gunpoint. The opinion states that 'rarely, if ever' is asylum granted on threats/psychological harm alone. Is there a bright-line rule in the 10th Circuit that physical injury is effectively required for a finding of persecution, or is this a fact-specific determination where extreme psychological terror could theoretically suffice? How severe must the non-physical harm be to cross the threshold?
Answer:
No, the Tenth Circuit has not established a bright-line rule requiring physical injury for a finding of past persecution, but *Jimenez v. Bondi* makes clear that the bar for establishing persecution based solely on threats and psychological harm is exceptionally high—so high that the court notes it has "rarely, if ever" granted asylum in such cases.

While the opinion explicitly acknowledges that "physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong," it reinforces the longstanding precedent that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). To cross the threshold without physical violence, the cumulative effect of the mistreatment must entail "suffering or harm... in a way regarded as offensive" that goes beyond "mere restrictions or sporadic threats." *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004); *Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App'x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners presented a relatively strong factual predicate for psychological harm: repeated death threats, an armed confrontation at their workplace, and an incident where their young son was grabbed and threatened at gunpoint. The petitioners also documented specific psychological consequences, including panic attacks and insomnia. Despite these facts, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA's denial. The court reasoned that while the events were "physically stressful or psychologically unsettling," fears stemming from threats—even those involving firearms and children—are generally insufficient to rise to the level of persecution absent actual physical infliction of harm.

The opinion suggests that for non-physical harm to suffice, the severity must exceed the facts present in *Jimenez*. The court pointed to prior unpublished decisions where asylum was denied despite "more extreme fact patterns," including applicants who were detained for ten hours (*Brandy*), threatened with machetes and robbed multiple times (*Carias-Mejia*), or subjected to numerous direct threats (*Chhetri*). By citing these cases alongside the holding in *Jimenez*, the court indicates that purely psychological trauma and threats, even when credible and terrifying, typically fall short of the statutory definition of persecution unless accompanied by some form of realized physical violence or perhaps prolonged detention/torture not present here.

Therefore, while no absolute rule mandates physical injury, *Jimenez* confirms that in practice, the Tenth Circuit requires a showing of harm significantly more severe than credible threats and resulting anxiety to find past persecution.

### How does the 'central reason' test apply when a petitioner has mixed motives (political activity vs. interfering with criminal enterprise)?
Question:
The court found that while the petitioners were active in the Liberal Party (a protected ground), the primary motive for the threats was Jimenez's interference with 'Don Rafa's' narcotics/prostitution operation (a private/criminal motive). The opinion cites the statutory requirement that the protected ground must be 'at least one central reason.' How do courts distinguish between a motive being 'tangential/subordinate' versus 'central' when both exist? Specifically, if the persecutors used political slurs ('snitches from Liberal Party') but were actually angry about a property dispute, does the use of political language automatically satisfy the nexus requirement, or can the court look behind the labels to find the true economic motive?
Answer:
Courts distinguish between a "central" reason and a "tangential" or "subordinate" one by looking behind the labels used by persecutors to identify the **predominant motive** driving the harm. The mere use of political slurs does not automatically satisfy the nexus requirement if the record demonstrates that the actual catalyst for the persecution was a private or criminal dispute.

Under the "one central reason" standard cited in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, a protected ground (such as political opinion) cannot be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." When mixed motives exist, the court must determine whether the protected ground was a core driver of the persecutor's actions or merely a convenient pretext.

In *Jimenez*, the Tenth Circuit illustrated this distinction by analyzing the timeline and specific content of the threats:

1.  **Temporal Proximity:** The court noted that while the petitioners faced generic threats related to their Liberal Party activity earlier, the escalated, targeted violence (gunpoint threats, assaults on employees) occurred only **after** Jimenez interfered with Don Rafa's narcotics and prostitution operation via a property dispute. The escalation correlated with the economic threat to Don Rafa, not the petitioners' ongoing political campaigning.
2.  **Substance Over Labels:** Although the attackers used political language (calling Jimenez a "snitch from Liberal Party"), the court looked behind these labels to find the true motive. The attackers explicitly accused Jimenez of being responsible for police searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property and acting on behalf of the prosecutor's office. The court reasoned that these accusations revealed the primary motive was **retaliation for interfering with a criminal enterprise**, not punishment for political beliefs.
3.  **Independence of Motives:** The opinion highlights that prior political threats from unknown actors did not deter the petitioners, whereas the intervention in the property dispute immediately triggered severe retaliation from Don Rafa's group. This separation allowed the court to conclude that the political affiliation was "merely tangential and subordinate," serving as a rhetorical device to intimidate rather than the substantive cause of the persecution.

Therefore, courts do not accept political rhetoric at face value when the factual record points to a distinct, non-protected primary motive. If the evidence shows that the harm would have occurred regardless of the victim's political opinion—driven instead by a desire to protect illicit profits or settle a private score—the nexus requirement is not met, even if political insults are employed during the commission of the act.

### Does a single negative interaction with a local police officer satisfy the burden of proving government 'unwillingness' to protect?
Question:
The petitioners argued the government was unwilling to protect them because one officer told them to 'leave' and admitted police were 'contributing to the problem.' The court rejected this, citing *Osorio-Morales* (7th Cir.) and stating that anecdotal evidence of individual apathy doesn't prove systemic failure. What is the evidentiary standard here? If an applicant has family connections within the justice system (as Jimenez did) but still feels unsafe reporting, must they exhaust every possible avenue of reporting before claiming futility? At what point does evidence of corruption among low-level officers become sufficient to prove state complicity or inability to control rogue elements?
Answer:
No. Under Tenth Circuit precedent, a single negative interaction with a local police officer—even one expressing apathy or suggesting the police are "contributing to the problem"—is insufficient, as a matter of law, to satisfy an applicant's burden of proving that a government is "unwilling or unable" to protect them from persecution.

**The Legal Standard: Systemic Failure vs. Individual Apathy**
To establish eligibility for asylum based on private persecution, an applicant must demonstrate that the government is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. This analysis hinges on whether the government had notice of the threat and a meaningful opportunity to respond. While an applicant is not strictly required to file a formal police report if doing so would be futile or dangerous, they must provide objective evidence supporting that claim of futility.

The court in *Jimenez v. Bondi* clarifies that evidence of "individual apathy" does not equate to proof of "systemic failure." Relying on *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th 738 (7th Cir. 2023), the *Jimenez* court held that a "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim." Instead, the record must reflect broader institutional breakdowns or a pattern of official complicity.

**Application to Your Facts**
In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that the Colombian government was unwilling to protect them because one patrol officer told Jimenez to "stay away" and admitted officers were "contributing to the problem." The Tenth Circuit rejected this argument for two primary reasons grounded in the specific facts of the opinion:

1.  **Lack of Exhaustion Due to Family Connections:** The court emphasized that Jimenez possessed significant advantages within the justice system that he failed to utilize. As a provisional lawyer and former intern for the National Prosecutor's Office, and with a father currently working in the criminal justice system, Jimenez had direct avenues to report the threats beyond a casual street-side conversation with a single beat cop. The court noted that neither Jimenez nor his father reported the wrongdoing or corruption through these channels. Without attempting to use these available resources, the petitioners could not prove that seeking help would have been futile.
2.  **Contradictory Evidence of Government Action:** The record contained affirmative evidence contradicting the claim of state complicity. The opinion notes that law enforcement had already attempted "several searches and seizures" at the property owned by the persecutor ("Don Rafa") to address criminal conduct there. This demonstrated that the state was actively trying to stop the perpetrators, undermining the argument that the government was aligned with them or powerless against them.

**Conclusion on Corruption and Rogue Elements**
Regarding your question about when corruption among low-level officers becomes sufficient: *Jimenez* suggests that isolated instances of corruption or discouragement by low-level officers are inadequate unless they are shown to represent the official policy or pervasive reality of the state apparatus. To prove state complicity or an inability to control rogue elements, an applicant must show more than anecdotal encounters; they must demonstrate that they exhausted reasonable avenues for protection (especially when possessing insider access like family connections) and that those avenues systematically failed due to governmental indifference or collusion. In *Jimenez*, the combination of unused family connections within the system and active police attempts to raid the persecutor's base meant the single negative interaction was legally insufficient to carry the burden of proof.

### What factors determine whether internal relocation is 'reasonable' when persecutors have alleged ties to national leadership?
Question:
The court concluded internal relocation was reasonable because the threats were localized to a specific neighborhood and the family lived safely in the countryside for three weeks. However, the petitioners argued their persecutor ('Don Rafa') is aligned with President Petro, implying nationwide reach. The opinion dismisses this as 'haphazardly briefed' and notes family members remain safe elsewhere. When evaluating 'reasonableness' of relocation, how much weight does the court give to speculative claims about a persecutor's high-level political connections? Does the burden shift entirely to the petitioner to prove those connections create a nationwide threat, or must the government affirmatively disprove it?
Answer:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that speculative assertions regarding a persecutor's high-level political connections carry little to no weight in the internal relocation analysis when those claims are unsupported by the record and contradicted by evidence of the petitioner's own safety elsewhere in the country.

**The Burden Remains on the Petitioner**
The court reaffirms that the burden rests entirely on the petitioner to demonstrate that internal relocation is unreasonable. The government is not required to affirmatively disprove every hypothetical avenue of persecution. In this case, the petitioners argued that their persecutor, "Don Rafa," was aligned with President Petro, implying a nationwide reach that would make relocation impossible. The court rejected this argument, characterizing it as "haphazardly briefed" and lacking evidentiary support. Because the petitioners failed to provide concrete evidence linking Don Rafa's local criminal activities to state-sponsored power capable of reaching them anywhere in Colombia, the court treated the claim of nationwide reach as mere speculation.

**Reasonableness is Grounded in Fact, Not Hypothesis**
When evaluating reasonableness, the court looks to objective facts in the record rather than theoretical risks. Two key factual findings drove the court's conclusion that relocation was reasonable:
1.  **Localized Nature of Threats:** The specific threats issued by the persecutors instructed the family to leave the *neighborhood*, not the country. There was no evidence in the record suggesting the threat extended beyond that specific locale.
2.  **Proof of Safety Elsewhere:** The record demonstrated that the family successfully lived for three weeks in the Bogota countryside (with Jimenez's mother) without incident prior to departing for the United States. Furthermore, other family members, including Jimenez's father who works within the Colombian criminal justice system, continue to reside in Colombia unharmed.

**Conclusion**
Under *Jimenez*, a petitioner cannot defeat an internal relocation finding simply by alleging a persecutor has ties to national leadership. Unless the petitioner provides substantial evidence that such ties translate into an actual ability to harm them nationwide, the court will rely on concrete indicators of safety—such as successful temporary residence in another region or the safety of relatives—to conclude that relocation is reasonable. Speculative claims about political alignment do not shift the burden to the government nor override factual evidence of localized danger and existing safety zones.

### Why did failure on asylum claims automatically result in denial of Withholding of Removal despite the lower burden of proof for persecution?
Question:
The conclusion states that because petitioners failed to demonstrate a well-founded fear for asylum, they 'necessarily fail' to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. I understand withholding requires a 'clear probability' of persecution (higher than asylum's 'well-founded fear'), but isn't it possible for facts to fall short of asylum eligibility due to discretion or specific statutory bars while still meeting the higher severity threshold for withholding? Or is the court saying that because they failed on the fundamental elements (existence of persecution and nexus), no amount of increased likelihood could save the claim?
Answer:
You have correctly identified the general rule: withholding of removal carries a higher burden of proof ("clear probability" of persecution) than asylum ("well-founded fear"), and an applicant can theoretically fail asylum due to discretionary denials or specific statutory bars while still qualifying for withholding.

However, the court's statement that the petitioners "necessarily fail" withholding because they failed asylum is not based on discretion or statutory bars. Instead, it rests on the fact that the petitioners failed to establish the **fundamental substantive elements** required for *both* forms of relief.

### The Shared Substantive Elements
Both asylum and withholding of removal require an applicant to prove they are a "refugee." As the opinion outlines in Section II.B, this requires proving three core elements:
1.  An incident rising to the level of **persecution**;
2.  A **nexus** between that persecution and a statutorily protected ground (e.g., political opinion); and
3.  That the persecution is committed by the government or forces the government is unable/unwilling to control.

The distinction between the two remedies lies primarily in the *degree* of likelihood required for future harm (well-founded fear vs. clear probability) and the discretionary nature of asylum. However, if an applicant fails to prove that persecution occurred at all, or fails to link that harm to a protected ground, they cannot satisfy the requirements for either remedy, regardless of how high or low the burden of proof regarding future risk might be.

### Application to *Jimenez v. Bondi*
In this specific opinion, the Tenth Circuit denied the asylum claim because the petitioners failed on these foundational factual predicates, not because of discretion or a statutory bar:

*   **Failure to Establish Persecution:** The court held that threats, emotional distress, and psychological harm without physical injury did not rise to the level of "persecution" under existing precedent (Section II.C). Since no "persecution" was established, there is no baseline harm from which to project a "clear probability" of future persecution for withholding purposes.
*   **Failure to Establish Nexus:** Even assuming the harm amounted to persecution, the court found the motive was personal/professional (a property dispute with a drug trafficker) rather than political. The court explicitly stated the political opinion was "tangential and subordinate" (Section II.D). Without a nexus to a protected ground, the definition of a refugee is not met for either claim.
*   **Government Protection/Relocation:** The court further found substantial evidence that the government was not unwilling/unable to protect them and that internal relocation was feasible (Sections II.E and II.F). These findings defeat the ability to show a fear of future persecution in any part of the country.

### Conclusion
The court's logic is that you cannot meet a *higher* standard of proof (withholding) if you have failed to prove the *underlying facts* necessary to meet even the *lower* standard (asylum).

As cited in the conclusion, relying on *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, if an applicant fails to demonstrate a "well-founded fear" because they haven't proven persecution exists or is linked to a protected trait, they mathematically and logically cannot demonstrate a "clear probability" of that same non-existent or non-qualifying harm occurring in the future. The failure was elemental, not procedural or discretionary.

### How does waiver occur in immigration appeals when an argument is mentioned in facts but not explicitly briefed as a legal error?
Question:
In Section D (Nexus), the court notes that petitioners did not brief arguments regarding their membership in a 'particular social group,' so the court limited review solely to 'political opinion.' Later, regarding relocation, arguments about economic burdens were dismissed as 'haphazardly briefed.' To what extent must an appellant explicitly frame factual assertions as distinct legal errors in their opening brief to preserve them for appellate review? If a fact pattern suggests a particular social group claim, does failure to cite relevant case law defining that group result in total waiver, even if pro se or under-resourced counsel might miss technical pleading requirements?
Answer:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit illustrates a fundamental principle of federal appellate practice: **issues mentioned in the statement of facts but not developed as distinct legal arguments in the briefing section are deemed waived.**

The court's treatment of the "particular social group" (PSG) claim and the "economic burdens" of relocation provides a clear roadmap for how strictly this rule is applied, even when the underlying facts might suggest a viable theory.

### The Doctrine of Waiver via Inadequate Briefing
Federal courts of appeals do not act as fact-finders or legal researchers for litigants. Their role is to review specific legal errors identified and argued by the appellant. Under Tenth Circuit precedent (and general federal practice), an issue is not preserved simply because the raw material for the argument exists in the record or the factual background. The appellant must explicitly frame those facts as a violation of law, cite relevant authority, and explain why the agency's decision was erroneous on that specific ground.

If an appellant fails to do this, the court treats the argument as abandoned. This prevents "sandbagging," where a party hints at an issue in the facts hoping the court will pick it up if their primary arguments fail.

### Application in *Jimenez*: The PSG Claim
Section D (Nexus) of the opinion offers a textbook example of this waiver. The petitioners' factual narrative clearly involved interactions with narco-traffickers and potential membership in a group defined by family or profession. However, the court noted:

> "Petitioners, however, did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group."

Because the opening brief focused exclusively on "political opinion," the court expressly **limited its review** to that single ground. It did not matter that the facts *could* support a PSG claim; because counsel failed to articulate the legal definition of the proposed group and apply it to those facts in their argument section, the court refused to consider it. The result was total forfeiture of that potential avenue for relief.

### Application in *Jimenez*: Relocation Burdens
The court applied a similar logic to the internal relocation analysis in Section F. The petitioners raised concerns about economic burdens and judicial infrastructure, but only in passing. The court characterized these points as **"haphazardly briefed."**

By using this terminology, the court signaled that merely listing hardships in a sentence or two is insufficient to meet the burden of persuasion on appeal. To survive waiver, an appellant must develop these points with specificity—explaining *why* the economic burden rises to the level of unreasonableness under existing case law and providing evidence from the record to support that conclusion. Because the petitioners failed to flesh out these arguments, the court dismissed them without substantive analysis.

### Does Pro Se Status or Resource Constraints Matter?
While courts sometimes construe pro se filings liberally, *Jimenez* demonstrates that this leniency has hard limits regarding issue preservation. The opinion does not carve out an exception for under-resourced counsel or complex fact patterns where a legal theory seems obvious. The burden remains on the petitioner to translate a "fact pattern" into a "legal argument."

As Judge Tymkovich's opinion reinforces, if you want the appellate court to consider a specific statutory ground (like PSG) or a specific factor (like economic hardship), you must:
1.  **Identify it explicitly** as a distinct error in your statement of issues;
2.  **Define the legal standard** governing that issue; and
3.  **Apply the record facts** to that standard with citation to controlling authority.

Failure to perform these steps results in waiver, regardless of how sympathetic the underlying facts may be or how strongly they hint at an alternative legal theory.
