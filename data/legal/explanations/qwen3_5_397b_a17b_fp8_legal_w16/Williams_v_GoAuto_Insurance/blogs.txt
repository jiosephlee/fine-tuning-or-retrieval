# Algorithmic Adjudication: How *Williams v. GoAuto* Validates Automated Contract Termination Under Louisiana's 'Strict Adherence' Standard

In the intricate world of insurance regulation, few doctrines are as unforgiving as Louisiana's requirement of "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures. For decades, Louisiana courts have treated deviations from these protocols as fatal errors, rendering cancellations void and leaving insurers exposed to liability for accidents occurring after a supposed termination. The logic is sound: when a citizen's financial safety net is at stake, the state demands precision. But what happens when the entity ensuring that precision is not a meticulous paralegal checking boxes, but an algorithm executing code milliseconds after an email hits a server? In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided October 3, 2025, the Fifth Circuit confronted this collision between archaic statutory rigidity and modern automation. By affirming the dismissal of claims against GoAuto, the court effectively ruled that in the digital age, "receipt" and "certification" can occur without human intervention or a wet-ink signature, signaling a significant shift in how administrative law interacts with automated contractual enforcement.

The factual matrix of *Williams* reads like a standard premium finance dispute until one examines the mechanics of the cancellation. The plaintiffs, former insureds whose policies were terminated for nonpayment via American Premium Assistance Company (APAC), argued that GoAuto failed to strictly adhere to Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550. Their contention rested on two technical deficiencies inherent in GoAuto's automated workflow. First, the system cancelled policies immediately upon receiving an electronic notice from APAC, yet the formal certification document was not stamped with a signature until later that morning by an APAC employee. Second, because the initial cancellation was triggered digitally between servers, no human at GoAuto physically possessed the request before the policy was dead. Under traditional interpretations of "strict adherence," these gaps—a pre-signature cancellation and a lack of immediate physical receipt—would seemingly invalidate the termination. The district court disagreed, and the Fifth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Southwick, affirmed.

The core of the Fifth Circuit's reasoning lies in its refusal to import extra-statutory requirements into the text of the law. The plaintiffs leaned heavily on the notion that "certifying" implies a human act of verification, typically evidenced by a signature preceding the operative event. The court dismantled this argument by pointing to the silence of the statute itself; nowhere does § 9:3550 explicitly mandate a signature. While acknowledging a lone Louisiana appellate decision (*Benitez v. Elsayed*) that criticized unsigned notices, Judge Southwick correctly noted that *Benitez* did not establish a per se rule that a missing signature invalidates a cancellation if all other substantive elements are met. The court reasoned that "strict adherence" is a shield against procedural sloppiness, not a sword for courts to legislate new hurdles. If the statute requires the *company* to certify, and the company's authorized system transmits data confirming compliance with statutory prerequisites, the functional requirement is met regardless of whether a specific employee's name appears on the digital packet at T-minus zero.

Even more consequential is the court's treatment of "receipt." The plaintiffs argued that because GoAuto's computer system cancelled the policy before an employee physically handled or stamped the document, GoAuto never technically "received" the request prior to acting. This argument attempts to anchor a digital transaction in physical metaphors—possession, custody, and manual handling. The Fifth Circuit rejected this temporal disconnect between digital ingestion and human review. Embracing a definition of receipt rooted in Black's Law Dictionary but applied through a modern lens, the court held that receipt occurs when the insurer takes possession or custody via its web services. The opinion offers a lucid description of API interactions: GoAuto's server acts as a "listener," waiting for APAC's call. Once that call comes and the data is ingested, possession has transferred. To require human review before cancellation would render automated systems legally impossible to operate under Louisiana law, creating a dissonance between legislative intent and technological reality that the court wisely refused to enforce.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Williams* represents a pragmatic evolution of the "strict adherence" standard rather than its erosion Critics might argue that automating life-altering decisions like insurance cancellation removes necessary human oversight, increasing the risk of error where algorithms lack nuance. However, the Fifth Circuit's analysis suggests that reliability in code can satisfy due process concerns just as effectively as reliability in clerks, provided the underlying statutory safeguards (such as prior notice to the insured) are intact. By decoupling "receipt" from physical possession and "certification" from pre-action signatures, the court prevents procedural formalism from becoming an absurdity trap. If every automated transaction required retroactive human validation to be legally effective at the moment of execution, entire sectors of fintech and insurtech would face paralyzing uncertainty regarding when contracts actually terminate.

The downstream effects of this decision extend well beyond Louisiana auto insurance premiums. As more industries migrate toward fully automated contract management—where smart contracts or simple APIs execute terminations based on predefined triggers—courts will increasingly face challenges similar to those in *Williams*. The Fifth Circuit's approach provides a blueprint for other jurisdictions grappling with similar statutes: look to function over form. # The Semantics of Certification: Why the Fifth Circuit Refused to Read a Signature Requirement Into La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550

In the highly regulated world of insurance premium financing, the difference between a valid cancellation and a wrongful termination of coverage often hinges on procedural minutiae. On October 3, 2025, the Fifth Circuit confronted this precise tension in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, affirming a district court's dismissal of claims brought by former insureds who argued their policies were improperly cancelled due to nonpayment. While Louisiana law mandates "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures, Judge Leslie Southwick's opinion for the panel draws a sharp line between strict compliance and judicial overreach. By refusing to imply a signature requirement where the statute demands only a "statement certifying" compliance, the Fifth Circuit has signaled a pragmatic, textualist approach to modern corporate automation that could reshape how regulated industries manage digital workflows.

The dispute arose from a common arrangement in the auto insurance market: plaintiffs financed their premiums through American Premium Assistance Company (APAC), which held a power of attorney to cancel policies upon default. When the plaintiffs missed payments, APAC initiated cancellations via an automated digital handshake with GoAuto Insurance. An email containing the requisite statutory certifications triggered GoAuto's system to cancel the policy immediately; physical documents bearing a signature stamp were generated and filed only later in the day. The plaintiffs sued, arguing that under Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550, the absence of a pre-cancellation signature rendered the notices defective. They leaned heavily on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a 2019 Louisiana appellate decision that criticized an unsigned notice for failing to identify a specific employee or purport to certify statements, suggesting such defects invalidate cancellations.

The Fifth Circuit's rejection of this argument rests on a disciplined reading of the statutory text. Judge Southwick noted that while Louisiana courts indeed require strict adherence to § 9:3550, that mandate does not invite courts to insert requirements the legislature omitted. The statute explicitly calls for a "statement certifying" four specific facts regarding the default and notice history but remains silent on the mechanics of authentication, such as signatures or named employees. The panel distinguished *Benitez* by observing that while the notice in that case failed to "purport to certify" anything, GoAuto's emails expressly stated that APAC "certifies" the required elements. In the court's view, the act of certification is substantive—it is the assertion of truth regarding the default status—not merely formalistic. By demanding a signature, the plaintiffs were effectively asking the court to legislate a new procedural hurdle, a step the Fifth Circuit declined to take absent clear direction from Louisiana jurisprudence.

Beyond the signature issue, the opinion offers a fascinating glimpse into how federal courts interpret traditional legal concepts like "receipt" in an era of algorithmic governance. The plaintiffs contended that GoAuto never truly "received" the cancellation request because the initial transmission was purely electronic and processed by computers before any human at GoAuto touched a document. Relying on standard dictionary definitions, the court concluded that "receipt" occurs when an entity takes possession or custody of information, regardless of whether that possession is effected by a server or a mailroom clerk. The description of GoAuto's web service as a "listener" waiting to be called underscores a shift in legal reality: when a corporation designs its systems to accept and act upon data inputs automatically, those inputs constitute legal receipt the moment they enter the system's domain. This reasoning validates high-volume, low-latency business models against challenges rooted in analog-era expectations of physical handling.

The doctrinal implications of *Williams* extend well beyond Louisiana insurance law. For regulated industries increasingly reliant on standardized digital certifications, the decision provides a shield against liability based on technical formalities not grounded in statutory text. Had the Fifth Circuit adopted the plaintiffs' view, companies utilizing automated compliance systems would face existential uncertainty, forced to retrofit human signing steps into millisecond-speed transactions to avoid litigation risks. Instead, the court prioritized functional compliance over ritualistic formality. However, this victory for efficiency comes with a caveat: it places significant weight on the clarity of the automated message itself. Because GoAuto's system explicitly used the verb "certifies," it survived scrutiny; had the template been ambiguous or merely declaratory without invoking certification language, the outcome might have differed under the logic of *Benitez*.

Ultimately, *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* serves as a reminder that "strict adherence" is not synonymous with "hyper-technical obstructionism." The Fifth Circuit correctly identified that while statutes protecting consumers from abrupt loss of coverage must be respected, they should not be weaponized to invalidate cancellations where every substantive safeguard has been met through modern means. By declining to certify questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court—a move reflecting judicial restraint and confidence in textual analysis—the panel reinforced that silence in a statute regarding signatures cannot be construed as a prohibition against unsigned digital processes. As businesses continue to digitize regulatory interactions, *Williams* stands as an authoritative precedent that the law adapts to technology so long as core statutory purposes are fulfilled with precision and clarity.



# Defining Digital 'Receipt': Possession, Custody, and the Obsolescence of Physical Delivery in Insurance Law

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided October 3, 2025, the Fifth Circuit confronted a question that sits at the uncomfortable intersection of rigid state statutory mandates and the seamless reality of modern automated commerce. The case required the court to determine whether an insurance company's fully automated cancellation of a policy—triggered by an email from a premium finance company before any human ever saw or signed the request—satisfied Louisiana's strict statutory requirements for policy termination. In affirming the district court, Judge Southwick's opinion does more than resolve a dispute over unpaid premiums; it quietly revolutionizes the concept of "receipt" in commercial law. By anchoring its analysis in the functional reality of Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) rather than the ceremonial rituals of physical delivery, the Fifth Circuit signals that strict adherence to statutory text need not imply adherence to antiquated methods of performance.

The stakes in *Williams* were high for Louisiana's insurance market. Under Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550, a premium finance company may cancel a policy only upon sending specific notices and certifications to the insurer. Louisiana courts have long demanded "strict adherence" to these procedures, holding that any defect renders a cancellation ineffective. The plaintiffs, former insureds whose policies were cancelled after missed payments, argued that GoAuto's procedure was fatally flawed on two fronts: first, that the certification lacked a human signature at the moment of transmission; and second, that GoAuto never technically "received" the request because its computer system acted autonomously before a human employee took physical custody of the printed documents. Had the court sided with the plaintiffs, it would have cast doubt on thousands of automated cancellations processed daily across industries that rely on machine-to-machine communication, potentially resurrecting lapsed policies based on procedural formalities irrelevant to actual notice.

The court's rejection of the signature requirement offers a pragmatic reading of statutory silence. The plaintiffs leaned heavily on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a Louisiana appellate decision suggesting that a lack of signature might undermine a certification. However, Judge Southwick correctly distinguished *Benitez*, noting that while the notice there failed to certify compliance entirely, GoAuto's emails explicitly contained the requisite statutory language. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that because the statute commands "the insurance premium finance company" to certify—not a specific natural person—a corporate entity can fulfill this duty through authorized automated systems. To require a wet-ink signature or even a digital signature from a named employee before an automated system could act would be to add a requirement the legislature did not enact. This interpretation respects the doctrine of strict adherence without transforming it into a trap for unwary businesses utilizing standard efficiency tools.

The most profound aspect of the opinion, however, lies in its treatment of "receipt." The plaintiffs argued that receipt requires physical possession by a human agent, pointing out that GoAuto's servers cancelled the policies immediately upon receiving an API call from APAC's system, hours before an APAC employee stamped and filed the hard copies in a cabinet shared with GoAuto. The district court had creatively dubbed that employee a "private carrier" to bridge the gap, but the Fifth Circuit wisely sidestepped this awkward fiction. Instead, relying on Black's Law Dictionary definitions of "receive" as taking possession or coming into custody from an outside source, the court held that GoAuto took possession the moment its web service accepted the data packet.

This reasoning is analytically sound and doctrinally significant. In describing the interaction between APAC and GoAuto as a "listener" waiting to be called by another system, Judge Southwick demystified the black box of automation for legal purposes. The court recognized that in 2025, a corporation acts through its code as surely as it acts through its clerks. If a server is configured to accept instructions from a partner entity and execute them immediately, the corporation has constructively—and actually—received those instructions. To hold otherwise would create absurd results where critical business transactions remain in limbo simply because no human has yet glanced at a screen or touched paper. This approach aligns federal diversity jurisprudence with technological reality without needing new legislation; it interprets existing concepts of possession and custody as agnostic to medium unless specified otherwise by statute:
> "GoAuto's method of receiving notice... satisfies [the] statute... [C]ompany computers do much that individuals once did."

By declining to certify these questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court—a move justified by precedent but notable given novel facts—the Fifth Circuit effectively established binding guidance for future cases involving paperless transactions within its jurisdiction (and persuasive authority elsewhere). # The High Bar for Certification: Why Unsettled State Law Isn't Enough to Invoke Federal Question Certification

In the complex ecosystem of automobile insurance litigation, few mechanisms are as critical—or as contentious—as the cancellation of a policy for nonpayment. When a premium finance company pulls the plug, the consequences for the insured can be catastrophic, leaving them exposed to liability without coverage. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided October 3, 2025, the Fifth Circuit faced a challenge to GoAuto's automated cancellation procedures under Louisiana's strict statutory regime. While the court's substantive holding affirms that modern digital workflows satisfy traditional statutory notice requirements, the decision's most enduring legacy may lie in its procedural posture. The plaintiffs' attempt to kick the unresolved questions of Louisiana statutory interpretation up to the Louisiana Supreme Court via certification was swiftly rebuffed. The opinion serves as a stark reminder that in the Fifth Circuit, the mere existence of unsettled state law is insufficient to trigger certification; the bar for invoking this extraordinary resource remains exceptionally high.

## The Automation of Cancellation and Statutory Strictness

To understand why certification was sought, one must first appreciate the tension between Louisiana's rigid insurance statutes and GoAuto's streamlined operations. Louisiana law demands "strict adherence" to specific procedures when a finance company exercises a power of attorney to cancel a policy due to nonpayment. The statute requires the finance company to send a notice certifying several facts, including the existence of a valid power of attorney and the status of the default. Historically, courts have treated any defect in this process as fatal to the cancellation, rendering the policy still in force—a outcome that can expose insurers to massive bad-faith liabilities.

The plaintiffs in *Williams* argued that GoAuto's fully automated system violated this strict adherence requirement. Specifically, they contended that because GoAuto's computer system canceled policies immediately upon receiving an electronic notice from APAC (the finance company), and because human signatures were only affixed to physical copies later in the day, the statutory requirement of "certification" and "receipt" was not met at the moment of cancellation. They posited that a certificate implies a human signer acting prior to the act, and that "receipt" implies physical possession by a human agent, not just data ingestion by a server. These were not frivolous arguments; they touched on how mid-20th-century statutory language applies to 21st-century algorithmic governance. The district court rejected these claims, and on appeal, the Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding that the statute does not explicitly require a pre-cancellation signature and that electronic transmission constitutes valid "receipt" under modern definitions.

## The Certification Gambit and the *Jefferson* Standard

Recognizing that no Louisiana appellate court had definitively ruled on whether a signature is prerequisite to certification or precisely what constitutes "receipt" in an automated environment, the plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify these questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. This is a common strategic move in diversity cases involving novel state law: if the federal circuit guesses wrong on state law, it risks error correction later; if it certifies, it gets the answer straight from the source. However, Judge Southwick's opinion makes short work of this request, leaning heavily on the circuit's precedent in *Jefferson v. Lead Industries Association*.

The court reiterated the principle that certification is an "important resource" that must not be depleted by overuse. Citing *Jefferson*, Judge Southwick emphasized that federal courts should be "chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason." Crucially, he noted that the mere fact that a term has not yet been defined by a state's highest court is not enough to warrant certification. The Fifth Circuit viewed itself as capable of resolving these interpretive issues using standard tools of statutory construction—looking at plain text, dictionary definitions, and analogous case law—without needing to burden the state supreme court docket. By defining "receipt" through Black's Law Dictionary and reasoning that statutes must adapt to technological realities ("company computers do much that individuals once did"), the panel concluded it could confidently predict how Louisiana courts would rule without formal guidance.

## Doctrinal Implications: Predictability Over Perfection

The refusal to certify in *Williams* underscores a pragmatic, perhaps even utilitarian, approach to federal-state judicial relations within the Fifth Circuit. The underlying philosophy appears to be that federal appellate judges are competent interpreters of state statutes and should only defer when state law is genuinely impenetrable or when conflicting lower-court precedents create true chaos. Here, while there was sparse authority on signatures and automation, there was no direct conflict among Louisiana intermediate courts that demanded resolution. The panel seemingly determined that answering these questions was straightforward enough that certification would delay justice without adding significant clarity.

This approach reinforces a high threshold for parties seeking certification. It signals that litigants cannot rely on novelty alone to pause proceedings for state-court input. Instead, they must demonstrate that the uncertainty is so profound that any federal prediction would be little more than a guess likely to destabilize important areas of state regulation. In *Williams*, despite billions of dollars riding on insurance cancellations across Louisiana, the Fifth Circuit deemed its own analysis sufficient. This suggests that unless there is active discord in state intermediate appellate courts or a question touching on fundamental constitutional rights unique to state jurisprudence, federal circuits will press forward with their best predictions rather than hitting pause for certification.
