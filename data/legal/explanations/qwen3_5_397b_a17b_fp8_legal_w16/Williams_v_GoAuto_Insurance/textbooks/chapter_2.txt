Chapter 2: # Governing Legal Framework: Louisiana's Strict Adherence Standard

To understand the dispute in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, one must first grasp the unique regulatory environment governing insurance cancellations in Louisiana. Unlike many jurisdictions that apply a standard of substantial compliance to administrative notices, Louisiana imposes a rigorous "strict adherence" requirement when a premium finance company seeks to cancel a policy due to nonpayment. This chapter outlines the specific statutory mechanism established by Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550 and explains the judicial philosophy that demands exact conformity with its terms. The central tension in *Williams* arises not from a disagreement over whether the statute applies, but rather from how strictly its textual requirements—specifically regarding certification and receipt—must be interpreted in a modern, automated business context.

## Statutory Text: Requirements for Notice and Certification under § 9:3550(G)(3)

The operative provision in this case is Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3), which details the precise steps a premium finance company must take to terminate an insurance policy funded through an installment plan. The statute empowers a finance company, such as American Premium Assistance Company (APAC), to request cancellation upon an uncured default. However, this power is conditional. The finance company must send a notice of cancellation to the insurer (GoAuto) accompanied by a statement "certifying" four distinct factual predicates. First, the statement must affirm that the underlying finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney authorizing cancellation. Second, it must certify that the agreement is currently in default and that this default has not been timely cured. Third, it must confirm that a notice of cancellation was previously sent to the insured, specifying the date of that mailing. Finally, it must verify that copies of the notice were dispatched to all other interested parties, such as lienholders or government agencies, listing their names and addresses.

Crucially, the statute dictates the method of transmission for these documents. The finance company may deliver the notice and certification by depositing them in the mail, using a private carrier, or transmitting them via electronic mail. Once the insurer receives this package containing both the notice and the required certifications, the statute mandates that the insurer "shall consider that cancellation... has been requested" and may proceed to cancel the contract. In *Williams*, the plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's automated system failed to meet these textual requirements because the certification lacked a human signature at the moment of transmission and because the physical "receipt" of documents occurred only after the computer systems had already effected cancellation. Understanding the specific language of subsection (G)(3) is therefore essential, as every element of GoAuto's defense rested on demonstrating that their digital workflow satisfied each enumerated condition.

## The Doctrine of Strict Adherence: Precedent from Delatte, Eaglin, and Britten

While statutory text provides the roadmap, Louisiana case law establishes the severity of penalties for straying from it. The Fifth Circuit in *Williams* relied on a trio of state appellate decisions to define the governing standard: "strict adherence." This doctrine was articulated clearly in *Delatte v. Lemotte*, where the court held that finance companies must follow statutory cancellation procedures exactly as written. The rationale behind this rigidity was further cemented in *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, which declared that "[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy." Under this precedent, there is no room for harmless error or substantial compliance; if a single step in the statutory sequence is flawed, the policy remains legally active despite nonpayment by the insured.

The origins and purpose of this strict standard were explored in *Britten v. Reavis*, another key citation in the *Williams* opinion. These precedents collectively create a high barrier for insurers and finance companies attempting to void coverage. In practical terms for litigants like Williams and Jenkins, this doctrine means that proving even a minor procedural irregularity—such as a missing date or an improperly formatted certificate—could theoretically reinstate their coverage and validate their claims for bad faith denial of benefits. However, as Judge Southwick noted in *Williams*, while "strict adherence" forbids courts from ignoring statutory steps, it also prohibits courts from inventing new ones. The doctrine requires fidelity to what the legislature wrote, not an expansion of requirements based on judicial speculation about what might be safer or more formalistic.

## Dual Functions of Strict Compliance: Fixing Cancellation Dates and Cure Periods

The Louisiana courts have justified this uncompromising approach by identifying two critical public policy functions served by strict compliance with § 9:3550. As summarized in *Britten* and reiterated in *Williams*, these functions are: (1) providing a clear method for fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) granting a minimum timeframe during which the default may be cured by the insured. Precision regarding timing is vital because insurance coverage determines liability exposure; ambiguity about when a policy ends could leave injured third parties without recourse or insurers liable for risks they believed they had terminated. Similarly, ensuring a guaranteed cure period protects consumers from losing essential coverage due to transient financial difficulties or clerical errors, provided they act within the statutory window.
