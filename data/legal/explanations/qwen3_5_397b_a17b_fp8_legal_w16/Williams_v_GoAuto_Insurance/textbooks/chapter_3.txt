Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 3: # Issue I: The Requirement of Signature on Certification Notices

The central dispute in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* regarding the validity of the policy cancellations hinges on the interpretation of a single verb within the Louisiana premium finance statute: "certifying." The plaintiffs, former insureds whose policies were terminated for nonpayment, argued that GoAuto Insurance Company and its financing partner, American Premium Assistance Company (APAC), failed to strictly adhere to Louisiana law because their cancellation notices lacked a human signature at the moment of cancellation. This chapter examines the Fifth Circuit's analysis of whether the statutory mandate for a finance company to "certify" specific facts implicitly requires an individual employee's signature prior to the effective date of cancellation. By dissecting the automated nature of GoAuto's procedures, the silence of the statutory text, and the principles of judicial restraint, we explore how courts navigate the intersection of rigid statutory compliance and modern corporate automation.

## Plaintiffs' Argument: 'Certifying' Implies Individual Identification and Signature

The plaintiffs' challenge to the cancellation procedure was rooted in the timeline of events surrounding the generation of the cancellation notice. Under GoAuto's operational protocol, when an insured failed to cure a default by the deadline, APAC's computer system automatically transmitted an email to GoAuto containing the notice of cancellation and the required statutory certifications. This electronic transmission triggered GoAuto's own system to immediately cancel the policy. Crucially, the physical document bearing a signature stamp from an APAC employee was not generated until later that morning, after the policy had already been terminated electronically. The plaintiffs contended that this sequence violated Louisiana's requirement for "strict adherence" to cancellation statutes. Their argument posited that the act of "certifying" is inherently personal; therefore, a valid certification must include the identification of a specific employee and their signature affixed before the cancellation takes effect. In their view, a computer-generated email lacking an immediate human signature could not satisfy the statutory duty to certify that the premium finance agreement was in default and that proper notice had been sent to the insured.

## Analysis of Statutory Silence and Lack of Explicit Signature Mandate

In addressing this argument, the Fifth Circuit began with the text of Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The court observed that while the statute meticulously lists four specific statements that must be certified—such as the existence of a valid power of attorney and the fact that default notices were sent—it makes no explicit reference to a signature. The legislative command is that the premium finance company send a statement "certifying" these facts. The court reasoned that if the Louisiana Legislature intended to impose a signature requirement, it could have easily included such language, as it has in other regulatory contexts. The absence of such a mandate suggested that "certifying" does not necessarily equate to "signing by an individual." The court emphasized that in an era where business transactions are increasingly digital and automated, imposing a requirement for a wet-ink signature or even a pre-timed digital signature would be adding a layer of formality not present in the law itself. The focus of the statute is on the content of the certification and its transmission, not on the manual authentication by a natural person at the precise second of transmission.

## Review of Conflicting or Ambiguous Louisiana Case Law (Benitez vs. Others)

To support their position, the plaintiffs pointed to *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a Louisiana state court decision where an appellate court noted deficiencies in a cancellation notice that was neither signed nor did it identify a specific employee who prepared it. However, upon closer examination, the Fifth Circuit distinguished *Benitez* from the instant case. In *Benitez*,the notice failed to "purport to 'certify'" any of the required statements at all; it was substantively deficient beyond just lacking a signature. By contrast, every notice sent by APAC in *Williams* explicitly stated that APAC "certifies" each ofthe four statutory elements.
