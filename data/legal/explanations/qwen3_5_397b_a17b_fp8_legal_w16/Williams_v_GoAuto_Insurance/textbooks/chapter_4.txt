Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 4: # Issue II: Constructive Receipt via Automated Web Services

The second pivotal issue in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* concerns the precise moment an insurer is deemed to have "received" a cancellation request under Louisiana law. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's automated system, which processed and acted upon cancellation emails from the premium finance company (APAC) before any human employee physically handled or stamped the documents, failed to satisfy the statutory requirement of receipt. This chapter explores how the Fifth Circuit reconciled traditional legal definitions of possession with the realities of modern corporate automation, ultimately concluding that electronic delivery to a functioning server constitutes valid receipt.

## The Statutory Trigger: Cancellation 'Upon Receipt' of Notice

To understand the dispute, one must first isolate the specific statutory mechanism at play. Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) dictates that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer... may proceed to cancel such contract." The statute creates a conditional trigger: the insurer's authority to cancel arises only after it has obtained "receipt" of the requisite documentation from the finance company. The plaintiffs contended that because GoAuto's computer system executed the cancellation immediately upon detecting an email—hours before an APAC employee applied a signature stamp and physically delivered a hard copy to a GoAuto file cabinet—the statutory condition precedent had not been met at the time of cancellation. Their argument hinged on the idea that "receipt" implies a level of human awareness or physical custody that an automated software process cannot replicate.

## Defining 'Receipt': Physical Possession vs. Electronic Delivery

The court began its analysis by acknowledging that neither the Louisiana statute nor the state's highest court had explicitly defined "receipt" in this specific context. In the absence of controlling state precedent, the Fifth Circuit turned to standard legal lexicography, citing Black's Law Dictionary. The dictionary defines "receipt" as "the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession," and "receive" as "to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." While these definitions emphasize possession, they do not inherently restrict possession to human hands or paper formats. The court reasoned that if a corporation takes custody of a communication through its designated agents—which include its computer systems—it has effectively "received" that communication. The critical factor is not whether a human eyes the document immediately, but whether the entity has successfully acquired control over the information sent by the outside source.

## The Mechanics of GoAuto's Web Service and Automated Cancellation Workflow

Applying this definition to the facts required a close examination of GoAuto's technical infrastructure. The record revealed a sophisticated interaction between APAC and GoAuto involving a "web service." As described by a GoAuto deponent, this web service acts as a digital listener, constantly waiting for calls from authorized external systems. When APAC's system detected a defaulted policy, it automatically transmitted an email containing the certification and cancellation request to GoAuto's web service. Upon receiving this data packet in the early morning hours, GoAuto's system validated the input and immediately updated its database to reflect the policy's cancellation. Only later in the day did an APAC employee print the documents, apply a signature stamp, and walk them to a shared filing cabinet where a GoAuto employee stamped them as "received." The court found that the statutory requirement was satisfied at the moment the web service accepted and processed APAC's electronic transmission. By programming its system to listen for and accept these specific instructions, GoAuto had constructively taken possession of the notice the instant it arrived digitally.

## Rejection of the 'Private Carrier' Theory in Favor of Direct Electronic Receipt

In reaching its conclusion, the appellate court distinguished its reasoning from that of the district court below. The district court had attempted to fit the facts into a different statutory pathway, suggesting that the APAC employee who walked the printed papers to the file cabinet acted as a "private carrier" delivering mail under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The Fifth Circuit noted that this theory was "imaginative and unconventional," particularly given the lack of Louisiana case law defining an internal employee as a private carrier in this context. More importantly, however, the appellate court determined that relying on such analogies was unnecessary. Because email is expressly listed as a valid method of sending notice under subsection (G)(3)(a), and because GoAuto's servers undeniably took possession of that email, there was no need to stretch definitions regarding physical carriers or human messengers. The direct electronic receipt rendered theories about walking papers across an office irrelevant to legal compliance.

## Modernizing Statutory Interpretation for Corporate Computer Systems

The ultimate holding on this issue reflects a pragmatic approach to statutory interpretation in a digital age. The court explicitly recognized that La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550 is a "modern statute set in current times," where corporate operations rely heavily on automation rather than manual processing alone. To require human intervention before a computer system could act on received data would impose an artificial constraint not found in the text of the law itself ("company computers do much that individuals once did").
