Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How does the Fifth Circuit define 'receipt' under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) in the context of automated server-to-server communication?
Question:
The opinion discusses whether GoAuto 'received' the cancellation request when its computer system automatically processed an email from APAC before any human employee saw it or stamped it. The court relied on Black's Law Dictionary definitions of 'receipt' involving taking possession. Does this ruling establish that digital 'possession' by a server counts as legal receipt even if no human agent has reviewed or acknowledged the document yet? How does this interact with the principle that an agent (the APAC employee stamping later) seemingly acted after the principal (GoAuto) had already effected the cancellation?
Answer:
Yes, under the Fifth Circuit's reasoning in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, digital "possession" by a server constitutes legal **receipt** under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), even if no human agent has yet reviewed or acknowledged the document.

The court anchored its analysis in the plain meaning of "receipt," relying on *Black's Law Dictionary* (12th ed. 2024) to define the term as "the act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession." The court reasoned that because the statute does not explicitly define "receipt" nor mandate human intervention, the definition extends to modern corporate realities where "company computers do much that individuals once did."

In this specific factual context, the court found that GoAuto effectuated receipt—and thus satisfied the statutory prerequisite for cancellation—the moment its computer system (described as a "listener" web service) accepted the automated email from APAC. The opinion explicitly rejects the argument that receipt requires a human employee to physically handle, stamp, or review the notice *before* the cancellation occurs. The court noted that while an APAC employee later affixed a signature stamp and a GoAuto employee eventually stamped the physical copy as "received," these actions occurred **after** the computer system had already processed the request and cancelled the policy. The court held this sequence permissible, stating: "What is necessary prior to cancellation is that the insurer had 'receipt' of notice... GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

Regarding your question on agency principles: The ruling does not view the subsequent human actions as creating a conflict where an agent acts after the principal has already finalized the transaction. Instead, the court views the automated server-to-server communication as the definitive moment the principal (GoAuto) took "possession, delivery, or custody" of the request. The later human steps (stamping and filing) are treated as administrative formalities rather than essential components of the statutory "receipt." Consequently, the cancellation is effective immediately upon digital ingestion by the server, rendering the timing of the human agent's subsequent validation irrelevant to the validity of the cancellation under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).
