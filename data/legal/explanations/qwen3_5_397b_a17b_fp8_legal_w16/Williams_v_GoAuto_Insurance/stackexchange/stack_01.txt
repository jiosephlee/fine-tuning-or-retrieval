Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does 'strict adherence' to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) implicitly require a human signature on cancellation notices?
Question:
In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit affirmed that Louisiana's requirement for 'strict adherence' to insurance cancellation statutes does not mandate a signature on the certification notice sent by the premium finance company. The court noted the statute uses the word 'certifying' but doesn't explicitly say 'signed.' Given that previous cases like *Benitez v. Elsayed* criticized notices lacking signatures (even if not holding them fatal per se), how do courts generally determine when 'strict adherence' crosses the line from following the text to adding extra-statutory requirements? Is there a doctrinal test for when a missing formality renders a statutory certification invalid?
Answer:
**Answer**

No, under the Fifth Circuit's analysis in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, "strict adherence" to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) does not implicitly require a human signature on the certification notice sent by a premium finance company.

While Louisiana courts consistently mandate "strict adherence" to the statutory cancellation procedures—meaning any defect in the process can render a cancellation ineffective (*Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*)—this standard requires fidelity to the **text of the statute**, not the addition of extra-statutory formalities.

### The Doctrine: Textualism Over Formalism
The core legal principle applied in *Williams* is that "strict adherence" serves to protect the insured by ensuring clear timing and cure periods (*Britten v. Reavis*), but it does not empower courts to insert requirements the legislature omitted.

Judge Southwick's opinion establishes a clear framework for determining when a missing formality invalidates a certification:

1.  **Statutory Silence is Dispositive:** The court noted that La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires a "statement certifying" specific facts but makes "no explicit reference to a signature." In the absence of explicit textual command, the court refused to read a signature requirement into the word "certifying."
2.  **Entity vs. Individual Action:** The statute specifies that it is "the insurance premium finance company" that effects cancellation by certifying information. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that because the statute assigns the duty to the corporate entity rather than a natural person, the company can satisfy the certification requirement without identifying a specific employee via name or signature.
3.  **Distinguishing *Benitez*:** You correctly identified *Benitez v. Elsayed* as a potential counterpoint. However, *Williams* clarifies the precedential value of *Benitez*. While *Benitez* criticized a notice that lacked a signature, it did not hold that the absence of a signature was *per se* fatal if other elements were present. In *Williams*, unlike in *Benitez*, the notices explicitly used the language "APAC certifies that..." thereby satisfying the substantive requirement of certification even without a handwritten or stamped signature at the moment of transmission.

### The Test for Invalidity
Based on *Williams*, there is no rigid multi-factor test for formalities; rather, there is a threshold inquiry: **Does the omission prevent the notice from performing the function mandated by the statute?**

*   **If yes:** The cancellation is invalid (e.g., failing to specify dates or interests, which defeats the purpose of fixing time and providing notice).
*   **If no:** The cancellation stands, even if traditional business formalities (like a signature) are delayed or automated.

The court emphasized that modern commercial realities involve automated systems where "company computers do much that individuals once did." Because GoAuto's automated email system successfully transmitted the required certifications to GoAuto's server ("receipt"), and those emails contained the mandatory statutory assertions, strict adherence was met. The subsequent affixing of a signature stamp by an APAC employee was deemed unnecessary for the validity of the initial cancellation act.

### Conclusion
Courts determine whether "strict adherence" has been violated by looking strictly at whether the statutory prerequisites were met, not whether common-law or customary business formalities were observed. Unless the statute explicitly demands a signature, or unless the lack thereof prevents the document from functioning as a valid certification under the text of § 9:3550(G), the cancellation remains effective.
