Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How does this ruling affect breach of good faith claims when underlying statutory cancellation procedures are deemed compliant?
Question:
The plaintiffs alleged breach of good faith and fair dealing based on improper cancellation procedures. Since the Fifth Circuit affirmed that GoAuto strictly adhered to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), does this automatically negate any bad faith claim as a matter of law in Louisiana? Or is it possible for an insurer to follow statutory technicalities perfectly but still act in bad faith due to other circumstances not covered by this specific procedural appeal?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes, under the specific facts and legal theory presented in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit's affirmation that GoAuto strictly adhered to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) effectively negates the plaintiffs' breach of good faith and fair dealing claims as a matter of law.

In this opinion, the court treats the statutory cancellation procedure not merely as a regulatory checklist, but as the exclusive definition of the insurer's obligations in this context. The plaintiffs' bad faith claim was derivative of their assertion that the cancellation itself was improper due to procedural defects (specifically, the lack of a pre-cancellation signature and the timing of physical "receipt"). Because the court held that GoAuto's automated electronic processes constituted strict adherence to the statute, the foundational premise of the bad faith claim—that GoAuto acted improperly—collapsed.

The court's reasoning relies on the principle that "strict adherence" to the statute does not invite courts to "add to the procedures." By concluding that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice and that electronic transmission constitutes valid "receipt," the court determined that GoAuto fulfilled every legal duty imposed upon it regarding cancellation. Since the duty of good faith and fair dealing in this context is bounded by the statutory framework governing cancellations, an insurer cannot act in bad faith by following the very procedures the legislature mandated and the court deemed sufficient.

The opinion does not leave open a pathway for a bad faith claim based on "other circumstances" because the plaintiffs' complaint was narrowly tailored to challenge the *validity* of the cancellation process itself. The court affirmed the dismissal of *all* claims with prejudice precisely because it found no statutory violation. Had there been independent allegations of bad faith unrelated to the mechanics of cancellation (e.g., fraudulent misrepresentation distinct from the cancellation notice, or refusal to pay a valid claim), those might survive. However, based strictly on this ruling, perfect compliance with § 9:3550(G) serves as a complete defense against a good faith claim predicated on alleged procedural improprieties in cancelling a financed policy.
