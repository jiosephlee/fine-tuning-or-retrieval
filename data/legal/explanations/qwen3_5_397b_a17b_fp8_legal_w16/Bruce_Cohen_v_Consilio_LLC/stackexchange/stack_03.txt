Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the procedural difference between raising a new claim for injunctive relief in a summary judgment brief versus amending the complaint?
Question:
The court dismissed Cohen's request for an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy because he didn't raise it until his summary judgment briefing, having withdrawn earlier requests in his complaint. I know Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15 governs amendments, but why couldn't the court just treat his briefing as a motion to amend or consider it liberally since it's related to the original wage theft allegations? At what point in litigation is it too late to pivot from seeking monetary damages to seeking specific equitable relief without formally amending pleadings?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, No. 24-2079 (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), the procedural distinction you are asking about turns on the fundamental requirement that **new claims must be raised through amended pleadings, not in opposition briefs.**

In Section II.D of the opinion, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's refusal to consider Cohen's request for an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy. The court noted that while Cohen's original complaint sought "injunctive relief" generally, he had previously withdrawn specific requests regarding the 2019 policy. He then attempted to reintroduce this specific theory for the first time in his summary judgment briefing.

The court rejected this approach, stating clearly: **"New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."** Citing *InfoDeli, LLC* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), the panel held that because Cohen never formally moved to amend his complaint to reinstate this specific request for equitable relief, the issue was not properly preserved for appeal.

To answer your specific questions:

*   **Why couldn't the court treat the briefing as a motion to amend?** While district courts have discretion under Rule 15 to liberally grant leave to amend, a party must still make a formal request or motion to trigger that discretion. A litigant cannot bypass the pleading requirements by simply asserting a new theory or form of relief in a brief opposing summary judgment. The Eighth Circuit indicated that because Cohen failed to follow the proper procedural channel (amending the complaint), the district court "understandably did not consider it," and thus the appellate court could not review it.
*   **When is it too late to pivot?** The opinion illustrates that once a party has withdrawn a specific request in their pleading and fails to formally amend before the summary judgment stage, attempting to revive that claim in a brief is procedurally fatal. The transition from seeking only monetary damages (or different equitable relief) to seeking specific performance or rescission requires an updated complaint to give fair notice to the opposing party and define the scope of the litigation.

In short, under Eighth Circuit precedent reflected in *Cohen*, a summary judgment brief is a vehicle for arguing existing claims based on the record, not a mechanism to introduce new causes of action or distinct forms of relief that were not pled.
