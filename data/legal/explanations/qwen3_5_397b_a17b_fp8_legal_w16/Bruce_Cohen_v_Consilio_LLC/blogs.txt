Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Commissioner's Exclusive Domain: Parsing Private Rights to Penalties Under the MPWA

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit drew a sharp line between compensatory recovery and punitive enforcement under Minnesota wage laws. While the court affirmed that private employees can sue to recover unpaid wages under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), it held that the statutory "average daily wage penalties" are the exclusive province of the state Commissioner. The decision in *Cohen* serves as a potent reminder that the presence of a private right of action does not automatically confer a private right to every remedy listed in a statute. By leaning heavily on textualism and the "knows how" canon of construction, Judge Kelly's opinion reinforces a growing judicial tendency to strictly cabin statutory penalties to the actors expressly designated by the legislature, potentially reshaping the leverage dynamic in wage-and-hour litigation across the Eighth Circuit.

The factual backdrop involves Consilio, a legal document review firm that eliminated overtime premium pay for its licensed attorney reviewers in 2019. Bruce Cohen sued on behalf of himself and a putative class, alleging violations of the MPWA, the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Crucially, before the appeal reached its conclusion, Consilio paid Cohen all alleged back wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA. The remaining dispute centered entirely on statutory penalties: Cohen sought hundreds of thousands of dollars in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA and additional penalties under the MFLSA and MWTA. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all counts, prompting Cohen's appeal.

The Eighth Circuit's analysis of the MPWA claim offers the most significant doctrinal takeaway. Cohen argued that Section 181.101's plain language authorized him to seek average daily wage penalties directly. The court disagreed, engaging in a close reading of the statute's mechanics. Section 181.101(a) explicitly details a process wherein the Commissioner serves a demand for payment; if the employer fails to comply within ten days, the Commissioner—not the employee—may charge and collect a penalty. While the statute notes that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee, it reserves only the right to "prosecute a claim for wages" to the individual worker.

To bolster this textual reading, the court employed the familiar "knows how" canon of construction. Judge Kelly pointed out that when the Minnesota Legislature intends to grant private parties the right to assess penalties, it knows exactly how to do so. The opinion cites Sections 181.13 and 181.14, which govern discharged or resigning employees; those statutes explicitly state that an employee "may charge and collect a penalty." The absence of similar language in Section 181.101 was deemed dispositive. Following precedent set by *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* and *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, the court concluded that because other sections explicitly empower employees to collect penalties while Section 181.101 does not, the legislature intentionally reserved punitive enforcement authority for the Commissioner alone. For private plaintiffs like Cohen, this limits recovery under this specific provision to compensatory damages—the unpaid wages themselves—stripping away what often serves as the primary multiplier in wage theft litigation.

While MPWA penalties were off the table due to statutory interpretation, Cohen's MFLSA claim presented a different hurdle: Article III standing and mootness. Because Consilio had already paid all outstanding wages and liquidated damages prior to judgment, Cohen no longer possessed a personal stake in those compensatory elements. His remaining argument relied solely on securing civil penalties payable to the Commissioner. Citing *Friends of Earth v. Laidlaw*, acknowledging that civil penalties can sometimes sustain standing by deterring future violations, Eighth Circuit nonetheless found unresolved questions regarding whether Consilio's voluntary cessation of its unlawful policy rendered Cohen's claim moot. If Consilio's policy change was permanent and recurrence unlikely, Cohen might lack a continuing interest sufficient for federal jurisdiction since he receives none of the penalty funds personally rather than merely receiving them via government distribution? # Voluntary Cessation and the Mootness Trap: When Full Payment Isn't Enough to Kill a Case

In the high-stakes world of wage-and-hour litigation, employers often operate under a comforting assumption: if you pay the plaintiff everything they are personally owed, the case is dead. The logic seems sound—if the individual has been made whole, where is the injury? Where is the controversy? But as the Eighth Circuit reminded us last week in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, this calculus changes dramatically when statutory civil penalties enter the equation. In an opinion that deftly navigates the intersection of state labor law and Article III standing, the court held that an employer's voluntary payment of back wages and liquidated damages does not automatically moot a plaintiff's claim for civil penalties payable to the state. The decision serves as a sharp warning to defense counsel: settling up with the named plaintiff may close the door on compensatory relief, but it leaves the window wide open for punitive sanctions if the underlying unlawful policy remains in force.

The facts of *Cohen* present a classic scenario in modern employment litigation. Bruce Cohen, an attorney document reviewer, sued his employer Consilio under various Minnesota statutes after the company eliminated premium overtime pay for licensed attorneys. Before the district court could rule on the merits, Consilio cut a check. They paid Cohen his full amount of alleged overtime wages plus liquidated damages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), totaling over $3,600 for Cohen individually and hundreds of thousands for the putative class. The parties even stipulated that Cohen had been paid "all overtime wages he alleges he is owed." Consilio moved for summary judgment, arguing that with the money paid, there was nothing left to litigate. The district court agreed, granting summary judgment across the board.

The Eighth Circuit, however, drew a critical distinction between what makes a plaintiff whole and what keeps a case alive in federal court. Writing for the panel, Judge Kelly affirmed the dismissal of Cohen's claims under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act and the Wage Theft Act, noting that those statutes simply did not authorize private citizens to seek the specific penalties Cohen requested. But regarding the MFLSA claim, the court hit pause. While Cohen no longer had a personal financial stake in recovering unpaid wages or liquidated damages—since he had already received them—he retained a potential interest in civil penalties. Crucially, under Minnesota law, these penalties are payable to the Commissioner of Labor and Industry, not to Cohen himself. This structural detail transformed the nature of the suit from a purely private dispute into one with public enforcement dimensions.

The crux of the Eighth Circuit's analysis rests on the doctrine of voluntary cessation and its interaction with Article III standing. Under Supreme Court precedent established in *Friends of Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, civil penalties can satisfy redressability requirements because they serve to deter future violations and encourage defendants to discontinue current unlawful conduct. Even though Cohen wouldn't pocket these penalty dollars, his status as a "citizen plaintiff" allows him to seek them as a proxy for enforcing public norms against ongoing illegality. The court reasoned that merely paying off past debts does not erase the threat of future harm if the employer continues to maintain an illegal policy. As long as Consilio's "no premium overtime" policy remained on the books, Cohen could arguably still suffer injury-in-fact through continued exposure to unlawful practices or by representing a class currently subjected to them. Consequently, full payment of arrears did not render his claim for prospective penalties moot.

This leads to perhaps the most significant procedural takeaway from *Cohen*: vacatur and remand based on jurisdictional uncertainty rather than substantive meritocracy. Because it was unclear whether Consilio had actually rescinded its controversial overtime policy or merely paused its enforcement during litigation, Eighth Circuit could not determine if "it [was] absolutely clear that their wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." That phrase—"absolutely clear"—is doing heavy lifting here. It reflects notoriously difficult standard defendants must meet when invoking voluntary cessation to defeat jurisdiction. # Procedural Peril: Waiver, New Claims, and the Limits of Summary Judgment Briefing

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, the court delivered a split decision that underscores a critical, often overlooked reality of federal litigation: substantive rights can be entirely forfeited through procedural missteps. While the panel affirmed the dismissal of Cohen's claims under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) on statutory interpretation grounds, the fate of his remaining claims under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and his request for injunctive relief hinged not on the merits of the alleged wage theft, but on how—and when—those arguments were presented. For practitioners, the opinion serves as a stark reminder that Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and appellate waiver doctrines are not mere technicalities; they are gatekeepers that can bar access to relief even when a statutory violation appears plausible.

The backdrop of the case involves a classic wage-and-hour dispute where Consilio, a legal document review firm, eliminated overtime premium pay for licensed attorneys. Although Consilio eventually paid out over $250,000 in back wages and liquidated damages to resolve the core monetary dispute under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Cohen pressed on, seeking substantial statutory penalties and injunctive relief. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio across the board. On appeal, while the Eighth Circuit found a genuine jurisdictional question regarding the mootness of the MFLSA penalty claim—vacating and remanding that specific issue—it summarily affirmed the dismissal of Cohen's MWTA notice claim and his request for an injunction based entirely on procedural default.

The disposition of the MWTA claim offers a masterclass in the dangers of inadequate briefing. Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice regarding changes to its overtime policy, a violation that could theoretically trigger penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032. However, in his opening brief before the Eighth Circuit, Cohen offered no legal authority supporting his entitlement to civil penalties under this specific statute, nor did he meaningfully argue why the district court's dismissal was erroneous given his prior stipulation that he had received all owed wages. The court, citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, declined to construct arguments for him. This application of waiver doctrine is ruthless but consistent with Eighth Circuit jurisprudence: an appellant cannot simply assert error; they must demonstrate it with cogent legal analysis. By failing to bridge the gap between the statutory text and his requested remedy in his brief, Cohen effectively conceded the point, leaving the district court's dismissal undisturbed regardless of whether Consilio actually violated the notice requirement.

Even more instructive is the court's handling of Cohen's request for injunctive relief. Cohen sought an order compelling Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. Yet, this specific formulation of injunctive relief appeared for the first time in his opposition brief to Consilio's motion for summary judgment; it was absent from his original complaint and any amended pleadings. The Eighth Circuit flatly rejected this attempt to expand the scope of litigation at the eleventh hour. Citing its recent decision in *InfoDeli, LLC*, the panel reiterated that new claims or distinct forms of relief must be raised through amended complaints governed by Rule 15(a), not smuggled into the case via opposition memoranda. This distinction is vital for litigators navigating summary judgment practice. Once discovery closes and briefing begins, the pleadings generally define the contours of the dispute. Attempting to alter those contours without leave of court or consent invites immediate forfeiture, as judges are under no obligation to consider theories or requests that were not properly pled and subjected to adversarial testing during discovery.

The contrast between the MFLSA remand and the MWTA/injunction affirmance highlights the asymmetry of procedural rules versus substantive jurisdiction. The court paused to examine Article III standing and mootness sua sponte regarding the MFLSA penalties because jurisdiction is a threshold requirement that cannot be waived by counsel; if Cohen lacks standing because Consilio voluntarily ceased its unlawful conduct, no amount of clever briefing could save the claim. Conversely, waiving a statutory penalty argument or failing to plead an injunction properly are errors attributable to counsel alone. The result is a fractured outcome where Cohen gets another bite at the apple on jurisdiction but loses forever on claims that likely possessed merit but were procedurally mishandled.

For employment lawyers and civil litigants alike, *Cohen* reinforces several bedrock principles that bear repeating. First, stipulations resolving monetary damages do not automatically preserve ancillary claims for penalties or equitable relief; those claims require independent statutory grounding and vigorous advocacy in appellate briefs. Second, Rule 15(a) remains a rigid boundary line during summary judgment phases; if a plaintiff realizes mid-stream that their prayer for relief needs adjustment or that a new theory applies, they must move formally to amend rather than hoping an opposing brief provides sufficient cover. Finally, appellate courts view summary judgment records with deference to what was actually argued below—if an issue wasn't raised with specificity in response to dispositive motions or supported by authority on appeal, it is treated as abandoned. In high-stakes wage litigation where statutory penalties can dwarf actual damages such as those Cohen sought totaling nearly $300,000—the cost of procedural discipline is far lower than price of forfeiture demonstrated here
