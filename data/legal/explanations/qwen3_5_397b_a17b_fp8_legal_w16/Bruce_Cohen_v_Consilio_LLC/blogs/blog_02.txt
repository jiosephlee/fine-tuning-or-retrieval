Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Voluntary Cessation vs. Mootness: The Jurisdictional Trap in Wage-and-Class Actions

In the high-stakes world of wage-and-hour litigation, employers often view full payment of back wages and liquidated damages as the golden ticket to dismissal. The logic is intuitive: if the plaintiff has been made whole, there is nothing left to fight about. But as the Eighth Circuit reminded us last week in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, No. 24-2079 (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), making a plaintiff financially whole does not necessarily extinguish the court's jurisdiction, particularly when statutory penalties designed to deter future misconduct remain at issue. In a nuanced decision that splits the difference between finality and Article III requirements, the court affirmed the dismissal of several state-law claims while vacating summary judgment on a Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim, sending the case back to the district court to answer a threshold question: Is the controversy actually dead, or just dormant?

The facts of *Cohen* illustrate a common trajectory in employment class actions. Bruce Cohen, an attorney document reviewer, sued his employer Consilio for failing to pay overtime premiums after the company instituted a new policy paying only base rates for all hours. Before the district court could rule on the merits of liability, Consilio cut a check. The company paid over $256,000 to cover overtime and liquidated damages for Cohen and similarly situated Minnesota employees, with Cohen personally receiving roughly $3,700. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid everything he was owed in terms of wages and damages. They then asked the district court to rule on their competing motions for summary judgment to resolve whether any statutory penalties were still available. The district court took the bait, granting summary judgment for Consilio across the board.

On appeal, the Eighth Circuit largely agreed with the lower court regarding the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Writing for the panel, Judge Kelly held that under the plain text of the MPWA, only the state Commissioner—not private employees—can seek "average daily wage penalties" for late payment of wages. Because the statute explicitly reserves penalty enforcement to the Commissioner upon a demand for payment, Cohen lacked a private right of action for those specific fines. Similarly, his MWTA claim regarding notice failures fell flat because he offered no legal authority supporting a private right to penalties under that specific provision once his wages were satisfied. These portions of the opinion serve as a straightforward lesson in statutory construction: where the legislature knows how to create a private penalty mechanism (as it did in other sections of Minnesota law) but omits it elsewhere, courts will not read one into existence.

However, the court's handling of the MFLSA claim reveals a far more complex jurisprudential puzzle involving Article III standing and the doctrine of mootness. Although Cohen conceded he received all owed wages and liquidated damages, he continued to pursue civil penalties under the MFLSA. Crucially, unlike back pay, these statutory penalties are payable to the state Commissioner, not to Cohen. This distinction triggered a jurisdictional alarm bell for the Eighth Circuit. Under Supreme Court precedent in *Friends of Earth v. Laidlaw*, civil penalties can sustain standing because they serve to redress injuries by deterring future violations. Yet, standing must persist throughout the litigation; if the underlying controversy disappears, the case becomes moot and federal courts lose their power to adjudicate it.

The crux of the Eighth Circuit's decision lies in its application of the "voluntary cessation" exception to mootness. It is well-settled law that a defendant cannot automatically moot a case simply by stopping their illegal conduct once sued; otherwise, bad actors could evade judicial review by pausing violations during litigation and resuming them afterward. For voluntary cessation to render a case moot, it must be "absolutely clear" that the wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur. Herein lay the fatal flaw in Consilio's victory below: while everyone agreed Cohen was paid what he was owed *for past work*, no one established whether Consilio had actually changed its overtime policy going forward.
