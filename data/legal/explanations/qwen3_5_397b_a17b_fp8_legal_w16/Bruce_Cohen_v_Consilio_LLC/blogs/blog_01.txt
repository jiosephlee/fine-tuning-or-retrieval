Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Commissioner's Exclusive Domain: Parsing Private Rights to Penalties Under the MPWA

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit drew a sharp line between an employee's right to recover unpaid wages and the state's exclusive prerogative to levy punitive penalties. While the case involves a familiar fact pattern—a legal document review firm eliminating overtime premiums for licensed attorneys—the appellate court's treatment of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) offers a significant lesson in statutory construction. By strictly adhering to the text of Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101, the panel affirmed that private plaintiffs cannot bypass the Commissioner of Labor and Industry to seek "average daily wage penalties," even when an employer has admittedly violated wage laws. The decision underscores a growing judicial reluctance to infer private causes of action for statutory penalties where the legislature has explicitly reserved that power for administrative enforcement.

The core of the dispute centered on whether Cohen, having already recovered his unpaid overtime and liquidated damages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), could still pursue additional civil penalties under the MPWA. Consilio had instituted a policy paying hourly licensed attorneys their base rate for all hours, effectively stripping them of overtime premiums. After Cohen filed suit, Consilio paid out over $256,000 to satisfy the class's back-wage and liquidated damage claims. Yet, Cohen pressed on, seeking roughly $172,000 in average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio, holding that the statute did not authorize such penalties in a private suit. The Eighth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Kelly, agreed, grounding its analysis in the plain language of the statute and the specific mechanics of enforcement it prescribes.

The court's reasoning hinged on the textual distinction between recovering "wages" and collecting "penalties." Section 181.101(a) explicitly empowers the Commissioner to serve a demand for payment and, if the employer fails to comply within ten days, to "charge and collect" both wages and a penalty. Crucially, the statute mandates that money collected by the Commissioner be paid to the employee, but it limits the employee's independent role to "prosecut[ing] a claim for wages." The Eighth Circuit read this silence regarding private penalty actions as deliberate. To bolster this reading, Judge Kelly invoked the *expressio unius* canon—or as Minnesota courts often phrase it, the "knows how" doctrine. The opinion noted that in adjacent statutes governing discharged or resigning employees (§§ 181.13 and 181.14), the Minnesota Legislature explicitly granted employees the right to "charge and collect a penalty." Because the Legislature knew how to confer private penalty rights when it wished to do so, its failure to include similar language in § 181.101 signaled an intent to reserve penalty enforcement solely for the Commissioner.

This interpretive move has profound doctrinal implications for wage-and-hour litigation in Minnesota and potentially beyond. It effectively bifurcates remedies: private suits are vehicles for making workers whole (back pay and liquidated damages), while public enforcement remains the exclusive channel for punishment (civil penalties). This structure prevents what some courts might view as a windfall for plaintiffs who have already been compensated for their actual losses, while simultaneously preserving the state's discretion in calibrating deterrents against employers. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: pleading a violation of § 181.101 is insufficient to trigger penalty liability in federal court; one must either involve the Commissioner or identify a different statutory hook that expressly authorizes private penal recovery. Ignoring this distinction risks dismissal at the summary judgment stage, regardless of the merits of underlying wage theft allegations.

While the MPWA analysis was definitive, the court's handling of Cohen's MFLSA claim introduced a procedural wrinkle that muddied an otherwise clean resolution. Because Cohen had stipulated that he received all owed wages and liquidated damages, his remaining claim sought only statutory penalties payable to the Commissioner, not himself. This raised immediate Article III standing and mootness concerns under *Friends of Earth v. Laidlaw*. If Consilio had voluntarily ceased its unlawful overtime policy such that there was no reasonable expectation of recurrence, Cohen's claim for prospective deterrence via penalties might be moot. Conversely, if the policy remained active, he might retain standing based on his status as a beneficiary of future deterrence rather than direct monetary gain.
