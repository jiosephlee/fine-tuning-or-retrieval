Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Procedural Pitfalls: Waiver, Amendment, and the Limits of Summary Judgment Briefing

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, the court delivered a split decision that underscores a harsh reality of federal appellate practice: even meritorious underlying wage disputes can founder on procedural shoals. While the panel affirmed the dismissal of claims under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) on substantive statutory grounds, its treatment of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) notice claim and the request for injunctive relief offers a masterclass in what *not* to do during summary judgment briefing. For practitioners navigating complex employment litigation, *Cohen* serves as a stark reminder that the mechanics of preservation and amendment are often just as decisive as the merits of the statutory interpretation.

The substantive backdrop involves Consilio's elimination of overtime premium pay for licensed attorney document reviewers. While the Eighth Circuit largely sided with Consilio on the MPWA claim—holding that only the state Commissioner, not private plaintiffs, may seek average daily wage penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101—the court's handling of Cohen's remaining claims reveals critical procedural failures. Specifically, Cohen's attempt to salvage his MWTA notice claim and his evolving strategy regarding injunctive relief collided with rigid application of waiver doctrines and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). These missteps resulted in the affirmation of dismissals that might otherwise have warranted deeper judicial scrutiny had they been properly framed below.

The fate of Cohen's MWTA claim illustrates the unforgiving nature of appellate waiver rules when faced with undeveloped arguments. Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice regarding changes to its overtime policy, a violation potentially triggering penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.032. Yet, on appeal, Cohen offered little more than a contention that the district court had ignored the claim. He pointed to no specific statutory authority granting him a private right to civil penalties for this specific violation, nor did he construct a coherent legal argument distinguishing this provision from others where penalties are explicitly reserved for the state. The Eighth Circuit, citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, declined to act as counsel for the appellant, noting that issues unsupported by argument or legal authority in the briefs are deemed waived. This outcome reinforces a fundamental principle: an appellate court reviews arguments, not mere assertions. Had Cohen's counsel anchored the MWTA claim in specific text or analogous case law demonstrating a private cause of action for notice violations, the analysis might have differed. Instead, the silence in the briefing proved fatal.

Even more instructive is the court's rejection of Cohen's shifting approach to injunctive relief. In his original complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctions tailored to rectify particular harms. However, in his opposition brief to Consilio's motion for summary judgment, he pivoted, requesting a broader order compelling Consilio to "rescind its illegal overtime policy" entirely. The district court understandably refused to consider this new formulation, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that refusal with brevity. Citing its recent precedent in *InfoDeli, LLC*, the panel reiterated that new claims or fundamentally altered requests for relief cannot be introduced for the first time in an opposition brief; they require a formal motion to amend under Rule 15(a). This distinction is vital for litigators managing case strategy. While advocacy naturally evolves as discovery progresses, the procedural vehicle for that evolution matters immensely. Attempting to expand the scope of liability or relief through briefing alone invites dismissal on procedural grounds, regardless of whether the underlying policy change might actually be unlawful.

These procedural stumbles stand in sharp contrast to the court's careful jurisdictional dance regarding Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim—a claim that survived only because it presented a live Article III controversy requiring further factual development. Unlike the waived MWTA claim or the improperly raised injunctive request, MFLSA penalties payable to Commissioner raised genuine questions about mootness depending on whether Consilio had voluntarily ceased its violative conduct permanently or temporarily merely due to litigation pressure (*Friends of Earth v. Laidlaw*). By vacating and remanding solely on this jurisdictional issue while affirming everything else based on waiver and improper amendment, *Cohen* draws a bright line between errors that go to substance versus those that go squarely to process and procedure: Waiver is final; jurisdiction is fluid until proven otherwise!

Ultimately! Ultimately! Ultimately! The ultimate lesson from *Cohen* extends beyond Minnesota wage law—it is universal across all areas!
