Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Standard of Review and Statutory Interpretation Principles

To understand the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, a student must first grasp the procedural lens through which the appellate court viewed the case. The court did not retry the facts or weigh the credibility of witnesses; rather, it evaluated whether the district court correctly applied the law to the undisputed record. This chapter explores the dual analytical framework employed by the court: the procedural standard governing summary judgment appeals and the substantive canons of statutory construction used to interpret Minnesota's wage laws. By mastering these concepts, you will see how the court moved from a broad review of the lower court's decision to a granular analysis of specific statutory text to resolve Cohen's claims for penalties.

## De Novo Review of Summary Judgment Motions

The foundation of the Eighth Circuit's analysis rests on the standard of review applied to the district court's grant of summary judgment. In *Cohen*, the court explicitly stated that it reviews such grants *de novo*. This Latin term, meaning "anew" or "from the beginning," signifies that the appellate court gives no deference to the district judge's legal conclusions. Unlike questions of fact, where an appellate court might only overturn a finding if it is "clearly erroneous," questions of law are reviewed with a fresh eye. The Eighth Circuit reiterated that summary judgment is proper only when "there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law."

This standard is critical because it defines the scope of the appellate court's power. In *Cohen*, because the parties had stipulated to many facts—such as Consilio paying Cohen all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages—the remaining disputes were purely legal: specifically, whether the relevant Minnesota statutes authorized private citizens to sue for civil penalties. Since no factual disputes remained regarding what Consilio paid or when, the Eighth Circuit was free to independently interpret the statutes without deferring to the district court's reading. If the appellate court determines that the lower court misinterpreted a statute, it must reverse or vacate that portion of the judgment, regardless of how reasonable the lower court's interpretation might have seemed.

## Viewing Evidence in the Light Most Favorable to the Non-Moving Party

Integral to *de novo* review in summary judgment cases is the requirement to view all evidence and reasonable inferences "in the light most favorable to the non-moving party." In this litigation, Cohen was the non-moving party below, having appealed Consilio's successful motion for summary judgment. This rule acts as a protective mechanism for plaintiffs, ensuring that their case is not dismissed prematurely if there is any ambiguity in how a jury might perceive contested facts. The court must assume that any version of events supported by some evidence belongs to Cohen.

However, in *Cohen*, this principle had limited practical impact on several key issues due to extensive stipulations between counsel. For instance, regarding Cohen's claim under Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), he admitted he had already been paid every dollar he claimed was owed for overtime and liquidated damages. Because these were binding admissions rather than disputed facts requiring inference-drawing, viewing them favorably toward Cohen did not alter their substance: he had been made whole financially regarding direct compensation. Consequently, while this standard usually serves as a high bar for defendants seeking dismissal, here it highlighted that Cohen's appeal relied entirely on novel interpretations of penalty statutes rather than gaps in evidentiary support for wage theft itself. Understanding when this standard applies—and when stipulations render it moot—is essential for predicting outcomes in employment litigation appeals.
