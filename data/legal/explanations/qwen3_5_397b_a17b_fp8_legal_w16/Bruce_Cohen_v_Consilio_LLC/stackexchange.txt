Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why can't an employee sue for 'average daily wage penalties' under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 if the statute says money collected goes to the employee?
Question:
I'm struggling with the court's interpretation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). The statute says that if the Commissioner collects a penalty, the money must be paid to the employee. It seems logical that this implies the employee has a right to seek those penalties directly if the Commissioner doesn't act. However, the court ruled that only the Commissioner can seek these penalties because the text explicitly grants that power to the Commissioner. How does the court distinguish between who *collects* the money versus who has the *cause of action* to initiate the penalty? Is this a strict textualist reading where 'expressio unius est exclusio alterius' applies?
Answer:
The court's distinction rests on a strict textual analysis of **who is authorized to initiate the penalty mechanism** versus **who receives the financial benefit** once that mechanism is successfully triggered.

Under Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a), the statute creates a specific administrative process for recovering unpaid wages and penalties. The text explicitly states that if an employer fails to pay after a demand, "**the commissioner may charge and collect** the wages earned ... and a penalty." The court contrasts this mandatory grant of authority to the Commissioner with the rights reserved for the employee. While the statute clarifies that it "does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages," it is silent regarding an employee's right to prosecute a claim for *penalties*.

The court resolves this silence using two primary interpretive tools:

1.  **Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius:** By explicitly granting the power to "charge and collect" penalties solely to the Commissioner, the legislature implicitly excluded all others (including employees) from exercising that power. The fact that the statute later mandates that "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee" addresses the *disposition* of funds, not the *authority* to sue. As the court notes, citing *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, the ability to enforce the penalty is limited to the Commissioner, even though the employee is the ultimate beneficiary of those funds.
2.  **Legislative Comparison ("Knows How" Canon):** The opinion highlights that when the Minnesota Legislature intends for an employee to directly assess and collect penalties, it knows exactly how to draft such a provision. The court points to Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, which govern discharged or resigning employees; those statutes explicitly state that the *employee* "may charge and collect a penalty." The absence of similar language in § 181.101 indicates a deliberate legislative choice to reserve penalty enforcement for the Commissioner alone.

Therefore, your intuition that the payout destination implies a right to sue is incorrect under this reading. The statute separates the **remedy** (payment to the employee) from the **enforcement mechanism** (action by the Commissioner). Because Cohen attempted to bypass the Commissioner and seek penalties directly through a private civil action under § 181.171, he lacked standing for that specific remedy, as § 181.171 only allows private suits for penalties "provided for in the section" violated—and § 181.101 provides no such private right of action for penalties.

### How does voluntary cessation of an illegal policy affect Article III standing when seeking civil penalties payable to the state?
Question:
In the MFLSA portion of the opinion, the court vacates summary judgment to check for mootness regarding statutory penalties. I understand that voluntary cessation usually doesn't moot a case seeking injunctive relief, but here Cohen is seeking civil penalties that would be paid to the Commissioner, not him. Since he already got his back wages and liquidated damages, does he still have a 'personal stake' in the outcome sufficient for standing under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*? If the penalty doesn't compensate him personally, how does stopping the violation make his claim for those specific penalties moot?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the short answer to your question is that **voluntary cessation does not automatically moot a claim for civil penalties**, even when those penalties are payable to the state rather than the plaintiff. However, whether the claim *actually* becomes moot depends entirely on whether it is "absolutely clear" that the illegal conduct will not recur.

Here is how the Eighth Circuit applies the doctrine in this specific context:

### 1. The General Rule: Voluntary Cessation Rarely Moors a Case
You correctly identified the tension: Cohen has already received his personal compensation (back wages and liquidated damages), and the remaining relief (statutory penalties) benefits the Commissioner, not him. Under normal circumstances, once a plaintiff receives full compensation for their individual injury, their "personal stake" might disappear.

However, the court relies on *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envt'l Servs.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), to explain that civil penalties serve a dual purpose. Even though they are paid to the government, they afford redress to citizen plaintiffs by:
> "[E]ncourag[ing] defendants to discontinue current violations and deterr[ing] them from committing future ones."

Because these penalties function as a deterrent against ongoing unlawful conduct, a plaintiff retains an interest in securing them as long as the threat of recurrence exists. Consequently, the Supreme Court has established that "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice."

### 2. The Exception: When It Becomes Moot
The court clarifies that while voluntary cessation is usually insufficient to moot a case, it **can** do so if the defendant meets a very high burden. A claim for penalties becomes moot only if:
> "Subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur."

If Consilio can prove it is "absolutely clear" their illegal overtime policy will never return, then Cohen loses his standing to seek penalties because there is no longer any unlawful conduct to deter or punish. At that point, he lacks a continuing personal interest in the outcome.

### 3. Application to *Cohen*
The Eighth Circuit did not decide whether Cohen currently has standing or whether the case is moot. Instead, they identified a factual dispute preventing them from making that determination based on the record before them:
*   **The Dispute:** While everyone agrees Cohen was paid his back wages, "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute."
*   **The Outcome:** Because it is unclear if the policy persists, the court cannot yet say it is "absolutely clear" the conduct won't recur.
    *   If the policy **remains**, Cohen's claim for penalties is viable (not moot).
    *   If the policy **is gone** and won't return, the claim may be moot due to lack of jurisdiction.

Therefore, the court vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA claim specifically to allow the District Court to determine this jurisdictional fact first. As Judge Kobes noted in his special concurrence, this threshold issue of mootness/standing should be decided before addressing the merits of the penalty claim.

**Summary:** Stopping the violation does not instantly strip you of standing to seek state-payable penalties. You retain standing unless the defendant proves it is *impossible* for them to restart the illegal behavior. Since Consilio hasn't met that heavy burden yet in this record, Cohen's potential entitlement to penalties keeps his "personal stake" alive for now.

### What is the procedural difference between raising a new claim for injunctive relief in a summary judgment brief versus amending the complaint?
Question:
The court dismissed Cohen's request for an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy because he didn't raise it until his summary judgment briefing, having withdrawn earlier requests in his complaint. I know Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15 governs amendments, but why couldn't the court just treat his briefing as a motion to amend or consider it liberally since it's related to the original wage theft allegations? At what point in litigation is it too late to pivot from seeking monetary damages to seeking specific equitable relief without formally amending pleadings?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, No. 24-2079 (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), the procedural distinction you are asking about turns on the fundamental requirement that **new claims must be raised through amended pleadings, not in opposition briefs.**

In Section II.D of the opinion, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's refusal to consider Cohen's request for an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy. The court noted that while Cohen's original complaint sought "injunctive relief" generally, he had previously withdrawn specific requests regarding the 2019 policy. He then attempted to reintroduce this specific theory for the first time in his summary judgment briefing.

The court rejected this approach, stating clearly: **"New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."** Citing *InfoDeli, LLC* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), the panel held that because Cohen never formally moved to amend his complaint to reinstate this specific request for equitable relief, the issue was not properly preserved for appeal.

To answer your specific questions:

*   **Why couldn't the court treat the briefing as a motion to amend?** While district courts have discretion under Rule 15 to liberally grant leave to amend, a party must still make a formal request or motion to trigger that discretion. A litigant cannot bypass the pleading requirements by simply asserting a new theory or form of relief in a brief opposing summary judgment. The Eighth Circuit indicated that because Cohen failed to follow the proper procedural channel (amending the complaint), the district court "understandably did not consider it," and thus the appellate court could not review it.
*   **When is it too late to pivot?** The opinion illustrates that once a party has withdrawn a specific request in their pleading and fails to formally amend before the summary judgment stage, attempting to revive that claim in a brief is procedurally fatal. The transition from seeking only monetary damages (or different equitable relief) to seeking specific performance or rescission requires an updated complaint to give fair notice to the opposing party and define the scope of the litigation.

In short, under Eighth Circuit precedent reflected in *Cohen*, a summary judgment brief is a vehicle for arguing existing claims based on the record, not a mechanism to introduce new causes of action or distinct forms of relief that were not pled.

### Did Cohen waive his MWTA notice claim by failing to cite specific statutory authority for penalties in his appeal?
Question:
Regarding the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim, the court affirmed dismissal partly because Cohen pointed to 'no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties' and offered 'no meaningful argument.' The court cites *Milligan* regarding waiver. My confusion is: Was this a failure of pleading (not stating a valid cause of action initially) or a failure of appellate advocacy (waiver by not briefing it properly)? If a statute allows penalties generally but not specifically for notice violations, is that a jurisdictional bar or just a merits defeat that shouldn't be waived so easily?
Answer:
This was a failure of **appellate advocacy** resulting in waiver, not a ruling on the merits of whether the statute permits such penalties.

The distinction lies in how the Eighth Circuit framed its rejection of the claim. In Section II.C, the court does not analyze the text of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) to determine if it authorizes private civil penalties for notice violations (as it did extensively for the MPWA claim in Section II.A). Instead, the court explicitly grounds its decision on procedural forfeiture:

> "Cohen... points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute. With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim."

By citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), the court applies the standard principle that an appellant waives any issue they fail to brief with adequate legal argument and authority. The phrase "we will not disturb" indicates that the appellate court is refusing to exercise its discretion to review the lower court's decision because the appellant failed to carry their burden of demonstrating error.

If this were a merits determination regarding jurisdiction or statutory interpretation, you would expect the court to engage in statutory construction similar to its analysis in Section II.A (e.g., examining whether the legislature "knew how" to grant private rights of action for penalties). The absence of such analysis confirms that the court never reached the question of whether the MWTA *could* support such a claim. Rather, it held that regardless of the statute's potential reach, Cohen forfeited his right to argue it by failing to cite specific authority or construct a meaningful argument on appeal.

Therefore, this is strictly a waiver based on inadequate briefing. It does not establish precedent that the MWTA lacks a private right of action for penalties; it merely establishes that Cohen cannot raise the issue without proper supporting argumentation.

### Why did Judge Kobes concur specially regarding attorney's fees and costs if they weren't explicitly discussed in the majority opinion?
Question:
Judge Kobes mentions in his special concurrence that he also vacates the ruling on attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest, yet these issues don't seem to appear in Part II of Judge Kelly's majority opinion. Are these items automatically tied to the underlying MFLSA claim such that vacating the main claim necessitates vacating fee awards? Or did Kobes identify an error that the majority overlooked, and if so, why didn't the majority address it directly?
Answer:
Judge Kobes's special concurrence addresses a procedural necessity that flows directly from the majority's decision to vacate the MFLSA claim, even though the majority opinion itself did not explicitly rule on fees, costs, or prejudgment interest.

In federal appellate practice, an award of attorney's fees and costs is generally considered **ancillary** to the underlying merits of the case. When an appellate court vacates a judgment on the primary claim, any derivative awards based solely on that claim typically cannot stand independently. This is because the legal basis for granting fees (e.g., a finding of liability or a specific statutory entitlement triggered by success on the MFLSA claim) has been erased by the vacatur.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, Judge Kelly's majority opinion in Part II.B vacates the summary judgment on Cohen's MFLSA claim and remands the case for the district court to determine whether the claim for statutory penalties is moot. Because the disposition of the MFLSA claim is now unsettled, any previous ruling by the district court regarding fees, costs, or prejudgment interest associated with that claim is procedurally premature and unsupported by a final resolution of liability.

Judge Kobes concurred specially to make this implicit consequence explicit. His statement—"But I also vacate the district court's ruling as to attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest"—serves two purposes:

1.  **Clarity of Mandate:** It ensures that when the case returns to the district court, there is no ambiguity that the fee award is wiped clean along with the merits judgment. Without this clarification, a litigant might argue that a separate fee order survived the vacatur of the main claim.
2.  **Judicial Economy:** It prevents the need for a subsequent appeal solely on the issue of fees once the district court resolves the mootness question on remand. By vacating these ancillary rulings now, the district court can reconsider them (if necessary) after determining whether Cohen retains standing to pursue penalties under the MFLSA.

Kobes did not identify a new error overlooked by the majority; rather, he highlighted a collateral consequence of the majority's holding. Since neither party briefed the specific issue of fees in relation to the mootness problem (as noted in his concurrence regarding standing), and since the majority focused strictly on Article III jurisdiction regarding penalties, Kobes used his special concurrence to cleanly sever the ancillary financial awards from the vacated claim, ensuring a fresh start on remand for all related issues.

### How does Minnesota's legislative history regarding discharged vs. resigned employees influence statutory interpretation of penalty rights?
Question:
The court relies heavily on comparing § 181.101 with §§ 181.13 and 181.14. The latter two sections explicitly allow employees who are discharged or quit to charge and collect penalties themselves. The court uses this contrast ('knows how') to argue that because § 181.101 lacks similar language for general wage non-payment, employees cannot seek penalties there. Is this canon of construction (*nosci tur a sociis* or similar) strong enough to override potential equitable arguments that employees should always have direct access to penalties when commissioners are overloaded?
Answer:
The court's reliance on the "knows how" canon (often associated with *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* or simply comparative statutory analysis) is indeed the controlling factor here, and under Minnesota state law principles applied by the Eighth Circuit, it effectively overrides the equitable arguments you raised regarding administrative burden.

In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed that an individual employee cannot seek average daily wage penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 for general wage non-payment. The court's reasoning rests entirely on a textual comparison of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) provisions.

**The "Knows How" Doctrine in Context**
The opinion explicitly contrasts § 181.101 with §§ 181.13 and 181.14. As the court notes, the legislature "knew how to empower an aggrieved employee with a statutory right to obtain penalties" because it did exactly that in the sections governing discharged (§ 181.13) and resigned (§ 181.14) employees. Those sections expressly authorize the employee to "charge and collect a penalty."

By contrast, § 181.101—which governs the general failure to pay wages at designated times—states only that if an employer fails to pay after a demand by the **Commissioner**, the **Commissioner** may charge and collect a penalty. The statute reserves to the employee only the right to "prosecute[] a claim for wages." Citing *Lagasse v. Horton*, the court applies the principle that legislative action in one statute combined with inaction in another demonstrates intentional exclusion. Because the text grants the penalty enforcement mechanism solely to the Commissioner in § 181.101, the court concludes the private right of action for penalties does not exist for the employee.

**Equitable Arguments vs. Legislative Intent**
Your question asks whether this canon is strong enough to override equitable concerns about Commissioner overload. Under the interpretive framework used in *Cohen*, the answer is yes. When a statute's language is deemed "plain and unambiguous," as the court found § 181.101 to be, Minnesota courts (and federal courts applying Minnesota law) assume this manifests legislative intent and must be given effect without resorting to extrinsic equities.

The court acknowledges the practical outcome: money collected by the Commissioner is paid to the employee, but the *authority* to initiate that penalty collection lies exclusively with the state agency. The opinion cites *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.* to reinforce that "the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner]." The judiciary's role, as portrayed in this opinion, is to enforce the statutory scheme as written by the legislature, not to rewrite it to alleviate potential administrative bottlenecks or to ensure direct access where the legislature has chosen an indirect route (employee sues for wages; Commissioner pursues penalties).

Therefore, while an equitable argument might hold sway in a policy debate or legislative hearing, it cannot overcome the clear textual distinction drawn by the Minnesota Legislature between sections allowing private penalty collection and those restricting it to the Commissioner. The "knows how" inference creates a definitive boundary that precludes private suits for penalties under § 181.101 regardless of the Commissioner's workload.
