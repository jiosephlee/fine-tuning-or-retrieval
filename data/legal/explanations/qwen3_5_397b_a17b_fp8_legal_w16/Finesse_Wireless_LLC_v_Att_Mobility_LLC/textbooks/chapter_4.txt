Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # Disposition and Impact on Damages

The culmination of *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* lies in the Federal Circuit's decisive reversal of the district court's denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement. This chapter examines the appellate court's final disposition, focusing on how the insufficiency of evidence regarding liability automatically dictates the fate of the monetary award. By understanding this sequence, law students learn that appellate review of patent damages is often contingent upon the threshold question of infringement; without a valid finding of liability, the quantification of harm becomes legally moot. The court's reasoning demonstrates that when a plaintiff fails to meet its burden of proof on every element of infringement for all asserted claims, the resulting verdict cannot stand, and any associated damages award must be vacated.

## Summary of Holdings: Reversal of JMOL Denials for Both Patents

The core holding of this opinion is that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the evidence presented at trial, specifically the testimony of Finesse's expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. For the '134 patent, the court determined that Finesse failed to prove the accused radios sampled "interference generating signals" as required by the claim language. Dr. Wells' testimony was internally contradictory; he initially mapped the claimed signals to a "modeled PIM path" that physically could not exist at the point of sampling described in the claims, and later attempted to pivot to unidentified inputs "x1 and x2" without ever explicitly mapping them to the claim limitations. Because Finesse bore the burden of proof and rested its case on this confused and self-contradictory testimony, the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law. Consequently, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL for noninfringement.

Similarly, for the '775 patent, the court held that substantial evidence did not support the jury's finding that the accused devices performed the seven specific multiplications required by the claims. While Finesse argued that using two unique signals could satisfy the "three signals" limitation through duplication, the fatal flaw was in demonstrating the mathematical operations themselves. The technical documentation relied upon by Dr. Wells showed only three distinct multiplications involving complex conjugates and moduli, not the seven specific combinations mandated by claim 1(d). Furthermore, neither Dr. Wells nor Finesse provided a coherent mapping showing how these three operations equated to the seven claimed steps, nor did they identify which subset of ten potential operations in Nokia's documents corresponded to the patented method. Given these evidentiary gaps, the court concluded that no reasonable jury could find infringement, leading to a reversal of the JMOL denial for the '775 patent as well.

## The Nexus Between Liability and Remedies: Why Vacating Infringement Necessitates Vacating Damages

Having reversed the infringement findings for both patents, the Federal Circuit addressed the damages award with brevity, illustrating a fundamental principle of civil procedure: remedies are derivative of liability. The district court had originally awarded Finesse a lump sum of over $166 million based on the jury's determination that AT&T and Nokia infringed valid claims throughout the remaining life of the patents. However, once an appellate court determines that no reasonable jury could have found infringement, there is no legal basis left to support a damages award. In this opinion, Chief Judge Moore explicitly states, "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims... we vacate the damages award."

This portion of the opinion serves as a critical lesson in judicial economy and logical necessity within patent litigation. The appellants had challenged both infringement and damages independently; however, because liability is a prerequisite for any remedy, resolving appeals against liability first renders separate analysis questions regarding damages calculation irrelevant unless some independent ground for liability remains intact (such as unchallenged claims or alternative theories).
