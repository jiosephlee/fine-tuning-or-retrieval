Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Infringement Analysis of the '134 Patent: The Sampling Limitation

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit's reversal of the infringement verdict for the '134 patent turns on a fundamental disconnect between the patent's claim language and the technical reality of the accused Nokia radios. The core dispute centers on Claim 1, which outlines a method for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference. To prove infringement, Finesse was required to demonstrate that the accused devices performed every step of the claimed method. The pivotal failure occurred at the very first step: the requirement to "over-sample... a passband of received signals" that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The court's analysis reveals that Finesse's expert witness provided testimony that was not only contradictory but logically impossible under the physics of the accused system, rendering the evidence insufficient to support the jury's verdict as a matter of law.

## Claim Construction: The Requirement to Sample a Passband Containing Both Signal Types Before Processing

Understanding the infringement analysis begins with the specific language of Claim 1[a] of the '134 patent. The claim requires a method comprising "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream." Crucially, the claim mandates that these "received signals" must simultaneously include two distinct components: "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The interference generating signals are defined by their capability to generate intermodulation products in-band of the signals of interest.

This limitation imposes a strict temporal and physical constraint on the accused device. For infringement to occur, the radio's receiver—specifically the analog-to-digital converter (ADC)—must capture a single passband where both types of signals exist concurrently *before* any digital processing or modeling occurs. If the "interference generating signals" do not exist at the moment the ADC samples the passband, or if they are created only after the sampling process has concluded, the literal requirements of the claim cannot be met. The district court and the parties accepted this construction; therefore, the burden rested entirely on Finesse to present evidence that the accused Nokia radios sample a physical reality containing both signal types at the input stage.

## The Expert's Initial Mapping: Identifying the 'Modeled PIM Path' as the 'Interference Generating Signal'

At trial, Finesse's burden of proof relied heavily on the testimony of its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. To establish that Nokia's radios infringed Claim 1[a], Dr. Wells utilized a Nokia technical document illustrating the signal flow within the accused device. In his direct testimony and expert report, Dr. Wells explicitly mapped the claim limitations to specific paths in this diagram. He identified the "downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference" signal as the claimed "signals of interest." More critically, he testified that the "modeled PIM path" constituted the claimed "interference generating signals."

Dr. Wells was consistent in this initial mapping throughout much of his examination. He stated unequivocally that the modeled PIM path was the signal causing intermodulation products in-band at the signal of interest. By equating the "modeled PIM path" withthe statutory "interference generating signals," Dr. Wells attempted to show that both elements were present on what he described as a "red path" enteringthe radio's receiver. This mapping formedthe bedrockof Finesse's infringement theory: if themodeled PIM pathis indeedan interferencegeneratingsignal enteringthesampler, thentheclaimlimitationissatisfied.
