Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the court treat technical diagrams in prior art or product documentation when an expert's verbal testimony conflicts with their own annotations?
Question:
A major issue in this case was Dr. Wells pointing to a diagram with a red path labeled 'DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path' and insisting these were two separate signals entering the ADC, even though physics dictates one of those paths is generated *after* sampling. He later mumbled about 'x1 and x2' but never clearly re-mapped them.

When an expert's verbal testimony seems physically impossible or contradicts the plain meaning of a technical document they are citing, how much weight should a jury theoretically give to their interpretation? Does the 'light most favorable to the verdict' standard protect an expert's bizarre interpretation of a diagram as long as they say it with confidence?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the short answer to your question is **no**. The "light most favorable to the verdict" standard does not protect an expert's interpretation when that interpretation is internally contradictory, physically impossible, and fails to map the evidence to the specific limitations of the patent claims.

In this case, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) specifically because Dr. Wells' testimony regarding the technical diagrams was too confused and inconsistent to constitute "substantial evidence."

### The Legal Standard: Substantial Evidence vs. Contradictory Testimony
While appellate courts generally view evidence in the light most favorable to the jury's verdict, this deference has limits. A verdict cannot stand if it relies on an expert's testimony that is self-contradictory or fails to address essential claim elements. As Chief Judge Moore wrote, "When the party with the burden of proof... rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard." The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to support the principle that no reasonable jury can find a limitation met based on contradictory opinions.

### Application to Dr. Wells' Diagram Interpretation
The court identified several fatal flaws in how Dr. Wells interpreted the Nokia technical diagram, which prevented his testimony from supporting the verdict:

**1. Failure to Map Testimony to Claim Limitations**
The core issue was not just that Dr. Wells was confused, but that he never successfully connected his changing story to the actual words of the patent claims.
*   **Initial Theory:** Dr. Wells explicitly mapped the "DL (TX) reference" to "signals of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" to "interference generating signals."
*   **The Physical Impossibility:** Cross-examination revealed that under this mapping, infringement was impossible because the "modeled PIM path" is generated *after* the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) samples the signal. Therefore, the ADC could not be sampling the "interference generating signals" as required by Claim 1[a].
*   **The Pivot:** When confronted with this impossibility, Dr. Wells vaguely mentioned "x1 and x2" as two signals on the red path. However, crucially, **he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claimed "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals."** He continued to insist throughout cross-examination that the relevant signals were still the DL (TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.

**2. The Jury Cannot Infer a Missing Link**
Finesse argued on appeal that Dr. Wells was simply laboring under a misimpression and actually meant x1 and x2 all along. The Federal Circuit rejected this, noting that inferring this connection required piecing together fragments of testimony from ninety pages apart in the record. The court stated there was "nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony" and that he offered "no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

Because Dr. Wells never affirmatively mapped x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms required for infringement, his mention of them did not cure the defect in his primary theory. The court held that this type of "confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict."

### Conclusion
An expert cannot save a deficient case by mumbling about alternative variables ("x1 and x2") without explicitly tying them back to the patent's claim language, especially when their primary explanation violates basic physics (sampling a signal before it exists). The Federal Circuit made it clear that when an expert's verbal testimony contradicts both physical reality and their own prior statements—and fails to bridge those contradictions with a clear mapping to the claims—the evidence is legally insufficient, regardless of how confidently it was delivered or how favorably one tries to view it post-verdict.
