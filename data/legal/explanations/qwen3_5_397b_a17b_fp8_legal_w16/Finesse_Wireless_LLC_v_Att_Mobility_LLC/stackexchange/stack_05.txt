Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the entire damages award instead of remanding for a recalculation based on valid claims?
Question:
The opinion states: 'Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims... we vacate the damages award.' The jury had awarded a lump sum for both patents combined ($166M).

Since every single asserted claim across both patents was found non-infringed due to lack of substantial evidence, I understand why there are no damages. But hypothetically, if only some claims were invalidated or found non-infringed while others stood, would the Federal Circuit still vacate a lump-sum award entirely? How do courts handle lump-sum damages when partial infringement findings are overturned on appeal?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit vacated the entire damages award because it reversed the finding of infringement for **every single asserted claim** across both patents-in-suit. Consequently, there was no legal basis left for any damages award.

To address your hypothetical: If the court had found that *some* claims were infringed while others were not, the outcome regarding the lump-sum award would likely differ, but the handling of such a scenario depends heavily on whether the jury's verdict allows for apportionment between the valid/infringed claims and the invalid/non-infringed ones.

### The Legal Standard: Lump-Sum Awards and Indivisibility

In patent cases, when a jury awards a single lump sum for multiple patents or multiple claims without specifying how much is attributed to each, the award is considered "indivisible." 

If an appellate court overturns the infringement finding for only a subset of those claims, it creates a problem: The reviewing court cannot determine what portion of the lump sum was attributable to the claims that were properly found infringed versus those that were erroneously found infringed. 

Under Federal Circuit precedent (which guides this analysis even though not explicitly cited in this specific short damages section), when a lump-sum award rests on a mix of correct and incorrect liability findings, and the record provides no way to disentangle them, the typical remedy is to **vacate the damages award and remand for a new trial on damages**. This ensures the patentee receives compensation only for the actual infringement proven, based on a fresh determination by a jury instructed on the surviving claims.

However, in *Finesse*, this complexity did not arise because **no claims survived**.

### Application to *Finesse Wireless*

The opinion is explicit about the scope of its reversal:
> "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for **all asserted claims**... we vacate the damages award."

The court analyzed two patents:
1.  **The '134 Patent:** The court reversed infringement for all asserted claims because Finesse's expert provided contradictory testimony regarding whether the accused devices sampled "interference generating signals" before they were generated—a physical impossibility. Without substantial evidence for any claim of this patent, zero damages could be attributed to it.
2.  **The '775 Patent:** The court reversed infringement for all asserted claims because Finesse failed to present evidence that the accused devices performed the required "seven claimed multiplications." The expert identified only three distinct multiplications and offered no mapping to the seven required by the claim language.

Since the jury's $166 million lump sum was predicated entirely on the infringement of these specific assertions, and *none* of those assertions stood upon review, there was no remaining foundation for *any* damages. There was no need to ask whether the award could be split or recalculated because there were no surviving findings of liability to support even a reduced calculation.

### Answering Your Hypothetical

If, hypothetically, the Federal Circuit had affirmed infringement on one claim of the '775 patent while reversing everything else:
*   They almost certainly would **not** have simply calculated a lower number themselves. Appellate courts do not act as fact-finders to recalculate damages based on new theories or reduced scopes.
*   Because the original verdict was a lump sum covering both patents and all claims, it would be impossible to know how much of that $166 million was intended for the single surviving claim.
*   In that scenario, standard practice dictates vacating the award and **remanding** to the district court for a new trial limited solely to determining damages for that single surviving claim (or potentially allowing parties to stipulate to a number if possible, though usually a retrial is necessary).

In summary, *Finesse* represents the cleanest case for vacation: total failure of proof on liability eliminates the need for remand instructions on apportionment because there is nothing left to compensate.
