Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the 'substantial evidence' standard apply when an expert witness gives contradictory testimony regarding claim element mapping?
Question:
In *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*, the Federal Circuit reversed the jury's infringement verdict for the '134 patent because the plaintiff's expert, Dr. Wells, gave confusing and self-contradictory testimony about which signals mapped to the 'interference generating signals' limitation. The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to say that self-contradictory testimony isn't enough to support a verdict. 

My confusion is: Usually, credibility determinations are strictly for the jury. At what point does an expert's inconsistency become so severe that an appellate court can say 'no reasonable jury' could have believed them, effectively overriding the jury's role as fact-finder? Is there a specific threshold for when contradictory testimony fails the substantial evidence test?
Answer:
There is no bright-line numerical threshold for when contradictory testimony fails the "substantial evidence" standard. Instead, the Federal Circuit applies a functional test: **does the contradiction leave the record devoid of any rational basis for the jury's finding?**

While credibility determinations are indeed the province of the jury, an appellate court can override those determinations when the expert's testimony is so internally inconsistent that it fails to prove an essential element of the claim as a matter of law. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court illustrates this principle by distinguishing between testimony that is merely "confusing" (which a jury might resolve) and testimony that is logically impossible or entirely unmapped to the claim limitations.

### The Legal Standard: *Johns Hopkins* and Logical Impossibility

The controlling precedent cited in *Finesse* is *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008). The doctrine established there—and applied in *Finesse*—is that when a party with the burden of proof relies exclusively on an expert who offers self-contradictory opinions regarding a critical claim limitation, no reasonable jury could rely on that testimony to find infringement.

This exception to the general rule of jury deference arises when:
1.  The expert explicitly maps evidence to a claim element;
2.  That mapping is shown to be physically or logically impossible (e.g., sampling a signal before it exists);
3.  The expert attempts to pivot to a new theory but fails to explicitly map the new evidence to the claim language; and
4.  The resulting record contains no coherent thread linking the accused product to every step of the patented method.

### Application in *Finesse Wireless*

In *Finesse*, Dr. Wells' testimony regarding the '134 patent failed this test because his contradiction created a logical vacuum regarding the "interference generating signals" limitation.

First, Dr. Wells consistently testified that the "modeled PIM path" constituted the "interference generating signals." However, under cross-examination, it became undeniable that the modeled PIM path is generated *downstream* of the RF ADC (the component responsible for sampling). As the court noted, "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated." Therefore, if Dr. Wells' initial mapping was correct, infringement was physically impossible.

Second, when confronted with this impossibility, Dr. Wells attempted to shift his theory, suggesting he was actually referring to input signals "x1 and x2." Crucially, however, he **never explicitly testified** that x1 and x2 mapped to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." He continued to refer to the "modeled PIM path" as the interference signal even while discussing x1 and x2.

The Federal Circuit held that this was not a simple credibility dispute for the jury to resolve. Because Dr. Wells never provided a clear, non-contradictory mapping of x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms, and his primary mapping was logically impossible, there was **"no clear or detailed explanation"** upon which a reasonable jury could base a verdict. The court emphasized that requiring a jury to piece together fragments of testimony from ninety pages apart to infer a mapping that the expert himself refused to articulate clearly exceeds the bounds of substantial evidence.

### Conclusion

The threshold for overturning a jury verdict based on contradictory expert testimony is met when the contradictions render the evidence **legally insufficient** rather than just debatable. If an expert's primary theory is disproven by physical laws or undisputed facts, and their alternative theory lacks explicit linkage to the claim language, an appellate court may conclude that "no reasonable jury" could have found infringement without engaging in speculation. In such cases, granting Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) does not usurp the jury's role; rather, it enforces the requirement that substantial evidence must exist in the record to support every element of the verdict.
