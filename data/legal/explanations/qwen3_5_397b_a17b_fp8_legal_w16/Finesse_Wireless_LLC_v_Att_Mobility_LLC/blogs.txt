Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Perils of Contradictory Expertise: Why Self-Impeaching Testimony Cannot Support a Jury Verdict

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, particularly within the technology sector, the line between a credible infringement theory and speculative storytelling often hinges on the precision of expert testimony. Yesterday, in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit drew that line sharply, reversing a district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and vacating a $166 million damages award. Writing for a unanimous panel, Chief Judge Moore delivered a potent reminder that while juries serve as the ultimate fact-finders, they cannot build a verdict on an expert's shifting sands. When a plaintiff's sole expert offers self-contradictory testimony regarding the fundamental architecture of an accused device, the evidence fails to meet the substantial evidence standard required to sustain a jury's finding of infringement.

The case centered on Finesse Wireless's assertions that AT&T and Nokia infringed patents related to mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. The dispute over U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 turned on a specific claim limitation requiring the accused radio to sample a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." To prove this element, Finesse relied entirely on the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who attempted to map these claim terms to a Nokia technical diagram. What followed was a forensic unraveling of Dr. Wells' methodology that ultimately doomed Finesse's case. Initially, Dr. Wells testified that the "interference generating signals" corresponded to a "modeled PIM path" shown in the diagram. However, under cross-examination, it became undeniable that this modeled path is generated digitally *after* the analog-to-digital conversion (sampling) occurs. Physically, the receiver cannot sample a signal that has not yet been created. This admission created a fatal logical gap: if the modeled PIM path is the interference signal, the claim limitation requiring pre-sampling presence cannot be met.

Rather than conceding the point or offering a coherent alternative, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot. He suggested that perhaps two other inputs labeled "x1" and "x2" on the diagram were the true signals of interest and interference generators. Yet, as Chief Judge Moore meticulously detailed, Dr. Wells never explicitly mapped x1 and x2 to the requisite claim terms during his testimony. Instead, he continued to refer to the "downlink TX reference" and the "modeled PIM path" as the relevant signals, creating a confusing tapestry of assertions that contradicted both physics and his own prior statements. The district court, attempting to salvage the verdict, inferred that Dr. Wells must have meant x1 and x2 all along, despite the lack of clear testimony supporting such a conclusion. The Federal Circuit rejected this judicial alchemy, holding that when a party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony without clarification, no reasonable jury could find infringement by substantial evidence.

This reasoning leans heavily on the precedent set in *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, where the court established that an expert's contradictory opinions can render evidence insufficient as a matter of law. The tension here lies between respecting the jury's province to weigh credibility and fulfilling the court's gatekeeping role to ensure legal sufficiency. Usually, inconsistencies in testimony are questions of weight for the jury to resolve; however, *Finesse* illustrates an exception where the inconsistency is so fundamental that it destroys the logical bridge between the evidence and the verdict. Dr. Wells did not merely offer a conflicting interpretation of data; he offered mutually exclusive physical realities regarding when and how signals were sampled. By failing to commit to a single, viable theory that satisfied every element of the claim, his testimony ceased to be substantive evidence and became mere speculation disguised as expertise.

The implications of *Finesse* extend well beyond this specific dispute over radio frequency interference. It signals a heightened scrutiny from appellate courts regarding "pivot" arguments—those last-minute theoretical shifts experts employ during cross-examination or rebuttal to patch holes in an infringement chart litigated at trial. Plaintiffs can no longer assume that if an expert mentions enough variables or pathways throughout hundreds of pages of transcript, a jury will be permitted to piece together a winning theory post-hoc. The opinion reinforces that infringement must be proven through clear mappings articulated during trial, not constructed by appellate briefs or judicial inference after a verdict is returned. For defense counsel in patent cases involving complex system architectures, *Finesse* provides robust ammunition for JMOL motions where plaintiff experts struggle to maintain consistency under rigorous cross-examination regarding signal flow and component interaction.

Ultimately, while Finesse also lost its battle regarding U.S.P.N.O.'975 due to similar evidentiary gaps concerning mathematical multiplications—a separate failure where three distinct operations could not magically constitute seven—the reversal on damages flows directly from total non-infringement across all asserted claims ($0 base means $0 royalties). But it is Chief Judge Moore's treatment of Dr.Wells' floundering analysis under section I(A) which will resonate most deeply with practitioners.The decision serves as cautionary tale:an expert witness must possess command over their narrative consistent with engineering reality; otherwise,courts remain willing pull rug out from beneath even largest jury awards when foundation proves illusory.In world increasingly driven sophisticated technologies clarity coherence aren't just preferable—they're prerequisites survival courtroom



# Prosecution History Estoppel and the 'Three Signals' Requirement: Distinguishing Disavowal from Clarification

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided yesterday by the Federal Circuit, Chief Judge Moore delivered a sharp rebuke to a patentee whose infringement theory collapsed under the weight of its own expert's contradictions and a fundamental misunderstanding of the accused technology's mathematical operations. While the decision results in a straightforward reversal of a $166 million damages award due to insufficient evidence of infringement, the court's handling of the '775 patent offers a sophisticated lesson in prosecution history estoppel. The Federal Circuit drew a critical line between surrendering claim scope regarding the *number* of unique input signals versus the *mechanism* by which those signals are processed. By rejecting AT&T's argument that Finesse had disavowed two-signal systems while simultaneously holding that no reasonable jury could find the seven claimed multiplications were performed, the court provided a nuanced roadmap for how amendments made to overcome prior art should be interpreted—not as broad surrenders of subject matter, but as precise clarifications of the claimed invention's boundaries.

The dispute centered on patents related to mitigating passive intermodulation (PIM) interference in wireless radios. For the '775 patent, the crux of the infringement analysis turned on Claim 1, which requires generating interference cancellation signals by digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" through seven specific third-order multiplications (e.g., $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$). During prosecution, Finesse amended its claims to specify "three signals" rather than "two or three signals" to distinguish its invention from the *Filipovic* prior art reference, which utilized only two inputs. AT&T argued that this amendment constituted a clear and unambiguous disavowal of any system utilizing fewer than three unique input signals. Since the accused Nokia radios reportedly used only two unique inputs ($x_1$ and $x_2$), AT&T contended infringement was impossible as a matter of law.

Chief Judge Moore's opinion carefully dismantles AT&T's estoppel argument by focusing on the rationale behind Finesse's amendment. The court noted that while Finesse did amend the claims to require "three signals," the primary distinction drawn against *Filipovic* was not merely numerical but functional: *Filipovic* addressed active IMPs, whereas Finesse's invention targeted passive IMPs. The Federal Circuit emphasized that for an amendment to act as a disavowal of claim scope, the patentee's statement must be "clear and unambiguous." Here, the record showed that Finesse intended to require three *separately identifiable* signals for the multiplication process, not necessarily three *unique* physical inputs. A system could theoretically use two unique inputs where one is copied or processed to serve as a third identifiable signal in the chain. Consequently, the court held that Finesse did not surrender coverage over products with two unique inputs, provided those inputs could be manipulated to satisfy the "three separately identifiable signals" limitation. This distinction preserves valuable claim territory for patentees who amend claims to overcome specific prior art teachings without intending to gut their protection against equivalent architectures.

However, saving the claim construction did not save the verdict. Even accepting that two unique inputs could theoretically satisfy the "three signals" requirement if properly processed, the evidence failed to show that the accused devices actually performed the requisite seven multiplications defined in the claim. Dr. Wells, Finesse's expert, relied on a Nokia technical document that depicted only three distinct multiplication operations involving modulus and complex conjugates (e.g., $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$). He offered no testimony mapping these three operations to the seven specific permutations required by the patent (such as $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$). The gap between identifying three distinct mathematical steps in an accused product and proving the performance of seven specific claimed steps proved fatal. The court rightly observed that Finesse's attempt on appeal to retrofit a mapping—suggesting that variations like complex conjugates counted toward the seven permutations—was unsupported by trial testimony and contradicted by the documentary evidence. Without evidence that all seven combinations were computed, literal infringement could not stand, regardless of how liberally one interpreted the "three signals" preamble.

The doctrinal implication of *Finesse* is significant for both litigators and prosecutors. For litigators defending against infringement accusations, this case reinforces that attacking an expert's failure to map every claim limitation remains a potent strategy, even when claim construction battles are lost. A favorable construction of "signals" cannot rescue a verdict if the accused device simply does not perform every step of the claimed algorithm. For patent drafters and prosecutors, the decision serves as a cautionary tale about precision during office actions. Had Finesse explicitly stated during prosecution that "three signals" meant "three unique physical inputs," they might have secured a stronger estoppel argument against AT&T—but at cost of narrowing their own scope conversely had they been clearer about their intent regarding signal copying they might have avoided ambiguity entirely More importantly it highlights that amendments are scrutinized contextually; courts will look at *why* an amendment was made before declaring what scope was surrendered If an amendment distinguishes based on function rather than structure expect courts to preserve structural equivalents unless explicitly disclaimed 

Ultimately *Finesse Wireless* illustrates that winning a Markman hearing is often just half battle The Federal Circuit demonstrated willingness separate questions interpretation from questions application refusing let favorable construction compensate lack evidentiary foundation With vacatur damages award remand likely enter judgment noninfringement outright case stands reminder robust technical proof essential element successful patent litigation especially complex telecommunications arena where mathematics governs liability



# Mapping Mathematical Claims to Reality: The Fatal Flaw in Equating Distinct Operations with Claimed Multiplications

In the high-stakes arena of wireless telecommunications litigation, patent holders often face the daunting task of mapping abstract mathematical claim limitations onto the concrete, proprietary architecture of accused devices. When those claims specify precise algebraic operations, the margin for error in infringement theory shrinks dramatically. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided September 24, 2025, the Federal Circuit delivered a stark reminder that substantial evidence requires more than a plausible narrative; it demands a rigorous, step-by-step correspondence between the claim language and the accused product's actual operation. Reversing the Eastern District of Texas's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), Chief Judge Moore's opinion dismantles an infringement verdict rooted in confused expert testimony and mathematically unsupported mappings, particularly regarding the specific multiplication requirements of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "'775 patent").

The case centered on technologies designed to mitigate passive intermodulation (PIM) interference in radio systems. While the court addressed two patents, the analysis of the '775 patent offers the most potent lesson for software and signal processing litigants. Claim 1 of the '775 patent is remarkably granular, requiring a method that generates interference cancellation signals by digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to produce exactly seven specific third-order products (e.g., $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$, etc.). At trial, Finesse's expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, attempted to prove infringement by pointing to a Nokia technical document showing the accused radios performing only three distinct multiplications involving modulus and complex conjugate operations. The jury accepted this theory, but the Federal Circuit found that no reasonable juror could bridge the gap between three observed operations and seven claimed multiplications without engaging in impermissible speculation.

The core failure in Finesse's case was the lack of evidentiary support for equating distinct mathematical operations. The accused devices performed operations like $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$ and $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x'_2$ (where $x'_2$ denotes a complex conjugate). Dr. Wells testified that these three distinct calculations satisfied the claim's requirement for seven multiplications. However, he offered no testimony explaining how three operations could legally or technically constitute seven separate instances of multiplication as defined by the claim language. The Federal Circuit rightly noted that the intrinsic evidence—the claim itself—lists seven unique combinations of three signals. To find infringement, there must be evidence that the accused device performs each of those seven specific computations. The court rejected the notion that performing a subset of operations, or operations that are mathematically related but structurally different (involving modulus rather than direct signal multiplication), could satisfy the limitation. By failing to present evidence that the accused device actually executed all seven claimed multiplications, Finesse left a fatal hole in its prima facie case.

Perhaps even more damaging to Finesse's position was its attempt to cure these deficiencies on appeal through arguments never presented to the jury. During oral argument before the Federal Circuit, Finesse pivoted, suggesting that Nokia's documentation actually revealed ten multiplications performed by the device, implying that a subset of those ten must match the seven claimed steps. Chief Judge Moore swiftly shut down this post-hoc rationalization. The appellate court emphasized that infringement must be proven based on evidence introduced at trial. Since neither Dr. Wells nor Finesse's counsel ever mapped these hypothetical ten multiplications to the seven claimed steps for the jury, nor explained which specific subset constituted infringement, this new theory was procedurally barred and factually unsupported. This reinforces a bedrock principle of federal practice: an appellant cannot manufacture a new evidentiary basis for liability after an adverse verdict simply because their original theory collapsed under cross-examination.

The decision also touches upon important nuances regarding prosecution history estoppel, though ultimately siding with Finesse on that narrow point before striking them down on evidentiary grounds. AT&T argued that Finesse had surrendered coverage of devices using only two unique input signals by amending their claims during prosecution to specify "three signals" to distinguish over prior art. The Federal Circuit disagreed, noting that Finesse's amendments were driven by distinctions between active and passive IMP mitigation rather than a clear disavowal of two-signal inputs where those inputs are manipulated to create three "separately identifiable" signals. While this preserved the theoretical scope of the claim against an enablement-style attack, it proved pyrrhic. Even if the claim covers devices with two physical inputs generating three logical signals, Finesse still failed to prove those logical signals underwent the seven specific multiplications required by limitation 1(d). The breadth of claim construction cannot compensate for a total absence of proof regarding individual claim elements.

This opinion serves as a critical cautionary tale for litigators handling complex software and algorithmic patents. In fields where invention lies in specific mathematical transformations, general assertions of functional equivalence are insufficient. Litigants must ensure their experts provide a granular mapping that accounts for every variable and operator specified in highly detailed method claims. Relying on diagrams that show "similar" processing flows without verifying the exact algebraic steps invites disaster at the JMOL stage. Furthermore, *Finesse* underscores that appellate courts will not act as a second jury to piece together fragmented testimony or accept new theories developed in briefing rooms long after the trial has concluded. When a claim demands seven distinct multiplications and the evidence shows only three fundamentally different operations, the gap is not one for a jury to fill—it is a legal bar to liability.
