Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the 'substantial evidence' standard apply when an expert witness gives contradictory testimony regarding claim element mapping?
Question:
In *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*, the Federal Circuit reversed the jury's infringement verdict for the '134 patent because the plaintiff's expert, Dr. Wells, gave confusing and self-contradictory testimony about which signals mapped to the 'interference generating signals' limitation. The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to say that self-contradictory testimony isn't enough to support a verdict. 

My confusion is: Usually, credibility determinations are strictly for the jury. At what point does an expert's inconsistency become so severe that an appellate court can say 'no reasonable jury' could have believed them, effectively overriding the jury's role as fact-finder? Is there a specific threshold for when contradictory testimony fails the substantial evidence test?
Answer:
There is no bright-line numerical threshold for when contradictory testimony fails the "substantial evidence" standard. Instead, the Federal Circuit applies a functional test: **does the contradiction leave the record devoid of any rational basis for the jury's finding?**

While credibility determinations are indeed the province of the jury, an appellate court can override those determinations when the expert's testimony is so internally inconsistent that it fails to prove an essential element of the claim as a matter of law. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court illustrates this principle by distinguishing between testimony that is merely "confusing" (which a jury might resolve) and testimony that is logically impossible or entirely unmapped to the claim limitations.

### The Legal Standard: *Johns Hopkins* and Logical Impossibility

The controlling precedent cited in *Finesse* is *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008). The doctrine established there—and applied in *Finesse*—is that when a party with the burden of proof relies exclusively on an expert who offers self-contradictory opinions regarding a critical claim limitation, no reasonable jury could rely on that testimony to find infringement.

This exception to the general rule of jury deference arises when:
1.  The expert explicitly maps evidence to a claim element;
2.  That mapping is shown to be physically or logically impossible (e.g., sampling a signal before it exists);
3.  The expert attempts to pivot to a new theory but fails to explicitly map the new evidence to the claim language; and
4.  The resulting record contains no coherent thread linking the accused product to every step of the patented method.

### Application in *Finesse Wireless*

In *Finesse*, Dr. Wells' testimony regarding the '134 patent failed this test because his contradiction created a logical vacuum regarding the "interference generating signals" limitation.

First, Dr. Wells consistently testified that the "modeled PIM path" constituted the "interference generating signals." However, under cross-examination, it became undeniable that the modeled PIM path is generated *downstream* of the RF ADC (the component responsible for sampling). As the court noted, "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated." Therefore, if Dr. Wells' initial mapping was correct, infringement was physically impossible.

Second, when confronted with this impossibility, Dr. Wells attempted to shift his theory, suggesting he was actually referring to input signals "x1 and x2." Crucially, however, he **never explicitly testified** that x1 and x2 mapped to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." He continued to refer to the "modeled PIM path" as the interference signal even while discussing x1 and x2.

The Federal Circuit held that this was not a simple credibility dispute for the jury to resolve. Because Dr. Wells never provided a clear, non-contradictory mapping of x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms, and his primary mapping was logically impossible, there was **"no clear or detailed explanation"** upon which a reasonable jury could base a verdict. The court emphasized that requiring a jury to piece together fragments of testimony from ninety pages apart to infer a mapping that the expert himself refused to articulate clearly exceeds the bounds of substantial evidence.

### Conclusion

The threshold for overturning a jury verdict based on contradictory expert testimony is met when the contradictions render the evidence **legally insufficient** rather than just debatable. If an expert's primary theory is disproven by physical laws or undisputed facts, and their alternative theory lacks explicit linkage to the claim language, an appellate court may conclude that "no reasonable jury" could have found infringement without engaging in speculation. In such cases, granting Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) does not usurp the jury's role; rather, it enforces the requirement that substantial evidence must exist in the record to support every element of the verdict.

### Does amending a claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' constitute prosecution history estoppel against two unique input signals?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, the defendant argued that Finesse surrendered claim scope covering devices with only two unique input signals because they amended the claim during prosecution to require 'three signals' to overcome a prior art reference (Filipovic) that used two inputs. However, the court held this wasn't a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal under *Continental Circuits*, noting the amendment was also based on distinguishing active vs. passive IMPs.

I'm struggling with the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel here. If an applicant narrows language to avoid a specific prior art reference, doesn't that automatically surrender the territory covered by that reference? How does the court distinguish between an amendment made to overcome a rejection versus one that acts as a general disclaimer of scope?
Answer:
No, amending a claim from "two or three signals" to "three signals" does not automatically constitute prosecution history estoppel (or disclaimer) against a device using only two *unique* input signals, provided the amendment was not made for reasons related to the number of unique inputs.

Under Federal Circuit law, a patentee surrenders claim scope during prosecution only if they make a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of that scope. *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019). This standard is exceptionally high; mere narrowing amendments are insufficient to trigger estoppel unless the reason for the amendment clearly indicates an intent to exclude the disputed subject matter.

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court addressed your exact scenario regarding the '775 patent. During prosecution, Finesse amended its claims to require "three signals" rather than the original "two or three signals" to overcome an obviousness rejection based on the *Filipovic* prior art reference, which utilized two inputs. The appellant argued this amendment surrendered coverage for devices with only two unique inputs.

However, the Federal Circuit rejected this argument because the record showed that Finesse distinguished its invention from *Filipovic* primarily on the basis of **active versus passive** intermodulation products (IMPs), not merely the count of unique input signals. The court noted that while Finesse amended the language to specify "three signals," the intrinsic evidence and arguments presented to the examiner demonstrated that the amendment was intended to require three *separately identifiable* signals, not necessarily three *unique* source signals.

Because Finesse's argument to the examiner focused on the nature of the IMPs (passive vs. active) and did not explicitly concede that two unique inputs were incapable of producing three separately identifiable signals (e.g., by using copies), there was no "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of systems with two unique inputs. Consequently, the amendment did not estop Finesse from asserting infringement against a device using two unique inputs to generate three separately identifiable signals for multiplication.

**Key Takeaway:** An amendment made to overcome a specific prior art reference does not act as a general disclaimer of all features shared with that reference. Estoppel applies only where the patentee's reasoning for the amendment unmistakably surrenders the specific territory now at issue. Here, since the distinction drawn was regarding the *type* of interference (passive/active) rather than strictly limiting the *number of unique sources*, the scope covering two unique inputs remained intact.

*(Note: While Finesse avoided estoppel on this specific issue, they ultimately lost on appeal because they failed to present substantial evidence that the accused device performed all seven claimed multiplications, regardless of how many input signals were used.)*

### Can a plaintiff rely on post-trial arguments or new mappings on appeal to cure a failure of proof regarding literal infringement?
Question:
For the '775 patent, the claim required seven specific multiplications. The expert only identified three distinct multiplications in the accused product and failed to explain how those three mapped to the seven claimed steps. On appeal, Finesse tried to argue new theories about subsets of ten multiplications found in Nokia documents that were never presented to the jury.

The court rejected this, stating the evidence wasn't presented to the jury. Does this mean that if an expert fails to provide a complete step-by-step mapping at trial, the plaintiff is absolutely barred from fixing that gap on appeal? Or could this have been saved if framed as an argument about inherent functionality rather than new evidence?
Answer:
Yes, under the reasoning in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, a plaintiff is effectively barred from curing a failure of proof regarding literal infringement on appeal by introducing new mappings or theories that were not presented to the jury.

The Federal Circuit's decision reinforces the principle that appellate review of a jury verdict is limited to the **substantial evidence** presented at trial. If an expert fails to provide a complete step-by-step mapping of every claim limitation to the accused product during trial, that gap constitutes a failure of proof that cannot be remedied by post-trial arguments or new interpretations of the evidence on appeal.

### The Legal Standard: Substantial Evidence and the Role of the Expert
In patent infringement cases, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving every element of the asserted claims. When relying on expert testimony to demonstrate literal infringement, that testimony must clearly map the accused product's features to each specific claim limitation. As the court noted, "A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial."

If the expert's testimony is contradictory, incomplete, or fails to address specific limitations (such as the seven required multiplications in the '775 patent), the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law. The jury cannot be expected to "piece together" a theory of infringement from ambiguous statements or fill in gaps left by the expert.

### Application in *Finesse*
The court's analysis of the '775 patent provides a direct answer to your question. The claim required **seven specific multiplications**. At trial, Finesse's expert, Dr. Wells:
1.  Identified only **three** distinct multiplications in the accused product based on a Nokia technical document.
2.  Failed to explain how those three multiplications mapped to or satisfied all seven claimed steps.
3.  Did not testify regarding any repetition of operations that would account for the missing steps.

On appeal, Finesse attempted to salvage its case by arguing:
*   That a subset of **ten** multiplications found in Nokia documents (never fully analyzed or mapped by the expert at trial) actually contained the required seven steps.
*   New theories about how signals like complex conjugates and moduli could be interpreted to fit the claim language.

The Federal Circuit rejected these arguments outright, stating: **"Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury."** The court emphasized that neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells had explained at trial which subset of the ten multiplications corresponded to the claimed seven, nor had they provided a valid mapping from the evidence to the claim requirements. Because no reasonable jury could have inferred infringement from the sparse and unmapped evidence presented, Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) was appropriate.

### Inherent Functionality vs. New Evidence
You asked if framing the argument as "inherent functionality" rather than new evidence would change the outcome. Based on *Finesse*, likely not, if doing so still requires establishing facts not placed before the jury.

"Inherence" is not a magic wand that allows a plaintiff to bypass its burden of production at trial. To prove infringement via inherence, there must still be evidentiary support—typically expert testimony or documentary proof—establishing that the accused product necessarily performs the claimed function. In *Finesse*, there was simply **no evidence** in the record showing that seven multiplications occurred; there was only evidence of three distinct ones and raw data listing ten potential operations without context or mapping provided by an expert during cross-examination or direct examination regarding those specific counts and combinations (Opinion Section I.B).

### What is the procedural difference between waiving an argument at Rule 50(a) versus failing to present sufficient evidence for a specific claim construction?
Question:
In the '775 patent analysis, the district court noted that AT&T waived their argument that 'S1, S2, and S3' must be *unique* signals because they didn't raise it properly in their Rule 50(a) JMOL motion. However, the Federal Circuit still reversed based on the lack of evidence for the *seven multiplications* requirement.

I am confused about the preservation rules here. If a defendant fails to preserve a specific claim construction argument (like uniqueness), can they still win on JMOL by attacking a different aspect of the same claim (like the number of mathematical operations)? How narrowly must a JMOL motion be drafted to preserve all possible grounds for reversal?
Answer:
You have identified a nuanced but critical distinction in federal civil procedure: the difference between **waiving a legal theory** due to procedural defects in a Rule 50(a) motion and **prevailing on JMOL** because the plaintiff failed to meet their burden of proof on an element that *was* properly preserved.

Based on *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the answer to your question is **yes**: A defendant can fail to preserve a specific claim construction argument (resulting in waiver of that specific theory) yet still win on JMOL by attacking a different, preserved aspect of the same claim where the evidence is legally insufficient.

Here is how the opinion illustrates this distinction:

### 1. The Waived Argument: Claim Construction ("Unique" Signals)
For the '775 patent, AT&T argued that the claim term "three signals S1, S2, and S3" required the signals to be **unique**. If accepted, this construction would mean the accused device (which used only two unique signals) could not infringe as a matter of law.

However, the Federal Circuit affirmed the district court's finding that this argument was **waived**. Under Fifth Circuit law (applicable here), a party must raise a specific argument in their Rule 50(a) motion to preserve it for appeal. Because AT&T failed to properly articulate this specific claim construction challenge at the Rule 50(a) stage, they were barred from arguing on appeal that the district court's construction was wrong or that the device failed under that specific construction.
> *Opinion Reference:* "At JMOL, the district court rejected Appellants' argument that 'S1, S2 and S3' must be unique signals as waived because Appellants failed to raise it at the Rule 50(a) stage."

### 2. The Prevailing Argument: Sufficiency of Evidence (Seven Multiplications)
Despite losing the battle on claim construction, AT&T won the war on infringement for the '775 patent by attacking a different limitation: the requirement that the device perform **seven specific multiplications**.

This argument was **not waived** because it went to the sufficiency of the evidence supporting the jury's verdict under the *existing* (uncontested) claim construction. Even accepting the district court's broader construction (that signals need not be unique), AT&T argued—and proved—that no reasonable jury could find infringement because Finesse's expert failed to provide evidence that the accused device actually performed all seven claimed mathematical operations.

The Federal Circuit agreed, noting that Finesse's expert only identified three distinct multiplications in the accused device and failed to map them to the seven required by the claim. Since Finesse bore the burden of proof and provided no substantial evidence for this essential element, JMOL was appropriate regardless of the waived claim construction argument.
> *Opinion Reference:* "We agree, however, that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications... Under these circumstances, no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications."

### Practical Takeaway for Drafting Rule 50(a) Motions
Your confusion regarding how narrowly a motion must be drafted is well-founded. *Finesse* highlights two key principles for preserving arguments:

1.  **Specificity for Legal Theories:** If you want to argue that a claim term should be construed differently than the court ruled, or that a specific factual scenario is excluded by a proper construction, you must explicitly state that legal theory in your Rule 50(a) motion. General assertions of "non-infringement" are often insufficient to preserve novel claim construction challenges.
2.  **Sufficiency Attacks are Broader:** You do not necessarily need to list every single evidentiary gap in your Rule 50(a) motion to argue insufficiency later. If you move for JMOL on non-infringement generally, you preserve the argument that *any* essential element lacks substantial evidence. As long as you put the plaintiff on notice that you believe their case is factually deficient, you can point out specific missing links (like the missing four multiplications) on appeal, even if you didn't detail exactly which math operations were missing in your initial motion.

In short, AT&T lost their chance to redefine "three signals," but they successfully used their preserved general non-infringement motion to show that Finesse simply didn't prove what happened inside black box—even under Finesse's own definition of terms.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the entire damages award instead of remanding for a recalculation based on valid claims?
Question:
The opinion states: 'Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims... we vacate the damages award.' The jury had awarded a lump sum for both patents combined ($166M).

Since every single asserted claim across both patents was found non-infringed due to lack of substantial evidence, I understand why there are no damages. But hypothetically, if only some claims were invalidated or found non-infringed while others stood, would the Federal Circuit still vacate a lump-sum award entirely? How do courts handle lump-sum damages when partial infringement findings are overturned on appeal?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit vacated the entire damages award because it reversed the finding of infringement for **every single asserted claim** across both patents-in-suit. Consequently, there was no legal basis left for any damages award.

To address your hypothetical: If the court had found that *some* claims were infringed while others were not, the outcome regarding the lump-sum award would likely differ, but the handling of such a scenario depends heavily on whether the jury's verdict allows for apportionment between the valid/infringed claims and the invalid/non-infringed ones.

### The Legal Standard: Lump-Sum Awards and Indivisibility

In patent cases, when a jury awards a single lump sum for multiple patents or multiple claims without specifying how much is attributed to each, the award is considered "indivisible." 

If an appellate court overturns the infringement finding for only a subset of those claims, it creates a problem: The reviewing court cannot determine what portion of the lump sum was attributable to the claims that were properly found infringed versus those that were erroneously found infringed. 

Under Federal Circuit precedent (which guides this analysis even though not explicitly cited in this specific short damages section), when a lump-sum award rests on a mix of correct and incorrect liability findings, and the record provides no way to disentangle them, the typical remedy is to **vacate the damages award and remand for a new trial on damages**. This ensures the patentee receives compensation only for the actual infringement proven, based on a fresh determination by a jury instructed on the surviving claims.

However, in *Finesse*, this complexity did not arise because **no claims survived**.

### Application to *Finesse Wireless*

The opinion is explicit about the scope of its reversal:
> "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for **all asserted claims**... we vacate the damages award."

The court analyzed two patents:
1.  **The '134 Patent:** The court reversed infringement for all asserted claims because Finesse's expert provided contradictory testimony regarding whether the accused devices sampled "interference generating signals" before they were generated—a physical impossibility. Without substantial evidence for any claim of this patent, zero damages could be attributed to it.
2.  **The '775 Patent:** The court reversed infringement for all asserted claims because Finesse failed to present evidence that the accused devices performed the required "seven claimed multiplications." The expert identified only three distinct multiplications and offered no mapping to the seven required by the claim language.

Since the jury's $166 million lump sum was predicated entirely on the infringement of these specific assertions, and *none* of those assertions stood upon review, there was no remaining foundation for *any* damages. There was no need to ask whether the award could be split or recalculated because there were no surviving findings of liability to support even a reduced calculation.

### Answering Your Hypothetical

If, hypothetically, the Federal Circuit had affirmed infringement on one claim of the '775 patent while reversing everything else:
*   They almost certainly would **not** have simply calculated a lower number themselves. Appellate courts do not act as fact-finders to recalculate damages based on new theories or reduced scopes.
*   Because the original verdict was a lump sum covering both patents and all claims, it would be impossible to know how much of that $166 million was intended for the single surviving claim.
*   In that scenario, standard practice dictates vacating the award and **remanding** to the district court for a new trial limited solely to determining damages for that single surviving claim (or potentially allowing parties to stipulate to a number if possible, though usually a retrial is necessary).

In summary, *Finesse* represents the cleanest case for vacation: total failure of proof on liability eliminates the need for remand instructions on apportionment because there is nothing left to compensate.

### How does the court treat technical diagrams in prior art or product documentation when an expert's verbal testimony conflicts with their own annotations?
Question:
A major issue in this case was Dr. Wells pointing to a diagram with a red path labeled 'DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path' and insisting these were two separate signals entering the ADC, even though physics dictates one of those paths is generated *after* sampling. He later mumbled about 'x1 and x2' but never clearly re-mapped them.

When an expert's verbal testimony seems physically impossible or contradicts the plain meaning of a technical document they are citing, how much weight should a jury theoretically give to their interpretation? Does the 'light most favorable to the verdict' standard protect an expert's bizarre interpretation of a diagram as long as they say it with confidence?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the short answer to your question is **no**. The "light most favorable to the verdict" standard does not protect an expert's interpretation when that interpretation is internally contradictory, physically impossible, and fails to map the evidence to the specific limitations of the patent claims.

In this case, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) specifically because Dr. Wells' testimony regarding the technical diagrams was too confused and inconsistent to constitute "substantial evidence."

### The Legal Standard: Substantial Evidence vs. Contradictory Testimony
While appellate courts generally view evidence in the light most favorable to the jury's verdict, this deference has limits. A verdict cannot stand if it relies on an expert's testimony that is self-contradictory or fails to address essential claim elements. As Chief Judge Moore wrote, "When the party with the burden of proof... rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard." The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to support the principle that no reasonable jury can find a limitation met based on contradictory opinions.

### Application to Dr. Wells' Diagram Interpretation
The court identified several fatal flaws in how Dr. Wells interpreted the Nokia technical diagram, which prevented his testimony from supporting the verdict:

**1. Failure to Map Testimony to Claim Limitations**
The core issue was not just that Dr. Wells was confused, but that he never successfully connected his changing story to the actual words of the patent claims.
*   **Initial Theory:** Dr. Wells explicitly mapped the "DL (TX) reference" to "signals of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" to "interference generating signals."
*   **The Physical Impossibility:** Cross-examination revealed that under this mapping, infringement was impossible because the "modeled PIM path" is generated *after* the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) samples the signal. Therefore, the ADC could not be sampling the "interference generating signals" as required by Claim 1[a].
*   **The Pivot:** When confronted with this impossibility, Dr. Wells vaguely mentioned "x1 and x2" as two signals on the red path. However, crucially, **he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claimed "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals."** He continued to insist throughout cross-examination that the relevant signals were still the DL (TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.

**2. The Jury Cannot Infer a Missing Link**
Finesse argued on appeal that Dr. Wells was simply laboring under a misimpression and actually meant x1 and x2 all along. The Federal Circuit rejected this, noting that inferring this connection required piecing together fragments of testimony from ninety pages apart in the record. The court stated there was "nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony" and that he offered "no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

Because Dr. Wells never affirmatively mapped x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms required for infringement, his mention of them did not cure the defect in his primary theory. The court held that this type of "confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict."

### Conclusion
An expert cannot save a deficient case by mumbling about alternative variables ("x1 and x2") without explicitly tying them back to the patent's claim language, especially when their primary explanation violates basic physics (sampling a signal before it exists). The Federal Circuit made it clear that when an expert's verbal testimony contradicts both physical reality and their own prior statements—and fails to bridge those contradictions with a clear mapping to the claims—the evidence is legally insufficient, regardless of how confidently it was delivered or how favorably one tries to view it post-verdict.
