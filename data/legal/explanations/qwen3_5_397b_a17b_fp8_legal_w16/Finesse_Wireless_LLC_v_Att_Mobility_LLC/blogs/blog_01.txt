Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Perils of Contradictory Expertise: Why Self-Impeaching Testimony Cannot Support a Jury Verdict

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, particularly within the technology sector, the line between a credible infringement theory and speculative storytelling often hinges on the precision of expert testimony. Yesterday, in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit drew that line sharply, reversing a district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and vacating a $166 million damages award. Writing for a unanimous panel, Chief Judge Moore delivered a potent reminder that while juries serve as the ultimate fact-finders, they cannot build a verdict on an expert's shifting sands. When a plaintiff's sole expert offers self-contradictory testimony regarding the fundamental architecture of an accused device, the evidence fails to meet the substantial evidence standard required to sustain a jury's finding of infringement.

The case centered on Finesse Wireless's assertions that AT&T and Nokia infringed patents related to mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. The dispute over U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 turned on a specific claim limitation requiring the accused radio to sample a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." To prove this element, Finesse relied entirely on the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who attempted to map these claim terms to a Nokia technical diagram. What followed was a forensic unraveling of Dr. Wells' methodology that ultimately doomed Finesse's case. Initially, Dr. Wells testified that the "interference generating signals" corresponded to a "modeled PIM path" shown in the diagram. However, under cross-examination, it became undeniable that this modeled path is generated digitally *after* the analog-to-digital conversion (sampling) occurs. Physically, the receiver cannot sample a signal that has not yet been created. This admission created a fatal logical gap: if the modeled PIM path is the interference signal, the claim limitation requiring pre-sampling presence cannot be met.

Rather than conceding the point or offering a coherent alternative, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot. He suggested that perhaps two other inputs labeled "x1" and "x2" on the diagram were the true signals of interest and interference generators. Yet, as Chief Judge Moore meticulously detailed, Dr. Wells never explicitly mapped x1 and x2 to the requisite claim terms during his testimony. Instead, he continued to refer to the "downlink TX reference" and the "modeled PIM path" as the relevant signals, creating a confusing tapestry of assertions that contradicted both physics and his own prior statements. The district court, attempting to salvage the verdict, inferred that Dr. Wells must have meant x1 and x2 all along, despite the lack of clear testimony supporting such a conclusion. The Federal Circuit rejected this judicial alchemy, holding that when a party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony without clarification, no reasonable jury could find infringement by substantial evidence.

This reasoning leans heavily on the precedent set in *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, where the court established that an expert's contradictory opinions can render evidence insufficient as a matter of law. The tension here lies between respecting the jury's province to weigh credibility and fulfilling the court's gatekeeping role to ensure legal sufficiency. Usually, inconsistencies in testimony are questions of weight for the jury to resolve; however, *Finesse* illustrates an exception where the inconsistency is so fundamental that it destroys the logical bridge between the evidence and the verdict. Dr. Wells did not merely offer a conflicting interpretation of data; he offered mutually exclusive physical realities regarding when and how signals were sampled. By failing to commit to a single, viable theory that satisfied every element of the claim, his testimony ceased to be substantive evidence and became mere speculation disguised as expertise.

The implications of *Finesse* extend well beyond this specific dispute over radio frequency interference. It signals a heightened scrutiny from appellate courts regarding "pivot" arguments—those last-minute theoretical shifts experts employ during cross-examination or rebuttal to patch holes in an infringement chart litigated at trial. Plaintiffs can no longer assume that if an expert mentions enough variables or pathways throughout hundreds of pages of transcript, a jury will be permitted to piece together a winning theory post-hoc. The opinion reinforces that infringement must be proven through clear mappings articulated during trial, not constructed by appellate briefs or judicial inference after a verdict is returned. For defense counsel in patent cases involving complex system architectures, *Finesse* provides robust ammunition for JMOL motions where plaintiff experts struggle to maintain consistency under rigorous cross-examination regarding signal flow and component interaction.

Ultimately, while Finesse also lost its battle regarding U.S.P.N.O.'975 due to similar evidentiary gaps concerning mathematical multiplications—a separate failure where three distinct operations could not magically constitute seven—the reversal on damages flows directly from total non-infringement across all asserted claims ($0 base means $0 royalties).
