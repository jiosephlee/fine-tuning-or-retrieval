Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## A Narrow View of Prosecution Disclaimer, a Broad Demand for Trial Proof

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility* is notable less for any dramatic doctrinal shift than for the way it separates two questions that patent litigants often blur together: what the claims mean, and whether the plaintiff actually proved infringement at trial. On the claim-scope side, the court took a fairly restrained view of prosecution disclaimer, refusing to read Finesse’s amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals” as a clear and unambiguous surrender of systems that use only two unique inputs. But that doctrinal moderation did not save the verdict. The court still reversed because the trial record never established that the accused radios performed the specific seven multiplications the claims required.

That combination makes the case a useful reminder for patent trial strategy. A party may win the construction fight and still lose the infringement verdict if its proof is too loose, too inconsistent, or too dependent on post hoc appellate gloss. And conversely, an accused infringer cannot assume that a prosecution-history argument will do the work of an evidentiary attack if the plaintiff’s proof is otherwise weak.

## The court’s restrained take on disclaimer

The most interesting doctrinal move in the opinion is what the court declined to do. AT&T and Nokia argued that Finesse’s prosecution history surrendered claim scope over products using only two unique signals, because the claims had been amended to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The Federal Circuit rejected that reading. It emphasized that prosecution disclaimer requires a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal, and it found the record did not rise to that level.

That is an important point. The panel acknowledged that Finesse had amended the claims in response to prior art and that the amendment added the “three signals” language. But it did not read that change as a sharp line excluding any implementation built from two unique inputs. Instead, the court accepted the district court’s view that the claim language required three “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily three distinct source signals in the sense urged by Appellants. In other words, the amendment narrowed the claim, but not in the fully exclusionary way AT&T and Nokia wanted.

Doctrinally, that is a fairly careful application of disclaimer law. The Federal Circuit has often said that prosecution history can inform meaning, but it should not be converted into an overbroad surrender rule. This opinion follows that path. The court did not treat a narrowing amendment as automatic proof of complete relinquishment of a related embodiment. That restraint matters because patent drafting and prosecution rarely produce perfect linguistic symmetry; if courts read every amendment as a full disavowal of adjacent territory, disclaimer would become far more expansive than the doctrine permits.

## But the trial proof still failed

The case did not turn on claim scope, though. It turned on proof. On the ’775 patent, the court accepted that Finesse had not clearly surrendered two-signal embodiments, but it still found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications.

Here the court was much less forgiving. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical document, but the panel concluded the document showed only three distinct multiplications, not the seven required by the claim. More importantly, Dr. Wells never clearly mapped those three multiplications onto the seven claimed operations. Finesse tried on appeal to reconstruct a mapping from other parts of the record, but the panel correctly rejected that effort because appellate briefing cannot supply what the jury never actually heard. The opinion is especially pointed on this score: where the expert’s explanation is incomplete and the document itself does not show the claimed sequence, the verdict cannot stand.

The court’s treatment of the ’134 patent is even more striking in that respect. There, the dispute centered on whether the accused radios sampled the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The Federal Circuit found Dr. Wells’ testimony internally inconsistent: at times he mapped those signals to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, but that mapping made little sense because the modeled PIM path was generated after the sampling step. When pressed, he pivoted to a different explanation, but never clearly tied x1 and x2 to the claim language. The court leaned on *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope* to hold that a self-contradictory expert opinion cannot carry the plaintiff’s burden.

That is a demanding evidentiary standard, but not an unreasonable one. The law does not require perfect testimony, but it does require a coherent path from the accused product to each claim limitation. Here the expert’s testimony wandered between competing mappings without ever fully reconciling them. The Federal Circuit was unwilling to let the jury verdict rest on that ambiguity.

## The practical lesson: claim construction is not enough

The real downstream significance of *Finesse* may be strategic. Litigants often spend enormous resources fighting over claim construction and prosecution history, assuming that victory there will determine the case. This decision says otherwise. The Federal Circuit was relatively moderate on disclaimer, but it was exacting on proof. That means patentees cannot rely on broad, elastic expert narratives that only loosely track the claim elements. If a claim requires a particular sequence of operations, or a particular relationship among signals, the trial record must show exactly how the accused system satisfies that requirement.

For accused infringers, the lesson is complementary. A prosecution-disclaimer argument may still be worth pressing, but it should not be the only defense. Here, even without a sweeping disclaimer ruling, the defendants prevailed because they exposed gaps and contradictions in the infringement evidence. That is often the stronger route: challenge the plaintiff’s technical mapping directly, and make sure the record preserves the failure of proof.

## A broader doctrinal takeaway

The opinion also reflects the Federal Circuit’s recurring preference for keeping legal doctrines in their lanes. Disclaimer doctrine is about what the claims mean after prosecution; JMOL doctrine is about whether the evidence at trial was enough. The court resisted collapsing one into the other. It did not say the claims were limited to three unique signals, and it did not rewrite the claim language to fit the accused product. Instead, it asked whether the plaintiff had proved what the claims, as construed, required. It had not.

That separation is healthy. It preserves the claim-construction framework while reminding litigants that infringement is ultimately an evidentiary question. *Finesse* is therefore a useful case not because it expands disclaimer doctrine, but because it shows the Federal Circuit will be cautious about finding surrender while still demanding disciplined, element-by-element proof at trial.
