Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: Disposition, Damages Consequences, and Lessons from the Opinion

## The relationship between liability findings and the damages award

The end of the opinion is short because the court’s liability rulings do most of the work. The jury had returned a single, lump-sum damages award of more than $166 million based on its findings that the asserted claims of both patents were infringed. Once the Federal Circuit concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the asserted claims of either the ’134 patent or the ’775 patent, the foundation for the damages award disappeared. In a patent case, damages compensate for infringement; if the infringement verdict is undone, the damages award based on that verdict cannot stand.

That is why the court did not treat damages as an independent island of error requiring a separate merits analysis. The verdict form and the record showed that the award was tied to the successful infringement theories. When those theories failed as a matter of law, the damages award could not survive.

## Review of the district court’s denial of a new trial on damages under Fifth Circuit abuse-of-discretion standards

The opinion briefly notes the standard of review for the district court’s denial of a new trial. Unlike JMOL, which the Federal Circuit reviews through the lens of the regional circuit’s JMOL standard, a new-trial denial is reviewed for abuse of discretion under Fifth Circuit law. That is a deferential standard. It asks whether the district court made a clear error of judgment in denying a retrial.

But the court did not need to apply that standard to any damages-specific argument in detail. Because the infringement verdicts were reversed, the court had already determined that the judgment on liability could not stand. Once liability fell, there was no need to decide whether the district court also abused its discretion in refusing a new trial on damages. The damages issues became unnecessary to resolve independently.

## Why the Federal Circuit did not need to resolve the damages challenges independently after reversing infringement

This is a recurring appellate pattern in patent cases. A damages challenge may be raised in the alternative, but if the appellate court reverses the underlying liability determination, it often vacates the damages award without reaching the finer damages questions. That is what happened here. The court expressly stated that because it reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents, it vacated the damages award.

This means the court did not decide whether the jury had properly valued the infringement, whether the lump-sum calculation was methodologically sound, or whether a new trial on damages would otherwise have been warranted. Those questions became moot in light of the liability ruling.

## Vacatur of the entire damages award as a consequence of noninfringement JMOL

“Vacate” means the appellate court nullifies the damages award rather than merely reducing or modifying it. Here, vacatur was complete because the damages award was not separated by patent or claim in a way that allowed any portion to survive. The jury had awarded one lump sum for the remaining life of the patents, and the appellate court reversed the verdicts on all asserted claims. With no infringement verdict left intact, there was nothing to support any part of the award.

This is an important procedural point for reading patent opinions. A damages award is only as secure as the liability findings that support it. When the court wipes out the liability determination entirely, vacatur of damages follows as a consequence rather than as a separate substantive ruling.

## Formal disposition: reversed and vacated, with costs to appellants

The opinion ends with the formal disposition: “REVERSED AND VACATED.” That means the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award. The court also awarded costs to the defendants-appellants. The costs line is routine but significant in showing that AT&T and Nokia prevailed on appeal.

For a law student, the dispositive language is worth reading carefully. The court did not simply reverse on one patent and remand for recalculation. It reversed the noninfringement rulings and vacated damages entirely, signaling a complete defeat for the plaintiff’s trial judgment.

## The opinion’s practical lesson on precision in expert claim mapping

The core practical lesson of the case is that infringement proof in a technically complex case must be precise, consistent, and intelligible. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, tried to map claim language onto portions of Nokia technical diagrams, but his testimony became self-contradictory and ambiguous. The Federal Circuit treated that ambiguity as fatal because Finesse bore the burden of proving infringement, and the jury could not be asked to infer a clear claim-mapping theory that the expert never actually presented.

This is especially important in patent cases involving signal-processing or other systems with multiple paths, transformations, and labels. It is not enough for an expert to point to a diagram and say it “looks like” the claim. The expert must explain, in a way the jury can understand, how each claimed limitation corresponds to the accused system.

## The danger of relying on diagrams and labels without accounting for system timing and signal generation order

The ’134 patent portion of the opinion shows a classic timing problem. Dr. Wells initially mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, but the accused system generated that modeled PIM path only after the RF ADC sampled the incoming signals. That created a logical contradiction: a signal cannot be sampled before it exists.

The appellate court emphasized this timing mismatch. A diagram label or color-coding convention cannot substitute for proof that the accused system actually performs the claimed sequence. When the claim requires sampling of a passband containing certain signals, the plaintiff must show that those signals are present at the relevant point in time. The court refused to accept an infringement theory that ignored the order in which the system actually generated and processed signals.

## The difference between claim-construction success and evidentiary sufficiency at trial

The ’775 patent portion illustrates another important lesson: winning a claim-construction battle does not automatically win the infringement case. The district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily unique signals. That construction gave Finesse some room, but it did not answer the separate question whether the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications.

Appellate review of claim construction asks what the claim means. Review of infringement asks whether the accused device meets that meaning with evidence sufficient for a reasonable jury. Even if the construction is favorable, the plaintiff still must prove the accused system satisfies every limitation. Here, Finesse could not bridge the gap between the Nokia document and the specific seven-multiplication claim language.

## How the case illustrates that appellate courts will not reconstruct an infringement theory the expert did not clearly present to the jury

Perhaps the most important appellate lesson is that the Federal Circuit will not rescue a poorly articulated expert theory by reconstructing it from scattered record citations after the fact. Finesse tried on appeal to assemble a clearer mapping from the Nokia materials and Dr. Wells’s testimony, but the court refused to do that work for it. The court stressed that the supposed mapping was not actually presented to the jury in a clear, coherent form.

That matters because substantial-evidence review is deferential to the verdict, but it still requires a record basis that a reasonable jury could have relied on. If the expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent, or if the key claim mapping is never plainly explained, the verdict can fail as a matter of law. In other words, a patent plaintiff cannot expect the appellate court to supply the missing logic between a technical diagram and the claim language. The proof has to be made at trial.
