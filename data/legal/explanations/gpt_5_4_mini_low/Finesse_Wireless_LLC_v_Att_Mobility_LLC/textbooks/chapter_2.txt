Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Appellate Standards and the Structure of the Infringement Inquiry

## Why regional circuit law governs review of JMOL and new-trial rulings

This opinion is a useful reminder that the Federal Circuit does not apply its own procedural law to every issue in a patent appeal. When the appeal concerns denial of judgment as a matter of law, or denial of a new trial, the Federal Circuit borrows the law of the regional circuit in which the district court sits. Because this case came from the Eastern District of Texas, the court applied Fifth Circuit standards to review both the JMOL rulings and the motion for a new trial. The patent-specific issues—such as claim construction and whether the evidence supported a finding of infringement—still fall within Federal Circuit patent law, but the appellate lens for the post-trial motions comes from the regional circuit.

That division matters because it affects the degree of deference the appellate court gives the jury and the trial judge. The parties were not asking only whether the patents were infringed as a matter of patent law; they were also asking whether the district court should have taken the case away from the jury or ordered a new trial after the verdict. The court’s first task was therefore to identify which body of law controlled each step of review.

## Fifth Circuit standard for reviewing denial of JMOL de novo

Under Fifth Circuit law, denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo. That means the appellate court does not defer to the district court’s judgment on the legal sufficiency of the evidence. Instead, it independently asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, is so one-sided that reasonable jurors could not have reached the result they did.

The opinion quotes the Fifth Circuit formulation: reversal is proper only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party” that no reasonable jury could reach a contrary conclusion. This is a demanding standard. It does not permit the appellate court simply to prefer one side’s account of the evidence. The court must uphold the verdict if the record contains enough evidence for a reasonable jury to find for the prevailing party.

In a patent case, this standard works alongside the ordinary federal rule that a party can obtain JMOL only when the evidence is legally insufficient to support the verdict. Here, the Federal Circuit applied that framework to the jury’s infringement findings. The question was not whether the panel thought the accused products infringed on a clean slate, but whether the trial record could sustain the jury’s verdict under the governing standard.

## Substantial evidence review of a jury’s infringement finding

The court also states the familiar substantial-evidence standard for reviewing a jury’s infringement finding. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party on the evidence presented. In practice, this standard is closely related to the JMOL inquiry, but it is framed from the perspective of appellate review of a jury’s factfinding.

This is important because infringement is often a mixed question of law and fact. Claim construction is a question of law, but comparing the properly construed claims to the accused device is a factual matter for the jury when the evidence is disputed. In this case, the court was not revisiting every patent issue itself. It was asking whether the evidence the plaintiff presented at trial was enough for a reasonable jury to find that the accused radios met the claim limitations.

The opinion repeatedly shows how this standard operates. The panel does not merely say that Finesse’s evidence was weak. It says the evidence did not rise to the level of substantial evidence because the expert’s testimony did not reliably connect the accused products to the claim limitations. That distinction is key: a verdict can survive even if the losing party offers a persuasive alternative theory, so long as the prevailing party offered enough evidence for a reasonable factfinder. But if the record lacks a coherent evidentiary basis for a critical limitation, substantial evidence is absent.

## Claim construction review: de novo with clear-error review for subsidiary factfinding

Claim construction is reviewed de novo, but the court notes that any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. This is a standard Federal Circuit formulation after *Teva*: the meaning of claim terms is ultimately a legal question, but if the district court resolves subsidiary factual disputes—especially disputes grounded in extrinsic evidence—those factual determinations receive clear-error review.

This opinion uses that framework to set the stage for the infringement analysis, though the panel’s main dispute was not about claim construction itself. In the ’775 patent discussion, for example, the district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not “unique.” That construction shaped the infringement inquiry, because Appellants argued the accused radios used only two unique signals, while Finesse argued three separately identifiable signals were enough. The appellate court did not need to redo the claim construction exercise from scratch because the construction issue was not the central source of error on appeal. Instead, it focused on whether the trial evidence matched even the district court’s construction.

The lesson for a student is that claim construction often supplies the framework for infringement, but the appeal may turn on whether the evidence fits that framework. A court can accept the construction and still reverse if the plaintiff failed to prove that the accused product met the construed limitation.

## The plaintiff’s burden of proof on infringement at trial

Finesse, as the patent owner and plaintiff, bore the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. That burden never shifts simply because the case goes to a jury. The plaintiff must present evidence showing each claim limitation is met, either literally or under the applicable infringement theory.

The opinion’s infringement analysis is built around that burden. For the ’134 patent, the critical issue was whether the accused radios sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” For the ’775 patent, the key issue was whether the accused radios generated cancellation signals using the claimed seven multiplications. In both instances, the court’s question was not whether the defendants disproved infringement, but whether Finesse affirmatively proved the missing elements.

That point is especially important when the plaintiff’s case depends on expert testimony. Patent infringement cases often require expert explanation because the technology is complex. But complexity does not relax the plaintiff’s burden. The plaintiff still has to provide a coherent evidentiary path from the accused product to every claim limitation.

## How the court distinguishes between legal claim-scope questions and evidentiary sufficiency questions

A major feature of the opinion is the court’s effort to separate two different kinds of disputes. One is a legal dispute about claim scope: what the claim language means, or whether prosecution history narrowed the claim. The other is an evidentiary dispute: whether the trial record shows the accused product falls within that scope.

The court’s treatment of the ’775 patent illustrates the difference. Appellants argued that Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two-signal implementations during prosecution. That is a legal claim-scope argument. The court rejected it, concluding there was no clear and unambiguous disavowal of the accused scope. But that did not end the case. Even after resolving claim scope in Finesse’s favor, the court still asked whether the evidence showed the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications. On that second question, the court ruled against Finesse.

This separation helps explain the structure of the opinion. A claim can be broad enough as a matter of law and yet still not be infringed because the trial evidence is insufficient. A plaintiff must win both battles: the claim must cover the accused product, and the proof at trial must establish that coverage with competent evidence.

## The significance of expert testimony in proving infringement

Expert testimony played the central role in both patents. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, was the principal witness connecting the Nokia technical documents to the asserted claims. The court’s analysis shows how patent infringement cases often rise or fall on whether the expert can present a stable, intelligible theory of mapping claim language onto technical features of the accused product.

For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells attempted to map “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” onto components shown in a Nokia document. For the ’775 patent, he tried to map three signals and seven multiplications onto the operations described in another Nokia document. In each instance, the court focused closely on what the expert actually said, not on what Finesse later argued the testimony meant.

This is a common appellate theme in patent cases: expert testimony is not enough merely because it sounds technical. It must actually support the legal elements of infringement. If the expert’s explanation is inconsistent, incomplete, or disconnected from the claim language, the testimony may not amount to substantial evidence.

## Why self-contradictory expert testimony can fail as a matter of law

The court’s most important evidentiary point is that a verdict cannot rest on self-contradictory expert testimony when the contradictions prevent a reasonable jury from finding a claim limitation met. The opinion relies on *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* for the proposition that when the party with the burden of proof bases its case on an expert who contradicts himself on a critical limitation, no reasonable jury can be expected to resolve the inconsistency in the plaintiff’s favor.

That principle is central to both patent claims in this case. With the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells repeatedly mapped the relevant signals to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, but that mapping created a timing problem because the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the sampling step. When challenged, he shifted his language, but he never clearly re-mapped the claim terms to a stable alternative such as x1 and x2. The court treated that as a fatal inconsistency, not as harmless imprecision.

The same basic problem arose with the ’775 patent. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document, but the document did not plainly show the seven claimed multiplications. The testimony and the document did not line up cleanly, and Finesse’s later attempt to reconstruct the mapping on appeal could not cure the evidentiary gap. The appellate court therefore concluded that the verdict could not stand as a matter of law.

For a law student, the lesson is that appellate courts do not treat every expert inconsistency as a mere credibility issue reserved for the jury. If the contradictions leave the record without a reliable evidentiary basis for an essential claim element, the court may hold that substantial evidence is lacking and reverse JMOL denial.
