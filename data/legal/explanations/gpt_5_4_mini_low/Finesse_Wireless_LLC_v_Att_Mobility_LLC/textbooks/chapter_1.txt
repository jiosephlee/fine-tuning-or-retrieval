Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Case Setting, Technology, and Procedural Posture

## The parties and the dispute

This appeal arises out of a patent infringement suit brought by Finesse Wireless LLC against AT&T Mobility LLC. Finesse is the patent owner. Nokia of America Corporation intervened because the accused technology was Nokia equipment used in AT&T’s network, and AT&T and Nokia appealed together as the defendants-appellants. The case therefore centers on whether certain Nokia radios used by AT&T practiced the methods claimed in two Finesse patents, and if not, whether the district court should have taken the infringement issue away from the jury after trial.

The Federal Circuit’s opinion is not mainly about network contracts or damages modeling in the abstract. Its starting point is the accused radio technology and the specific claim language Finesse relied on. That means a law student reading the opinion should pay close attention to the relationship between the patent claims, the technical documentation, and the expert testimony at trial. The appeal turns on whether the evidence was enough to support the jury’s finding of infringement.

## The asserted patents

Finesse asserted claims from two patents: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775. Both patents concern ways of reducing interference caused by intermodulation products, or IMPs, in radios. Although the patents describe different methods, they share the same basic problem: unwanted signal mixing can create new signals that interfere with desired communications.

The ’134 patent, in broad terms, claims a method for isolating “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” estimating the resulting intermodulation products, and canceling them. The ’775 patent claims a method for generating intermodulation product cancellation signals in a receiver, using three signals and a set of multiplications described in the claim. The court’s later infringement analysis depends on whether the accused radios actually performed these claimed steps, as the claims require.

## Intermodulation products and passive intermodulation

The opinion explains the technology in a practical way. Radios are meant to operate on different frequencies so their signals do not interfere. But when signals encounter certain physical obstacles or imperfect components, they can mix and produce new frequencies. Those newly created frequencies are called intermodulation products, or IMPs. They are problematic because they can appear in-band, meaning within the frequency range of a desired signal, and can therefore disrupt communication.

When the mixing is caused by passive objects rather than active circuitry, the resulting interference is called passive intermodulation, or PIM. The opinion gives examples of passive causes such as metal fences, loose cable connections, and rusted connectors. This distinction matters because the patents are directed at canceling passive intermodulation, and the accused feature is a PIM cancellation function in Nokia radios.

For a student reading the opinion, the key point is that the patents do not merely concern generic signal processing. They target a particular interference problem in radio systems, and they claim specific signal-processing methods for identifying and canceling that interference.

## Internal PIM versus external PIM

The opinion also distinguishes between internal and external PIM. Internal PIM arises from mechanical issues inside the radio. External PIM comes from sources outside the radio. That distinction matters because the accused functionality involves how the radio system samples and models signals in relation to where the unwanted intermodulation is generated.

In the infringement analysis, the timing and location of signal generation become important. If a claimed “interference generating signal” is said to be sampled by the accused receiver, but the evidence shows that signal is not even generated until after sampling occurs, then the plaintiff may fail to prove infringement. That is exactly the kind of problem the Federal Circuit later identifies in its discussion of the ’134 patent.

## The accused feature: Nokia radios’ PIM cancellation functionality

Finesse accused AT&T of using Nokia radios that include a PIM cancellation, or PIM-C, feature. The theory was that the radios’ signal-processing operations corresponded to the patented methods. Finesse relied heavily on a Nokia technical document and on its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, to map the accused functionality onto the claim limitations.

That mapping task is central to the case. In patent cases, it is not enough to show that the accused device performs something generally similar to the invention. The plaintiff must prove that the accused product meets each claim limitation, either literally or under the proper infringement doctrine. Here, the dispute focused on whether the accused radios performed the specific signal sampling, signal isolation, and multiplication steps required by the claims.

## Trial outcome and damages award

The case was tried to a jury in the Eastern District of Texas. The jury found all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed. It also awarded Finesse a lump-sum damages amount of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents.

A lump-sum award means the jury selected a single amount intended to compensate for future use through the end of the patent term, rather than awarding a running royalty. That award became relevant only because the infringement findings were challenged after trial. Once the Federal Circuit concluded that the infringement verdicts could not stand, it vacated the damages award as well.

## Post-verdict motions

After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia filed several post-trial motions. They sought judgment as a matter of law, often abbreviated JMOL, on noninfringement and on damages. They also requested a new trial. A JMOL motion asks the court to enter judgment despite the jury verdict because the evidence, viewed in the required light, cannot support the verdict as a matter of law. A new trial motion asks the court to set aside the verdict for reasons such as evidentiary error, improper instructions, or a verdict against the great weight of the evidence.

The district court denied those requests. The defendants then appealed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and the denial of a new trial on damages. The Federal Circuit ultimately reversed the noninfringement rulings and, because there could be no damages award without infringement, vacated the damages award.

## Appellate jurisdiction and issues on appeal

The Federal Circuit had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), which gives it appellate jurisdiction over patent cases. On appeal, the court addressed two main issues: whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, and whether the damages award should be set aside.

The court’s procedural posture matters because it shapes the standard of review. The appeal was not a fresh trial of the facts. Instead, the court reviewed whether the trial evidence was legally sufficient to support the jury’s infringement findings. When a court reverses JMOL on noninfringement, as the Federal Circuit did here, the damages award cannot survive, so the separate damages challenge becomes largely moot in practical effect.
