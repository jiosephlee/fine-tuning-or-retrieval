Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: The ’134 Patent—Sampling Limitation, Expert Mapping, and Failure of Proof

## The ’134 Patent’s Objective: Reducing IMP Interference by Isolating and Cancelling Interference-Generating Signals

The ’134 patent is directed to a technique for reducing intermodulation product, or IMP, interference in radios. The basic idea is that when transmitted signals encounter obstacles or imperfections in the radio environment, they can combine and create new unwanted frequencies. Those unwanted frequencies are the intermodulation products. The patent’s solution is to identify the signals that generate those products, estimate the resulting interference, and then cancel it out. In other words, the patent claims a digital method for detecting the relevant signals, computing what interference they will produce, and subtracting that interference from the received signal path.

This case matters because the plaintiff, Finesse, had to prove not just that the accused radios performed some kind of interference cancellation, but that they performed the specific steps recited in the asserted claim. For the ’134 patent, the Federal Circuit focused on a threshold problem in the plaintiff’s proof: whether the accused radios sampled the required signals in the manner the claim demands.

## Representative Claim 1 and the Importance of Limitation 1[a]

Claim 1 is the representative claim for the analysis. The court centered its discussion on limitation 1[a], which requires “over-sampling” a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, where those received signals include both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The claim then proceeds to require isolation of those signals, computation of an estimate of the intermodulation products, cancellation of those products, and closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustment.

For infringement, this first limitation was crucial. If the accused radios did not sample a passband that actually included both categories of signals, then the infringement theory would fail at the outset. The court treated that limitation as a structural and temporal requirement: the claimed “interference generating signals” must be part of the signal set being sampled, not generated later in the processing chain.

## The Claim Requirement That the Receiver Sample Both Kinds of Signals

The court emphasized that Finesse, as the plaintiff, bore the burden of proving that the accused radios sampled a passband containing both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” That sounds straightforward, but in practice it required Finesse to connect the claim language to the actual operation of the accused Nokia radios in a precise way.

The problem was not simply whether the radios performed interference cancellation. It was whether the specific signals identified by Finesse were present at the claimed time and place in the accused architecture. Limitation 1[a] requires the receiver’s RF ADC to sample received signals that already include the interference-generating signals. If the asserted “interference generating signals” are created only later, after the sampling step, then the claim limitation is not met.

## Dr. Wells’s Reliance on Nokia Technical Documents

At trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, based his theory on Nokia technical documents showing how the accused radios functioned. This is common in patent cases: experts often use technical manuals, block diagrams, and design documents to map claim language onto an accused system. The expert’s job is to explain to the jury how the claim elements correspond to the accused device.

But the strength of that approach depends on the clarity and consistency of the mapping. Here, the Federal Circuit concluded that Dr. Wells’s testimony was not consistent enough to support the verdict.

## His Express Mapping of “Signals of Interest” to the DL(TX) Reference

Dr. Wells clearly and repeatedly identified the “signals of interest” as the downlink transmit reference, or DL(TX) reference, shown in the Nokia materials. He testified to that mapping more than once, both in live testimony and in his expert report. That part of the infringement theory was not the main point of dispute.

This matters because once an expert makes an express claim mapping, that testimony defines the theory the jury is asked to accept. Here, Dr. Wells tied one claim element to one signal path with specificity. The problem arose when he tried to do the same with the “interference generating signals.”

## His Express Mapping of “Interference Generating Signals” to the Modeled PIM Path

Dr. Wells also expressly mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path. That was the critical move in Finesse’s theory. The modeled PIM path was part of the Nokia documentation, and Dr. Wells treated it as the claim’s “interference generating signals.”

That mapping created a serious doctrinal and factual problem. If the modeled PIM path is the interference-generating signal, then the claim requires that signal to be present in the passband the receiver samples. But the accused architecture, as the defense pointed out on cross-examination, generated the modeled PIM path only after the RF ADC had already sampled the incoming signals. In other words, the alleged interference-generating signal was downstream from the sampling step, not upstream where the claim required it to be.

## The Critical Temporal and Architectural Problem

This is the core of the Federal Circuit’s reversal. Patent claims often impose a sequence of operations, and infringement requires the accused system to perform those operations in the required order. Here, the claim required sampling a received passband containing the interference-generating signals before later processing steps. But Dr. Wells’s own mapping placed the interference-generating signals in a modeled path generated after sampling.

That made the theory internally inconsistent. A signal cannot be sampled as part of the received passband if it is not generated until after the sampling occurs. The court treated that as a fatal flaw in the proof.

## Cross-Examination and the Attempted Pivot to x1 and x2

On cross-examination, Appellants pressed this point. They argued that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because the modeled PIM path was created downstream, after sampling. Faced with that objection, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony and said there were “two signals” on the red path, referring to x1 and x2 in the Nokia document.

That pivot might have mattered if Dr. Wells had clearly testified that x1 and x2, rather than the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, were the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” But he did not do that. Instead, he kept returning to the earlier mapping, saying the red path was the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. The result was ambiguity, not clarification.

## Why the Court Found the Pivot Unclear and Incomplete

The Federal Circuit was explicit that Dr. Wells never clearly tied x1 and x2 to the claim language. He referred to x1 and x2 as inputs, but he did not say with precision that x1 was the signal of interest and x2 was the interference-generating signal, or vice versa. Nor did he explain how the shift from the modeled PIM path to x1 and x2 affected the original mapping.

That omission mattered because the court was not willing to infer a new, workable infringement theory from scattered remarks. Patent infringement must be proven by evidence presented to the jury, not by appellate reconstruction after the fact. The expert’s testimony remained contradictory: he had expressly mapped the claims to one set of signals, then suggested another set without clearly substituting one for the other.

## The District Court’s Attempt to Infer a Workable Theory from Scattered Testimony

The district court tried to make sense of Dr. Wells’s testimony by inferring that when he referred to the red path, he must have meant x1 and x2. In effect, the court reconstructed a coherent story from fragments spread across the trial record. It concluded that the jury could find the RF ADC sampled both required categories of signals.

The Federal Circuit rejected that approach. It emphasized that the district court’s inference was not supported by clear testimony from Dr. Wells. The expert never gave the jury a clean explanation that aligned x1 and x2 with the claim terms. Instead, the district court had to piece together statements made at different points in the record, some of them many pages apart, to create a consistent theory.

## The Federal Circuit’s Rejection of Inferential Reconstruction

The appellate court held that this kind of inferential reconstruction could not sustain the verdict. The key problem was that the plaintiff’s own expert was the only source connecting the accused device to the claim limitation, and his testimony was self-contradictory. The court was not persuaded that the jury could reasonably rely on a theory the expert never clearly presented.

The Federal Circuit stressed that the evidence must support the specific infringement theory actually offered at trial. If the expert’s testimony is so confused that the jury would have to guess which signal corresponds to which claim element, then the plaintiff has not met its burden. The court viewed Dr. Wells’s testimony as falling into that category.

## Johns Hopkins v. Datascope and Contradictory Expert Testimony

To explain why the verdict could not stand, the court relied on Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. In that case, the court had held that when an expert’s testimony is internally contradictory, no reasonable jury could rely on it to find literal infringement of the challenged limitation. The principle is not that an expert must be flawless, but that a verdict cannot rest on testimony that changes so materially that it no longer supports a coherent infringement theory.

That precedent fit this case closely. Dr. Wells repeatedly said the claimed signals were the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, but the modeled PIM path could not have been sampled as claimed because it was created after the RF ADC operated. His later references to x1 and x2 did not cure the problem because he never clearly mapped those values to the claim elements. The court thus treated the testimony as insufficient under the substantial-evidence standard.

## Holding as to the ’134 Patent: No Substantial Evidence, So Denial of JMOL Reversed

The Federal Circuit ultimately held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the asserted ’134 claims on this record. Because the plaintiff’s expert testimony was unclear, contradictory, and failed to establish that the accused radios sampled a passband containing the claimed “interference generating signals,” the jury’s verdict lacked substantial evidence.

Accordingly, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the ’134 patent. Once that ruling fell, the damages award could not stand either, but the key point for this chapter is the infringement analysis: the plaintiff lost because its own expert’s mapping did not support the claimed sequence of signal sampling and interference cancellation.
