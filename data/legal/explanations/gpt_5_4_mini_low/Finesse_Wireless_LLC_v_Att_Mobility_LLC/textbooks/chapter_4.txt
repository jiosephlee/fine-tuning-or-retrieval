Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: The ’775 Patent—Claim Scope, Prosecution History, and the Seven-Multiplication Requirement

## The ’775 patent’s objective: receiver-side cancellation of passive IMPs using transmitter-signal copies

The Federal Circuit treated the ’775 patent as a different infringement problem from the ’134 patent. The patent is directed to interference cancellation in a receiver, specifically the cancellation of passive intermodulation products, or PIMs, using copies of transmitter signals. In the court’s summary, the claimed method generates intermodulation product cancellation signals “continuously and near real time” from transmitter-signal copies, with the goal of cancelling passive IMPs created in passive components after the transmitter’s high-powered amplifier and filter. That framing matters because the court did not decide the case on a general technical disagreement about radio interference. Instead, it focused on what the claim language actually required and whether the trial evidence matched those requirements.

## Representative claim 1 and the detailed recitation of seven third-order multiplications

Claim 1 was the representative claim for the ’775 patent. The portion that mattered on appeal was limitation 1[d], which requires generating the cancellation signals based on a power-series description of the nonlinear process and “given three signals S1, S2 and S3,” digitally multiplying and filtering seven specific products. Those products are listed explicitly in the claim: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. The court treated that list as important, not surplusage. The opinion makes clear that infringement did not turn merely on whether the accused system used some kind of multiplication or cancellation logic; it turned on whether the accused radios performed the particular set of seven multiplications recited in the claim.

## District court claim construction: “three signals S1, S2 and S3” must be separately identifiable, not necessarily unique

Before trial, the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” That construction came from the court’s reading of the intrinsic evidence, supplemented by inventor testimony that the signals could be the same or different. In other words, the district court did not accept AT&T and Nokia’s position that the claim required three distinct input signals with no overlap. Instead, it held only that the claim required three signals that could be individually identified in the operation of the method.

That construction narrowed the appellate dispute. The Federal Circuit did not have to decide whether the district court’s interpretation was right in every respect. The key question became whether, even under that construction, the trial evidence supported the jury’s finding of infringement.

## Appellants’ argument that prosecution amendments surrendered systems using only two unique input signals

AT&T and Nokia argued that Finesse had narrowed the claim during prosecution and effectively surrendered systems that used only two unique input signals. Their theory was that the applicant originally pursued broader language—“two or three signals”—and then amended the claim to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to overcome prior art. Appellants said that history showed the patentee gave up coverage of methods that could be implemented with only two unique inputs, which would have been relevant because the accused radios were said to operate with only two signals, x1 and x2.

This was a prosecution-history disclaimer argument. In patent law, the public record of how the applicant distinguished prior art can limit claim scope, but only if the applicant’s statements or amendments amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender. The Federal Circuit’s analysis stayed within that rule.

## The examiner’s rejection over Filipovic and the amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals”

The prosecution record showed that the examiner rejected a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious over Filipovic and other references. Finesse responded that Filipovic dealt with active intermodulation, not the passive intermodulation addressed by the claimed invention. The applicant also amended the claim to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and to include the seven multiplications. That history gave Appellants some footing for a narrowing argument, but it was not enough by itself to prove disavowal.

The court emphasized that the amendment occurred in the context of distinguishing the prior art’s subject matter, not necessarily in the context of limiting the claim to three unique physical inputs. The prosecution record therefore had to be read carefully.

## Why the Federal Circuit found no clear and unambiguous disavowal of systems with two unique inputs

The Federal Circuit rejected the view that the prosecution history clearly and unambiguously surrendered systems with two unique input signals. The court said there was no “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope over products that used two unique inputs. It read the amendment as doing less than Appellants claimed: at most, it required three separately identifiable signals.

That distinction is important. A claim can require three separately identifiable signals even if two of them are derived from or related to the same physical source. The court therefore declined to read the prosecution history as imposing a uniqueness requirement that the claim language itself did not state.

## The court’s acceptance that the claim does not require three unique input signals

Having rejected the surrender argument, the Federal Circuit accepted the district court’s basic understanding that the claim does not require three unique input signals. That point was not the decisive one, however. The court did not reverse because the claim needed three truly different inputs and the accused radios had only two. Instead, it moved to the separate and ultimately dispositive problem: the evidence still had to show that whatever signals were present, the accused radios performed the seven specific products listed in limitation 1[d].

That is a useful lesson in reading patent opinions. Even if a party wins a claim-construction issue, infringement can still fail for lack of proof on the remaining claim elements.

## The decisive evidentiary issue: the claim still requires performance of seven listed multiplications

The court’s infringement analysis turned on the fact that the claim explicitly required seven multiplications. The district court and the parties focused on whether the accused radios had three signals or two, but the Federal Circuit treated the seven-multiplication requirement as independently necessary. In other words, once the claim construction issue was resolved against Appellants’ prosecution-history theory, Finesse still had to prove that the accused radios actually performed the full set of claimed operations.

This is where substantial evidence review became critical. The jury’s verdict could stand only if a reasonable jury could find, based on trial evidence, that the accused radios satisfied that limitation. The court concluded they could not.

## Dr. Wells’s infringement theory based on Nokia technical documents

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied heavily on Nokia technical documents to map the accused radios onto the claim. His theory was that the radios multiplied three separately identifiable signals and that the operations shown in the Nokia document corresponded to the seven multiplications in the claim. He testified that the accused radios satisfied the limitation by following the structure shown in the document.

But the opinion repeatedly highlights a problem with that testimony: Dr. Wells did not provide a clean, stable explanation of how the document’s signals and operations matched the claim language. The court saw that instability as fatal.

## The Nokia document’s actual operations: modulus and complex-conjugate expressions rather than the seven claimed products

The Federal Circuit pointed out that the Nokia document did not simply list the seven claim products. Instead, it involved modulus and complex-conjugate operations. The document listed only three distinct multiplications, including expressions like x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|. Those are not, on their face, the same as the seven products recited in the claim.

That difference mattered because infringement had to be shown by proof, not by general similarity. The court was unwilling to infer that a document involving different mathematical expressions necessarily satisfied the exact seven-product requirement without a clear explanation connecting the two.

## The three distinct multiplications identified at trial

At trial, Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document. The district court nonetheless concluded those three distinct multiplications could satisfy the seven-multiplication limitation because each could correspond to multiple claim-listed products. The Federal Circuit disagreed with that move.

The opinion makes clear that identifying three distinct operations is not enough unless there is evidence showing how those operations map onto each of the seven claimed multiplications. The record here did not supply that mapping in a way the jury could rely on.

## Why three distinct multiplications could not simply be treated as equivalent to the seven claimed multiplications without a trial-supported mapping

The court rejected the idea that the seven listed claim multiplications could be collapsed into three merely because some of them might be mathematically related. The claim language itself listed seven separate products, and the accused-document evidence did not establish that those products were all performed. The court was especially concerned that Finesse’s mapping required assumptions the jury never heard clearly explained at trial.

The point is not that a patent claim can never be satisfied by functions expressed differently in an accused system. It is that the plaintiff must explain the equivalence with evidence. Here, the court found that explanation missing.

## The absence of testimony explaining how the accused operations correspond to each of the seven recited claim terms

This was the heart of the Federal Circuit’s analysis. Dr. Wells did not testify, in a clear and itemized way, how each accused operation corresponded to each of the seven recited multiplications. The opinion stresses that he did not walk the jury through a complete mapping. Instead, Finesse tried to reconstruct the mapping later, including on appeal, by stitching together statements from different parts of the record.

That approach was too late and too speculative for substantial-evidence review. The jury needed evidence at trial that could support a finding on the seven-multiplication limitation, and the court held it did not have that evidence.

## The court’s rejection of appellate-stage efforts to repair the mapping

On appeal, Finesse attempted to explain the mapping more concretely, but the Federal Circuit refused to accept a repaired theory that was not presented to the jury in a usable form. The opinion is explicit that appellate counsel’s reconstruction could not substitute for trial testimony and exhibits. If the theory was not clearly presented below, it could not support the verdict on appeal.

This is a recurring theme in appellate review of jury verdicts: the court asks whether the trial record, not later advocacy, supports the finding.

## The oral-argument reference to ten multiplications and why it did not cure the evidentiary deficiency

Finesse also argued at oral argument that Nokia’s materials showed not just seven multiplications, but ten. The Federal Circuit was not persuaded. Simply pointing to a larger set of operations does not solve the problem unless the record identifies which subset corresponds to the seven claim-required multiplications. The court noted that neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells explained how to get from the ten listed operations to the seven required by the claim, or how those ten related to the three distinct multiplications Dr. Wells had identified.

So the “ten multiplications” point did not cure the core problem. The issue was not whether the document contained many mathematical expressions. The issue was whether the accused radios performed the specific seven that the claim demanded, and the record did not answer that question.

## Holding as to the ’775 patent: no reasonable jury could find the seven-multiplication limitation satisfied

The Federal Circuit therefore held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the ’775 patent. Even accepting the district court’s construction that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” need only be separately identifiable and not unique, Finesse still failed to prove that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. The court concluded that the evidence was insufficient to support the verdict, reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, and, because noninfringement disposed of liability, vacated the damages award.

The practical takeaway is that claim construction is only the first step. Once the claim meaning is set, the patentee must still prove each required operation with trial evidence that a reasonable jury can actually use. Here, the Federal Circuit found that the proof fell short on the ’775 patent’s central mathematical limitation.
