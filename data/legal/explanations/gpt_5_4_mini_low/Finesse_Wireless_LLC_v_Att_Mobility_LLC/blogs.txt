Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Expert Testimony Collapses: Finesse Wireless and the Federal Circuit’s Renewed Policing of Infringement Proof

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is important for a familiar but recurring reason: patent infringement cases often turn not on a single dramatic factual dispute, but on whether the patentee’s expert can actually connect the accused product to the claim language in a coherent way. Here, the court held that the jury’s infringement verdicts could not stand where the patentee’s expert never clearly mapped the accused radios to key limitations and instead gave internally inconsistent testimony that the patentee tried to patch together after the fact.

That holding matters beyond these two patents. In software, signal-processing, and systems cases, experts frequently rely on architecture diagrams, shorthand labels, and vendor documents to explain how an accused product works. *Finesse Wireless* is a reminder that those tools do not substitute for a clean evidentiary bridge from the product to the claim. The Federal Circuit was not willing to let a verdict rest on inference stacked upon confusion.

## The court’s core move: no substantial evidence from contradictory testimony

Chief Judge Moore’s opinion is strongest on the basic Rule 50 question: whether there was enough evidence for a reasonable jury to find infringement. The court answered no, and it did so by focusing on the expert testimony itself.

On the ’134 patent, the dispute turned on a claim requirement that the accused radios sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, repeatedly mapped those claim terms to a “downlink TX reference” and a “modeled PIM path.” But that mapping created a timing problem: the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the sampling step, so it could not be the signal being sampled. When pressed, Dr. Wells shifted ground and suggested that the real referents were “x1 and x2,” but he never clearly tied those symbols to the claim terms, and he did not retract his earlier mapping. The court treated that as fatal. It invoked *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* for the proposition that a plaintiff cannot carry its burden through an expert’s self-contradictory opinion.

That is the opinion’s most consequential point. The Federal Circuit did not merely say that the testimony was imperfect or that the defense had a persuasive alternative reading. It said the testimony was so confused that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on it. That is a high bar, and the court was careful to frame the problem as evidentiary insufficiency rather than mere credibility.

## The ’775 patent: three signals, seven multiplications, and too much appellate reconstruction

The same pattern appears on the ’775 patent, though the doctrinal issue is slightly different. The claim required generating cancellation signals through seven specified multiplications involving “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” Finesse argued the accused radios used only two unique signals but still satisfied the claim because the district court had construed the signals as “separately identifiable,” not necessarily unique. The Federal Circuit did not accept AT&T and Nokia’s broader prosecution-history argument that Finesse had surrendered two-signal embodiments. But it still reversed because the proof at trial did not show the claimed seven multiplications.

That part of the opinion is notable for what it refuses to do. Finesse tried on appeal to reconstruct a mapping from the Nokia document to the asserted claim limitations, and then to supplement that with references to additional multiplications not squarely explained to the jury. The court was unimpressed. It stressed that Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications, not the seven required by the claim, and that Finesse had not explained how any of the additional operations in the document mapped onto the claim language. In other words, the court would not let the patentee assemble infringement out of fragments that were never coherently tied together at trial.

This is a meaningful distinction. The Federal Circuit is not saying experts must speak in a perfect one-to-one correspondence between claim terms and product architecture. Inference is still allowed. But the inference has to be grounded in testimony the jury actually heard, not an appellate brief’s reconstructed theory of the case.

## A line-drawing case about inference versus repair

The opinion is best understood as a line-drawing case about the limits of inferential proof. Patent plaintiffs often rely on inference because accused systems are complex and vendors do not hand over clean labels like “signal of interest” or “interference generating signal.” The law permits experts to infer mappings from documents, design data, and operational descriptions.

What *Finesse Wireless* rejects is a different move: using an expert’s later clarification, counsel’s argument, or appellate reconstruction to repair a trial record that never clearly established the required mappings. The Federal Circuit’s point is not that the jury must receive a simplistic diagram. It is that the expert must provide a stable theory of infringement.

That insistence fits comfortably with *Johns Hopkins*, but *Finesse Wireless* may sharpen the doctrine in a useful way. The opinion emphasizes not just contradiction, but the failure to identify which symbols or components actually corresponded to the claim limitations. That matters in technical cases where experts use vendor nomenclature, variable names, and schematic labels as shorthand. If those labels are ambiguous, the expert must say so and explain the mapping with enough precision that the jury can follow it. Otherwise, the case risks becoming a puzzle whose missing pieces are supplied only after the verdict.

## Practical implications for patent trial practice

The downstream effect of this decision will likely be felt most in cases built around technical diagrams and source documents. Patent litigators will want to audit expert reports and trial testimony for exactly the kind of ambiguity that sank Finesse’s proof here: Are the expert’s labels stable throughout the record? Does the expert ever switch from one mapping to another without acknowledgment? Are key claim limitations actually matched to specific product components, or merely to a general region of the diagram?

For defendants, the opinion offers a useful cross-examination roadmap. If the expert’s story depends on a document that contains multiple candidate signals, multiple multiplications, or multiple stages of processing, the defense should force the witness to commit to a single mapping and then test whether that mapping is temporally and logically coherent. If the witness keeps moving the goalposts, *Finesse Wireless* suggests the verdict may be vulnerable.

For plaintiffs, the lesson is the opposite. Do not assume that a jury will infer the right mapping from a familiar-looking architecture figure. Spell it out. Make sure the report and testimony connect each claim limitation to the accused system in a way that survives sustained questioning. Especially in software and RF cases, where symbols and intermediate signals proliferate, clarity is not optional; it is the proof.

## Bottom line

*Finesse Wireless* is not a sweeping change in patent law. It is, however, a sharp reaffirmation that infringement verdicts cannot rest on expert testimony that is internally inconsistent and only retrospectively made coherent. The Federal Circuit is signaling that “reasonable inference” has limits: the inference must come from the trial record, not from an after-the-fact reconstruction of what the expert must have meant. That is a modest but important constraint, and one that should matter in the next generation of technically dense patent trials.



# Three Signals, Seven Multiplications, and No Infringement: What *Finesse Wireless* Says About Mathematical Claim Limitations

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is a useful reminder that when patent claims are written in mathematical or algorithmic terms, infringement still has to be proved the old-fashioned way: element by element, with the actual operations matched to the actual claim language. That may sound obvious, but in practice DSP and software cases often invite a kind of functional hand-waving—especially when an expert can point to a diagram, a block labeled “model,” or a general description of how a system works and then infer that the claimed steps must be happening somewhere inside the box. The panel was not willing to accept that approach here.

The opinion is also notable for what it does not do. The court declined to hold that Finesse had surrendered systems using only two unique input signals when it amended the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” But that victory for the patentee was academic, because the panel still concluded that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven multiplications recited in the claim. That combination makes the case especially instructive: even without a clean prosecution-history-surrender ruling, the patentee still lost because its proof did not map the accused functionality onto the claim with enough precision.

## The claim language did the heavy lifting

The asserted claim of the ’775 patent requires generating cancellation signals “based on a power series description of a non-linear process,” and “includes generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering” seven specified products:  
S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3.

That language matters because it is not just describing a general class of nonlinear processing. It specifies a particular set of multiplicative combinations. The Federal Circuit focused on that specificity. It did not treat the seven multiplications as a loose illustrative list or as interchangeable embodiments of “some nonlinear math.” Instead, it treated them as claim limitations that had to be shown in the accused system.

That is the core doctrinal move here. For algorithmic claims, the temptation is to collapse the claim into a result—here, interference cancellation through nonlinear modeling—but the panel insisted on the steps that the claim actually recited.

## Why the two-signal argument did not carry the day

Appellants argued that Finesse had effectively claimed away two-signal embodiments during prosecution, because the applicant changed the claim from “two or three signals” to “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The panel rejected that argument. It read the prosecution history as showing an attempt to overcome a rejection based on Filipovic, but not a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of systems that use two unique inputs. In the court’s view, the amendment required three separately identifiable signals, but not necessarily three distinct unique sources.

That part of the opinion is important because it resists overreading prosecution amendments in a way that would transform a drafting change into a surrender of broad claim scope. The Federal Circuit has been fairly demanding about prosecution disclaimer, and this decision stays in line with that approach.

But the real action came next.

## The fatal gap: no proof of the seven claimed multiplications

Even though the court declined to impose a “three unique signals” requirement, it still held that Finesse failed on infringement because the evidence did not show the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, relied on Nokia technical documents. Those documents, however, did not cleanly disclose the specific set of claimed operations. The panel emphasized that the document involved modulus and complex-conjugate operations, and that it listed only three distinct multiplications, not the seven claimed products.

That distinction—between “distinct” multiplications and repeated or functionally similar operations—is where the case becomes especially significant. The court was not persuaded by the idea that three listed multiplications could somehow stand in for seven claimed ones without a clear mapping. Nor was it enough that the expert suggested there may have been additional mathematical expressions elsewhere in the document. The panel stressed that neither the expert testimony nor the record explained which operations corresponded to which claimed multiplications.

In other words, the court did not allow a patentee to bridge an evidentiary gap by counting from three to seven through inference alone.

## Complex conjugates and modulus operations are not the same thing as the claim

One of the more technical but important features of the opinion is the court’s treatment of the Nokia document’s use of complex conjugates and modulus terms. The Federal Circuit treated those as meaningful differences, not as cosmetic variations that could be waved into the claim language.

That is a sensible result. In signal processing, whether a term is x2, x2′, or |x2| can matter a great deal. Patent litigants sometimes try to treat these as all part of the same mathematical family, especially if the ultimate output is similar. But the claim here did not just say “combine three signals in a nonlinear way.” It identified a particular set of products. If the accused device uses conjugates or modulus operations, the patentee has to show why those operations satisfy the claim as written, not merely why they might seem close in an engineering sense.

The opinion therefore reinforces a broader trend: when claims invoke math, the Federal Circuit expects claim language, expert testimony, and technical documents to line up with unusual care.

## A broader message for DSP and algorithmic claims

The downstream effect of *Finesse Wireless* may be felt most acutely in cases involving digital signal processing, wireless systems, and other algorithm-heavy technologies. These disputes often turn on whether a diagram, code pathway, or model output “really” performs the claimed step. The Federal Circuit is signaling that “really” is not enough. The question is whether the record proves the actual claimed operations.

That does not mean patentees can never prove infringement of mathematical claims through expert testimony. Of course they can. But the testimony has to be coherent, internally consistent, and anchored in the accused system’s actual functioning. Here, the panel was plainly troubled that the expert identified one set of signals when pressed in one direction, then appeared to pivot without a clear explanation when confronted with the fact that the modeled PIM path was generated after sampling. On the multiplication issue, the expert identified only three distinct multiplications and did not supply a reliable bridge to the seven recited in the claim.

That is a powerful warning for future litigants: if the claim recites specific math, you have to prove specific math.

## Bottom line

*Finesse Wireless* is not just another noninfringement reversal. It is a fairly crisp statement that mathematical patent claims will be enforced as mathematical patent claims. The Federal Circuit was unwilling to stretch prosecution history into a surrender of two-signal systems, but it was equally unwilling to let the patentee win on a fuzzy theory that three disclosed multiplications somehow satisfied seven claimed ones.

For patent owners, the lesson is that algorithmic claims need algorithmic proof. For accused infringers, the case offers a useful roadmap for attacking infringement theories that rely on generalized descriptions instead of exact mappings. And for the Federal Circuit, the decision fits a familiar pattern: rigor on claim-specific proof, especially where the patent tries to claim a particular computational method rather than a broad functional result.



## A Narrow View of Prosecution Disclaimer, a Broad Demand for Trial Proof

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility* is notable less for any dramatic doctrinal shift than for the way it separates two questions that patent litigants often blur together: what the claims mean, and whether the plaintiff actually proved infringement at trial. On the claim-scope side, the court took a fairly restrained view of prosecution disclaimer, refusing to read Finesse’s amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals” as a clear and unambiguous surrender of systems that use only two unique inputs. But that doctrinal moderation did not save the verdict. The court still reversed because the trial record never established that the accused radios performed the specific seven multiplications the claims required.

That combination makes the case a useful reminder for patent trial strategy. A party may win the construction fight and still lose the infringement verdict if its proof is too loose, too inconsistent, or too dependent on post hoc appellate gloss. And conversely, an accused infringer cannot assume that a prosecution-history argument will do the work of an evidentiary attack if the plaintiff’s proof is otherwise weak.

## The court’s restrained take on disclaimer

The most interesting doctrinal move in the opinion is what the court declined to do. AT&T and Nokia argued that Finesse’s prosecution history surrendered claim scope over products using only two unique signals, because the claims had been amended to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The Federal Circuit rejected that reading. It emphasized that prosecution disclaimer requires a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal, and it found the record did not rise to that level.

That is an important point. The panel acknowledged that Finesse had amended the claims in response to prior art and that the amendment added the “three signals” language. But it did not read that change as a sharp line excluding any implementation built from two unique inputs. Instead, the court accepted the district court’s view that the claim language required three “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily three distinct source signals in the sense urged by Appellants. In other words, the amendment narrowed the claim, but not in the fully exclusionary way AT&T and Nokia wanted.

Doctrinally, that is a fairly careful application of disclaimer law. The Federal Circuit has often said that prosecution history can inform meaning, but it should not be converted into an overbroad surrender rule. This opinion follows that path. The court did not treat a narrowing amendment as automatic proof of complete relinquishment of a related embodiment. That restraint matters because patent drafting and prosecution rarely produce perfect linguistic symmetry; if courts read every amendment as a full disavowal of adjacent territory, disclaimer would become far more expansive than the doctrine permits.

## But the trial proof still failed

The case did not turn on claim scope, though. It turned on proof. On the ’775 patent, the court accepted that Finesse had not clearly surrendered two-signal embodiments, but it still found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications.

Here the court was much less forgiving. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical document, but the panel concluded the document showed only three distinct multiplications, not the seven required by the claim. More importantly, Dr. Wells never clearly mapped those three multiplications onto the seven claimed operations. Finesse tried on appeal to reconstruct a mapping from other parts of the record, but the panel correctly rejected that effort because appellate briefing cannot supply what the jury never actually heard. The opinion is especially pointed on this score: where the expert’s explanation is incomplete and the document itself does not show the claimed sequence, the verdict cannot stand.

The court’s treatment of the ’134 patent is even more striking in that respect. There, the dispute centered on whether the accused radios sampled the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The Federal Circuit found Dr. Wells’ testimony internally inconsistent: at times he mapped those signals to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, but that mapping made little sense because the modeled PIM path was generated after the sampling step. When pressed, he pivoted to a different explanation, but never clearly tied x1 and x2 to the claim language. The court leaned on *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope* to hold that a self-contradictory expert opinion cannot carry the plaintiff’s burden.

That is a demanding evidentiary standard, but not an unreasonable one. The law does not require perfect testimony, but it does require a coherent path from the accused product to each claim limitation. Here the expert’s testimony wandered between competing mappings without ever fully reconciling them. The Federal Circuit was unwilling to let the jury verdict rest on that ambiguity.

## The practical lesson: claim construction is not enough

The real downstream significance of *Finesse* may be strategic. Litigants often spend enormous resources fighting over claim construction and prosecution history, assuming that victory there will determine the case. This decision says otherwise. The Federal Circuit was relatively moderate on disclaimer, but it was exacting on proof. That means patentees cannot rely on broad, elastic expert narratives that only loosely track the claim elements. If a claim requires a particular sequence of operations, or a particular relationship among signals, the trial record must show exactly how the accused system satisfies that requirement.

For accused infringers, the lesson is complementary. A prosecution-disclaimer argument may still be worth pressing, but it should not be the only defense. Here, even without a sweeping disclaimer ruling, the defendants prevailed because they exposed gaps and contradictions in the infringement evidence. That is often the stronger route: challenge the plaintiff’s technical mapping directly, and make sure the record preserves the failure of proof.

## A broader doctrinal takeaway

The opinion also reflects the Federal Circuit’s recurring preference for keeping legal doctrines in their lanes. Disclaimer doctrine is about what the claims mean after prosecution; JMOL doctrine is about whether the evidence at trial was enough. The court resisted collapsing one into the other. It did not say the claims were limited to three unique signals, and it did not rewrite the claim language to fit the accused product. Instead, it asked whether the plaintiff had proved what the claims, as construed, required. It had not.

That separation is healthy. It preserves the claim-construction framework while reminding litigants that infringement is ultimately an evidentiary question. *Finesse* is therefore a useful case not because it expands disclaimer doctrine, but because it shows the Federal Circuit will be cautious about finding surrender while still demanding disciplined, element-by-element proof at trial.



## Reversing the Verdict, Vacating $166 Million: The Fragility of Lump-Sum Damages After Total Infringement Failure

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is, on its face, a liability decision. But its most immediate practical effect is on damages: the panel reversed JMOL on noninfringement for every asserted claim and therefore vacated the entire $166 million lump-sum award without ever reaching the district court’s damages rulings. That makes the case a sharp reminder of how vulnerable large patent verdicts can be when they rest on a single infringement theory that carries the whole case.

The decision also has a broader strategic lesson. In cases tried on a unified expert narrative, liability and damages are often intertwined in a way that can make the damages fight look central right up until the point liability falls away. Once the Federal Circuit concludes there is no substantial evidence of infringement, the carefully developed damages record becomes legally beside the point.

## The court’s path to vacatur

The panel’s damages holding is short, but consequential. After reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of both the ’134 and ’775 patents, Chief Judge Moore wrote that the court “vacate[s] the damages award.” The opinion does not discuss the merits of the reasonable-royalty analysis, apportionment, or any of the other post-trial disputes the parties pressed below. Nor does it reach the motion for a new trial on damages. That restraint is doctrinally unremarkable: once liability disappears entirely, the damages verdict cannot stand.

What matters more is the structure of the case that led to that result. Finesse won a single, global lump-sum award for the remaining life of both patents. That kind of award can be efficient, but it also creates a binary vulnerability. If the verdict is tied to an all-claims, all-patents infringement theory, and that theory collapses on appeal, the damages award collapses with it. There is no partial recovery to preserve unless some claims or some accused functionality survive.

## The hidden fragility of the lump sum

This opinion underscores a point practitioners know but sometimes underappreciate in the heat of trial: lump-sum damages are only as durable as the infringement theory supporting them. Here, the jury awarded a single $166,303,391 figure for the remaining patent life. That makes sense if the jury believes the accused radios infringe everything at issue and the patents together justify a unified royalty. But it also means the award has no internal insulation against a Federal Circuit reversal on liability.

The fragility is especially pronounced where the same expert is doing double duty. Dr. Wells was the central infringement witness, and his testimony was the focal point of the panel’s noninfringement analysis. The opinion repeatedly emphasizes that his mapping of claim terms to Nokia’s technical diagrams was confused, internally inconsistent, and in some respects self-contradictory. That matters for damages even though the court never reaches damages methodology, because the same expert story that tries to establish the scope and operation of the accused technology often frames the royalty case as well. When that narrative is undermined, the damages theory can lose much of its force before the court even gets to it.

## What the Federal Circuit did not decide

It is worth being precise about what this decision does not say. The Federal Circuit did not hold that lump-sum damages are suspect as a category. It did not reject the district court’s damages rulings on their own terms. And it did not address whether the royalty base, royalty rate, or any alleged instructional error would have required a new trial had infringement remained intact.

Instead, the panel took the narrower but more decisive route: no substantial evidence supported infringement, so the damages award had no foundation. That is a clean appellate disposition, but it also means the case offers an object lesson in sequencing. If a defendant has a strong liability issue, it may be possible to render a protracted damages battle irrelevant. Conversely, for a patentee, the lesson is that the damages case cannot be allowed to function as a substitute for a disciplined infringement theory.

## Downstream effects in the Eastern District of Texas and beyond

For litigants in the Eastern District of Texas, the practical message is straightforward. A trial strategy built around a single, sweeping technical narrative may produce a very large verdict, but it also creates a single point of failure on appeal. That will likely encourage defendants to sharpen Rule 50 records around claim-by-claim and limitation-by-limitation evidence, especially where the patentee’s expert testimony is ambiguous or contradictory. The Federal Circuit’s reliance here on the insufficiency of the testimony suggests that post-trial preservation can pay off if the record makes the expert’s mapping difficult to sustain.

For patentees, the decision cuts in the opposite direction. It suggests greater value in separating infringement theories by claim group, accused feature, or technical mode of operation where possible. A damages presentation that is too tightly fused to one umbrella infringement theory may look efficient to a jury, but it may also become brittle on appeal. The more the award depends on the premise that everything infringes in the same way, the more a single liability defect threatens the whole verdict.

## The larger doctrinal point

The opinion’s damages consequence is a reminder that patent damages are downstream of liability in a very literal sense. That may sound obvious, but in large-tech patent cases it is easy for damages to become the center of gravity at trial. This case shows that the Federal Circuit will not hesitate to sweep away a large award when the liability proof is too thin. In that setting, the damages debate can become legally moot even after a full jury trial and extensive post-trial briefing.

That is not a new rule, but it is a forceful illustration of one. If a patent plaintiff wants a durable monetary judgment, it needs more than a persuasive royalty model. It needs an infringement record that can survive appellate scrutiny. In *Finesse Wireless*, that foundation did not hold, and the result was not just reversal on liability, but the disappearance of the entire $166 million award.
