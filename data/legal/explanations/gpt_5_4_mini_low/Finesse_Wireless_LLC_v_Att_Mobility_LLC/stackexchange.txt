Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How can the Federal Circuit reverse a jury's infringement verdict on JMOL if it must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The jury found infringement, and the district court denied JMOL. The opinion says the Federal Circuit reviews the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law and asks whether reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict, viewing the evidence favorably to the winner. But then it still reverses on both patents. What exactly makes a case cross the line from 'the jury could believe this witness' into 'no reasonable jury could find infringement'? Is the key point that the expert testimony was too internally inconsistent to count as substantial evidence at all?
Answer:
The key is that “viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict” does **not** mean the court must accept any statement a witness made, no matter how confused, unsupported, or internally contradictory it is.

Under the standard the Federal Circuit recited from Fifth Circuit law, JMOL is proper when the evidence, even taken favorably to the verdict winner, “**points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party** that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The infringement verdict must also be supported by **substantial evidence**—i.e., evidence on which a reasonable jury could actually rely. So the question is not whether some testimony exists in the record, but whether that testimony, taken as a whole, could reasonably support each claim limitation.

That is exactly how the court handled **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility**.

## 1. The court did not “reweigh” credibility; it found there was no coherent evidentiary basis
For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells repeatedly mapped the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to the **DL(TX) reference** and the **modeled PIM path**. But when confronted with the fact that the modeled PIM path is generated **after** the RF ADC samples signals, he pivoted and gave confusing testimony about “x1 and x2.” The problem was that he never clearly and consistently testified that **x1 and x2** were the claimed signals. The court says Finesse had to “piece together” that theory from testimony separated by many pages, and even then the testimony remained inconsistent.

That is why the Federal Circuit relied on **Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.**: when the party with the burden of proof “rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony,” the court may conclude the evidence is insufficient. In other words, a jury is free to believe a witness, but it is not free to build a verdict on testimony that never actually supplies a stable factual basis for the required claim element.

## 2. “Light most favorable” still requires evidence on every element
For the ’775 patent, the problem was different but related. Finesse needed evidence that the accused radios performed the **seven claimed multiplications**. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document, but the Federal Circuit found that the document showed only **three distinct multiplications**, and Dr. Wells never explained how those three mapped onto the seven required multiplications. Finesse tried to supply that mapping on appeal, but the court said that evidence was **not presented to the jury**.

So even granting every reasonable inference in Finesse’s favor, there was still a gap: no evidence from which a reasonable jury could find the specific “seven multiplications” limitation satisfied.

## 3. The line is crossed when the proffered proof becomes too incoherent or incomplete to be “substantial evidence”
So yes—your instinct is basically right. The Federal Circuit reversed because the expert testimony was not merely “disputed”; it was **self-contradictory and unclear on the very claim elements that had to be proved**. That kind of testimony cannot always count as substantial evidence.

The practical rule is:

- if a witness’s testimony is merely **credible vs. not credible**, that is for the jury;
- if the testimony is **internally inconsistent, speculative, or fails to supply a necessary claim-element mapping**, the court can hold that **no reasonable jury** could find infringement on that record.

That is the doctrinal bridge between “we view the evidence favorably to the verdict” and “we still reverse JMOL.” The favorable-view rule does not require the court to pretend that incoherent testimony proves what it never clearly said.

### Why did the court treat the expert's contradictory mapping of claim elements as legally insufficient rather than just a credibility issue for the jury?
Question:
For the '134 patent, Finesse's expert seems to have first mapped the claimed 'interference generating signals' to the 'modeled PIM path,' but that path was generated only after the RF ADC sampling step. Then, under cross, he seemed to pivot toward x1 and x2, but never clearly remapped them to the claim limitations. I thought contradictions in testimony usually go to weight and credibility, which juries decide. Why did the Federal Circuit say this kind of self-contradictory expert testimony cannot support the verdict? Is there a doctrinal rule that an expert has to provide a clear element-by-element mapping for infringement, and if the mapping is materially inconsistent the testimony stops being substantial evidence?
Answer:
Because at JMOL the question is **not** whether a witness might be more or less believable in the abstract; it is whether the record contains **substantial evidence from which a reasonable jury could find every claim limitation proved**.

That is the key doctrinal move in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*. The Federal Circuit treated Dr. Wells’s testimony as legally insufficient because his infringement theory was **internally inconsistent on a material point**, and the court could not identify a coherent, element-by-element mapping that would let a reasonable jury find limitation 1[a] satisfied.

## Why this is more than a “credibility” issue

Ordinarily, contradictions in testimony are for the jury to sort out. But that principle has limits. If the contradictions leave the party with the burden of proof without a **clear evidentiary basis** for a required claim element, then the testimony does not amount to substantial evidence.

The court relied on *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, where it held that an expert’s contradictory testimony could not support literal infringement because the expert’s own statements negated the theory needed to meet the claim limitation. The panel in *Finesse* applied that same reasoning:

- Dr. Wells repeatedly mapped **“interference generating signals”** to the **modeled PIM path**.
- But the modeled PIM path was generated **after** the RF ADC sampling step.
- That creates a logical mismatch, because the claim required the accused receiver to sample a passband containing the “interference generating signals.”
- When pressed, Wells did not clearly remap the limitation to **x1 and x2**; instead, he continued to describe the red path as the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path.

So the problem was not merely that Wells was impeached on cross. The problem was that his testimony never supplied a **coherent, consistent mapping** of the claimed elements to the accused system.

## The doctrinal rule

Yes, in practice, infringement testimony must do an **element-by-element mapping**. A patentee must prove each claim limitation, and expert testimony is sufficient only if it clearly connects the accused structure or process to those limitations.

When the expert’s own testimony is materially inconsistent such that:

1. the only express mapping conflicts with the claim requirement, and  
2. the witness does not clearly correct or replace that mapping,

then the testimony may fail as a matter of law because it is no longer substantial evidence.

That is exactly what the court said here:

> “This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.”

And:

> “Even if Dr. Wells did testify x1 and x2 are the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals,’ he repeatedly testified the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path correspond to the claimed signals, and offered no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony.”

## Bottom line

The Federal Circuit was not reweighing ordinary credibility. It was saying that **a jury cannot base infringement on an expert theory that never clearly maps the claim limitation and is internally inconsistent in a way that leaves the required element unproven**. Once the testimony becomes too contradictory to establish a necessary claim element, it stops being substantial evidence and cannot sustain the verdict.

### In an infringement case, how precise does an expert's claim-element mapping have to be at trial?
Question:
A lot of the court's reasoning seems to turn on the fact that Dr. Wells never clearly testified that x1 and x2 were the claimed 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals,' and that Finesse tried to reconstruct that mapping later by piecing together different parts of the record. Is that generally not allowed? In other words, can a patentee rely on inferences from charts, documents, and scattered testimony, or does the expert need to explicitly identify, in a coherent way, where each claim limitation is found in the accused product? I'm trying to understand whether this is a patent-specific evidentiary requirement or just a general substantial-evidence principle.
Answer:
Yes — but the real rule is not that an expert must use any magic words. The rule is that the patentee must present **substantial evidence** that each claim limitation is met, and the expert’s testimony has to do that in a way a reasonable jury can actually follow.

In **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility**, the Federal Circuit reversed because Finesse tried to bridge a gap in Dr. Wells’s testimony by **piecing together scattered statements and inferences** that were never clearly presented as a coherent claim-element mapping. The court emphasized that Dr. Wells:

- repeatedly mapped the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to the **DL(TX) reference** and the **modeled PIM path**, not to x1 and x2;
- never clearly testified that **x1 and x2** were the claimed signals; and
- left Finesse to infer that mapping from parts of the record “over ninety pages apart.”

The court’s point was not that infringement experts must produce a perfectly formatted chart at trial. It was that **a jury cannot be asked to infer a crucial claim-element mapping from a confused, contradictory record when the expert never clearly identified it**. The Federal Circuit said this kind of “confusing change of course” was not enough, citing **Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.**, where contradictory expert testimony could not support a verdict.

So the answer to your question is:

- **You can rely on inference and documentary evidence**, but only if the evidence, taken as a whole, reasonably supports the finding that each limitation is met.
- **You cannot reconstruct an essential infringement theory on appeal from scattered snippets if the expert never clearly made that mapping at trial.**
- This is **not a special patent-only evidentiary rule** so much as an application of the ordinary **substantial-evidence standard** to patent infringement.

The Federal Circuit was essentially saying: if the patentee’s proof depends on an expert’s claim chart, the expert must explain the mapping clearly enough that the jury can reasonably find infringement. If the testimony is internally inconsistent or leaves the key correspondence unclear, the verdict may fail for lack of substantial evidence.

### Why wasn't changing the claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' considered prosecution disclaimer?
Question:
For the '775 patent, the accused system apparently used only two unique signals, x1 and x2, and Finesse had amended its claims during prosecution from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' after an obviousness rejection over Filipovic. My instinct was that this amendment should surrender two-signal systems. But the Federal Circuit says there was no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal, partly because Finesse also argued Filipovic dealt with active rather than passive IMPs. How can an amendment narrowing the literal text from 'two or three' to 'three' still not give up two-signal embodiments? Is this because disclaimer is a claim-construction doctrine requiring clarity beyond what is needed for ordinary narrowing amendment purposes?
Answer:
Yes — this is basically a **claim-construction / prosecution-disclaimer** point, and the Federal Circuit is applying the usual rule that **not every narrowing amendment or prosecution argument amounts to a surrender of claim scope**.

### Short answer

Changing the claim from **“two or three signals”** to **“three signals S1, S2 and S3”** does **not automatically** mean the patentee gave up all two-signal embodiments, because prosecution disclaimer requires a **“clear and unambiguous”** disavowal of that scope. The court held that Finesse’s prosecution history did not meet that standard.

### Why the amendment was not enough

The key is that the amendment did **not stand alone**. The Federal Circuit read the prosecution history as showing that Finesse overcame the obviousness rejection **in part by distinguishing Filipovic on a different ground**: Filipovic was said to be limited to **active IMPs**, whereas the claimed invention addressed **passive IMPs** as well.

So, even though Finesse amended the claim language, the court concluded that the amendment did **not clearly say**: “we are giving up all two-signal systems.” Instead, the record supported a narrower inference: the claim now required **three separately identifiable signals**, but not necessarily **three unique input signals**.

That is why the court said:

> “At most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals.”

### Why that matters for disclaimer

Prosecution disclaimer is not triggered merely because:

- the claim was narrowed,
- the patentee distinguished prior art,
- or the patentee made an amendment that could be read as narrower.

Those things may affect ordinary claim scope, but disclaimer is stronger than that. It requires a **clear and unmistakable surrender** of the disputed meaning.

So your instinct is understandable, but the legal standard is stricter than “the claim got narrower.” The court is asking: **Did the prosecution history clearly tell the public that two-signal embodiments were abandoned?** Here, the court said no.

### The practical distinction

The opinion draws a line between:

1. **Literal textual narrowing** — the claim now says “three signals S1, S2 and S3”; and  
2. **Disclaimer of all two-signal systems** — which would need a much clearer record.

Because Finesse’s prosecution history still left room to argue that the claim covered **three identifiable signals**, even if two of them might be copies or related signals, the court declined to treat the amendment as a waiver of all two-signal embodiments.

### Bottom line

Yes: **disclaimer is a claim-construction doctrine that demands clarity beyond ordinary amendment-based narrowing.** In this case, the Federal Circuit thought the prosecution history showed **some narrowing**, but not a **clear and unambiguous surrender** of two-signal embodiments.

### What is the legal difference between 'three separately identifiable signals' and 'three unique signals' in this patent claim?
Question:
The district court construed 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to mean 'separately identifiable' but not necessarily 'unique,' and the Federal Circuit seems to agree with that construction. I'm struggling to understand what that means in practice. If a system has only two unique input signals, how can it still satisfy a requirement for three signals? Does this mean one of the signals can be just a duplicate or another version of one of the others? And if so, what limits are there—could a signal, its conjugate, and its magnitude all count as separate signals, or does that become too far removed from the claim language?
Answer:
The short answer is that **“three separately identifiable signals” is broader than “three unique signals.”** Under the Federal Circuit’s discussion in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility*, the claim language was not read to require **three different source inputs**; it was read to require **three signals that can be distinguished from one another in the claimed operation**.

## What that means

If a claim says “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” but the court construes that as **“separately identifiable”** rather than **“unique,”** then the claim does **not** necessarily demand three physically or mathematically distinct underlying inputs.

So, in principle, a system with only **two unique inputs** could still fall within the claim if one of those inputs is used twice in a way that makes three distinguishable signal references. That is exactly the sort of idea the district court accepted here when it said “two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals” could satisfy its construction.

The Federal Circuit did **not** reject that construction. Instead, it said:

- Finesse had **not clearly and unambiguously disclaimed** coverage of products using two unique signals;
- therefore, the claim did **not require any specific number of unique input signals**; but
- the accused radios still did **not** infringe because Finesse failed to show the required **seven claimed multiplications** were actually performed.

## The practical limit

The limit is that “separately identifiable” still has to mean something real. It cannot be an empty label you can slap onto anything.

So yes, a signal and a copy of that signal might count as two of the three, **if the claim and the evidence support treating them as distinct signal elements in the multiplication chain**. But merely pointing to related mathematical quantities does not automatically solve the problem.

That is why the court was skeptical of Finesse’s attempt to treat:

- a signal,
- its complex conjugate, and
- its modulus

as interchangeable “signals” for claim purposes. The opinion says the Nokia document involved **modulus and complex conjugate operations**, and Finesse never clearly mapped those operations onto the seven claimed multiplications. In other words, the issue was not just whether there were “three identifiable things,” but whether those things matched the **claimed signal structure and arithmetic**.

## Bottom line

So the difference is:

- **Unique signals** = three different underlying inputs.
- **Separately identifiable signals** = three distinguishable signal elements, which may include repeated or derived versions of the same underlying input.

But even under the broader construction, the patentee still must prove that the accused system performs the **specific claimed multiplications** with those signals. In *Finesse Wireless*, that proof was missing.

### Why did the court say the accused radios did not perform the claimed 'seven multiplications' if some of the products might overlap mathematically?
Question:
The '775 patent claims generating an nth-order ICS by digitally multiplying and filtering seven listed products involving S1, S2, and S3. The district court seemed to think that if the accused radios produced three distinct multiplications, those could correspond to the seven claimed ones because some would collapse when the inputs are not unique. But the Federal Circuit rejected that. Is the court saying the claim requires evidence that all seven listed multiplication operations are actually performed, even if some of them would evaluate to the same algebraic result? In other words, does the claim focus on the number of computational operations performed rather than the number of distinct outputs produced?
Answer:
Yes — the Federal Circuit is treating limitation 1[d] as requiring proof that the **recited multiplication operations themselves** are performed, not merely that the accused device can be described as producing a few algebraically similar or overlapping results.

That is the key point in the opinion.

### What the court focused on
For the ’775 patent, the claim does not just say “generate an n-th order ICS from three signals.” It says that this is done by:

> “digitally multiplying and filtering”  
> **S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3**

The court read that as requiring evidence that those **seven listed multiplications** are performed. It did not accept the district court’s reasoning that three distinct multiplications could suffice because some of the seven might “collapse” or overlap when inputs are not unique.

### Why overlap did not save infringement
The Federal Circuit said the record showed only **three distinct multiplications** in the Nokia document, and:

- the document used **modulus and complex conjugate operations**, not the exact claim language;
- there was **no evidence that any multiplications are repeated** so that all seven claimed multiplications are performed;
- Dr. Wells **did not explain how the three listed multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications**.

So even if, mathematically, some claimed products could end up equal when the same signal is used more than once, that does **not** relieve the patentee of proving the accused radios actually perform the **seven claimed digital multiplication/filtering operations**.

### Bottom line
The court was not saying that infringement requires seven different numerical outputs. It was saying that the accused radios had to be shown to perform the **seven claimed computational steps**, and Finesse failed to provide substantial evidence of that. Overlap in results does not substitute for proof of the claimed operations.

### Why couldn't Finesse rely on Nokia documents and appellate explanations to show the seven claimed multiplications were present?
Question:
The opinion says Finesse tried on appeal and at oral argument to explain how Nokia's technical documents showed not just seven multiplications but ten, and how those should map to the claim. But the court says that evidence was never actually presented to the jury in a way that supported infringement. Why isn't it enough that the underlying documents were in evidence? Does patent infringement proof require the expert to walk the jury through the exact mapping at trial, rather than letting the appellate court infer it from raw exhibits? I'm trying to understand the boundary between permissible reliance on admitted technical documents and impermissible post hoc attorney reconstruction.
Answer:
Because infringement has to be proved **to the jury on the trial record**, not reconstructed later on appeal.

In this opinion, the Federal Circuit treats Finesse’s argument as an attempt to **fill gaps in Dr. Wells’s trial testimony after the fact**. That is not enough under the substantial-evidence standard.

### The key doctrine
To sustain a jury verdict of infringement, the patentee must present **evidence from which a reasonable jury could find every claim limitation met**. On JMOL review, the court asks whether the evidence **actually presented at trial** supports the verdict. It does **not** ask whether the appellate court can, by piecing together exhibits and lawyer arguments, build a plausible infringement theory.

### Why the Nokia documents alone were not enough
The court did not say the Nokia documents were irrelevant. It said the problem was that:

1. **Dr. Wells did not clearly explain the mapping** from the Nokia document to the seven claimed multiplications.
2. The documents themselves showed only **three distinct multiplications**, not the seven required by the claim.
3. Finesse’s later explanation on appeal and at oral argument required **new inferences and a new mapping** that were never actually presented to the jury.

The court was explicit:  
> “Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury.”

That is the critical point. Under appellate review, the court will not accept a litigant’s **post hoc reconstruction** of what the expert supposedly meant if the trial testimony did not actually make that case.

### Why the expert’s role mattered
This is a patent case, so expert testimony is often how the jury learns to connect technical documents to claim language. But the expert still has to **walk the jury through the limitation-by-limitation mapping** in a coherent way.

Here, the Federal Circuit found that Dr. Wells:
- identified only **three multiplications** at trial;
- did not explain how those three mapped onto **seven claim-required multiplications**; and
- could not be rescued by Finesse’s later appellate theory.

So yes, in practical terms, **the expert had to do the mapping at trial**. Raw technical exhibits do not substitute for testimony establishing how the accused system meets each claim element.

### Bottom line
The boundary is this:

- **Permissible:** An expert relies on admitted technical documents and explains, step by step, how they satisfy the claim.
- **Impermissible:** Counsel later mines the documents and tries to supply missing claim-element mappings that the expert never actually gave the jury.

That is why the Federal Circuit reversed: the verdict was not supported by substantial evidence because Finesse’s infringement theory for the seven multiplications was not actually proved at trial.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the damages award instead of reaching the defendants' separate damages arguments?
Question:
The defendants appealed both infringement and damages, including the denial of a new trial on damages, and the jury had awarded a large lump-sum royalty for the remaining life of the patents. But once the Federal Circuit reversed on noninfringement, it simply vacated the damages award. Is that just because damages automatically fall away when there is no liability, making the damages issues moot? And if the court had affirmed infringement on one patent but not the other, would it typically remand for a new damages trial instead of vacating the whole award?
Answer:
Yes. In **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility**, the Federal Circuit vacated the damages award **because it reversed infringement on all asserted claims**. Once there is **no liability for infringement**, there is no legal basis for a damages award, so the damages issue becomes moot. That is why the court said, in one sentence, that “[b]ecause we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.”

A few points of doctrine are doing the work here:

1. **Damages depend on liability.**  
   Patent damages are only recoverable if the accused party is found to infringe at least one valid asserted claim. If infringement falls away entirely, the damages verdict cannot stand.

2. **The court did not need to reach the separate damages arguments.**  
   AT&T and Nokia had also challenged the damages ruling and the denial of a new trial on damages, but once the court held there was **no infringement at all**, those arguments no longer mattered to the outcome. The damages award was not merely erroneous in amount; it had **no surviving liability foundation**.

3. **“Vacate” is the right disposition when the entire award is unsupported.**  
   Vacatur removes the damages judgment from the books because the infringement verdict that supported it has been reversed.

On your second question: **yes, in the usual course, if infringement survived as to only some asserted claims or some products, the court would generally not vacate damages in full.** Instead, it would typically **remand** for the district court to determine whether the damages award can be sustained or whether a **new damages trial** is needed, depending on whether the original lump-sum verdict can be separated from the claims or theories that failed.

But that is not what happened here. The opinion makes clear that the Federal Circuit reversed noninfringement as to **all asserted claims of both patents**, so there was nothing left to support any damages award at all. That is why the court did not reach the defendants’ separate damages issues.
