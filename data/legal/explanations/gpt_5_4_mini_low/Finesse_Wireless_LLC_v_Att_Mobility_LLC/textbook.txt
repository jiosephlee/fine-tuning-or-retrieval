Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Chapter 1: Case Setting, Technology, and Procedural Posture

## The parties and the dispute

This appeal arises out of a patent infringement suit brought by Finesse Wireless LLC against AT&T Mobility LLC. Finesse is the patent owner. Nokia of America Corporation intervened because the accused technology was Nokia equipment used in AT&T’s network, and AT&T and Nokia appealed together as the defendants-appellants. The case therefore centers on whether certain Nokia radios used by AT&T practiced the methods claimed in two Finesse patents, and if not, whether the district court should have taken the infringement issue away from the jury after trial.

The Federal Circuit’s opinion is not mainly about network contracts or damages modeling in the abstract. Its starting point is the accused radio technology and the specific claim language Finesse relied on. That means a law student reading the opinion should pay close attention to the relationship between the patent claims, the technical documentation, and the expert testimony at trial. The appeal turns on whether the evidence was enough to support the jury’s finding of infringement.

## The asserted patents

Finesse asserted claims from two patents: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775. Both patents concern ways of reducing interference caused by intermodulation products, or IMPs, in radios. Although the patents describe different methods, they share the same basic problem: unwanted signal mixing can create new signals that interfere with desired communications.

The ’134 patent, in broad terms, claims a method for isolating “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” estimating the resulting intermodulation products, and canceling them. The ’775 patent claims a method for generating intermodulation product cancellation signals in a receiver, using three signals and a set of multiplications described in the claim. The court’s later infringement analysis depends on whether the accused radios actually performed these claimed steps, as the claims require.

## Intermodulation products and passive intermodulation

The opinion explains the technology in a practical way. Radios are meant to operate on different frequencies so their signals do not interfere. But when signals encounter certain physical obstacles or imperfect components, they can mix and produce new frequencies. Those newly created frequencies are called intermodulation products, or IMPs. They are problematic because they can appear in-band, meaning within the frequency range of a desired signal, and can therefore disrupt communication.

When the mixing is caused by passive objects rather than active circuitry, the resulting interference is called passive intermodulation, or PIM. The opinion gives examples of passive causes such as metal fences, loose cable connections, and rusted connectors. This distinction matters because the patents are directed at canceling passive intermodulation, and the accused feature is a PIM cancellation function in Nokia radios.

For a student reading the opinion, the key point is that the patents do not merely concern generic signal processing. They target a particular interference problem in radio systems, and they claim specific signal-processing methods for identifying and canceling that interference.

## Internal PIM versus external PIM

The opinion also distinguishes between internal and external PIM. Internal PIM arises from mechanical issues inside the radio. External PIM comes from sources outside the radio. That distinction matters because the accused functionality involves how the radio system samples and models signals in relation to where the unwanted intermodulation is generated.

In the infringement analysis, the timing and location of signal generation become important. If a claimed “interference generating signal” is said to be sampled by the accused receiver, but the evidence shows that signal is not even generated until after sampling occurs, then the plaintiff may fail to prove infringement. That is exactly the kind of problem the Federal Circuit later identifies in its discussion of the ’134 patent.

## The accused feature: Nokia radios’ PIM cancellation functionality

Finesse accused AT&T of using Nokia radios that include a PIM cancellation, or PIM-C, feature. The theory was that the radios’ signal-processing operations corresponded to the patented methods. Finesse relied heavily on a Nokia technical document and on its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, to map the accused functionality onto the claim limitations.

That mapping task is central to the case. In patent cases, it is not enough to show that the accused device performs something generally similar to the invention. The plaintiff must prove that the accused product meets each claim limitation, either literally or under the proper infringement doctrine. Here, the dispute focused on whether the accused radios performed the specific signal sampling, signal isolation, and multiplication steps required by the claims.

## Trial outcome and damages award

The case was tried to a jury in the Eastern District of Texas. The jury found all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed. It also awarded Finesse a lump-sum damages amount of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents.

A lump-sum award means the jury selected a single amount intended to compensate for future use through the end of the patent term, rather than awarding a running royalty. That award became relevant only because the infringement findings were challenged after trial. Once the Federal Circuit concluded that the infringement verdicts could not stand, it vacated the damages award as well.

## Post-verdict motions

After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia filed several post-trial motions. They sought judgment as a matter of law, often abbreviated JMOL, on noninfringement and on damages. They also requested a new trial. A JMOL motion asks the court to enter judgment despite the jury verdict because the evidence, viewed in the required light, cannot support the verdict as a matter of law. A new trial motion asks the court to set aside the verdict for reasons such as evidentiary error, improper instructions, or a verdict against the great weight of the evidence.

The district court denied those requests. The defendants then appealed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and the denial of a new trial on damages. The Federal Circuit ultimately reversed the noninfringement rulings and, because there could be no damages award without infringement, vacated the damages award.

## Appellate jurisdiction and issues on appeal

The Federal Circuit had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), which gives it appellate jurisdiction over patent cases. On appeal, the court addressed two main issues: whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, and whether the damages award should be set aside.

The court’s procedural posture matters because it shapes the standard of review. The appeal was not a fresh trial of the facts. Instead, the court reviewed whether the trial evidence was legally sufficient to support the jury’s infringement findings. When a court reverses JMOL on noninfringement, as the Federal Circuit did here, the damages award cannot survive, so the separate damages challenge becomes largely moot in practical effect.

# Chapter 2: Appellate Standards and the Structure of the Infringement Inquiry

## Why regional circuit law governs review of JMOL and new-trial rulings

This opinion is a useful reminder that the Federal Circuit does not apply its own procedural law to every issue in a patent appeal. When the appeal concerns denial of judgment as a matter of law, or denial of a new trial, the Federal Circuit borrows the law of the regional circuit in which the district court sits. Because this case came from the Eastern District of Texas, the court applied Fifth Circuit standards to review both the JMOL rulings and the motion for a new trial. The patent-specific issues—such as claim construction and whether the evidence supported a finding of infringement—still fall within Federal Circuit patent law, but the appellate lens for the post-trial motions comes from the regional circuit.

That division matters because it affects the degree of deference the appellate court gives the jury and the trial judge. The parties were not asking only whether the patents were infringed as a matter of patent law; they were also asking whether the district court should have taken the case away from the jury or ordered a new trial after the verdict. The court’s first task was therefore to identify which body of law controlled each step of review.

## Fifth Circuit standard for reviewing denial of JMOL de novo

Under Fifth Circuit law, denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo. That means the appellate court does not defer to the district court’s judgment on the legal sufficiency of the evidence. Instead, it independently asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, is so one-sided that reasonable jurors could not have reached the result they did.

The opinion quotes the Fifth Circuit formulation: reversal is proper only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party” that no reasonable jury could reach a contrary conclusion. This is a demanding standard. It does not permit the appellate court simply to prefer one side’s account of the evidence. The court must uphold the verdict if the record contains enough evidence for a reasonable jury to find for the prevailing party.

In a patent case, this standard works alongside the ordinary federal rule that a party can obtain JMOL only when the evidence is legally insufficient to support the verdict. Here, the Federal Circuit applied that framework to the jury’s infringement findings. The question was not whether the panel thought the accused products infringed on a clean slate, but whether the trial record could sustain the jury’s verdict under the governing standard.

## Substantial evidence review of a jury’s infringement finding

The court also states the familiar substantial-evidence standard for reviewing a jury’s infringement finding. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party on the evidence presented. In practice, this standard is closely related to the JMOL inquiry, but it is framed from the perspective of appellate review of a jury’s factfinding.

This is important because infringement is often a mixed question of law and fact. Claim construction is a question of law, but comparing the properly construed claims to the accused device is a factual matter for the jury when the evidence is disputed. In this case, the court was not revisiting every patent issue itself. It was asking whether the evidence the plaintiff presented at trial was enough for a reasonable jury to find that the accused radios met the claim limitations.

The opinion repeatedly shows how this standard operates. The panel does not merely say that Finesse’s evidence was weak. It says the evidence did not rise to the level of substantial evidence because the expert’s testimony did not reliably connect the accused products to the claim limitations. That distinction is key: a verdict can survive even if the losing party offers a persuasive alternative theory, so long as the prevailing party offered enough evidence for a reasonable factfinder. But if the record lacks a coherent evidentiary basis for a critical limitation, substantial evidence is absent.

## Claim construction review: de novo with clear-error review for subsidiary factfinding

Claim construction is reviewed de novo, but the court notes that any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. This is a standard Federal Circuit formulation after *Teva*: the meaning of claim terms is ultimately a legal question, but if the district court resolves subsidiary factual disputes—especially disputes grounded in extrinsic evidence—those factual determinations receive clear-error review.

This opinion uses that framework to set the stage for the infringement analysis, though the panel’s main dispute was not about claim construction itself. In the ’775 patent discussion, for example, the district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not “unique.” That construction shaped the infringement inquiry, because Appellants argued the accused radios used only two unique signals, while Finesse argued three separately identifiable signals were enough. The appellate court did not need to redo the claim construction exercise from scratch because the construction issue was not the central source of error on appeal. Instead, it focused on whether the trial evidence matched even the district court’s construction.

The lesson for a student is that claim construction often supplies the framework for infringement, but the appeal may turn on whether the evidence fits that framework. A court can accept the construction and still reverse if the plaintiff failed to prove that the accused product met the construed limitation.

## The plaintiff’s burden of proof on infringement at trial

Finesse, as the patent owner and plaintiff, bore the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. That burden never shifts simply because the case goes to a jury. The plaintiff must present evidence showing each claim limitation is met, either literally or under the applicable infringement theory.

The opinion’s infringement analysis is built around that burden. For the ’134 patent, the critical issue was whether the accused radios sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” For the ’775 patent, the key issue was whether the accused radios generated cancellation signals using the claimed seven multiplications. In both instances, the court’s question was not whether the defendants disproved infringement, but whether Finesse affirmatively proved the missing elements.

That point is especially important when the plaintiff’s case depends on expert testimony. Patent infringement cases often require expert explanation because the technology is complex. But complexity does not relax the plaintiff’s burden. The plaintiff still has to provide a coherent evidentiary path from the accused product to every claim limitation.

## How the court distinguishes between legal claim-scope questions and evidentiary sufficiency questions

A major feature of the opinion is the court’s effort to separate two different kinds of disputes. One is a legal dispute about claim scope: what the claim language means, or whether prosecution history narrowed the claim. The other is an evidentiary dispute: whether the trial record shows the accused product falls within that scope.

The court’s treatment of the ’775 patent illustrates the difference. Appellants argued that Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two-signal implementations during prosecution. That is a legal claim-scope argument. The court rejected it, concluding there was no clear and unambiguous disavowal of the accused scope. But that did not end the case. Even after resolving claim scope in Finesse’s favor, the court still asked whether the evidence showed the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications. On that second question, the court ruled against Finesse.

This separation helps explain the structure of the opinion. A claim can be broad enough as a matter of law and yet still not be infringed because the trial evidence is insufficient. A plaintiff must win both battles: the claim must cover the accused product, and the proof at trial must establish that coverage with competent evidence.

## The significance of expert testimony in proving infringement

Expert testimony played the central role in both patents. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, was the principal witness connecting the Nokia technical documents to the asserted claims. The court’s analysis shows how patent infringement cases often rise or fall on whether the expert can present a stable, intelligible theory of mapping claim language onto technical features of the accused product.

For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells attempted to map “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” onto components shown in a Nokia document. For the ’775 patent, he tried to map three signals and seven multiplications onto the operations described in another Nokia document. In each instance, the court focused closely on what the expert actually said, not on what Finesse later argued the testimony meant.

This is a common appellate theme in patent cases: expert testimony is not enough merely because it sounds technical. It must actually support the legal elements of infringement. If the expert’s explanation is inconsistent, incomplete, or disconnected from the claim language, the testimony may not amount to substantial evidence.

## Why self-contradictory expert testimony can fail as a matter of law

The court’s most important evidentiary point is that a verdict cannot rest on self-contradictory expert testimony when the contradictions prevent a reasonable jury from finding a claim limitation met. The opinion relies on *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* for the proposition that when the party with the burden of proof bases its case on an expert who contradicts himself on a critical limitation, no reasonable jury can be expected to resolve the inconsistency in the plaintiff’s favor.

That principle is central to both patent claims in this case. With the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells repeatedly mapped the relevant signals to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, but that mapping created a timing problem because the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the sampling step. When challenged, he shifted his language, but he never clearly re-mapped the claim terms to a stable alternative such as x1 and x2. The court treated that as a fatal inconsistency, not as harmless imprecision.

The same basic problem arose with the ’775 patent. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document, but the document did not plainly show the seven claimed multiplications. The testimony and the document did not line up cleanly, and Finesse’s later attempt to reconstruct the mapping on appeal could not cure the evidentiary gap. The appellate court therefore concluded that the verdict could not stand as a matter of law.

For a law student, the lesson is that appellate courts do not treat every expert inconsistency as a mere credibility issue reserved for the jury. If the contradictions leave the record without a reliable evidentiary basis for an essential claim element, the court may hold that substantial evidence is lacking and reverse JMOL denial.

# Chapter 3: The ’134 Patent—Sampling Limitation, Expert Mapping, and Failure of Proof

## The ’134 Patent’s Objective: Reducing IMP Interference by Isolating and Cancelling Interference-Generating Signals

The ’134 patent is directed to a technique for reducing intermodulation product, or IMP, interference in radios. The basic idea is that when transmitted signals encounter obstacles or imperfections in the radio environment, they can combine and create new unwanted frequencies. Those unwanted frequencies are the intermodulation products. The patent’s solution is to identify the signals that generate those products, estimate the resulting interference, and then cancel it out. In other words, the patent claims a digital method for detecting the relevant signals, computing what interference they will produce, and subtracting that interference from the received signal path.

This case matters because the plaintiff, Finesse, had to prove not just that the accused radios performed some kind of interference cancellation, but that they performed the specific steps recited in the asserted claim. For the ’134 patent, the Federal Circuit focused on a threshold problem in the plaintiff’s proof: whether the accused radios sampled the required signals in the manner the claim demands.

## Representative Claim 1 and the Importance of Limitation 1[a]

Claim 1 is the representative claim for the analysis. The court centered its discussion on limitation 1[a], which requires “over-sampling” a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, where those received signals include both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The claim then proceeds to require isolation of those signals, computation of an estimate of the intermodulation products, cancellation of those products, and closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustment.

For infringement, this first limitation was crucial. If the accused radios did not sample a passband that actually included both categories of signals, then the infringement theory would fail at the outset. The court treated that limitation as a structural and temporal requirement: the claimed “interference generating signals” must be part of the signal set being sampled, not generated later in the processing chain.

## The Claim Requirement That the Receiver Sample Both Kinds of Signals

The court emphasized that Finesse, as the plaintiff, bore the burden of proving that the accused radios sampled a passband containing both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” That sounds straightforward, but in practice it required Finesse to connect the claim language to the actual operation of the accused Nokia radios in a precise way.

The problem was not simply whether the radios performed interference cancellation. It was whether the specific signals identified by Finesse were present at the claimed time and place in the accused architecture. Limitation 1[a] requires the receiver’s RF ADC to sample received signals that already include the interference-generating signals. If the asserted “interference generating signals” are created only later, after the sampling step, then the claim limitation is not met.

## Dr. Wells’s Reliance on Nokia Technical Documents

At trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, based his theory on Nokia technical documents showing how the accused radios functioned. This is common in patent cases: experts often use technical manuals, block diagrams, and design documents to map claim language onto an accused system. The expert’s job is to explain to the jury how the claim elements correspond to the accused device.

But the strength of that approach depends on the clarity and consistency of the mapping. Here, the Federal Circuit concluded that Dr. Wells’s testimony was not consistent enough to support the verdict.

## His Express Mapping of “Signals of Interest” to the DL(TX) Reference

Dr. Wells clearly and repeatedly identified the “signals of interest” as the downlink transmit reference, or DL(TX) reference, shown in the Nokia materials. He testified to that mapping more than once, both in live testimony and in his expert report. That part of the infringement theory was not the main point of dispute.

This matters because once an expert makes an express claim mapping, that testimony defines the theory the jury is asked to accept. Here, Dr. Wells tied one claim element to one signal path with specificity. The problem arose when he tried to do the same with the “interference generating signals.”

## His Express Mapping of “Interference Generating Signals” to the Modeled PIM Path

Dr. Wells also expressly mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path. That was the critical move in Finesse’s theory. The modeled PIM path was part of the Nokia documentation, and Dr. Wells treated it as the claim’s “interference generating signals.”

That mapping created a serious doctrinal and factual problem. If the modeled PIM path is the interference-generating signal, then the claim requires that signal to be present in the passband the receiver samples. But the accused architecture, as the defense pointed out on cross-examination, generated the modeled PIM path only after the RF ADC had already sampled the incoming signals. In other words, the alleged interference-generating signal was downstream from the sampling step, not upstream where the claim required it to be.

## The Critical Temporal and Architectural Problem

This is the core of the Federal Circuit’s reversal. Patent claims often impose a sequence of operations, and infringement requires the accused system to perform those operations in the required order. Here, the claim required sampling a received passband containing the interference-generating signals before later processing steps. But Dr. Wells’s own mapping placed the interference-generating signals in a modeled path generated after sampling.

That made the theory internally inconsistent. A signal cannot be sampled as part of the received passband if it is not generated until after the sampling occurs. The court treated that as a fatal flaw in the proof.

## Cross-Examination and the Attempted Pivot to x1 and x2

On cross-examination, Appellants pressed this point. They argued that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because the modeled PIM path was created downstream, after sampling. Faced with that objection, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony and said there were “two signals” on the red path, referring to x1 and x2 in the Nokia document.

That pivot might have mattered if Dr. Wells had clearly testified that x1 and x2, rather than the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, were the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” But he did not do that. Instead, he kept returning to the earlier mapping, saying the red path was the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. The result was ambiguity, not clarification.

## Why the Court Found the Pivot Unclear and Incomplete

The Federal Circuit was explicit that Dr. Wells never clearly tied x1 and x2 to the claim language. He referred to x1 and x2 as inputs, but he did not say with precision that x1 was the signal of interest and x2 was the interference-generating signal, or vice versa. Nor did he explain how the shift from the modeled PIM path to x1 and x2 affected the original mapping.

That omission mattered because the court was not willing to infer a new, workable infringement theory from scattered remarks. Patent infringement must be proven by evidence presented to the jury, not by appellate reconstruction after the fact. The expert’s testimony remained contradictory: he had expressly mapped the claims to one set of signals, then suggested another set without clearly substituting one for the other.

## The District Court’s Attempt to Infer a Workable Theory from Scattered Testimony

The district court tried to make sense of Dr. Wells’s testimony by inferring that when he referred to the red path, he must have meant x1 and x2. In effect, the court reconstructed a coherent story from fragments spread across the trial record. It concluded that the jury could find the RF ADC sampled both required categories of signals.

The Federal Circuit rejected that approach. It emphasized that the district court’s inference was not supported by clear testimony from Dr. Wells. The expert never gave the jury a clean explanation that aligned x1 and x2 with the claim terms. Instead, the district court had to piece together statements made at different points in the record, some of them many pages apart, to create a consistent theory.

## The Federal Circuit’s Rejection of Inferential Reconstruction

The appellate court held that this kind of inferential reconstruction could not sustain the verdict. The key problem was that the plaintiff’s own expert was the only source connecting the accused device to the claim limitation, and his testimony was self-contradictory. The court was not persuaded that the jury could reasonably rely on a theory the expert never clearly presented.

The Federal Circuit stressed that the evidence must support the specific infringement theory actually offered at trial. If the expert’s testimony is so confused that the jury would have to guess which signal corresponds to which claim element, then the plaintiff has not met its burden. The court viewed Dr. Wells’s testimony as falling into that category.

## Johns Hopkins v. Datascope and Contradictory Expert Testimony

To explain why the verdict could not stand, the court relied on Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. In that case, the court had held that when an expert’s testimony is internally contradictory, no reasonable jury could rely on it to find literal infringement of the challenged limitation. The principle is not that an expert must be flawless, but that a verdict cannot rest on testimony that changes so materially that it no longer supports a coherent infringement theory.

That precedent fit this case closely. Dr. Wells repeatedly said the claimed signals were the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, but the modeled PIM path could not have been sampled as claimed because it was created after the RF ADC operated. His later references to x1 and x2 did not cure the problem because he never clearly mapped those values to the claim elements. The court thus treated the testimony as insufficient under the substantial-evidence standard.

## Holding as to the ’134 Patent: No Substantial Evidence, So Denial of JMOL Reversed

The Federal Circuit ultimately held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the asserted ’134 claims on this record. Because the plaintiff’s expert testimony was unclear, contradictory, and failed to establish that the accused radios sampled a passband containing the claimed “interference generating signals,” the jury’s verdict lacked substantial evidence.

Accordingly, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the ’134 patent. Once that ruling fell, the damages award could not stand either, but the key point for this chapter is the infringement analysis: the plaintiff lost because its own expert’s mapping did not support the claimed sequence of signal sampling and interference cancellation.

# Chapter 4: The ’775 Patent—Claim Scope, Prosecution History, and the Seven-Multiplication Requirement

## The ’775 patent’s objective: receiver-side cancellation of passive IMPs using transmitter-signal copies

The Federal Circuit treated the ’775 patent as a different infringement problem from the ’134 patent. The patent is directed to interference cancellation in a receiver, specifically the cancellation of passive intermodulation products, or PIMs, using copies of transmitter signals. In the court’s summary, the claimed method generates intermodulation product cancellation signals “continuously and near real time” from transmitter-signal copies, with the goal of cancelling passive IMPs created in passive components after the transmitter’s high-powered amplifier and filter. That framing matters because the court did not decide the case on a general technical disagreement about radio interference. Instead, it focused on what the claim language actually required and whether the trial evidence matched those requirements.

## Representative claim 1 and the detailed recitation of seven third-order multiplications

Claim 1 was the representative claim for the ’775 patent. The portion that mattered on appeal was limitation 1[d], which requires generating the cancellation signals based on a power-series description of the nonlinear process and “given three signals S1, S2 and S3,” digitally multiplying and filtering seven specific products. Those products are listed explicitly in the claim: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. The court treated that list as important, not surplusage. The opinion makes clear that infringement did not turn merely on whether the accused system used some kind of multiplication or cancellation logic; it turned on whether the accused radios performed the particular set of seven multiplications recited in the claim.

## District court claim construction: “three signals S1, S2 and S3” must be separately identifiable, not necessarily unique

Before trial, the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” That construction came from the court’s reading of the intrinsic evidence, supplemented by inventor testimony that the signals could be the same or different. In other words, the district court did not accept AT&T and Nokia’s position that the claim required three distinct input signals with no overlap. Instead, it held only that the claim required three signals that could be individually identified in the operation of the method.

That construction narrowed the appellate dispute. The Federal Circuit did not have to decide whether the district court’s interpretation was right in every respect. The key question became whether, even under that construction, the trial evidence supported the jury’s finding of infringement.

## Appellants’ argument that prosecution amendments surrendered systems using only two unique input signals

AT&T and Nokia argued that Finesse had narrowed the claim during prosecution and effectively surrendered systems that used only two unique input signals. Their theory was that the applicant originally pursued broader language—“two or three signals”—and then amended the claim to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to overcome prior art. Appellants said that history showed the patentee gave up coverage of methods that could be implemented with only two unique inputs, which would have been relevant because the accused radios were said to operate with only two signals, x1 and x2.

This was a prosecution-history disclaimer argument. In patent law, the public record of how the applicant distinguished prior art can limit claim scope, but only if the applicant’s statements or amendments amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender. The Federal Circuit’s analysis stayed within that rule.

## The examiner’s rejection over Filipovic and the amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals”

The prosecution record showed that the examiner rejected a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious over Filipovic and other references. Finesse responded that Filipovic dealt with active intermodulation, not the passive intermodulation addressed by the claimed invention. The applicant also amended the claim to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and to include the seven multiplications. That history gave Appellants some footing for a narrowing argument, but it was not enough by itself to prove disavowal.

The court emphasized that the amendment occurred in the context of distinguishing the prior art’s subject matter, not necessarily in the context of limiting the claim to three unique physical inputs. The prosecution record therefore had to be read carefully.

## Why the Federal Circuit found no clear and unambiguous disavowal of systems with two unique inputs

The Federal Circuit rejected the view that the prosecution history clearly and unambiguously surrendered systems with two unique input signals. The court said there was no “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope over products that used two unique inputs. It read the amendment as doing less than Appellants claimed: at most, it required three separately identifiable signals.

That distinction is important. A claim can require three separately identifiable signals even if two of them are derived from or related to the same physical source. The court therefore declined to read the prosecution history as imposing a uniqueness requirement that the claim language itself did not state.

## The court’s acceptance that the claim does not require three unique input signals

Having rejected the surrender argument, the Federal Circuit accepted the district court’s basic understanding that the claim does not require three unique input signals. That point was not the decisive one, however. The court did not reverse because the claim needed three truly different inputs and the accused radios had only two. Instead, it moved to the separate and ultimately dispositive problem: the evidence still had to show that whatever signals were present, the accused radios performed the seven specific products listed in limitation 1[d].

That is a useful lesson in reading patent opinions. Even if a party wins a claim-construction issue, infringement can still fail for lack of proof on the remaining claim elements.

## The decisive evidentiary issue: the claim still requires performance of seven listed multiplications

The court’s infringement analysis turned on the fact that the claim explicitly required seven multiplications. The district court and the parties focused on whether the accused radios had three signals or two, but the Federal Circuit treated the seven-multiplication requirement as independently necessary. In other words, once the claim construction issue was resolved against Appellants’ prosecution-history theory, Finesse still had to prove that the accused radios actually performed the full set of claimed operations.

This is where substantial evidence review became critical. The jury’s verdict could stand only if a reasonable jury could find, based on trial evidence, that the accused radios satisfied that limitation. The court concluded they could not.

## Dr. Wells’s infringement theory based on Nokia technical documents

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied heavily on Nokia technical documents to map the accused radios onto the claim. His theory was that the radios multiplied three separately identifiable signals and that the operations shown in the Nokia document corresponded to the seven multiplications in the claim. He testified that the accused radios satisfied the limitation by following the structure shown in the document.

But the opinion repeatedly highlights a problem with that testimony: Dr. Wells did not provide a clean, stable explanation of how the document’s signals and operations matched the claim language. The court saw that instability as fatal.

## The Nokia document’s actual operations: modulus and complex-conjugate expressions rather than the seven claimed products

The Federal Circuit pointed out that the Nokia document did not simply list the seven claim products. Instead, it involved modulus and complex-conjugate operations. The document listed only three distinct multiplications, including expressions like x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|. Those are not, on their face, the same as the seven products recited in the claim.

That difference mattered because infringement had to be shown by proof, not by general similarity. The court was unwilling to infer that a document involving different mathematical expressions necessarily satisfied the exact seven-product requirement without a clear explanation connecting the two.

## The three distinct multiplications identified at trial

At trial, Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document. The district court nonetheless concluded those three distinct multiplications could satisfy the seven-multiplication limitation because each could correspond to multiple claim-listed products. The Federal Circuit disagreed with that move.

The opinion makes clear that identifying three distinct operations is not enough unless there is evidence showing how those operations map onto each of the seven claimed multiplications. The record here did not supply that mapping in a way the jury could rely on.

## Why three distinct multiplications could not simply be treated as equivalent to the seven claimed multiplications without a trial-supported mapping

The court rejected the idea that the seven listed claim multiplications could be collapsed into three merely because some of them might be mathematically related. The claim language itself listed seven separate products, and the accused-document evidence did not establish that those products were all performed. The court was especially concerned that Finesse’s mapping required assumptions the jury never heard clearly explained at trial.

The point is not that a patent claim can never be satisfied by functions expressed differently in an accused system. It is that the plaintiff must explain the equivalence with evidence. Here, the court found that explanation missing.

## The absence of testimony explaining how the accused operations correspond to each of the seven recited claim terms

This was the heart of the Federal Circuit’s analysis. Dr. Wells did not testify, in a clear and itemized way, how each accused operation corresponded to each of the seven recited multiplications. The opinion stresses that he did not walk the jury through a complete mapping. Instead, Finesse tried to reconstruct the mapping later, including on appeal, by stitching together statements from different parts of the record.

That approach was too late and too speculative for substantial-evidence review. The jury needed evidence at trial that could support a finding on the seven-multiplication limitation, and the court held it did not have that evidence.

## The court’s rejection of appellate-stage efforts to repair the mapping

On appeal, Finesse attempted to explain the mapping more concretely, but the Federal Circuit refused to accept a repaired theory that was not presented to the jury in a usable form. The opinion is explicit that appellate counsel’s reconstruction could not substitute for trial testimony and exhibits. If the theory was not clearly presented below, it could not support the verdict on appeal.

This is a recurring theme in appellate review of jury verdicts: the court asks whether the trial record, not later advocacy, supports the finding.

## The oral-argument reference to ten multiplications and why it did not cure the evidentiary deficiency

Finesse also argued at oral argument that Nokia’s materials showed not just seven multiplications, but ten. The Federal Circuit was not persuaded. Simply pointing to a larger set of operations does not solve the problem unless the record identifies which subset corresponds to the seven claim-required multiplications. The court noted that neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells explained how to get from the ten listed operations to the seven required by the claim, or how those ten related to the three distinct multiplications Dr. Wells had identified.

So the “ten multiplications” point did not cure the core problem. The issue was not whether the document contained many mathematical expressions. The issue was whether the accused radios performed the specific seven that the claim demanded, and the record did not answer that question.

## Holding as to the ’775 patent: no reasonable jury could find the seven-multiplication limitation satisfied

The Federal Circuit therefore held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the ’775 patent. Even accepting the district court’s construction that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” need only be separately identifiable and not unique, Finesse still failed to prove that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. The court concluded that the evidence was insufficient to support the verdict, reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, and, because noninfringement disposed of liability, vacated the damages award.

The practical takeaway is that claim construction is only the first step. Once the claim meaning is set, the patentee must still prove each required operation with trial evidence that a reasonable jury can actually use. Here, the Federal Circuit found that the proof fell short on the ’775 patent’s central mathematical limitation.

# Chapter 5: Disposition, Damages Consequences, and Lessons from the Opinion

## The relationship between liability findings and the damages award

The end of the opinion is short because the court’s liability rulings do most of the work. The jury had returned a single, lump-sum damages award of more than $166 million based on its findings that the asserted claims of both patents were infringed. Once the Federal Circuit concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the asserted claims of either the ’134 patent or the ’775 patent, the foundation for the damages award disappeared. In a patent case, damages compensate for infringement; if the infringement verdict is undone, the damages award based on that verdict cannot stand.

That is why the court did not treat damages as an independent island of error requiring a separate merits analysis. The verdict form and the record showed that the award was tied to the successful infringement theories. When those theories failed as a matter of law, the damages award could not survive.

## Review of the district court’s denial of a new trial on damages under Fifth Circuit abuse-of-discretion standards

The opinion briefly notes the standard of review for the district court’s denial of a new trial. Unlike JMOL, which the Federal Circuit reviews through the lens of the regional circuit’s JMOL standard, a new-trial denial is reviewed for abuse of discretion under Fifth Circuit law. That is a deferential standard. It asks whether the district court made a clear error of judgment in denying a retrial.

But the court did not need to apply that standard to any damages-specific argument in detail. Because the infringement verdicts were reversed, the court had already determined that the judgment on liability could not stand. Once liability fell, there was no need to decide whether the district court also abused its discretion in refusing a new trial on damages. The damages issues became unnecessary to resolve independently.

## Why the Federal Circuit did not need to resolve the damages challenges independently after reversing infringement

This is a recurring appellate pattern in patent cases. A damages challenge may be raised in the alternative, but if the appellate court reverses the underlying liability determination, it often vacates the damages award without reaching the finer damages questions. That is what happened here. The court expressly stated that because it reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents, it vacated the damages award.

This means the court did not decide whether the jury had properly valued the infringement, whether the lump-sum calculation was methodologically sound, or whether a new trial on damages would otherwise have been warranted. Those questions became moot in light of the liability ruling.

## Vacatur of the entire damages award as a consequence of noninfringement JMOL

“Vacate” means the appellate court nullifies the damages award rather than merely reducing or modifying it. Here, vacatur was complete because the damages award was not separated by patent or claim in a way that allowed any portion to survive. The jury had awarded one lump sum for the remaining life of the patents, and the appellate court reversed the verdicts on all asserted claims. With no infringement verdict left intact, there was nothing to support any part of the award.

This is an important procedural point for reading patent opinions. A damages award is only as secure as the liability findings that support it. When the court wipes out the liability determination entirely, vacatur of damages follows as a consequence rather than as a separate substantive ruling.

## Formal disposition: reversed and vacated, with costs to appellants

The opinion ends with the formal disposition: “REVERSED AND VACATED.” That means the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award. The court also awarded costs to the defendants-appellants. The costs line is routine but significant in showing that AT&T and Nokia prevailed on appeal.

For a law student, the dispositive language is worth reading carefully. The court did not simply reverse on one patent and remand for recalculation. It reversed the noninfringement rulings and vacated damages entirely, signaling a complete defeat for the plaintiff’s trial judgment.

## The opinion’s practical lesson on precision in expert claim mapping

The core practical lesson of the case is that infringement proof in a technically complex case must be precise, consistent, and intelligible. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, tried to map claim language onto portions of Nokia technical diagrams, but his testimony became self-contradictory and ambiguous. The Federal Circuit treated that ambiguity as fatal because Finesse bore the burden of proving infringement, and the jury could not be asked to infer a clear claim-mapping theory that the expert never actually presented.

This is especially important in patent cases involving signal-processing or other systems with multiple paths, transformations, and labels. It is not enough for an expert to point to a diagram and say it “looks like” the claim. The expert must explain, in a way the jury can understand, how each claimed limitation corresponds to the accused system.

## The danger of relying on diagrams and labels without accounting for system timing and signal generation order

The ’134 patent portion of the opinion shows a classic timing problem. Dr. Wells initially mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, but the accused system generated that modeled PIM path only after the RF ADC sampled the incoming signals. That created a logical contradiction: a signal cannot be sampled before it exists.

The appellate court emphasized this timing mismatch. A diagram label or color-coding convention cannot substitute for proof that the accused system actually performs the claimed sequence. When the claim requires sampling of a passband containing certain signals, the plaintiff must show that those signals are present at the relevant point in time. The court refused to accept an infringement theory that ignored the order in which the system actually generated and processed signals.

## The difference between claim-construction success and evidentiary sufficiency at trial

The ’775 patent portion illustrates another important lesson: winning a claim-construction battle does not automatically win the infringement case. The district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily unique signals. That construction gave Finesse some room, but it did not answer the separate question whether the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications.

Appellate review of claim construction asks what the claim means. Review of infringement asks whether the accused device meets that meaning with evidence sufficient for a reasonable jury. Even if the construction is favorable, the plaintiff still must prove the accused system satisfies every limitation. Here, Finesse could not bridge the gap between the Nokia document and the specific seven-multiplication claim language.

## How the case illustrates that appellate courts will not reconstruct an infringement theory the expert did not clearly present to the jury

Perhaps the most important appellate lesson is that the Federal Circuit will not rescue a poorly articulated expert theory by reconstructing it from scattered record citations after the fact. Finesse tried on appeal to assemble a clearer mapping from the Nokia materials and Dr. Wells’s testimony, but the court refused to do that work for it. The court stressed that the supposed mapping was not actually presented to the jury in a clear, coherent form.

That matters because substantial-evidence review is deferential to the verdict, but it still requires a record basis that a reasonable jury could have relied on. If the expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent, or if the key claim mapping is never plainly explained, the verdict can fail as a matter of law. In other words, a patent plaintiff cannot expect the appellate court to supply the missing logic between a technical diagram and the claim language. The proof has to be made at trial.
