Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Chapter 1: The Alleged Pump-and-Dump Scheme and the Case’s Procedural Posture

## The defendants’ social media presence and claimed status as skilled traders

The case begins with a group of defendants who built large followings on social media by presenting themselves as knowledgeable and successful stock traders. According to the indictment, they posted frequently about their own trading activity and cultivated an audience that trusted their market advice. That background matters because the government’s theory is not just that the defendants traded securities, but that they used their online influence as the mechanism for the fraud.

In a securities-fraud case like this, the alleged fraud is tied to how the defendants communicated with the market. The indictment says that their followers were not random investors; they were people who saw the defendants as credible sources of trading insight. That made the social-media posts especially important, because the posts allegedly carried an appearance of expertise and authenticity that helped induce followers to act.

## How the alleged pump-and-dump scheme operated: buying, pumping through misrepresentations, and dumping at inflated prices

The indictment describes a classic “pump and dump” scheme. In simplified form, the government alleges that the defendants first bought a security, then “pumped” up its price by making false and misleading statements about it, and then “dumped” their own holdings by secretly selling once the price had been driven up by the market reaction to those statements. The alleged profit came from selling at the inflated price created by the very misinformation they spread.

That sequence is central to understanding the case. The “pump” was not just enthusiastic commentary or optimistic prediction. The government says the defendants posted false information about their trading positions and the likely future price of the securities, creating a misleading picture that encouraged others to buy. If other investors bought in reliance on those posts, their purchases would help drive the price higher. The “dump” then occurred when the defendants sold into that price increase, capturing the gain before the market corrected.

The indictment alleges that this was not a one-off event but a coordinated scheme involving multiple defendants. It says they collectively used social media to manipulate trading interest and market price, then reaped profits when the price rose. The alleged total profit from the scheme was $114 million, which gives some sense of the scale the government attributed to the conduct.

## The indictment’s allegation that followers were induced to buy securities based on false and misleading posts

A key allegation in the indictment is that the defendants’ posts induced their followers to purchase securities. This detail is important because it connects the misrepresentations to the alleged harm. The theory is that the defendants did not merely express opinions or discuss the market; they allegedly misled investors into buying securities they otherwise might not have purchased, or buying them at prices they otherwise would not have paid.

That is why the indictment frames the conduct as fraud “in connection with” securities. The alleged deception was not collateral to the securities transactions. It was the means by which the defendants allegedly caused followers to part with money in the market. In other words, the false posts were the engine of the scheme.

## The alleged profits from the scheme

The indictment alleges that the defendants profited $114 million from the scheme. In a pump-and-dump prosecution, the profit figure is not itself an element of the offense, but it helps illustrate the alleged motive and the structure of the fraud. The government’s theory is that the defendants’ financial gain came from the artificial price inflation they caused through deception.

That alleged profit also matters to the later legal dispute. The defendants argued that the government’s theory was really about depriving followers of “valuable economic information,” not about taking money or property. The government responded that the scheme was designed to induce investors to spend money on securities through falsehoods, which is a more traditional fraud theory. The profit allegation supports that view by showing that the defendants allegedly benefited from the money investors put into the market.

## The charged offenses: conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and substantive securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2

The superseding indictment charged the defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Section 1348 is the federal securities-fraud statute at issue, and § 1349 supplies the conspiracy charge. Section 2 provides aiding-and-abetting liability.

For the student reading the opinion, the important point is that the case was not about whether the defendants were accused of wrongdoing in a general sense. The real question was whether the indictment adequately alleged the specific federal offenses. That is why the Fifth Circuit focused on whether the indictment pleaded a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” under the securities-fraud statute.

## Knight’s guilty plea and the remaining defendants’ motion to dismiss

One defendant, Knight, pleaded guilty. The remaining defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. A motion to dismiss an indictment is a pretrial challenge. At that stage, the court does not decide whether the government can prove its case at trial; instead, it asks whether the indictment adequately alleges an offense.

That distinction is critical. The defendants were not arguing, at this stage, that the government had no evidence or that the allegations were false. They were arguing that, even if the allegations were accepted as true, the indictment still failed as a matter of law. That set up the appellate issue: whether the indictment’s allegations, taken as true, stated a federal securities-fraud offense.

## The district court’s reasoning: no allegation of deprivation of a traditional property interest

The district court dismissed the indictment because it believed the government had not alleged that the defendants schemed to deprive their followers of a traditional property interest. Instead, the court understood the indictment as alleging only that the defendants deprived their followers of potentially valuable economic information. On that view, the indictment was said to rest on an inadequate theory of fraud.

This reasoning reflects an important limitation in federal fraud law: not every form of deceit is necessarily fraud under the federal statutes. The district court treated the case as if the government were trying to convert misinformation into a property deprivation simply because the information was economically valuable. The Fifth Circuit later rejected that framing, but the district court’s analysis is the reason the case reached the appellate court.

## The government’s appeal and the question presented to the Fifth Circuit

The government appealed, arguing that the indictment did in fact allege a fraud scheme. The core question before the Fifth Circuit was whether the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. Put more concretely, the appellate court had to decide whether the allegations described merely the deprivation of information, or instead a deceptive scheme that induced investors to part with money by buying securities.

That procedural posture frames the rest of the opinion. The Fifth Circuit was not deciding guilt or innocence. It was deciding whether the indictment, accepted as true, was legally sufficient to go forward. The court ultimately answered yes and reversed the dismissal.

# Chapter 2: How Courts Evaluate the Sufficiency of an Indictment

## De novo review of a district court’s dismissal of an indictment

The Fifth Circuit began by identifying the procedural posture of the case: the district court had dismissed the superseding indictment before trial, and the government appealed that dismissal. At that stage, the court of appeals reviewed the issue **de novo**, meaning it gave no deference to the district court’s legal conclusion that the indictment was insufficient. That standard matters because the appellate court was not deciding whether the government could prove its case, only whether the charging document legally stated an offense.

This framing also explains why the opinion is careful to focus on the indictment itself. The question on appeal was not whether the defendants actually engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme or whether the evidence would be strong enough at trial. The only question was whether the indictment, on its face, adequately alleged a federal securities-fraud offense.

## The rule that courts must accept the indictment’s allegations as true at this stage

When a court evaluates an indictment’s sufficiency, it must take the allegations in the indictment as true. The Fifth Circuit quoted its prior cases for the rule that the court asks whether, assuming the allegations are accurate, the indictment states an offense. This is a critical procedural limitation. It means the court does not weigh competing factual narratives, resolve disputes about intent, or decide whether the defendants can produce a nonfraudulent explanation for their conduct.

That approach is especially important in a case like this one, where the indictment alleges that defendants posted false and misleading information to induce followers to buy securities. The court did not test the truth of those allegations. Instead, it asked whether those allegations, if true, fit the statutory offense of securities fraud.

## The three core functions of an indictment: pleading the elements, giving notice, and protecting against double jeopardy

The opinion restates the familiar functions of an indictment. First, it must enumerate the prima facie elements of the offense charged. Second, it must fairly inform the defendant of the charges so the defendant can prepare a defense. Third, it must be specific enough to protect the defendant against a later prosecution for the same offense, satisfying double jeopardy concerns.

These functions help explain why an indictment must do more than state a label like “fraud.” It must tell the defendant what conduct the government says was unlawful and what legal elements that conduct satisfies. Here, the indictment described the alleged pump-and-dump scheme in enough detail to identify the securities-fraud theory being pursued: defendants allegedly bought securities, posted false and misleading statements to inflate prices, induced others to buy, and then sold at a profit.

## The requirement that an indictment allege each material element of the offense

The Fifth Circuit emphasized that a sufficient indictment must allege each **material element** of the offense. This is the central doctrinal point of the procedural section. An element is “material” if it is part of what the government must prove to obtain a conviction. If the indictment leaves out an essential element, it fails as a matter of law even if the narrative seems suspicious or morally blameworthy.

In this case, the defendants’ argument was not that the indictment lacked detail about the scheme. Instead, they argued that it failed to allege the kind of property deprivation required by the fraud statutes. So the sufficiency question turned on whether the indictment alleged the legally required elements of securities fraud: a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud. The court’s later discussion of Ciminelli and Kousisis addressed exactly that issue.

## Why the appeal concerned legal sufficiency, not proof at trial

A common mistake when reading indictment-sufficiency cases is to treat them like miniature trials. That is not what the Fifth Circuit was doing. The court’s role was limited to deciding whether the indictment stated an offense on its face. It did not ask whether the defendants really misled investors, how much money they made, or whether the victims suffered actual losses.

This distinction explains why the court rejected the district court’s focus on whether the indictment alleged deprivation of a “traditional property interest.” At the indictment stage, the court was not deciding the merits of the fraud theory after evidence had been presented. It was deciding whether the allegations, if accepted as true, fit within the statutory definition of securities fraud.

## How this standard limited the Fifth Circuit’s task to whether an offense was stated

Because of the indictment standard, the Fifth Circuit’s task was narrow. It had to determine whether the charging instrument, taken as true, alleged facts that satisfy the legal elements of securities fraud. Once the court concluded that the indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced followers to buy securities and that they did so with the intent to obtain money, the legal sufficiency problem was resolved.

That is why the opinion’s procedural section matters so much. It explains why the court did not need to decide whether the defendants’ scheme was successful, whether the victims were ultimately harmed in a net sense, or whether the defendants’ conduct was especially sophisticated. The only question was whether the indictment stated an offense. The Fifth Circuit held that it did, and that conclusion shaped the rest of the opinion.

# Chapter 3: The Governing Fraud Framework Under § 1348 and Its Relationship to Mail and Wire Fraud

## The statutory language of 18 U.S.C. § 1348

The Fifth Circuit begins its analysis with the securities fraud statute itself. Section 1348 makes it a crime to devise or execute a scheme either to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” or to “obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” The court treats these two clauses as defining the basic federal securities-fraud offense the indictment must plausibly allege. That matters because the district court had dismissed the indictment on the theory that it did not allege deprivation of a “traditional property interest.” The Fifth Circuit’s task was therefore to decide whether the indictment fit within the statutory concept of fraud as understood in the federal fraud cases.

## The Fifth Circuit’s observation that there is limited circuit precedent directly construing § 1348

The opinion emphasizes that there is “scant caselaw” in the Fifth Circuit interpreting § 1348. That limited precedent means the court does not treat the statute as having a large body of independent circuit doctrine. Instead, it looks to other fraud decisions for guidance. This is a common move when a statute borrows familiar fraud language but has not yet generated much direct appellate interpretation. The court signals that, in this setting, the important task is to identify the core fraud concepts that Congress incorporated into § 1348 and to apply them consistently with the broader fraud tradition.

## Why courts use mail and wire fraud cases to interpret securities fraud concepts

The court explains that § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” so courts often give the terms similar treatment. That means mail and wire fraud cases supply the interpretive vocabulary for understanding what counts as a “scheme to defraud,” what “intent to defraud” requires, and why money or property must be the object of the scheme. The court does not say securities fraud is identical to mail or wire fraud in every respect, but it does treat those cases as highly persuasive because the statutory phrasing and doctrinal structure are closely related. For the reader, the key point is that the court is not inventing a new securities-fraud standard from scratch; it is applying familiar federal fraud principles to a securities context.

## The two required elements: a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud

Relying on its own recent decision in Greenlaw, the Fifth Circuit states that securities fraud requires two elements: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The opinion then explains each one. This is the core analytical framework for the rest of the case. Once the defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception, the dispute narrowed to whether that deception amounted to a scheme to defraud and whether the indictment adequately alleged the necessary fraudulent intent.

## Meaning of a scheme to defraud: material deception aimed at obtaining something of value

A “scheme to defraud” is not just any dishonest conduct. The court describes it as a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the victim. That formulation is important because it ties deceit to a property-oriented objective. The focus is not merely on lying in the abstract, but on lying in a way designed to induce the victim to part with money or property. In this case, that distinction is central to the court’s rejection of the district court’s dismissal. The government alleged that defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities. In the court’s view, that allegation describes a classic fraud-type scheme because the alleged deception was used to cause victims to part with money.

## Meaning of intent to defraud: intent to deceive plus intent to cause harm from the deceit

The court also defines “intent to defraud” as an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm to result from the deceit. That formulation helps distinguish fraud from mere deceitful conduct that does not aim at a legally cognizable injury. But the opinion makes clear that “harm” in this setting does not mean that the defendant must specifically intend to leave the victim economically worse off in a net sense. Instead, the relevant harm is the deprivation of money or property through the fraudulent scheme. This point becomes important when the defendants argue that they only intended to profit, not to harm anyone. The Fifth Circuit ultimately rejects that argument because the statute does not require proof that the victim ended up with a worse bargain overall.

## The importance of money or property as the object of fraud

The opinion repeatedly returns to a central principle: for federal fraud, the object of the scheme must be money or property. That principle is what distinguishes actionable fraud from the mere deprivation of information or some other intangible interest. The district court had relied on the notion that the indictment alleged only deprivation of valuable economic information. The Fifth Circuit rejects that framing because the indictment alleges more: that defendants misled followers into buying securities, thereby obtaining the followers’ money. The object of the scheme was not simply to withhold truthful market information; it was to induce a transfer of money through false pretenses. In the court’s analysis, that places the indictment squarely within the traditional money-or-property concept that federal fraud statutes protect.

## Defendants’ concession that the indictment adequately alleged deception, narrowing the dispute

The defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception. That concession significantly narrowed the issue before the court. If deception was not in dispute, the only remaining question was whether the indictment alleged deception of the kind that federal fraud law treats as criminal: deception used to obtain money or property, with the requisite fraudulent intent. The court’s framework shows why that concession mattered. Once deception was admitted, the dispute became one about the legal significance of the deception, not about whether false statements were pleaded at all. That is why the opinion focuses so heavily on what counts as a scheme to defraud and what counts as intent to defraud under the securities-fraud statute.

# Chapter 4: Scheme to Defraud After *Ciminelli*—Why This Indictment Alleges Fraudulent Inducement, Not a Mere Right-to-Control Theory

## The defendants’ argument that the indictment alleged only deprivation of valuable economic information

The first major issue in *United States v. Constantinescu* was whether the superseding indictment alleged fraud in the legally relevant sense. The defendants argued that it did not. In their view, the indictment said only that they misled their social-media followers by withholding or distorting valuable market information, thereby interfering with the followers’ ability to make informed investment decisions. If that were all the government had pleaded, the case would begin to resemble a “right-to-control” theory—an approach the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*. The district court accepted that framing and dismissed the indictment because it believed the government had failed to allege the deprivation of a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit’s task, then, was not to decide whether the defendants behaved dishonestly in some broad sense. The question was narrower: did the indictment allege a scheme to obtain money or property by fraud, or did it allege only that the defendants deprived victims of useful information?

## *Ciminelli*’s facts and the Supreme Court’s rejection of the right-to-control theory

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, it helps to see what *Ciminelli* actually held. There, the government prosecuted the defendant for wire fraud under a theory that the victim was deprived of “the right to control [its] assets” because it lacked valuable information when making an economic decision. The Supreme Court rejected that approach. It explained that the federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not an abstract entitlement to accurate information for its own sake. So if the government’s theory is simply that the defendant lied or concealed facts in a way that impaired the victim’s decisionmaking, that is not enough unless the deception was aimed at obtaining money or property.

That distinction matters a great deal. *Ciminelli* did not say that all fraud cases involving misstatements about economic matters fail. Rather, it rejected a theory in which the “property” supposedly taken was the victim’s right to make informed choices. The Court treated that as too intangible and too disconnected from the kind of property the fraud statutes cover.

## What the right-to-control theory means: withholding information necessary for discretionary economic decisions

The opinion carefully explains the right-to-control theory as a doctrine under which the government tries to prove fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information needed to make discretionary decisions. In practical terms, the theory focuses on information rather than money: the harm is that the victim was unable to choose as wisely as it otherwise would have.

That is why the Fifth Circuit describes the theory as one in which the victim’s property interest is said to be “the right to control [its] assets.” The Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* held that this is not a traditional property interest. A person may have a practical interest in being well-informed, but the fraud statutes do not criminalize every lie that makes a business or investor less informed. There must be a scheme aimed at obtaining money or property.

## Why valuable economic information alone is not a traditional property interest under the federal fraud statutes

The opinion emphasizes that “valuable economic information” by itself is not enough. This is the core lesson the Fifth Circuit draws from *Ciminelli*. A defendant cannot be convicted of fraud merely because his lies caused someone to make a worse economic decision. The statutes are not designed to punish deception as such; they are designed to punish deceit used to obtain property.

This is an important doctrinal limitation. It prevents the fraud statutes from becoming a general anti-misrepresentation law covering any dishonesty that affects commercial behavior. In other words, the law requires more than the loss of truthful information. It requires a targeted deprivation of money or property.

## The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that this case did not rest on a right-to-control theory

The Fifth Circuit held that the indictment against Constantinescu and the other defendants did not rely on a right-to-control theory at all. That was the decisive move. The indictment did not say the defendants merely deprived followers of information that would have helped them decide whether to invest. Instead, it alleged that the defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities and thereby part with their money.

That difference is what saved the indictment. The court treated the government’s theory as one of obtaining money through fraud, not one of interfering with a victim’s informational autonomy. The defendants were not accused simply of making investment decisions harder for others; they were accused of tricking followers into purchasing securities so the defendants could profit.

## The indictment’s allegation that defendants induced followers to purchase securities by means of misrepresentations

The indictment alleged a classic “pump and dump” scheme. According to the government, the defendants bought securities, posted false and misleading statements about those securities on social media to inflate demand and price, induced their followers to buy, and then sold their own holdings at a profit. The key allegation for the court’s analysis was that the followers were “induced to purchase the security” by the defendants’ misrepresentations.

That language matters because it describes a transaction in which the victims were caused to part with money. The followers were not merely deprived of information in the abstract; they were allegedly manipulated into buying securities they otherwise might not have purchased. The court treated that as an allegation of fraudulently obtaining money through false pretenses.

## The role of fraudulent inducement as recognized in *Kousisis*

The Fifth Circuit relied heavily on the Supreme Court’s then-recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. *Kousisis* confirmed that the fraud statutes cover a fraudulent-inducement theory: a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The critical point is that the fraud is complete when the defendant obtains money or property by false pretenses, even if the victim receives something of value in return.

The Fifth Circuit used *Kousisis* to show that this case fits the fraudulent-inducement model. The defendants allegedly used false statements to induce purchases of securities. That is not a claim that the victims were harmed only by being denied accurate information. It is a claim that the victims were tricked into transferring money in a transaction initiated by fraud.

## Why fraudulent inducement protects money and property even when deception concerns the terms or circumstances of the transaction

One of the most important teaching points in the opinion is that fraudulent inducement covers deception about the circumstances of a transaction, not just deception about whether anything of value changes hands. The government does not have to prove that the victim received nothing in return or that the victim suffered a net financial loss. Under *Kousisis*, the relevant question is whether the defendant used deceit to obtain the victim’s money or property.

That is why the court rejected the defendants’ argument that their followers received securities that may have had market value. The presence of some value in what the victim received does not eliminate fraud if the victim was induced to make the purchase by false statements. The fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” between the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss.

## The court’s bottom-line conclusion that the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to deprive victims of money through fraud

The Fifth Circuit’s bottom-line conclusion was straightforward: this indictment alleged more than a deprivation of information. It alleged a scheme in which the defendants used false and misleading statements to induce followers to buy securities, thereby parting with money. That is enough to allege a scheme to defraud under the securities fraud statute.

So, although *Ciminelli* bars a prosecution that rests only on the victim’s loss of informational control, it did not help these defendants. The court viewed the government’s case as a traditional fraud case grounded in fraudulent inducement. Because the indictment alleged that the defendants obtained money by deceit, it sufficiently pleaded a protected property deprivation and survived dismissal.

# Chapter 5: Intent to Defraud After *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit’s Holding, and the Opinion’s Significance

## The defendants’ argument that they intended to profit rather than to harm their followers

The second major issue in *United States v. Constantinescu* was whether the superseding indictment adequately alleged an “intent to defraud.” The defendants tried to recast the case as one about self-enrichment, not victim harm. In their view, the indictment showed only that they wanted to make money from their trading strategy, while their followers received securities that were allegedly worth the market price they paid. On that account, the followers were not really “harmed,” and the fraud statutes should not apply.

That argument mattered because fraud crimes have long involved some tension between a defendant’s gain and a victim’s loss. If the defendants could persuade the court that fraud requires proof that they sought to worsen the victim’s bottom line, then the indictment might fail even if the posts were false and even if the defendants profited from the scheme. The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing.

## *Kousisis* and the Supreme Court’s rejection of a requirement that the defendant seek to worsen the victim’s bottom line

The court’s central answer came from the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. There, the Court rejected the idea that fraud liability depends on proving that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. The Fifth Circuit described *Kousisis* as foreclosing the defendants’ attempt to turn lack of net economic harm into a defense.

The key point in *Kousisis* is that a fraud defendant violates the statute when he uses deceit to obtain the victim’s money or property, even if he gives something back of equal value and even if he does not intend to cause a net loss. The Fifth Circuit quoted *Kousisis* for the proposition that the fraud statutes are satisfied when the defendant schemes to “obtain” the victim’s money or property, “regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” That principle was decisive here because the indictment alleged that the defendants used false social-media posts to induce purchases of securities. The focus, then, was on what the defendants sought to obtain through deceit, not whether they aimed to inflict a measurable financial loss.

## Why the fraud statutes focus on obtaining the victim’s money or property through deceit, not on proving net pecuniary loss

The court’s reasoning reflects the property-based structure of federal fraud law. Under the statute as the court understood it, the government must show that the defendant used deception to obtain money or property. It need not show that the defendant’s scheme was designed to make the victim poorer in the end. That distinction is important. A fraud case can exist even when the victim receives something of value in return, so long as the defendant’s deceit induced the transfer of money or property.

The Fifth Circuit emphasized that the indictment alleged just that: the defendants posted false and misleading statements “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” In other words, the alleged deception was not merely informational; it was instrumental. The misinformation was alleged to be the means by which the defendants caused their followers to part with money in exchange for securities. That is enough, at the pleading stage, to allege an intent to defraud.

## The significance of the allegation that defendants posted false information so others would be induced to buy

The indictment’s wording did a lot of work for the government. It did not merely say that the defendants spoke inaccurately or exaggerated their own trading success. It alleged a deliberate causal chain: the defendants posted false and misleading information in order to induce purchases. That language supports both the “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” elements because it shows purposeful deception aimed at obtaining money through securities transactions.

The court treated that allegation as sufficient even without further detail about how the victims were ultimately affected in the market. The pleading standard is not proof beyond a reasonable doubt; it is whether the indictment alleges the elements of the offense. On that question, the statement that the defendants used false posts to induce purchases was enough to allege that they meant to obtain money by deception.

## Why followers’ receipt of securities at market value did not defeat the fraud theory at the indictment stage

The defendants argued that their followers got what they paid for: market-value securities. The Fifth Circuit said that does not defeat the fraud theory. Under *Kousisis*, a defendant can be liable even when he provides something of value in return, so long as the defendant obtained the victim’s money or property through deceit. The court specifically noted that the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, and that receipt of something of equal value does not eliminate the offense.

This is an important teaching point: fraud law is not limited to “net loss” cases. A defendant cannot avoid liability simply by claiming that the victim was not made worse off in the end. If the defendant used false statements to induce the transfer of money or property, the fraud may be complete once the property is obtained. That is why the court said the defendants’ argument failed at the indictment stage.

## The court’s statement that intending to profit is not meaningfully different from intending the deprivation when money is the object of the fraud

The Fifth Circuit also rejected the defendants’ attempt to distinguish profit from harm. The indictment alleged that the defendants made false posts, induced purchases, and then sold their own holdings for a profit. The court said that, in this context, the fact that the defendants intended to profit was “a distinction without a difference.” Why? Because the scheme’s object was to obtain money through deceit, and the alleged profit motive showed that the defendants “had money in mind.”

That reasoning reflects a practical view of fraudulent schemes. In a pump-and-dump case, the defendants’ gain is typically tied directly to the victims’ induced purchases. The fraud does not become non-fraud simply because the defendants were motivated by enrichment rather than by an explicit desire to impoverish others. The court’s point was that the fraudulent inducement of purchases itself is the injury relevant to the fraud statutes.

## Use of Fifth Circuit and out-of-circuit authority to reinforce the absence of a mirror-image loss requirement

The court reinforced its conclusion with additional authority. It cited *United States v. Baker* for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” relationship between the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss. That is, the government does not have to prove that whatever the defendant gained was exactly matched by an equivalent loss to the victim. The court also cited a recent Seventh Circuit case applying *Kousisis* in a similar setting, showing that other courts are reading the Supreme Court’s decision to reject a strict net-loss requirement.

These citations helped situate the opinion within a broader post-*Ciminelli* and post-*Kousisis* landscape. After *Ciminelli*, courts are careful not to expand fraud beyond traditional property interests. But after *Kousisis*, they also reject the argument that fraud requires proof of economic impoverishment in the end. The Fifth Circuit used those authorities to show that the indictment fit squarely within the modern, property-based fraud framework.

## The Fifth Circuit’s holding that the indictment adequately alleged both scheme and intent to defraud

The court ultimately held that the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. On the intent question, the allegation that defendants posted false and misleading information to induce securities purchases was enough. The court reasoned that the scheme alleged an intent to obtain money by deceit, and that is what the fraud statutes require. The defendants’ profit motive did not undermine that conclusion.

This holding is important because it resolves the sufficiency question at the pleading stage, not after trial. The Fifth Circuit did not decide whether the government could prove its allegations. It held only that the indictment alleged enough to state the offense. That is a narrower, but still significant, ruling.

## The disposition: reversal of the dismissal and remand for further proceedings

Because the indictment adequately alleged a scheme and intent to defraud, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings. That disposition means the prosecution may continue in the district court. The defendants may still raise defenses later, but they cannot obtain dismissal simply by arguing that the indictment failed to allege a traditional property deprivation or a victim net loss.

## The opinion’s significance for securities fraud prosecutions involving social media manipulation and for the post-*Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* understanding of property-based fraud

The opinion is significant in two ways. First, it strengthens the government’s ability to prosecute social-media-driven pump-and-dump schemes as securities fraud. The court treated false promotional posts that induce securities purchases as allegations of property fraud, not merely as allegations of misleading market commentary. That matters in modern markets, where social-media influence can rapidly move prices and investors.

Second, the opinion illustrates how lower courts are applying *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* together. *Ciminelli* limits fraud to traditional property interests and rejects a free-floating right-to-control theory. But *Kousisis* makes clear that the government does not need to prove a net-loss or bottom-line-harm theory once the defendant uses deceit to obtain money or property. *Constantinescu* sits at that intersection: the indictment survived because it alleged fraudulent inducement to part with money, not merely the deprivation of information. For students reading fraud cases after these Supreme Court decisions, that is the key doctrinal lesson.
