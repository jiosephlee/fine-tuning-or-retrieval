Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Must Securities Fraud Aim to Harm Investors? The Fifth Circuit Says No—and That Matters

## A broader fraud theory for modern markets

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* matters because it pushes federal securities-fraud doctrine further away from a loss-centered model of fraud and squarely toward a deception-centered one. In a “pump and dump” case involving social-media stock promotion, the defendants argued that the indictment failed because it did not allege they sought to deprive investors of a traditional property interest or to leave them economically worse off. The court rejected that framing. Relying heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, the panel held that the government need only allege that defendants used material falsehoods to obtain money or property; it need not also allege an intent to inflict net pecuniary harm on the victims.

That holding may sound technical, but its implications are not. If the object of fraud is obtaining investors’ money through false pretenses, the government does not have to prove that the fraudsters wanted to “hurt” investors in the intuitive sense of causing them to come out behind. In markets, where buyers often receive something that may look superficially equivalent in value—shares, contracts, services, or access—the court’s approach gives prosecutors a much clearer path.

## The court’s move: from “harm” to “money in mind”

The defendants’ argument tried to leverage the distinction the Supreme Court drew in *Ciminelli* between traditional property and the so-called right to control information. The Fifth Circuit had little trouble dispatching that point. This indictment did not rest on a theory that the victims were deprived merely of economically valuable information. Instead, it alleged that defendants misled followers into purchasing securities, thereby causing those followers to part with money.

The more interesting issue was intent. The defendants said the indictment alleged only that they wanted to profit, not that they intended to harm anyone. The panel treated that as a nonstarter after *Kousisis*. There, the Supreme Court rejected the idea that fraud requires proof the defendant sought to worsen the victim’s bottom line. What matters is whether the defendant schemed to obtain money or property through material deception. The Fifth Circuit imported that logic into the securities context and concluded that the indictment adequately alleged intent to defraud because it said the defendants posted false information “so that other investors were induced to purchase” the securities.

That is the key analytical move: fraud is not limited to schemes that aim at net economic injury. It includes schemes aimed at extracting money by lies, even if the defendant believes the transaction might leave the victim with something of some value in return. In the court’s words, the defendants had “money in mind.”

## Why this matters in pump-and-dump cases

This is particularly important in pump-and-dump prosecutions because those schemes often work by creating the appearance of a genuine investment opportunity. The promoter may point to a stock, make an upbeat prediction, and then quietly unload shares after the price rises. The buyer gets stock, which may have some market value at the moment of purchase, so the defense often tries to recast the case as one about misleading optimism or withheld information rather than outright theft.

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion cuts off that line of defense at the indictment stage. If the government alleges that the promoter used falsehoods to induce purchases, that is enough even if the buyer received securities nominally worth what was paid, or even if the defendant did not specifically intend the buyer to suffer a direct loss. The fraud, on this view, is complete when money is obtained by material misrepresentation.

That doctrinal posture is a significant advantage for prosecutors in cases involving online trading influencers, crypto promoters, and other modern market intermediaries who monetize trust and attention. These cases often involve a blend of puffery, selective disclosure, and outright misstatement. By emphasizing that fraud does not require an allegation of net victim loss, the court gives the government a broader and cleaner theory to work with.

## The practical and doctrinal tradeoff

There is a real doctrinal elegance to the panel’s reasoning. It tracks *Kousisis* closely and avoids reviving a right-to-control theory by another name. It also fits comfortably with the longstanding idea that fraud punishes deceitful acquisition of property, not just schemes that leave a victim worse off in some balance-sheet sense.

But the opinion also illustrates the continuing expansion of federal fraud law into spaces where market actors often operate with mixed motives and messy information. In securities markets, every trade involves some combination of valuation, expectation, and risk. If the government need not show that the defendant intended to make the victim economically worse off, then the line between aggressive promotion and criminal fraud becomes easier to cross—especially where the promoter is simultaneously trading on the same asset being hyped.

That does not mean ordinary optimism or self-interested trading becomes criminal. Material falsity still matters, and the indictment here alleged a classic deception: false representations about trading positions and price potential designed to trigger purchases before a coordinated dump. But the opinion confirms that once the government can plead materially false inducement to part with money, the absence of a pleaded intent to cause net loss is no defense.

## The downstream effect

The likely downstream effect is to strengthen federal charging authority in online securities manipulation cases, particularly where victims receive some tangible asset in exchange for their money. Defense counsel will still be able to contest materiality, scienter, reliance, and the details of the scheme. But arguments that the defendant merely sought profit, or that the victim got something of apparent value back, will carry less weight after *Constantinescu*.

Just as importantly, the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning reinforces a broader trend after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*: fraud doctrine is being re-centered on deception plus acquisition of money or property, not on some separately required showing of economic injury. In modern trading and promotional practices, that is likely to make the criminal law more adaptable—and more expansive. Whether that is a feature or a bug will depend on one’s view of federal fraud law. But after *Constantinescu*, one thing is clear: in securities fraud, the government does not have to prove that defendants set out to make investors poorer. It is enough that they set out to get investors’ money by lying.
