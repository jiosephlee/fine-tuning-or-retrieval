Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# From ‘Right to Control’ to Fraudulent Inducement: How the Fifth Circuit Reframed Pump-and-Dump Liability After *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is important because it shows how federal fraud doctrine is settling after the Supreme Court’s rejection of the “right-to-control” theory in *Ciminelli v. United States* and its later embrace of fraudulent inducement in *Kousisis v. United States*. The question in the case was whether an indictment charging a social-media “pump and dump” scheme alleged a cognizable fraud, or instead merely alleged that investors were deprived of potentially useful market information. The district court took the narrower view and dismissed. The Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment alleged something more concrete: that defendants used false statements to induce followers to part with money by buying securities.

That distinction is doing a lot of work. If the government had charged only that defendants distorted information in a way that affected followers’ trading choices, *Ciminelli* would loom large. But once the alleged deception is framed as a device to get investors to spend money on securities they otherwise would not have purchased, the case looks much more like classic fraud. That is the doctrinal move the Fifth Circuit makes, and it likely will matter well beyond this one indictment.

## The court’s central move

The defendants argued that the indictment was really about information, not property. According to that theory, their social-media posts supposedly deprived followers of economically valuable information about the stocks and the defendants’ positions. That is exactly the sort of “right-to-control” theory the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli*, where the government cannot convert a deprivation of information into a property fraud.

The Fifth Circuit was not persuaded, and its reasoning is straightforward. The indictment did not stop at saying defendants polluted the marketplace with misleading posts. It alleged that they posted false information “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” thereby parting with money. In other words, the theory was not that followers were deprived of information abstractly; it was that they were tricked into buying securities. That is a fraudulent-inducement theory, and *Kousisis* says that theory remains squarely within the federal fraud statutes.

The court emphasized that distinction carefully. *Ciminelli* rejected a theory that treated “the right to valuable economic information” as the property interest itself. But *Kousisis* explained that fraudulent inducement is different because the victim actually parts with money or property, even if the fraudster provides something in return. On that view, the injury is not merely informational. It is transactional.

## Why *Kousisis* mattered

The real doctrinal significance of the opinion is how heavily it leans on *Kousisis* to preserve fraud prosecutions after *Ciminelli*. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* made clear that fraud does not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. That matters here because the defendants tried to recast the scheme as one aimed at profit, not harm.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing. A defendant’s purpose of making money by deceiving investors is enough; the statutes do not require a separate showing that the defendant sought to inflict net loss or that the victim received no value at all. The court quoted *Kousisis* for the proposition that fraud is complete when the defendant induces a deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses. That is an important clarification for prosecutors in securities cases, where defendants often argue that victims may have received something of market value and that the scheme was therefore merely manipulative rather than fraudulent.

The panel’s answer is that market value is not a safe harbor. Even if the victims received securities that were worth something, the fraud lies in the induced transaction itself. If the purchase was procured by lies designed to enrich the speaker, the indictment states a fraud offense.

## What this means for securities fraud cases

The decision gives prosecutors a fairly clean roadmap for post-*Ciminelli* securities-fraud cases involving social media, influencers, or other modern market intermediaries. The key will be how the indictment is drafted. If the government can allege that false statements were used to induce purchases, not simply to manipulate information flows, *Constantinescu* suggests the case will fit comfortably within traditional fraud doctrine.

That may seem like a narrow distinction, but in practice it is a meaningful one. Securities markets run on information, and many manipulative schemes involve false statements that distort what investors know. After *Ciminelli*, defendants will continue to argue that such cases are really about information rights. *Constantinescu* responds by recentering the analysis on the transaction: did the deception cause investors to hand over money? If so, the case looks like fraud rather than a stripped-down information offense.

At the same time, the opinion does not suggest that every misleading market statement is actionable as fraud. The government still must allege and prove material deception, a scheme to defraud, and intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit did not expand the statute so much as it refused to let *Ciminelli* swallow longstanding inducement-based fraud theories.

## The broader doctrinal takeaway

The deeper lesson of *Constantinescu* is that *Ciminelli* may narrow one theory of fraud without seriously destabilizing the rest of the doctrine. Defendants have hoped that *Ciminelli* would force courts to treat many deception cases as mere information harms. But *Kousisis* blunted that effort by reaffirming that the fraud statutes still reach schemes designed to induce the victim to part with money or property, even where the victim receives something of value in return.

That is why the Fifth Circuit’s language about “money in mind” is so important. The opinion treats the defendants’ profit motive as evidence of fraudulent intent, not as an escape hatch from it. In doing so, it makes clear that the law does not ask whether the scheme was intended to inflict a net economic loss. It asks whether the object of the deception was to obtain money or property.

That approach preserves a broad swath of federal fraud prosecutions, especially in the securities context, while still honoring *Ciminelli*’s rejection of a freestanding informational-property theory. Whether one sees that as a principled distinction or a convenient prosecutorial workaround depends on how much weight one gives to form versus substance. But for now, the Fifth Circuit has made its choice: when false social-media pitches are used to induce stock purchases, federal fraud law applies.

## Bottom line

*Constantinescu* is a useful example of how lower courts are adapting to the Supreme Court’s recent fraud decisions. The Fifth Circuit did not deny the force of *Ciminelli*. Instead, it confined *Ciminelli* to cases where the government tries to turn information itself into property. Once the indictment alleges that deception induced investors to spend money on securities, *Kousisis* supplies the rest of the answer.

For securities prosecutors, that is a strong signal. For defendants, it is a warning that after *Ciminelli*, the path to dismissal is narrower than it first appeared. And for fraud doctrine more generally, the case suggests that the right-to-control theory may be dead, but fraudulent inducement remains very much alive.



# Must Securities Fraud Aim to Harm Investors? The Fifth Circuit Says No—and That Matters

## A broader fraud theory for modern markets

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* matters because it pushes federal securities-fraud doctrine further away from a loss-centered model of fraud and squarely toward a deception-centered one. In a “pump and dump” case involving social-media stock promotion, the defendants argued that the indictment failed because it did not allege they sought to deprive investors of a traditional property interest or to leave them economically worse off. The court rejected that framing. Relying heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, the panel held that the government need only allege that defendants used material falsehoods to obtain money or property; it need not also allege an intent to inflict net pecuniary harm on the victims.

That holding may sound technical, but its implications are not. If the object of fraud is obtaining investors’ money through false pretenses, the government does not have to prove that the fraudsters wanted to “hurt” investors in the intuitive sense of causing them to come out behind. In markets, where buyers often receive something that may look superficially equivalent in value—shares, contracts, services, or access—the court’s approach gives prosecutors a much clearer path.

## The court’s move: from “harm” to “money in mind”

The defendants’ argument tried to leverage the distinction the Supreme Court drew in *Ciminelli* between traditional property and the so-called right to control information. The Fifth Circuit had little trouble dispatching that point. This indictment did not rest on a theory that the victims were deprived merely of economically valuable information. Instead, it alleged that defendants misled followers into purchasing securities, thereby causing those followers to part with money.

The more interesting issue was intent. The defendants said the indictment alleged only that they wanted to profit, not that they intended to harm anyone. The panel treated that as a nonstarter after *Kousisis*. There, the Supreme Court rejected the idea that fraud requires proof the defendant sought to worsen the victim’s bottom line. What matters is whether the defendant schemed to obtain money or property through material deception. The Fifth Circuit imported that logic into the securities context and concluded that the indictment adequately alleged intent to defraud because it said the defendants posted false information “so that other investors were induced to purchase” the securities.

That is the key analytical move: fraud is not limited to schemes that aim at net economic injury. It includes schemes aimed at extracting money by lies, even if the defendant believes the transaction might leave the victim with something of some value in return. In the court’s words, the defendants had “money in mind.”

## Why this matters in pump-and-dump cases

This is particularly important in pump-and-dump prosecutions because those schemes often work by creating the appearance of a genuine investment opportunity. The promoter may point to a stock, make an upbeat prediction, and then quietly unload shares after the price rises. The buyer gets stock, which may have some market value at the moment of purchase, so the defense often tries to recast the case as one about misleading optimism or withheld information rather than outright theft.

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion cuts off that line of defense at the indictment stage. If the government alleges that the promoter used falsehoods to induce purchases, that is enough even if the buyer received securities nominally worth what was paid, or even if the defendant did not specifically intend the buyer to suffer a direct loss. The fraud, on this view, is complete when money is obtained by material misrepresentation.

That doctrinal posture is a significant advantage for prosecutors in cases involving online trading influencers, crypto promoters, and other modern market intermediaries who monetize trust and attention. These cases often involve a blend of puffery, selective disclosure, and outright misstatement. By emphasizing that fraud does not require an allegation of net victim loss, the court gives the government a broader and cleaner theory to work with.

## The practical and doctrinal tradeoff

There is a real doctrinal elegance to the panel’s reasoning. It tracks *Kousisis* closely and avoids reviving a right-to-control theory by another name. It also fits comfortably with the longstanding idea that fraud punishes deceitful acquisition of property, not just schemes that leave a victim worse off in some balance-sheet sense.

But the opinion also illustrates the continuing expansion of federal fraud law into spaces where market actors often operate with mixed motives and messy information. In securities markets, every trade involves some combination of valuation, expectation, and risk. If the government need not show that the defendant intended to make the victim economically worse off, then the line between aggressive promotion and criminal fraud becomes easier to cross—especially where the promoter is simultaneously trading on the same asset being hyped.

That does not mean ordinary optimism or self-interested trading becomes criminal. Material falsity still matters, and the indictment here alleged a classic deception: false representations about trading positions and price potential designed to trigger purchases before a coordinated dump. But the opinion confirms that once the government can plead materially false inducement to part with money, the absence of a pleaded intent to cause net loss is no defense.

## The downstream effect

The likely downstream effect is to strengthen federal charging authority in online securities manipulation cases, particularly where victims receive some tangible asset in exchange for their money. Defense counsel will still be able to contest materiality, scienter, reliance, and the details of the scheme. But arguments that the defendant merely sought profit, or that the victim got something of apparent value back, will carry less weight after *Constantinescu*.

Just as importantly, the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning reinforces a broader trend after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*: fraud doctrine is being re-centered on deception plus acquisition of money or property, not on some separately required showing of economic injury. In modern trading and promotional practices, that is likely to make the criminal law more adaptable—and more expansive. Whether that is a feature or a bug will depend on one’s view of federal fraud law. But after *Constantinescu*, one thing is clear: in securities fraud, the government does not have to prove that defendants set out to make investors poorer. It is enough that they set out to get investors’ money by lying.



# Section 1348 as Mail-and-Wire Fraud in Securities Clothing: The Fifth Circuit’s Borrowing Problem

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is important for a reason that goes beyond a single “pump and dump” prosecution: it shows how far courts are willing to carry ordinary fraud doctrine into the securities context. The panel had little Fifth Circuit precedent to work with on 18 U.S.C. § 1348, so it did what courts often do in that situation—it borrowed heavily from mail- and wire-fraud cases. That move helped the government survive a motion to dismiss. But it also raises a deeper question: when § 1348 is interpreted as if it were just mail and wire fraud “in securities clothing,” do we gain doctrinal coherence, or do we risk flattening a statute that is aimed at a very different market setting?

## What the Fifth Circuit held

The court reversed dismissal of an indictment charging defendants with running a social-media-based pump-and-dump scheme. According to the indictment, they misrepresented their trading positions and the likely price of securities to their followers, induced those followers to buy, and then sold into the inflated price. The district court had held that the indictment failed because it did not allege deprivation of a traditional property interest—only the loss of “potentially valuable economic information.”

The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing. Relying on *Ciminelli* and especially *Kousisis*, the panel said the indictment was not a right-to-control case at all. It alleged fraudulent inducement: the defendants allegedly used false statements to get followers to part with money in exchange for securities. That is enough, the court said, because § 1348 reaches schemes to obtain money or property by deception. The panel also rejected the notion that the government had to plead or prove that defendants intended net economic harm. It was enough that the scheme aimed to obtain money through material falsehoods, even if victims received securities that had some value.

## The court’s method: borrowing first, differentiating later

The opinion is notable less for its bottom-line result than for its method. The panel explicitly said that securities fraud under § 1348 borrows “key concepts” from mail and wire fraud, and then used that borrowed framework to define both the “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” elements. That approach is understandable. Congress drafted § 1348 in broad terms, the Fifth Circuit has not developed much standalone doctrine under it, and the Supreme Court’s recent fraud decisions have narrowed some of the government’s older theories. In that landscape, cross-referencing the general fraud cases offers a stable doctrinal anchor.

But there is a cost. Securities fraud is not just fraud plus a traded asset. The securities markets involve price formation, disclosure norms, intermediaries, and a regulatory structure that is distinct from ordinary commercial deception. A lie in a market is not always best understood as a lie that induces a one-off transfer of property. Sometimes it is a manipulation of market conditions, investor behavior, or price signals—harms that are real even if they do not fit neatly into the old property-centric fraud template.

By recasting the case as straightforward fraudulent inducement, the court avoided a more difficult issue: whether securities fraud should have its own account of harm, one that takes market integrity and price manipulation seriously even when the government cannot point to a classic deprivation of money at the moment of the lie. The panel’s approach is doctrinally clean, but it may be too clean for securities markets.

## What *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* did here

The court’s reliance on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* is persuasive as far as it goes. *Ciminelli* rejected the “right-to-control” theory, which had tried to convert lost information into property. That theory does not fit comfortably here, and the panel was right to say the indictment alleged more than mere informational deprivation.

*Kousisis* is the more important case for the government. It made clear that fraud does not require a net-loss theory and that a defendant can commit fraud by tricking a victim into parting with money, even if the victim receives something of value in return. That logic maps easily onto pump-and-dump allegations. If the defendants induced purchases through falsehoods, the fact that buyers received securities does not automatically erase the fraud.

Still, there is a subtle move in the opinion that deserves attention. The court treats the fraud as complete because the defendants induced followers to buy securities under materially false pretenses. That works under *Kousisis*, but it also means the opinion leans heavily on a general fraud concept of transactional deception rather than on a securities-specific concept of market manipulation. In other words, the conduct is prosecuted as fraud against individual buyers, even though the harm alleged is really a distortion of the market for the securities themselves.

## The doctrinal implications

The main downstream effect is that § 1348 may continue to converge with mail and wire fraud, at least in the Fifth Circuit. That convergence has advantages: it gives prosecutors a familiar toolkit and reduces the need to reinvent fraud doctrine for each statute. It also limits the defense bar’s ability to argue that securities fraud requires some special, extra element beyond deceptive inducement.

But the convergence may also make § 1348 look less like a securities statute and more like a carbon copy of general fraud law. That could matter in future cases where the alleged misconduct is not a classic misrepresentation to a buyer, but something more market-specific—selective disclosure, coordinated trading, misleading hype, or other conduct that manipulates price without fitting neatly into a bilateral transaction model. If courts keep importing mail-and-wire-fraud categories without adapting them to market structure, they may leave too little room for the distinctive ways securities fraud harms investors.

There is also a broader interpretive question lurking here. When Congress created § 1348, it did so against the backdrop of securities enforcement, not just generic fraud. One could read that as an invitation to use general fraud doctrine only as a baseline, not as the whole theory. The panel, by contrast, treats the securities statute as essentially parasitic on mail and wire fraud. That approach is workable, but it may not fully account for why securities fraud was enacted in the first place.

## Bottom line

*Constantinescu* is a strong government win and a predictable one after *Kousisis*. The indictment alleges a familiar fraud story: deception, inducement, and profit. But the opinion is also a reminder that § 1348 doctrine is still being built by analogy rather than by a distinctly securities-law theory of fraud. That may be enough for now. The harder question is whether it will be enough when the next case involves market manipulation that does not look so much like a lie to a buyer, but more like a lie to the market itself.



# Finfluencers, Criminal Fraud, and the Future of Market Manipulation Cases

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is an important marker in the government’s growing campaign against social-media-driven stock promotion. At its core, the case asks a deceptively simple question: when does online hype about a security cross the line from puffery or bad investing advice into criminal fraud?

The court’s answer is that an indictment can survive if it alleges not just deceptive commentary, but a scheme to induce investors to part with money through false statements about trading positions and the security’s prospects, followed by secret sales for profit. That may sound straightforward, but it matters a great deal. The opinion signals that prosecutors do not need to repackage these cases as “right-to-control” prosecutions or prove a net economic loss to victims. If the charging instrument alleges fraudulent inducement to buy securities, the fraud statutes can apply even in a world of meme stocks, finfluencers, and real-time online trading communities.

## The court’s framing of the fraud theory

The district court had dismissed the indictment on the theory that the government alleged only deprivation of valuable economic information, not a traditional property interest. That approach tracks the Supreme Court’s rejection of the “right-to-control” theory in *Ciminelli v. United States*. But the Fifth Circuit held that the indictment here went further.

The key move in the opinion is its reliance on *Kousisis v. United States*, where the Supreme Court reaffirmed that a fraudulent-inducement theory is distinct from right-to-control fraud. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment alleged that defendants falsely portrayed their trading positions and the potential price of the securities in order to induce followers to buy. That, the court reasoned, is a scheme to obtain money or property: the victims parted with cash to purchase securities on the basis of misrepresentations. The fraud is not merely that followers lacked good information. The fraud is that they were allegedly tricked into transferring money.

That distinction is doctrinally significant. After *Ciminelli*, defense lawyers have had some success arguing that market-manipulation allegations really amount to information-rights cases in disguise. This opinion cuts against that move. Where the government pleads that false statements were used to induce purchases, *Constantinescu* treats the case as classic fraud, not a novel theory about control over information.

## Secret selling and false position claims do the heavy lifting

One of the most useful aspects of the opinion is its implicit roadmap for what facts matter most in these prosecutions. The indictment did not rest on general claims that the defendants were influential traders or that they talked up stocks online. It alleged specific mechanics: they bought securities, pumped prices through false or misleading posts, induced followers to buy, and then dumped their holdings secretly.

That sequence matters because it ties deception to financial extraction. False statements about one’s own trading positions are especially potent in this setting. If an influencer claims to be buying or holding a stock, followers may infer there is independent market intelligence or genuine confidence behind the recommendation. If the influencer is actually unloading the position, the statement is not just misleading; it is part of the inducement structure that turns social trust into a profit engine.

For prosecutors, that means secret selling allegations will likely remain central. They help show both the scheme and the intent to defraud. They also make the case look less like a disputed opinion about market prospects and more like a coordinated deception designed to transfer money from retail traders to the promoters.

## The court’s view of “intent to defraud” is broad, but not novel

The defendants argued that the indictment failed because it alleged they intended to profit, not to harm their followers. The Fifth Circuit rejected that contention by leaning heavily on *Kousisis*, which held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off.

That is an important clarification in the market-manipulation context. Defendants in pump-and-dump cases often claim that everyone understood the speculative nature of the trade, or that followers still received a security that could have had value. But the court’s reasoning suggests that the absence of net loss, or the presence of some nominal value in return, does not defeat fraud if the defendant used material falsehoods to obtain money.

That said, the opinion is not saying every exaggerated stock pitch is criminal. It is saying that when the government alleges a deliberate deception intended to cause purchases, the fact that the defendant was seeking profit rather than vindictive harm does not take the case outside the fraud statutes. In other words, motive to enrich oneself can be enough when the means are fraudulent inducement.

## What this means for finfluencer cases

This decision will likely strengthen the government’s hand in cases involving online stock promoters, especially where the evidence shows coordinated posting, undisclosed positions, and suspicious trading around the posts. It is easier for prosecutors to frame these cases as traditional fraud when there is a clean sequence of deception and cash-out. The opinion also suggests that district courts should be cautious about dismissing indictments too early on abstract theory grounds when the charging document pleads concrete misstatements and a money-making scheme.

For practitioners advising influencers, brokers, or content creators, the boundary lines remain important. Lawful touting, aggressive optimism, and even recklessness are not automatically crimes. But the risk rises sharply when a promoter makes factual claims about holdings, intent, or market expectations while secretly trading against the audience’s interests. The presence of disclaimers will not necessarily save a defendant if the overall course of conduct is alleged to be deceptive. Nor will the argument that followers were “informed traders” necessarily matter if the alleged falsehoods were material to the decision to buy.

The hardest cases will still be the gray-zone ones: opinions dressed up as certainty, promotional language that borders on falsehood, or partial disclosures that omit enough context to mislead but not enough to make a clean falsity allegation. *Constantinescu* does not resolve those cases. But it does tell lower courts that when the indictment alleges false claims about trading positions and price prospects, coupled with secret liquidation for profit, the fraud statutes can reach the conduct without inventing a new property theory.

## Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit has not created a new doctrine here; it has applied existing fraud principles to a modern fact pattern. But that is exactly why the opinion matters. Social-media stock promotion is now a recurring enforcement target, and *Constantinescu* shows how traditional fraud law can be adapted to digital-market manipulation without stretching beyond the Supreme Court’s recent limits in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*.

For the government, the message is encouraging: plead the money transfer, the false statements, and the dump. For defense counsel, the lesson is to scrutinize whether the government has truly alleged deception that induced a property transfer, or only online hype and informational asymmetry. That line will define the next generation of finfluencer prosecutions.
