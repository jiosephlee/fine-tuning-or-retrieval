Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How much of mail/wire fraud doctrine can courts import into 18 U.S.C. § 1348 securities fraud cases?
Question:
The opinion says there is 'scant caselaw' on § 1348 in the Fifth Circuit, so the court borrows concepts from mail and wire fraud cases, including Ciminelli and Kousisis. Is that always appropriate? Section 1348 has two clauses and is specifically about securities, while the mail and wire fraud statutes have their own wording and history. Are courts treating § 1348 as basically a securities-specific analogue to mail/wire fraud, or are there meaningful doctrinal limits on importing those cases? I'm wondering whether the Fifth Circuit's reasoning depends on textual similarities, legislative purpose, or just precedent by analogy.
Answer:
Not wholesale. The Fifth Circuit’s point in **Constantinescu** is narrower: because there is “scant caselaw” construing § 1348 in the circuit, courts may **borrow “key concepts”** from mail- and wire-fraud doctrine **so long as those concepts fit § 1348’s text**. The opinion expressly says § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and that courts therefore often give the terms “similar treatment.” But that is an analogy-based move, not a blank check.

### What the court treated as transferable
The court imported the familiar fraud elements of:

- a **scheme to defraud**, and
- **intent to defraud**,

and then used **Ciminelli** and **Kousisis** to explain what those concepts mean when the object is money or property. In the opinion’s words, securities fraud is committed when a defendant “engaged in deception” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of the fraud.

### The limit
The limit is the statute’s own language. Section 1348 is not just a carbon copy of the mail/wire fraud statutes; it has **two clauses** and is specifically framed around securities. So courts should not import doctrines that:

1. **conflict with § 1348’s text**, or
2. **smuggle back in a theory the Supreme Court has rejected**.

That is exactly how the Fifth Circuit uses **Ciminelli**: not as a generic federal-fraud gloss, but to reject a theory that would make fraud turn only on depriving victims of “valuable economic information.” The court says that would be a right-to-control theory, and that is not a traditional property interest. But it also says the indictment here is different because it alleges **fraudulent inducement to part with money to buy securities**—which fits the money/property language.

Likewise, **Kousisis** is imported only for the proposition that the fraud statutes do **not** require the victim to suffer net economic loss. The opinion uses that to reject the defendants’ claim that because followers might have received securities of market value, there was no fraud. The court says that is no answer where the object was to obtain the victims’ money through deception.

### Bottom line
So the Fifth Circuit is **not** treating § 1348 as identical to mail/wire fraud. It is treating it as a **securities-specific fraud statute that uses similar core fraud concepts**, which can be informed by mail/wire-fraud precedent **where the statutory language overlaps**. The doctrinal borrowing is therefore **real but limited**: useful for defining “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud,” but not for importing theories that the text of § 1348—or the Supreme Court’s fraud cases—do not support.
