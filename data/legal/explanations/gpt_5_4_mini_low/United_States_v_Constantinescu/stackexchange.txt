Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why was the indictment dismissed for failure to state an offense if the government alleged obvious lies in a pump-and-dump scheme?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The defendants were indicted for securities fraud based on allegedly false social-media posts used to pump stock prices, which seems like classic fraud. But the district court still dismissed the indictment for failure to state an offense. At the indictment stage, does the court assume all factual allegations are true and only ask whether those facts match the statutory elements? If so, what exactly was the district court saying was missing here, and why did the Fifth Circuit think that was a legal error rather than just a disagreement about the facts?
Answer:
The key point is that the district court did **not** say the government’s allegations were unbelievable or insufficiently detailed. It said something narrower and more legal: **even taking the allegations as true, the indictment did not charge a fraud offense because it treated the wrong thing as the object of the scheme.**

### 1. What a court does on a motion to dismiss an indictment
Yes — at the indictment stage, the court generally **takes the indictment’s allegations as true** and asks whether those allegations, if proved, would satisfy the elements of the charged offense.

The Fifth Circuit says exactly that in this opinion:

> “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”

So the issue is not whether the defendants really lied or whether this was really a pump-and-dump scheme. The issue is whether the indictment alleged conduct that, as a matter of law, fits within the securities-fraud statute.

### 2. What the district court thought was missing
The district court focused on **property**. It concluded the indictment failed because it allegedly described only a scheme to deprive followers of **valuable economic information**, not a traditional property interest.

That tracks the Supreme Court’s rejection of the old “right-to-control” theory in **Ciminelli**: fraud statutes do not reach schemes whose only object is to deprive a victim of economically valuable information.

So the district court’s legal view was essentially:  
- the indictment alleges deception, yes; but  
- it does **not** allege a scheme to obtain money or property in the legally required sense.

### 3. Why the Fifth Circuit said that was error
The Fifth Circuit held the district court read the indictment too narrowly.

The indictment did **not** merely allege that defendants lied to deprive followers of information. It alleged that they:

- posted false and misleading statements,
- induced followers to **buy securities**,
- thereby caused followers to part with **money**, and
- allowed defendants to profit from the resulting price increase and later sales.

That is why the Fifth Circuit said the case was closer to **fraudulent inducement** than to the rejected right-to-control theory. In the court’s words, the indictment alleged defendants “induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property.”

### 4. The intent argument
The district court also appears to have thought the indictment failed because it alleged defendants wanted to **profit**, not necessarily to **harm** victims.

The Fifth Circuit said that is not a defect. Under **Kousisis**, fraud does not require that the defendant intend the victim to suffer a net economic loss. It is enough that the defendant uses deception to obtain the victim’s money or property. The opinion emphasizes:

> “the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm”

and

> “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”

So the fact that defendants hoped to make money, rather than to “injure” followers for its own sake, does not defeat the charge.

### 5. Bottom line
The district court dismissed because it viewed the indictment as alleging only the deprivation of **information**, not **property**. The Fifth Circuit reversed because the indictment also alleged that the lies were used to **induce purchases of securities**, which is an allegation that defendants schemed to obtain money by fraud.

So this was not a factual disagreement. It was a legal disagreement over the **theory of fraud** the indictment alleged. The Fifth Circuit held that, accepting the indictment’s allegations as true, that theory is enough to state securities fraud.

### How is 'fraudulent inducement' different from the Supreme Court's rejected 'right-to-control' theory after Ciminelli?
Question:
The opinion says the indictment was valid because it alleged a 'fraudulent-inducement theory,' not a 'right-to-control theory.' I'm having trouble seeing the line between them. In both situations, the victim is misled by false information when deciding whether to enter a transaction. If the followers here were tricked by false posts into buying stocks, why isn't that just depriving them of 'valuable economic information' needed to make an investment decision, which Ciminelli says is not enough? What makes this case about obtaining money or property rather than merely interfering with informed decisionmaking?
Answer:
The line is **what the falsehood is being used to obtain**.

## 1. “Right-to-control” after *Ciminelli*
In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the theory that fraud exists merely because the victim was deprived of **valuable economic information** needed to make a discretionary decision. The problem with that theory is that it treats the victim’s “right to control” its assets as the property interest itself.

So if the scheme is only:

- lie to a person,
- affect how they decide,
- but **do not seek to obtain their money or property**,

that is not enough under federal fraud law after *Ciminelli*.

## 2. Fraudulent inducement
By contrast, a **fraudulent-inducement theory** alleges that the defendant used lies to **cause the victim to part with money or property**.

That is exactly how the Fifth Circuit describes the indictment here:

- defendants posted false information,
- followers were induced to **purchase securities**,
- and thus followers **parted with money**.

The opinion quotes *Kousisis* for the proposition that fraudulent inducement is when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.”

## 3. Why this case is not just about “valuable economic information”
The district court thought the indictment sounded like the followers were merely deprived of information that would help them make better investment decisions. The Fifth Circuit says that is too narrow a reading of the indictment.

The indictment alleges more than misinformation affecting decisionmaking. It alleges a scheme to:

- **induce purchases of securities**,
- artificially inflate prices,
- and let defendants **profit by selling**.

That means the object of the scheme was the followers’ **money**, not just their informed choice.

## 4. The practical distinction
A useful way to think about it:

- **Right-to-control theory**: “I lied so you made a worse-informed decision.”
- **Fraudulent inducement**: “I lied so you gave me your money/property.”

Only the second is enough after *Ciminelli*.

## 5. Why the victim’s getting something back does not defeat fraud
The opinion also relies on *Kousisis* to say fraud does **not** require net economic loss. Even if the victims received securities that had market value, the fraud is complete if the defendant **obtained money by materially false pretenses**.

So the key is not whether the victims got something of value in return. The key is whether the scheme was designed to **obtain money or property** through deception.

## Bottom line
The opinion treats this as valid because the indictment alleges a scheme to **induce purchases and capture the victims’ money**, not merely to interfere with informed decisionmaking. That puts it on the fraudulent-inducement side of the line, not the rejected *Ciminelli* right-to-control theory.

### Why does buying a security count as being deprived of money or property if the buyer receives shares in return?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit says the indictment alleges a property deprivation because the followers were induced to purchase securities, meaning they parted with their money. But the followers also got stock in exchange, and the opinion even notes they may have received market-value securities. I thought fraud usually required cheating someone out of property in a more direct sense. Why is the transfer of money itself enough here, even if the victim receives something of equal or at least nonzero value in return? Is the theory that the victim's property interest lies in the money paid, the terms of the bargain, or the fact that consent was induced by fraud?
Answer:
Because, under the fraud statutes, the protected property interest is the **money the buyer parts with**, not a guarantee of a bad bargain or a net economic loss.

That is the key move in *United States v. Constantinescu*. The Fifth Circuit explains that the indictment does **not** rest on a “right-to-control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli*. Instead, it alleges a classic **fraudulent-inducement** scheme: the defendants used false statements to induce followers to **buy securities**, i.e. to hand over money. Once that happens, the property element is satisfied because the victim has been deprived of money by deception.

The fact that the buyer receives shares in return does not defeat the fraud charge. As the opinion says, quoting *Kousisis*, a defendant can be liable even if he “provides something . . . of equal value in return.” The fraud statutes do **not** require proof that the victim ended up economically worse off overall. They require that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property through deceit.

So the theory is not:

- “the victim lost money because the shares were worthless,” or
- “the victim was deprived of the right to make an informed decision” standing alone.

It is:

- “the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce the victim to part with money.”

That is why the court says the indictment adequately alleges that defendants “induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property.” The loss of money itself is the deprivation, even if the buyer received stock in exchange. The presence of some value in return may matter to damages or loss amount, but it does not erase the fraudulent taking.

### After Kousisis, does federal fraud no longer require intent to cause economic loss to the victim?
Question:
The part I'm struggling with most is the 'intent to defraud' analysis. The defendants argued they only intended to profit, not to harm their followers, and the Fifth Circuit said that distinction doesn't matter after Kousisis. Does that mean federal fraud statutes now require only intent to obtain money or property through deception, even if the defendant doesn't care whether the victim ends up worse off overall? If so, how does that fit with older formulations saying intent to defraud includes intent to cause harm? Is 'harm' now satisfied just by inducing someone to transfer money under false pretenses?
Answer:
**Short answer: mostly yes, but with an important qualification.**

After **Kousisis**, federal fraud no longer requires proof that the defendant specifically intended to make the victim **economically worse off on net**. What the government must still show is that the defendant **intended to deceive and, by that deception, obtain the victim’s money or property**.

That is exactly how the Fifth Circuit frames it in **United States v. Constantinescu**. The court says:

- securities fraud requires a **“scheme to defraud”** and an **“intent to defraud”**;
- the “intent to defraud” element still includes an intent to **deceive** and to **cause some harm to result from the deceit**; but
- under **Kousisis**, the fraud statutes do **not** require that the defendant seek to leave the victim **economically worse off**.  
  The defendant violates the statute so long as he schemes to **obtain the victim’s money or property** through deception.  

So the key point is this: **“harm” is no longer limited to net economic loss.** In a fraud case, the relevant harm can be the **deceptive deprivation of money or property itself**.

That is why the Fifth Circuit rejected the defendants’ argument that they only meant to profit, not to harm their followers. The court called that distinction “**without a difference**” because the indictment alleged that they **misled followers into buying securities so defendants could obtain money**. Under the opinion’s reading of **Kousisis**, that is enough.

So, to your last question: **yes, harm is satisfied by inducing someone to part with money or property under materially false pretenses**, even if the victim may receive something of equal or near-equal value, and even if the defendant’s main aim was self-enrichment rather than making the victim poorer overall.

The older “intent to cause harm” language is still in the doctrine, but **Constantinescu** shows it should be read in this narrower sense: the required harm is the **fraudulent obtaining of money or property**, not necessarily a separate intent to cause a net financial loss.

### What exactly is the property-holder in this pump-and-dump theory: the social-media followers, the market, or someone else?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly says the defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities and thereby obtained money or property. But in a pump-and-dump scheme, the defendants usually sell into a market at an inflated price, and the counterparties may not always be the exact followers who saw the posts. So whose property is the indictment really focused on? Does it matter for § 1348 whether the deceived person and the person from whom the money is obtained are the same? I'm trying to understand whether the Fifth Circuit is treating this as direct victim-to-defendant fraud or something more market-wide.
Answer:
The Fifth Circuit is treating the **followers/investors who were induced to buy** as the relevant property holders, not the market as an abstract whole.

The key point in **United States v. Constantinescu** is that the indictment is framed as a **fraudulent-inducement** case: the defendants allegedly used false social-media posts “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and thus “part with their money or property.” The court repeatedly says the object of the scheme was to obtain **the followers’ money**. That is the property interest § 1348 protects here.

So, to your first question: the indictment is **not** focused on a generalized deprivation of “valuable economic information” or a market-wide injury. The opinion expressly rejects that Ciminelli-style framing. Instead, it says the scheme alleges that the defendants tricked followers into buying securities, i.e. into handing over money.

On your second question: **no, the deceived person and the person whose money is obtained do not have to be different people**—and in this opinion the court does not require any such separation. Quite the opposite: it says the fraud statutes are satisfied when the defendant “schem[es] to obtain the victim’s money or property,” even if the victim receives something of value in return and even if the defendant’s primary motive is profit rather than direct economic harm to the victim.

So, as the Fifth Circuit sees it, this is **direct fraud against the induced purchasers**, not merely a market-wide manipulation theory. The “market” matters only as the mechanism by which the false statements allegedly inflated the price; the protected property interest is the money the followers parted with when they bought the securities.

### How much of mail/wire fraud doctrine can courts import into 18 U.S.C. § 1348 securities fraud cases?
Question:
The opinion says there is 'scant caselaw' on § 1348 in the Fifth Circuit, so the court borrows concepts from mail and wire fraud cases, including Ciminelli and Kousisis. Is that always appropriate? Section 1348 has two clauses and is specifically about securities, while the mail and wire fraud statutes have their own wording and history. Are courts treating § 1348 as basically a securities-specific analogue to mail/wire fraud, or are there meaningful doctrinal limits on importing those cases? I'm wondering whether the Fifth Circuit's reasoning depends on textual similarities, legislative purpose, or just precedent by analogy.
Answer:
Not wholesale. The Fifth Circuit’s point in **Constantinescu** is narrower: because there is “scant caselaw” construing § 1348 in the circuit, courts may **borrow “key concepts”** from mail- and wire-fraud doctrine **so long as those concepts fit § 1348’s text**. The opinion expressly says § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and that courts therefore often give the terms “similar treatment.” But that is an analogy-based move, not a blank check.

### What the court treated as transferable
The court imported the familiar fraud elements of:

- a **scheme to defraud**, and
- **intent to defraud**,

and then used **Ciminelli** and **Kousisis** to explain what those concepts mean when the object is money or property. In the opinion’s words, securities fraud is committed when a defendant “engaged in deception” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of the fraud.

### The limit
The limit is the statute’s own language. Section 1348 is not just a carbon copy of the mail/wire fraud statutes; it has **two clauses** and is specifically framed around securities. So courts should not import doctrines that:

1. **conflict with § 1348’s text**, or
2. **smuggle back in a theory the Supreme Court has rejected**.

That is exactly how the Fifth Circuit uses **Ciminelli**: not as a generic federal-fraud gloss, but to reject a theory that would make fraud turn only on depriving victims of “valuable economic information.” The court says that would be a right-to-control theory, and that is not a traditional property interest. But it also says the indictment here is different because it alleges **fraudulent inducement to part with money to buy securities**—which fits the money/property language.

Likewise, **Kousisis** is imported only for the proposition that the fraud statutes do **not** require the victim to suffer net economic loss. The opinion uses that to reject the defendants’ claim that because followers might have received securities of market value, there was no fraud. The court says that is no answer where the object was to obtain the victims’ money through deception.

### Bottom line
So the Fifth Circuit is **not** treating § 1348 as identical to mail/wire fraud. It is treating it as a **securities-specific fraud statute that uses similar core fraud concepts**, which can be informed by mail/wire-fraud precedent **where the statutory language overlaps**. The doctrinal borrowing is therefore **real but limited**: useful for defining “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud,” but not for importing theories that the text of § 1348—or the Supreme Court’s fraud cases—do not support.
