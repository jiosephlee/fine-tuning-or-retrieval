Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why isn't testimony that the defendant previously had the same gun barred by Rule 404(b) as propensity evidence?
Question:
I'm confused about the court's treatment of D.F.'s and C.F.'s testimony that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. My instinct was that evidence of prior acts is usually restricted by Rule 404(b), especially if it risks inviting the jury to think "he had a gun before, so he must have had it this time too." But the Eighth Circuit calls this "intrinsic evidence" and says it shows continuous possession and control of the same weapon. What exactly makes prior possession of the same firearm "intrinsic" rather than Rule 404(b) evidence? Is this basically an exception whenever the government can tie the prior act to the identical gun, or is there a narrower doctrinal rule here?
Answer:
Because the testimony was not being used to show **“he’s the kind of person who carries guns”**; it was being used to show **he possessed this same gun**.

That is the key distinction.

## 1) What Rule 404(b) covers
Rule 404(b) bars evidence of **other acts** when the point of the evidence is to prove propensity — i.e., “because he did it before, he likely did it again.”

So if the government were offering evidence that Cutbank once owned some unrelated firearm just to suggest he is a gun person, that would raise a classic 404(b) problem.

## 2) Why this testimony was treated as intrinsic
The Eighth Circuit accepted the district court’s view that D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony about seeing Cutbank with the **same sawed-off Marlin .22** before the charged incident was **intrinsic** to the possession charge.

The opinion relies on the doctrine that prior possession of the **same firearm** is directly probative of the charged possession because it tends to show **ownership, control, and continuing possession** of that weapon. The court quotes *United States v. Battle* for the proposition that:

> “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

So the relevance is not “he did a similar bad thing before.” It is “this is the same weapon, and the earlier sightings help prove he possessed the weapon on the charged date.”

## 3) Why that makes it “intrinsic” rather than 404(b)
“Intrinsic” evidence is evidence that is part of the charged offense itself, or so intertwined with it that it helps prove the offense without requiring a separate 404(b) analysis.

Here, the prior sightings of the same rifle were treated as part of the proof of the possession count because they helped establish a **continuous chain of possession** of the identical firearm. The opinion expressly says the district court viewed the testimony as:

- “intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge,” and
- evidence of “continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.”

That means the testimony was not being offered as a separate “other act” at all; it was offered as direct proof of the charged possession.

## 4) Is this a blanket exception for the same gun?
Not quite, but it is a **narrow and recurring rule** in firearm-possession cases.

The important limiting principle is that the evidence must be tied to the **same firearm** and must genuinely help prove the charged possession — usually by showing ownership, control, identification, or continuity of possession. That is what makes it probative of the offense rather than merely prejudicial character evidence.

So the rule is narrower than “any prior gun evidence is intrinsic if the government says it’s the same gun.” The government still has to show that the earlier possession meaningfully relates to the charged possession. But when the testimony is about the **same weapon**, and especially when it helps establish the defendant’s continuous possession or control, the Eighth Circuit treats it as outside Rule 404(b).

## 5) Bottom line
The testimony was not barred by Rule 404(b) because it was not offered to prove Cutbank’s propensity to possess guns. It was offered to prove he possessed **this specific gun**, and the court treated that as **intrinsic evidence** of the charged § 922(g) offense.

So the doctrinal move is:

- **other gun, or prior gun use offered for character** → likely 404(b);
- **same gun, prior possession offered to prove ownership/control/continuous possession** → often intrinsic, and admissible subject to ordinary relevance and Rule 403 limits.

### How did the Confrontation Clause issue disappear just because the declarant later testified at trial?
Question:
Before trial, the defense wanted an instruction telling the jury not to treat D.F.'s out-of-court statements to police as substantive evidence until D.F. herself testified. The court denied that request, and the opinion says there was no Confrontation Clause problem because D.F. later testified in person. I'm struggling with the timing issue. If officers repeat an accuser's statements before the accuser testifies, doesn't the jury still hear testimonial hearsay at that point? Does the later appearance of the declarant automatically cure any Sixth Amendment issue, or only if the defendant has a full opportunity to cross-examine her? Also, how do courts distinguish between testimony offered merely to explain police conduct and testimony that effectively conveys the truth of the accusation?
Answer:
The short answer is: **the Confrontation Clause problem disappears only if the declarant actually testifies and is subject to cross-examination on the relevant statement**. That is what the Eighth Circuit is relying on in *Cutbank* when it says:

> “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person.”  
> *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010).

So the timing point is not that the out-of-court statement was magically constitutional at the moment the officer repeated it. Rather, under the doctrine the court is applying, **the Sixth Amendment concern is cured once the declarant later comes to court and can be cross-examined**. The Constitution guarantees the defendant an opportunity to confront the witness, not a particular order of proof.

That said, there is an important caveat: **the witness must be available for meaningful cross-examination about the statement**. If the declarant testifies but refuses to answer, claims privilege, or otherwise cannot be questioned about the substance of the accusation, the confrontation problem may remain. *Cutbank* does not address such a failure; the opinion says simply that **D.F. testified in person**, which was enough for the court.

On your hearsay-timing concern: yes, the jury may hear the statement before the declarant takes the stand. But that does not automatically create a Confrontation Clause violation if:

1. the statement is **not admitted for its truth** at that stage, or
2. the declarant later testifies and is available for cross-examination.

That is why the court here also emphasized that it “**did not admit hearsay during the trial**” and that D.F.’s statements were used to provide **context for police conduct and knowledge**, not as substantive proof of Cutbank’s guilt.

### “Explain police conduct” vs. “backdoor accusation”
Courts draw this line by asking whether the testimony is genuinely offered for a non-hearsay purpose—such as explaining why officers acted as they did—or whether, in substance, it is just a way of conveying the accusation to the jury for its truth.

- **Permissible**: “We went to that location because we received a report of a disturbance.”
- **Problematic**: “We went there because D.F. said Cutbank pointed a gun at her.”

The second example is functionally accusatory; it risks being used substantively even if labeled “context.” The Eighth Circuit in *Cutbank* avoided that issue by saying the record showed the court **did not admit hearsay** and by pointing to the fact that **D.F. later testified**.

So the doctrinal answer is:

- **Later testimony can cure the Confrontation Clause issue**, but only if the declarant actually testifies and is subject to cross-examination.
- **If the statement was admitted only for context**, there may be no hearsay or confrontation problem in the first place.
- **If the statement was effectively offered for its truth before the declarant testified**, the later appearance usually cures the Sixth Amendment concern so long as the defense gets a real cross-examination opportunity.

In *Cutbank*, the court treated both points as satisfied.

### Why wasn't the witness's statement that her sister was killed by a gun excluded under Rule 403?
Question:
During cross-examination about inconsistent statements, D.F. explained that she was not familiar with guns and did not like guns because her sister had been killed by one. That seems very emotionally charged, and I would have thought the defense would have a strong Rule 403 argument that it was unfairly prejudicial in a felon-in-possession case involving an alleged domestic assault. The court says the testimony was responsive to cross-examination and that no unfair prejudice was apparent. Is the idea that the defense "opened the door" by pressing her about why her prior descriptions were inconsistent? More generally, how do appellate courts evaluate prejudice when testimony is sympathetic but not directly probative of an element of the offense?
Answer:
The short answer is yes: the defense’s cross-examination is what made the testimony come in, and that matters a lot under Rule 403.

Rule 403 does **not** exclude evidence merely because it is upsetting or sympathetic. It excludes evidence only if its **probative value is substantially outweighed** by a danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, misleading the jury, and so on. “Unfair prejudice” in this context means an **improper** tendency to move the jury on an emotional basis rather than a rational one.

Here, the Eighth Circuit treated D.F.’s statement as a **responsive explanation** for why her prior statements about the gun were inconsistent:

> “This testimony responded to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements.”

That is the key point. Defense counsel was trying to impeach her by pressing on her inconsistent descriptions and memory of the gun. In response, she explained that she was not familiar with guns and disliked them because her sister had been killed by one. The court evidently viewed that explanation as probative of **why her earlier statements differed**, not as an independent emotional appeal.

The court also said:

> “Cutback points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, and none is apparent.”

That tells you how the Rule 403 balance came out on appeal: the panel saw the testimony as having a legitimate explanatory value, with no obvious risk that the jury would decide the felon-in-possession charge based on sympathy for D.F. rather than the evidence.

A few doctrinal points explain why the argument failed:

1. **Opening the door / responsive evidence**
   - When one side attacks a witness’s credibility on cross-examination, the witness is generally allowed to give an explanation that makes the challenged testimony intelligible.
   - So yes, in practical terms, the defense “opened the door” by pursuing the inconsistency issue. The court did not use that phrase, but that is the idea.

2. **Wide discretion under Rule 403**
   - Appellate courts give trial judges broad discretion on these balancing questions.
   - The opinion cites *Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn* for that reason.
   - Unless the record shows a serious risk of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury, the appellate court will not second-guess the ruling.

3. **Context matters**
   - The testimony did not go to an element of the offense. It was collateral background offered to explain a witness’s prior inconsistency.
   - That kind of evidence is often allowed if it helps the jury evaluate credibility.

4. **No preservation problem on the sister-point**
   - The opinion also notes that C.F. mentioned the sister’s death without a defense objection. That undercuts any claim that this was a uniquely inflammatory surprise requiring exclusion.

So the appellate court’s reasoning is basically: the statement was **responsive, explanatory, and only minimally prejudicial if at all**; therefore, Rule 403 did not require exclusion.

More generally, appellate courts ask whether the evidence had an **improper emotional effect that substantially outweighed its probative value**. Sympathy alone is not enough. If the evidence helps explain a witness’s conduct, memory, bias, or inconsistency, courts are usually reluctant to call it unfairly prejudicial—especially when it arises directly from the opposing party’s cross-examination.

### Why couldn't defense counsel impeach the witness by reading from the police report if the report described her prior inconsistent statements?
Question:
The opinion says the district court let defense counsel ask C.F. about what she told police, but would not allow counsel to read portions of the police report into the record without proper foundation because the report was not authored by C.F. I'm confused about the mechanics here. If the goal is impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, why isn't it enough that the report memorializes what she allegedly said? Does the defense need to establish that C.F. adopted the report, or call the officer who wrote it, or satisfy some separate authentication requirement before using it as extrinsic impeachment evidence? I'm trying to understand the interaction between Rule 613 and authentication rules in this setting.
Answer:
Because **Rule 613 only gets you so far**.

What defense counsel was trying to do here was not simply ask C.F. about her own prior statement; counsel wanted to **introduce the police report itself** as impeachment evidence. That triggers a separate problem: **the report had to be admissible as a piece of evidence in its own right**, which means it had to be **authenticated**.

The Eighth Circuit’s answer in *Cutbank* is basically:

1. **You may cross-examine the witness about her prior inconsistent statement**  
   The district court allowed that. Counsel could ask C.F. what she told police, which is the classic Rule 613 use.

2. **But if you want to read the report aloud as extrinsic evidence, you must authenticate it**  
   The court relied on *United States v. Cumbie*, which it quoted for the proposition that “extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, [but] such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.”

So the key distinction is:

- **Questioning C.F. about what she said** = no need to prove the report itself is genuine, because you are impeaching through her testimony.
- **Reading from the police report** = you are now offering the report as evidence, so you must show what it is and where it came from.

That is why the district court said the report was not enough just because it **memorialized** allegedly inconsistent statements. A police report is **not C.F.’s statement** just because it records what an officer says she said. Unless the defense established some basis to use that document—typically by **calling the officer who wrote it**, or otherwise authenticating it, or showing that C.F. **adopted** it as her own statement—the court could properly refuse to let counsel read from it.

In short, **Rule 613 governs the impeachment use of prior statements; authentication governs the document you are using to prove the statement.** The opinion treats those as separate requirements, and the defense failed the second one.

### How can the sentencing court use 'another felony offense' for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) when the defendant wasn't separately convicted of that felony?
Question:
At sentencing, the court applied the four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm "in connection with another felony offense," apparently based on the underlying conduct involving domestic abuse/kidnapping/assault, even though the opinion doesn't mention a separate conviction for those offenses in this federal case. The commentary says the enhancement applies regardless of whether a charge was brought or a conviction obtained. Is that common across the Guidelines? What standard of proof applies when the sentencing judge finds that separate felony conduct occurred, and what evidence can the court rely on? The opinion also notes that the PSR's factual statements were adopted without objection—does failing to object effectively concede those facts for Guidelines purposes?
Answer:
Yes. Under the Guidelines, a sentencing court may apply § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) even if the defendant was never separately charged with, or convicted of, the “other felony offense.” The opinion says exactly that: the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” USSG § 2K2.1 cmt. n.14(C).

### 1) How can the court use uncharged conduct?
Because the Guidelines routinely allow sentencing courts to consider relevant conduct proved at sentencing, not just conduct that resulted in a conviction. This is not unique to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The application note for this enhancement expressly contemplates uncharged conduct, and the Eighth Circuit relied on that note in Cutbank.

Here, the district court used the underlying domestic-abuse/kidnapping/assault facts described in the PSR to find the firearm was possessed “in connection with another felony offense.” The Eighth Circuit held that was proper because the PSR’s factual statements were adopted without objection.

### 2) What standard of proof applies?
The opinion does not explicitly recite the burden, but in federal sentencing the usual standard for guideline facts is **preponderance of the evidence**. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s factual finding only for **clear error**. See the court’s citation to *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929–30 (8th Cir. 2013).

So the sequence is:

- **At sentencing:** the judge finds the enhancement facts by a preponderance of the evidence.
- **On appeal:** the finding is reviewed for clear error.

### 3) What evidence can the court rely on?
The court can rely on the **PSR**, so long as its factual assertions are supported and not objected to. That is exactly what happened here: “the district court adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements.” The Eighth Circuit then treated those facts as establishing the necessary felony conduct.

That is a standard sentencing practice: courts may rely on the PSR, trial evidence, and other reliable information at sentencing. In this opinion, the key point is that the PSR’s account was enough because Cutbank did not dispute it.

### 4) Does failing to object concede the facts?
For Guidelines purposes, **yes, effectively**. If the defendant does not object to the PSR’s factual assertions, the sentencing court may accept them as true. Cutbank’s failure to object meant the district court could rely on those facts to apply the enhancement, and the Eighth Circuit said there was no error, “much less clearly err[or].”

So in practical terms:

- **No objection to PSR factual statements = those facts are treated as admitted/accepted for sentencing.**
- A defendant who wants to contest a guideline enhancement must object specifically to the PSR facts supporting it.

### Bottom line
The enhancement did not require a separate conviction. The Guidelines expressly allow reliance on uncharged felony conduct, the sentencing court could find that conduct by a preponderance of the evidence, and the unobjected-to PSR supplied the factual basis.

### Why did the court treat Cutbank's § 3553(a) argument as a substantive-reasonableness issue instead of a Guidelines-calculation error?
Question:
Cutbank argued that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produced a sentence greater than necessary under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), especially because the ACCA guideline would have yielded a lower total offense level than the otherwise-applicable calculations. The Eighth Circuit responded that the district court first had to correctly calculate the Guidelines, then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors justified a variance, and it presumed the within-Guidelines sentence was reasonable. I'm having trouble seeing the boundary here. If the defendant's real complaint is that the enhancement overstates the seriousness of the conduct in light of the ACCA framework, is that purely a substantive reasonableness argument? Or could there be a doctrinal argument that the enhancement should not apply at all because it double counts conduct already captured elsewhere in the sentence?
Answer:
Because, on the record in *Cutbank*, the complaint was not that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was legally unavailable, but that its application made the advisory range too high as a matter of sentencing policy under § 3553(a).

That distinction matters.

## 1. Guidelines calculation vs. § 3553(a) variance

The court followed the ordinary sentencing sequence:

1. **Correctly calculate the Guidelines range**  
   The district court adopted the PSR’s facts and applied the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The Eighth Circuit said that was not clearly erroneous because the facts supported that the firearm was used “in connection with another felony offense.”

2. **Then consider whether § 3553(a) calls for a variance**  
   Cutbank’s argument that the sentence was “greater than necessary” because the ACCA framework would have produced a lower offense level was treated as a request for a lower sentence **after** the Guidelines were correctly calculated. That is a classic **substantive-reasonableness** argument, not a Guidelines-calculation error.

The opinion says exactly that: after citing *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*, the court explains that the district court must first calculate the range, and only then consider whether § 3553(a) factors justify departure or variance.

## 2. Why the ACCA comparison did not make the enhancement erroneous

Cutbank’s real point was: “If the ACCA guideline would have yielded offense level 34, why should I get 35 with the enhancement?”

But the opinion answers that directly: the Guidelines themselves resolve the comparison. **USSG § 4B1.4(b) says the greater offense level applies.** So the existence of a lower ACCA offense level does not invalidate the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. It just means that once all applicable provisions are calculated, the higher one governs.

So the argument is not:

- “the enhancement cannot be applied under the Guidelines”; it is
- “even if the enhancement applies, the resulting sentence is too severe.”

That is a § 3553(a) argument.

## 3. What about double counting?

A double-counting argument could be doctrinally viable **if** the defendant could show that the Guidelines prohibit using the same conduct twice, or that the same facts are being counted in a way the Guidelines do not allow.

But that is not how the Eighth Circuit framed Cutbank’s claim here. The opinion treats the enhancement as independently supported by the facts and authorized by the commentary to § 2K2.1. It does **not** identify any guideline provision saying the conduct underlying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) cannot also coexist with the ACCA calculation.

In other words, nothing in the opinion suggests impermissible double counting. The issue was not whether the enhancement was barred; it was whether the resulting within-Guidelines sentence was too high in light of § 3553(a).

## 4. Why the court presumed reasonableness

Once the district court correctly calculated the range, the Eighth Circuit applied the normal presumption that a **within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable**. Because the district court noted its review of the PSR and the § 3553(a) factors, the appellate court treated Cutbank’s point as an argument the district court considered and rejected—not as a calculation mistake.

## Bottom line

Cutbank’s ACCA comparison did not show that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was wrongly applied. It showed only that he thought the resulting range was too harsh. That makes it a **substantive reasonableness** argument, not a **procedural Guidelines error**.

### What does the harmless-error analysis mean when the court says the evidentiary rulings 'did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict'?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly brushes aside the evidentiary issues and ends by saying any error would have been harmless because the government's evidence was substantial. I understand harmless error in the abstract, but I'm confused about how searching the review actually is in a criminal case. Does the appellate court ask whether the verdict surely would have been the same, whether there was overwhelming evidence, or whether the challenged evidence likely affected the jury? And when the defendant argues cumulative error, how do courts decide whether a series of individually minor rulings adds up to substantial prejudice?
Answer:
The court is applying the ordinary **harmless-error** standard for nonconstitutional evidentiary errors: an evidentiary mistake does **not** require reversal unless it **substantially influenced the verdict**.

In the opinion, the Eighth Circuit quotes its usual formulation:

> “we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.”

And then, in the familiar shorthand from *DeMarce*, it says the challenged rulings are harmless if they:

> “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.”

So the court is **not** asking whether the evidence was merely relevant or whether the ruling was technically wrong. It is asking a practical appellate question: **was the mistake important enough that it probably affected the jury’s decision?**

### How searching is the review?

In a criminal case, for ordinary evidentiary error, the Eighth Circuit does **not** require the government to prove beyond all doubt that the verdict would have been the same. But the court does look at the **whole record** and asks whether the properly admitted proof was so strong that the error could only have had a minor effect.

That is exactly what the panel does here. After discussing the challenged rulings, it says:

> “The government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial. Thus, the district court’s evidentiary rulings ‘did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.’”

So the court’s reasoning is:

1. even assuming error,
2. the remaining evidence of guilt was substantial,
3. therefore the disputed rulings were not likely to have mattered in a way that would justify reversal.

That is the same as saying the error was **harmless in light of the record as a whole**.

### “Overwhelming evidence” vs. “likely affected the jury”

Those ideas are closely related, but they are not quite the test.

- **“Overwhelming evidence”** is a way of describing why an error was harmless.
- The actual question is whether the error **may have substantially swayed the jury** or had more than a slight influence.
- If the properly admitted evidence is strong enough, the court will usually conclude the error was harmless because the jury likely would have reached the same result anyway.

That is what the opinion means when it emphasizes the government’s “substantial” testimonial and physical evidence.

### Cumulative error

The opinion also states the Eighth Circuit rule on cumulative error:

> “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.”

This means the defendant cannot win by simply stacking several weak complaints together. The court asks whether the combined effect of the errors, taken together, created **substantial prejudice** — in other words, whether the overall fairness of the trial was materially undermined.

So:

- if the alleged errors are minor or not errors at all, they usually do **not** add up to reversible prejudice;
- if there are multiple actual errors, the court looks at their **combined impact** on the verdict.

In *Cutbank*, the court found no such cumulative prejudice because the evidentiary rulings were either proper or, at most, had only a slight effect in the face of strong evidence.

### Bottom line

In this opinion, “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict” means the court concluded that any evidentiary error was **harmless** because the jury was not likely to have been substantially swayed by it. And on cumulative error, the defendant must show more than a collection of minor issues — he must show **substantial prejudice** from the combined effect of actual errors.
