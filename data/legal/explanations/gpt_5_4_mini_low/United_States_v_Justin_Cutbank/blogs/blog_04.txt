Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# A Quiet Appellate-Practice Lesson in *Cutbank*: Undeveloped Arguments, Judicial Frustration, and Issue Inflation

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Cutbank* is, on the surface, a routine affirmance in a felon-in-possession case with ugly facts and a long criminal history. But beneath the black-letter holdings sits a useful appellate-practice lesson: how courts respond when an appellant gives them almost nothing to work with, and when the appellee, sensing weakness, tries to turn a sparse appeal into a broader battlefield than the appellant actually chose.

Judge Loken’s opinion is notable less for doctrinal innovation than for tone. Again and again, the court emphasizes that Cutbank offered “no cases and almost no analysis” to support his claims. And when the government attempted to frame a supposed additional issue that Cutbank had not actually pressed, the court swatted it away with a bit of dry impatience: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” That line says a lot about how appellate judges manage their docket, their briefing, and their patience.

## Thin briefing rarely gets a generous doctrinal response

The opinion’s treatment of Cutbank’s evidentiary arguments is a familiar appellate pattern. The court did not need to write a long discussion about the nuances of Rule 404(b), limiting instructions, or impeachment with extrinsic evidence because Cutbank did not do the work required to earn one. The opinion repeatedly notes the absence of authority and analysis, and that matters. Appellate courts are not research assistants for underdeveloped briefs. When a party fails to engage the governing standard, cite relevant authority, or explain why a ruling mattered in context, the court usually resolves the issue at a high level of generality and moves on.

That is exactly what happened here. The court found the prior firearm observations intrinsic to the possession charge, treated the statements to police as either contextual or cured by the witness’s actual testimony, and viewed the challenged “sister was killed by a gun” testimony as responsive to cross-examination and not unfairly prejudicial. None of that is especially novel, but the opinion’s real point is procedural: once the appellant has not built a serious argument, the court is unlikely to do that work for him.

Practitioners should read this as a reminder that appellate persuasion is cumulative. A weak issue can still be won, but only if the brief gives the panel a reason to see it as more than a complaint. Here, the court’s brisk treatment suggests that Cutbank’s briefing choices left the panel with little incentive to linger.

## Preservation and harmlessness do a lot of quiet work

A second lesson is the extent to which preservation and harmless-error principles narrow the field before merits analysis even begins. Several of Cutbank’s objections were either weakly preserved, addressed in real time by the district court, or undermined by the fact that the challenged witnesses testified in court. The opinion also leans heavily on harmlessness: even if some evidentiary rulings were questionable, the government’s proof was “substantial,” and the verdict was not meaningfully likely to have turned on the challenged pieces of testimony.

This is the kind of appellate posture that often makes unpublished-or-routine opinions feel terse. Once the court decides that any arguable error did not affect substantial rights, there is little reason to excavate the fine points. In practical terms, that means counsel who want meaningful review need to do more than identify a technical issue; they need to preserve it cleanly, explain why it is prejudicial, and situate it within the trial record in a way that gives the court a path to reversal.

## The court’s brief rebuke of the government is just as instructive

If the appellant’s briefing was thin, the appellee’s instincts were a little too expansive. The opinion pauses to note that the government’s brief tried to recast an unraised matter as if it were a distinct appellate claim. The court’s response is unusually pointed for a routine criminal appeal: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.”

That sentence deserves attention because it captures an often-overlooked appellate dynamic. Appellees are sometimes tempted to overbrief. If the appellant raises one weak issue, the instinct is to answer not only that issue but also a constellation of adjacent complaints, maybe in hopes of preempting trouble or signaling confidence. But overdoing that can backfire. It can make a narrow appeal look broader than it is, invite the court to notice questions it otherwise might not have addressed, and irritate a panel that prefers parties to keep the case within its actual bounds.

There is a strategic discipline here: respond fully to what the appellant actually raised, but don’t manufacture additional terrain. Appellate judges notice when briefs are trying to litigate the case the way counsel wishes it had been presented, rather than the way it was.

## Sentencing: little doctrinal movement, but a reminder about guideline layering

The sentencing discussion is more conventional, but it reinforces another practice point. Cutbank argued that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement somehow conflicted with the “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” language of § 3553(a). The court did not indulge that framing. It relied instead on the standard sequence: calculate the Guidelines correctly, assess the PSR’s factual findings, and then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a variance. The district court did that, imposed a within-Guidelines sentence, and received the usual appellate presumption of reasonableness.

What matters here is not only that the court affirmed, but how little room there was for a sentencing challenge once the record was set. The PSR’s facts were adopted without objection, the Guidelines calculation was internally coherent, and the district court said enough to show it had considered the statutory factors. In that setting, appellate arguments about the “core principle” of § 3553(a) tend to sound abstract rather than error-identifying.

The broader doctrinal point is that appellate sentencing review is often record-driven in the most literal sense. If counsel wants to contest the interaction between a specific enhancement and the overall sentence, the argument has to be developed at sentencing, preserved, and tied to concrete facts. Otherwise, the court will default to procedural regularity and defer to the district judge’s discretion.

## The bigger takeaway

*Cutbank* is not a case that changes the law. But it does reveal something important about appellate decisionmaking: briefing quality shapes not only the outcome, but the court’s willingness to engage. Sparse arguments tend to get sparse answers. Weak preservation narrows the field. And appellees who try to turn a thin appeal into a sprawling one may find the panel less interested, not more.

For practitioners, that is the lesson hiding in Judge Loken’s tone. Appellate litigation is not just about having the right issue; it is about presenting it with enough structure, authority, and precision that the court sees a reason to devote serious attention to it. In *Cutbank*, the appellant did not do that, and the appellee tried to go beyond the lines. The court, for its part, simply drew the lines back where they belonged.
