Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic, Not Propensity: The Eighth Circuit’s Expansive View of Prior Gun Possession in *United States v. Cutbank*

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Cutbank* is not just another routine affirmance in a felon-in-possession case. It is a useful window into how far courts are willing to let prosecutors go in proving firearm possession with evidence that looks, at first glance, like classic prior-bad-act material. The panel approved testimony that two witnesses had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle on prior occasions, treating that evidence as “intrinsic” to the charged offense rather than as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. That matters because once evidence is labeled intrinsic, the normal safeguards associated with other-act evidence largely fall away.

The practical effect is significant. In gun cases, especially felon-in-possession prosecutions, the government often lacks direct proof of contemporaneous possession and instead builds its case from witness recollection, physical evidence, and inference. *Cutbank* confirms that in the Eighth Circuit, evidence of earlier possession of the same firearm can be a powerful and relatively easy route around Rule 404(b) whenever the government can frame it as showing ownership, control, or “continuous possession.”

## The court’s reasoning

The key evidentiary ruling came before trial, when Cutbank sought to bar D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had seen him with the sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. He argued the testimony was impermissible propensity evidence. The district court disagreed, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing its prior decision in *United States v. Battle*, which held that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

That is the core move. The panel did not treat the witnesses’ testimony as evidence of a defendant’s bad character or general gun familiarity. Instead, it viewed the prior sightings of the same rifle as part of the proof of possession itself. If the jury is deciding whether the defendant possessed this particular gun on the night in question, evidence that he had repeatedly possessed that same gun before is said to illuminate a fact in issue, not merely his disposition.

The opinion also frames the evidence as relevant to “continuous possession” before the incident. That phrasing is important. It suggests that the government is not limited to a single snapshot in time. If it can show that the firearm was the same weapon, and that the defendant had it before and during the charged episode, prior sightings become part of the narrative of ongoing possession rather than a separate bad act.

## A broad label with broad consequences

The opinion reflects a broader trend in firearm cases: once the government can tie an uncharged episode to the charged weapon, “intrinsic evidence” becomes a very flexible category. That flexibility helps prosecutors because it avoids the formal Rule 404(b) analysis: no need to identify a permissible nonpropensity purpose, no need to litigate whether the evidence is too remote or too prejudicial in relation to that purpose, and less pressure to give limiting instructions.

That does not mean the testimony escapes all scrutiny. Rule 403 still applies, and courts can exclude evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. But in practice, once a court concludes the evidence goes to identity, ownership, or possession of the same gun, Rule 403 usually does not do much work. The probative value is treated as obvious.

What is striking about *Cutbank* is how little the panel pauses over the risk that the “intrinsic” label can swallow the 404(b) rule in firearm cases. The witnesses’ testimony was not about a discrete event on the night of the offense; it concerned prior occasions when they had seen Cutbank with the same rifle. That is certainly probative of possession. But it is also the kind of evidence that can invite the jury to reason, consciously or not, that a defendant who habitually carried the gun must be the kind of person who possessed it again. The court never really engages that danger.

## What this means for defense lawyers

For defense counsel, *Cutbank* underscores the difficulty of drawing a line between evidence of possession and evidence of other wrongdoing in firearm prosecutions. If the government can connect prior conduct to the same firearm, it will often characterize that proof as direct evidence of the charged offense, not as a separate “other act.” That makes motions in limine harder, especially where witnesses can say they saw the weapon before and during the charged period.

The most promising defense strategy may be to attack the connective tissue. Was it really the same gun? How reliable is the witness’s identification? How close in time were the prior observations? Is the government really proving possession, or just trying to make the defendant look like a gun person? Those are the questions that matter if the defense wants to force the court to confront prejudice rather than accept the intrinsic-evidence label at face value.

There is also a broader litigation lesson here: the more the government can frame a case as one of “continuous possession,” the easier it is to admit prior sightings and prior handling of the firearm. That may be especially true in cases involving modified weapons, where witnesses can identify distinctive features like a sawed-off barrel or stock. The more distinctive the gun, the easier it is for the prosecution to argue that earlier sightings are not just relevant but confirmatory.

## The doctrinal takeaway

*Cutbank* is not doctrinally revolutionary, but it is revealing. The Eighth Circuit is comfortable treating prior possession of the same weapon as direct proof of a felon-in-possession charge. That is a sensible evidentiary principle in the abstract: possession is often proved circumstantially, and prior familiarity with the weapon can be probative. But the opinion also shows how quickly “intrinsic” evidence can become a catchall for uncharged conduct that bolsters the government’s story.

For now, defendants in the Eighth Circuit will face an uphill battle when they try to exclude testimony that they were seen with the same gun before the charged offense. The decision suggests that, at least in this circuit, the line between propensity evidence and proof of possession is thin indeed. And in practice, that may make Rule 404(b) less of a gatekeeping rule than a label the government can often avoid altogether.
