Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# When the Firearm Guideline Beats ACCA: Sentencing Logic in *United States v. Cutbank*

## Why this case matters

The sentencing question in *United States v. Cutbank* is a useful reminder that “career criminal” cases do not always turn on the Armed Career Criminal Act’s own guideline. Sometimes, the ordinary firearm guideline, plus its enhancements, produces a higher offense level than the Armed Career Criminal Guideline does on its own. When that happens, the Guidelines instruct the court to use the higher number. That is what happened here, and the Eighth Circuit had little trouble affirming.

The opinion is also a neat illustration of a recurring theme in federal sentencing appeals: once the district court has correctly calculated the advisory range, arguments framed as attacks on the Guidelines often collapse into the more familiar claim that the sentence is too high under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The panel treated Cutbank’s “parsimony principle” objection in exactly that way.

## The guideline math

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and was also found by the jury to qualify for ACCA’s enhanced penalties. The Presentence Investigation Report started with USSG § 2K2.1, the ordinary firearm guideline, and arrived at a total offense level of 35 after several enhancements: the sawed-off firearm base offense level, the altered serial number increase, the reckless-endangerment increase for fleeing police, and—most importantly here—a four-level bump under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm “in connection with another felony offense.”

That level was then compared with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4. The opinion notes that the ACCA guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34. Because the Guidelines direct courts to apply the greater offense level, the PSR and district court used 35. That is the core of the sentencing issue: ACCA status did not cap the range at the armed-career-criminal number. It only supplied one possible benchmark. The firearm guideline, with enhancements, beat it.

That result is not exotic; it is built into the structure of the Guidelines. But it matters because defendants often assume ACCA is the ceiling, when in fact it can be overtaken by a more heavily enhanced offense-level calculation under § 2K2.1.

## The real fight: guideline error or reasonableness challenge?

Cutbank argued on appeal that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) created a sentence that conflicted with § 3553(a)’s command that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The panel effectively answered: that is not a Guidelines-calculation argument at all, at least not once the district court’s factual findings and the PSR are accepted.

Why? Because the enhancement applies if the firearm was used or possessed “in connection with another felony offense,” and the commentary makes clear that this is so “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The district court adopted the PSR’s factual account without objection, and the Eighth Circuit therefore saw no clear error in the application of the enhancement. Once that step was complete, there was no calculation error left to correct.

At that point, Cutbank’s complaint became what it really was: a claim that the resulting within-range sentence was too severe. The panel dealt with that under the usual appellate framework, presuming a within-Guidelines sentence reasonable and asking whether the district court had meaningfully considered the § 3553(a) factors. The court had done so.

## What the panel’s reasoning signals

The most interesting feature of the opinion is its quiet insistence on the distinction between Guidelines law and sentencing policy. A defendant can certainly argue that a guideline mechanically ratchets up punishment in a way that overstates culpability. But that argument belongs in the district court’s § 3553(a) analysis, not in a claim that the court “misapplied” the Guidelines, unless there is a genuine calculation error.

That distinction matters because appellate review is far more deferential once the range is correctly calculated. The Eighth Circuit quoted the usual line: a district court must begin by correctly calculating the range, and only then may it consider whether the statutory factors warrant a variance. If the court does that, a within-range sentence comes to the court of appeals with a presumption of reasonableness. *Cutbank* shows how hard it is to dislodge that presumption when the offense conduct is violent and the record is aggravating.

## The limited force of the parsimony principle

Cutbank’s invocation of the “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” principle was not frivolous, but the opinion shows its limits in practice. The parsimony clause is a command to the sentencing judge, not a free-standing appellate veto. Once the district court has correctly calculated the range and explained that it considered the relevant factors, appellate review is narrow. The court need not give a rote recitation of every factor, and the Eighth Circuit will presume that arguments presented to the sentencing judge were considered and rejected absent evidence to the contrary.

In other words, the parsimony principle does real work at sentencing, but it rarely functions as a stand-alone reason to reverse a sentence that falls within the correctly calculated range—especially where, as here, the defendant’s conduct included domestic terror, assault, kidnapping-like confinement, and violence against officers.

## Bottom line

*Cutbank* is not a major sentencing-law case, but it is a clean example of how ACCA and § 2K2.1 can interact. The ordinary firearm guideline, enhanced for possession in connection with another felony offense, can outpace the Armed Career Criminal Guideline. When that happens, the “sentencing issue” on appeal is often less about guideline mechanics than about whether the district court should have varied downward. Here, the Eighth Circuit had little difficulty saying no.
