Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# 1. Case Overview, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## The charged offense and the case’s overall shape

United States v. Justin Cutbank is a federal criminal appeal arising from a felon-in-possession prosecution under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). The government charged Cutbank, who had an extensive felony record, with unlawfully possessing a firearm after having been convicted of prior felonies. The case also implicated the Armed Career Criminal Act, or ACCA, because the jury found that Cutbank had at least three prior violent felony convictions. That finding exposed him to an enhanced sentencing framework and helped drive the length of the sentence ultimately imposed.

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion is structured around two broad categories of issues. First, Cutbank challenged several evidentiary and procedural rulings that he said undermined the fairness of his trial. Second, he challenged a sentencing enhancement under the advisory Sentencing Guidelines, specifically USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which increased his guidelines range. The court rejected all of his arguments and affirmed both the conviction and the sentence.

## The events of November 19–20, 2020

The factual story begins with a violent domestic episode in the home of Cutbank’s girlfriend, D.F. During the night of November 19 to 20, 2020, Cutbank had been drinking and using methamphetamine. According to D.F.’s testimony, he awakened her, pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. While holding her there, he made paranoid accusations about “listening devices” in the house and people outside waiting to harm him. He kept her in the closet at gunpoint for what she described as hours, threatened to kill her, and struck her across the face with the gun.

The home was not empty. D.F.’s two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F., were also present. At some point, Cutbank ordered D.F. to wake the others. Eventually, he fled the home, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the gun with him. D.F. then called police. These facts matter because they supplied the government’s theory of firearm possession and also formed part of the background for the later sentencing issues.

## The second incident and the police response

Police soon received a separate call from a house about half a mile away from D.F.’s residence. Someone reported that a man was in the garage and refused to leave. When officers arrived, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage. Over the next several hours, he made erratic statements and claimed he had a weapon. When officers attempted to detain him, he resisted violently, including biting an officer in the face and neck and grabbing the officer’s gun.

This second incident mattered for two reasons. First, it provided additional evidence tying Cutbank to a firearm and to a course of conduct continuing after the events at D.F.’s home. Second, his conduct during the arrest became relevant to sentencing, including the later discussion of whether he recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police.

## The physical evidence recovered along the route

Investigators searched the area Cutbank likely traveled between D.F.’s house and the garage. Along that path they found D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA recovered from the gun matched Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the weapon as one they had seen Cutbank with before the night in question. This combination of physical and testimonial evidence was central to the government’s proof that Cutbank possessed the firearm charged in the indictment.

The rifle’s condition also mattered. The PSR later described it as a sawed-off firearm, which affected the base offense level under the Guidelines. That detail became part of the sentencing calculation, even though the appellate opinion’s main sentencing dispute focused on a different enhancement.

## The trial and the jury’s findings

A jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. The jury also found that he had been convicted of at least three violent felonies, triggering ACCA’s enhanced penalties under § 924(e). That finding was important because it significantly increased the statutory sentencing exposure and framed the district court’s later decision about the appropriate sentence within the advisory Guidelines range.

The opinion emphasizes that the evidence was substantial. The government presented both witness testimony and physical evidence linking Cutbank to the rifle. That strength of proof becomes relevant later when the court discusses whether any evidentiary mistakes could have affected the verdict or were instead harmless.

## The sentence imposed and the issues on appeal

At sentencing, the district court imposed a term of 292 months’ imprisonment, which fell within the advisory Guidelines range calculated by the court. The PSR recommended several enhancements, including a four-level increase under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense. Cutbank argued on appeal that this enhancement was incorrectly applied and that the resulting sentence was greater than necessary under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a).

He also appealed a series of evidentiary and procedural rulings from the trial. These included disputes over testimony that he had possessed the rifle before the charged incident, a request for a limiting instruction regarding statements made to police, testimony about D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun, and the court’s handling of attempted impeachment of C.F. with police reports. The Eighth Circuit rejected each argument and affirmed the judgment in full.

Understanding this procedural posture is essential for reading the rest of the opinion. The court is not retrying the case; it is reviewing the district court’s discretionary rulings for abuse of discretion or clear error, and it is reviewing the sentence for procedural and substantive reasonableness. The strength of the evidence, the jury’s ACCA finding, and the within-guidelines sentence all shape the court’s deferential analysis.

# 2. Prior Possession Evidence and the Line Between Intrinsic Proof and Rule 404(b)

## Cutbank’s pretrial motion to exclude testimony about earlier sightings of the rifle

One of Cutbank’s first trial-related challenges was aimed at testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the same sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the night of the charged offense. Before trial, he moved to keep that testimony out. His argument was that if the witnesses told the jury they had seen him with the gun on earlier occasions, the jury would use that evidence to conclude that he had a bad character or a criminal disposition and therefore likely possessed the firearm on the charged night. That is the kind of inference Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) is designed to limit.

## The defense theory: prior sightings as improper propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)

Rule 404(b) generally bars evidence of other acts when the only point is to show that a person acted in conformity with a bad character trait. In a criminal case, that means the government cannot usually prove guilt by showing that the defendant has handled weapons before and therefore must have done so again. Cutbank’s position was that testimony about earlier sightings of the rifle was just that: evidence of prior bad acts introduced to suggest he was the sort of person who possessed guns illegally.

## The government’s theory: intrinsic evidence showing ownership, control, and continuous possession of the same weapon

The government responded that the testimony was not “other acts” evidence at all. Instead, it was intrinsic to the charged possession offense because it helped identify the very firearm found along the route between the two locations and helped show Cutbank’s continuous possession of that same weapon. In a felon-in-possession case, identity of the gun and dominion over it are central issues. Evidence that the same people had seen Cutbank with the same weapon before the charged events makes it more likely that the gun recovered by police was his gun and that his possession on the charged night was knowing and not accidental.

## The district court’s ruling allowing D.F. and C.F. to testify about prior observations

The district court accepted the government’s view and allowed the testimony. It concluded that the prior sightings were intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge, not inadmissible propensity evidence. At trial, D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a “handful of times” before the incident, and C.F. likewise testified that she had seen the gun used during the incident “once or twice” before. The court treated this as evidence that helped the jury determine whether Cutbank possessed the same firearm recovered after the incident.

## The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on United States v. Battle: prior possession of the same firearm as directly relevant to later possession

The court of appeals agreed and relied on its earlier decision in United States v. Battle. Battle states the key point: prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that firearm because it helps establish ownership or control. That principle matters because the disputed evidence was not about some unrelated gun or some general history of gun use. It was about the same sawed-off rifle recovered after the charged events. When the government must prove knowing possession of a particular firearm, earlier observations of the defendant with that same weapon can be powerful proof of identity and control rather than impermissible character evidence.

## How the testimony helped prove knowing possession of the recovered sawed-off rifle

The evidence did more than suggest Cutbank had access to guns in general. It linked him specifically to the rifle that police found near the route he likely took from D.F.’s home to the garage where he was later found. That link mattered because the prosecution’s case depended on showing that the rifle was his and that he knowingly possessed it during the events in question. D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony supported that inference by showing that the gun was not a new or unfamiliar object introduced into the story after the fact, but a weapon both witnesses had seen him with before.

## The appellate court’s treatment of Cutbank’s failure to meaningfully challenge the district court’s reasoning

The Eighth Circuit also noted that Cutbank’s appellate briefing did not meaningfully engage with the district court’s explanation. He did not provide authority undermining the court’s conclusion that the testimony was intrinsic evidence relevant to possession and continuous control of the firearm. That mattered because appellate argument is not just a complaint that evidence hurt the defense; it must explain why the trial court’s legal characterization was wrong. Here, Cutbank offered little beyond the assertion that the testimony should have been treated as propensity evidence.

## Why the court saw no evidentiary error in admitting the prior-possession testimony

Because the testimony went directly to possession of the same recovered firearm, the court held that its admission was not error. The key distinction was between evidence offered merely to show bad character and evidence offered to prove an element of the charged offense. The prior sightings fell into the second category. For that reason, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s ruling and treated the testimony as proper proof of the felon-in-possession charge rather than forbidden Rule 404(b) evidence.

# 3. Trial Management, Hearsay, Cross-Examination, and Evidentiary Discretion

## Cutbank’s request for a limiting instruction concerning D.F.’s statements to police before she testified

One of Cutbank’s pretrial complaints was about how the jury would hear evidence of statements D.F. made to police after the incident. He asked the district court to instruct the jury in advance that it could not use those statements as proof of what Cutbank actually did unless and until D.F. later testified to those events herself. His concern was that the statements might be treated as hearsay or might raise Confrontation Clause problems if the jury heard them before the declarant took the stand.

The district court declined to give the instruction at that stage. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit treated that ruling as a routine exercise of trial management. The key point is that a trial judge does not have to give a limiting instruction the moment evidence is mentioned if the judge plans to give an appropriate instruction later and if the evidence is being admitted for a proper purpose.

## The government’s position that officers would testify for context and not for inadmissible hearsay purposes

The government responded that the officers’ testimony about D.F.’s statements was not being offered as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct. Instead, it was meant to explain why the officers acted as they did and what information they had when they responded. That matters because testimony offered to show the effect on the listener, or to provide context for police conduct, is not necessarily hearsay.

The appellate court accepted that framing. It noted that the record did not show the district court actually admitted hearsay during the trial. In other words, the government’s evidence was being used in a conventional and permissible way: to give the jury a coherent picture of the investigation and the officers’ actions, not to prove the truth of the out-of-court statements themselves.

## Standard of review for refusal to give a limiting instruction: abuse of discretion

The Eighth Circuit reviewed the refusal to give an immediate limiting instruction for abuse of discretion. That is a deferential standard. It means the appellate court does not ask whether it would have handled the matter differently, but only whether the trial court’s decision was outside the range of reasonable choices.

The court relied on its prior decision in United States v. Bennett, which says a trial judge need not issue a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is admitted if the judge later gives an appropriate instruction at the close of trial. Here, because the district court acted within that discretion and the trial record did not show improper use of the statements, there was no reversible error.

## Why the court found no hearsay problem on the record

The court’s hearsay analysis was brief because the record did not reveal a hearsay violation. The important distinction is between statements that are admitted for their truth and statements admitted for some other reason, such as explaining police conduct. Cutbank’s argument assumed the former, but the trial record did not support that assumption.

This is a recurring appellate theme: an objection based on hearsay must be tied to what the evidence was actually used to prove. If the statements were not offered as truth, then the hearsay rule is not implicated in the same way. The Eighth Circuit therefore concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion by declining Cutbank’s requested instruction.

## Why the Confrontation Clause was satisfied once D.F. testified in person at trial

Cutbank also tried to frame the issue as a Confrontation Clause problem. The court rejected that argument for a simple reason: D.F. did testify at trial. Under the Sixth Amendment, the core concern is whether the defendant has an opportunity to confront and cross-examine the declarant. If the declarant actually appears in court and testifies, the constitutional concern is ordinarily satisfied.

The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Charboneau for that proposition. Because D.F. was present, testified, and could be cross-examined, there was no Confrontation Clause violation from the jury hearing references to her earlier statements.

## D.F.’s cross-examination about prior inconsistent statements concerning the gun

A separate issue arose during D.F.’s cross-examination. The defense used prior inconsistent statements to challenge her testimony about the gun, including her uncertainty about whether she saw it on the night in question. In response, D.F. explained that part of her confusion stemmed from the fact that she was not familiar with guns and did not like them.

That answer then led into a more emotionally charged statement: she did not like guns because her sister had been killed by one. Cutbank moved to strike that portion of the testimony. The district court denied the motion, and the Eighth Circuit upheld that ruling.

The key point is that the testimony came in as an explanation of why D.F. may have made inconsistent statements. Once the defense attacked her credibility on that subject, her explanation was relevant to rehabilitate her testimony and to help the jury understand her reactions.

## Her explanation that she was unfamiliar with guns and disliked them because her sister had been killed by a gun

The appellate court treated this explanation as a direct response to the defense’s impeachment. When a witness is confronted with prior inconsistent statements, the witness may be allowed to explain why those statements were made. D.F.’s reference to her sister’s death was part of that explanation.

Cutbank argued that the statement should have been stricken, but he did not show that it created unfair prejudice or confusion significant enough to outweigh its probative value. The court invoked Rule 403, which permits exclusion of relevant evidence only when its unfair prejudice substantially outweighs its value, or when it creates a serious risk of misleading the jury or wasting time. The panel saw no such problem here.

## The motion to strike that testimony and the Rule 403 prejudice analysis

The Eighth Circuit emphasized that evidentiary rulings like this are entrusted to the district court’s broad discretion. The trial judge is in the best position to evaluate the tone, context, and likely impact of testimony. Absent a clear showing of unfair prejudice, appellate courts are reluctant to second-guess those calls.

That is why the court found no abuse of discretion in refusing to strike the remark about D.F.’s sister. The defense had opened the door by pressing D.F. on inconsistency and memory, and her explanation did not appear to inject an improper emotional issue that overwhelmed the trial.

## C.F.’s similar mention of D.F.’s sister and the absence of a defense objection

The same general subject came up again when C.F. testified as a government witness and mentioned that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun. Unlike with D.F., the defense did not object to C.F.’s remark. That absence of objection mattered because it weakened any claim of reversible error and suggested the defense did not view the statement as so prejudicial at the time that immediate intervention was required.

The court did not dwell on this point, but it reinforced the larger message: this was not the kind of inflammatory or outcome-determinative testimony that would justify reversal. The appellate court saw the trial judge as managing a difficult factual trial within the bounds of ordinary discretion.

## The defense effort to impeach C.F. with prior statements made to a police investigator

The defense next tried to impeach C.F. by showing that she had made prior statements to police that differed from her trial testimony about the gun. The district court allowed the defense to ask about what C.F. had told the investigator. But the court stopped defense counsel from reading portions of the police report into the record because the report itself was not properly authenticated as C.F.’s statement.

That distinction is important. A lawyer may cross-examine a witness about prior statements, but if counsel wants to introduce a document as extrinsic proof of the inconsistency, the document must first be shown to be what the proponent claims it is.

## The district court’s requirement that the police report be properly authenticated before its contents could be read into the record

The trial judge required proper foundation before allowing the police report to be read. In practical terms, that means someone had to establish that the report accurately reflected C.F.’s own prior statement or otherwise met the requirements for admissibility. Because the report was not authored by C.F., the court would not let the defense simply read it into evidence without authentication.

The Eighth Circuit agreed. The panel cited United States v. Cumbie for the proposition that extrinsic evidence used to impeach by prior inconsistent statement still must be authenticated. So even if the goal is only impeachment, the document cannot bypass the ordinary evidentiary requirements.

## Use of extrinsic impeachment evidence and the authentication requirement under United States v. Cumbie

This part of the opinion illustrates an important trial-evidence principle. Prior inconsistent statements can be powerful impeachment tools, but they do not become admissible automatically just because a witness denies or minimizes them. The proponent must still establish the document’s identity and reliability enough to get it in front of the jury.

The district court’s insistence on authentication was therefore not a technical obstacle for its own sake. It was a straightforward application of the rules governing documentary evidence. The Eighth Circuit saw no abuse of discretion in enforcing that foundation requirement.

## The appellate court’s criticism of the government for unnecessarily broadening the issues on appeal

The opinion includes a brief aside criticizing the government for trying to expand the appeal beyond what Cutbank had actually challenged. The panel observed that the government described an issue as if Cutbank had made a broader claim that the district court had “thwarted” his cross-examination, even though he had not clearly raised that argument in the way the government suggested.

The court’s comment is minor, but it is a useful reminder about appellate practice. Appellate courts prefer parties to focus on the issues actually preserved and presented, rather than recasting them in more dramatic terms. Still, because the claimed error lacked merit in any event, the court did not need to decide much about preservation.

## The broader principle that trial judges have substantial leeway in controlling the admission and use of evidence

Taken together, these rulings show the Eighth Circuit’s willingness to defer to the district court’s day-to-day management of trial evidence. The trial judge controlled when and how statements came in, whether a limiting instruction was needed immediately, whether testimony created unfair prejudice, and whether a document had been properly authenticated before it could be used for impeachment.

The court also made clear that even if one of these rulings had been questionable, reversal would still require prejudice. Because the government’s evidence was strong, and because the challenged rulings were minor in the context of the whole trial, the panel concluded that none of the evidentiary issues deprived Cutbank of a fair trial. The opinion thus shows how appellate courts review trial management: not by micromanaging each ruling, but by asking whether the district court stayed within the bounds of discretion and whether any error actually affected the verdict.

# 4. Harmless Error, Cumulative Error, and Why the Conviction Was Affirmed

## The harmless-error standard for evidentiary rulings in criminal cases

After rejecting each of Cutbank’s individual evidentiary and procedural complaints, the Eighth Circuit turned to a more basic point: even if one or more rulings had been mistaken, that would not automatically require reversal. Appellate courts do not reverse every trial error. In criminal cases, an erroneous evidentiary ruling warrants reversal only if it affected the defendant’s substantial rights—in practical terms, if the mistake likely mattered to the verdict.

The opinion uses the familiar harmless-error formulation from Eighth Circuit precedent: the court will reverse only if the jury “may have been substantially swayed” by the challenged evidence. Put differently, the question is not whether the trial was perfect, but whether the alleged error likely influenced the outcome. If the answer is no, the error is harmless and the conviction stands.

## The requirement that the defendant show the jury may have been substantially swayed by the challenged evidence

Cutbank could not meet that burden. The court emphasized that a defendant must do more than point to a disputed ruling and assert unfairness; he must show a real possibility that the jury’s verdict turned on the challenged evidence. That is a demanding standard where the government’s proof is strong.

Here, the court concluded that any evidentiary missteps “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” That language matters. It shows the court was looking not for theoretical error, but for practical effect. If the remaining evidence overwhelmingly supports the conviction, then even a questionable ruling is unlikely to have changed the jury’s view of the case.

## The court’s view that the government’s case was substantial both physically and testimonialy

The Eighth Circuit described the government’s case as substantial on both testimonial and physical grounds. That is the core of the harmless-error analysis in this opinion. The court did not need to parse every challenged ruling in isolation once it was satisfied that the overall proof of guilt was strong.

The testimony came from the victim, D.F., and from C.F., both of whom connected Cutbank to the firearm and the events of the night in question. Their accounts placed Cutbank with the gun before and during the incident. In addition, officers recovered the rifle along the path Cutbank likely took after fleeing, along with D.F.’s cell phone and a butcher-style knife. Most importantly, DNA on the gun matched Cutbank. That physical evidence independently tied him to the weapon.

Because the proof was strong from multiple directions, the court viewed the challenged rulings as unlikely to have changed the outcome. The opinion makes clear that when the government has direct eyewitness testimony plus corroborating physical evidence, appellate courts are much less likely to find prejudice from disputed evidentiary rulings.

## Key evidence supporting conviction: D.F.’s account, C.F.’s identification, recovered firearm, and DNA match

The court’s harmless-error conclusion rests on the particular evidence presented at trial. D.F. testified that Cutbank pointed the gun at her, kept her in a closet at gunpoint, struck her with the gun, and fled with it. She also identified the firearm as one she had seen him with before. C.F. likewise identified the weapon as one she had seen Cutbank use before the incident. Their testimony was important not merely because it described the events, but because it linked Cutbank to the specific rifle recovered by police.

The physical evidence reinforced those accounts. Police found the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle near the route Cutbank likely took between the two locations. DNA testing matched Cutbank to the firearm. That kind of forensic evidence is especially powerful in a possession case because it provides an objective connection between the defendant and the gun.

Taken together, this evidence made the prosecution’s case far more than a close credibility contest. The jury had eyewitness testimony, the recovered weapon, and DNA evidence. Against that background, the court concluded that any marginal evidentiary ruling was unlikely to have altered the verdict.

## Why any alleged evidentiary missteps would have had at most a slight influence on the verdict

This is where the harmless-error analysis does real work. Several of Cutbank’s objections concerned the admission or use of testimony that helped explain his prior possession of the rifle or provided context for the events of the night. Even if one assumed that one of those rulings was imperfect, the appellate court found no reason to think the jury would have acquitted without it.

That conclusion depends on the strength and redundancy of the proof. If one piece of evidence is challenged, but the same fact is established by other admissible evidence, the impact of the challenged material is reduced. Here, the evidence that Cutbank possessed the gun before and during the offense came from multiple witnesses and from DNA. So the court reasoned that the disputed testimony likely added little to what the jury already heard from other sources.

This is why the court could say the rulings had only a “slight influence,” if any. The question is not whether the evidence was relevant or emotionally charged, but whether it was outcome-determinative. The court concluded it was not.

## The cumulative-error doctrine and its requirement of substantial prejudice

Cutbank also could not win by aggregating the claimed mistakes. The cumulative-error doctrine recognizes that several minor errors, none independently reversible, can sometimes combine to deprive a defendant of a fair trial. But the standard is still demanding. The defendant must show substantial prejudice from the combined effect of the errors.

The Eighth Circuit rejected that argument as well. Because it found no significant individual error and no meaningful prejudice from any alleged mistake, there was no basis for concluding that the trial as a whole was unfair. In other words, adding together harmless or near-harmless rulings does not create reversible error unless their combined effect actually undermines confidence in the verdict.

## The Eighth Circuit’s conclusion that Cutbank failed to show prejudice from the combined effect of the challenged rulings

The court’s treatment of cumulative error was brief because its premise was straightforward: the challenged rulings, whether viewed separately or together, did not create substantial prejudice. The government’s case was too strong for that. The jury heard detailed testimony from the victim, corroboration from another witness, and forensic evidence tying Cutbank to the gun. On that record, the appellate court saw no realistic possibility that the alleged errors, alone or in combination, changed the result.

This part of the opinion also reflects the court’s confidence in the trial judge’s discretion over evidence. Many of Cutbank’s complaints concerned ordinary evidentiary calls—whether testimony was intrinsic, whether a limiting instruction was needed, whether certain impeachment material had been properly authenticated. Those are the kinds of rulings appellate courts usually review deferentially. Once the court determined that none of them caused meaningful prejudice, the cumulative-error theory collapsed.

## How this analysis independently supports affirmance of the conviction

The harmless-error and cumulative-error analysis independently supports affirmance, even apart from the court’s earlier conclusion that the individual evidentiary rulings were correct. This is an important appellate move. The court did not need to hold that every ruling was perfect; it only needed to decide that any possible imperfections did not warrant a new trial.

For a law student, the lesson is that appellate review of trial error has two layers. First, the court asks whether there was error. Second, even if there was, it asks whether the error mattered. In Cutbank’s case, the Eighth Circuit answered the second question decisively: the evidence of guilt was substantial, the challenged rulings likely had little effect, and the defendant showed no substantial prejudice. That is why the conviction was affirmed.

# 5. Sentencing Under ACCA and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement

## The ACCA finding and the PSR’s guideline calculation

Cutbank’s sentencing appeal comes after the jury found him guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and also found that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions. That finding mattered because it triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act’s enhanced penalty scheme. In other words, once the jury found the qualifying prior convictions, the statutory and guideline landscape changed dramatically: Cutbank was no longer being sentenced as an ordinary felon-in-possession defendant.

The Presentence Investigation Report then translated the facts of the case into an advisory Guidelines range. It recommended a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm and Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence. It then added three levels because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number, two levels because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and four levels under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. The resulting total offense level was 35. With Criminal History Category VI, the advisory range became 292 to 365 months.

That framework is important because the Eighth Circuit’s sentencing analysis begins with the premise that the district court must calculate the Guidelines correctly. If the Guidelines range is wrong, that is a procedural problem; if the range is correct, the court then asks whether the sentence is reasonable under § 3553(a).

## The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement

Cutbank’s main sentencing argument was directed at the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). That provision applies when a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense.” The guideline commentary makes clear that the enhancement applies even if the defendant was never separately charged with, or convicted of, that other felony. The court relied on application note 14(C) for that point.

The panel treated the district court’s finding as supported by the PSR facts. At sentencing, Cutbank did not object to the PSR’s factual account, including the descriptions of his prior conduct and the events tied to the firearm offense. That matters because unobjected-to PSR facts may be treated as admitted for sentencing purposes. So when the district court concluded that the gun was used in connection with another felony, it did so on a record that Cutbank had not disputed. The Eighth Circuit therefore saw no clear error, and certainly no reversible error, in applying the enhancement.

## Why the court rejected the § 3553(a) argument

Cutbank tried to recast the guideline issue as a broader sentencing policy objection. He argued that applying the four-level enhancement produced a sentence that conflicted with the “parsimony principle” in § 3553(a), which says a sentence must be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes of sentencing.

The court was not persuaded. Its reasoning was that the Guidelines must be calculated correctly before the district court weighs the § 3553(a) factors. The Supreme Court cases the panel cited make that sequencing explicit. Under Gall, the district court must begin by correctly calculating the advisory range. Under Rosales-Mireles, an appellate court reviewing reasonableness must first ensure that there was no significant procedural error, including a miscalculation of the range. Cutbank’s argument did not show that the enhancement was unlawful; it amounted to a disagreement with how the properly calculated range fit within the broader sentencing factors. That is not enough to invalidate the sentence.

The panel also noted that the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded an offense level of 34, but the Guidelines instruct the court to use the greater offense level when multiple calculations apply. So even in ACCA cases, the guideline system still depends on the interaction of the various offense-level rules.

## Procedural reasonableness and substantive reasonableness

This part of the opinion is a useful example of the difference between procedural and substantive sentencing review. Procedural reasonableness concerns whether the district court followed the required steps: correctly calculating the Guidelines, considering the PSR, and taking the § 3553(a) factors into account. Substantive reasonableness asks whether the sentence chosen is too harsh or too lenient in light of the circumstances.

Cutbank’s argument about the enhancement primarily attacked procedural correctness, because he challenged the guideline calculation itself. The court concluded that the calculation was correct. Once that was resolved, the question became whether the 292-month sentence was substantively unreasonable. Because the sentence fell within the properly calculated Guidelines range, it was entitled to a presumption of reasonableness on appeal in the Eighth Circuit.

The district court’s sentencing explanation also supported that result. The judge reviewed the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda and considered the § 3553(a) factors. The Eighth Circuit said that is enough; a judge does not have to recite each factor mechanically, only make clear that the factors were actually considered.

## Why the sentence was affirmed

The facts supporting the sentence were serious. Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms. The instant offense was not a simple possession case: it involved domestic abuse, confinement of the victim at gunpoint, threats, assault, and later violence against a police officer when he resisted arrest. Those facts gave the district court substantial grounds to impose a lengthy sentence within the advisory range.

Because the district court correctly calculated the Guidelines, properly applied the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, and reasonably weighed the § 3553(a) factors, the Eighth Circuit found no procedural error and no abuse of sentencing discretion. The 292-month sentence was affirmed.
