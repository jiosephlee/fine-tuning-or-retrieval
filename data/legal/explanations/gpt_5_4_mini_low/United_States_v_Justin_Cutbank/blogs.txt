Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic, Not Propensity: The Eighth Circuit’s Expansive View of Prior Gun Possession in *United States v. Cutbank*

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Cutbank* is not just another routine affirmance in a felon-in-possession case. It is a useful window into how far courts are willing to let prosecutors go in proving firearm possession with evidence that looks, at first glance, like classic prior-bad-act material. The panel approved testimony that two witnesses had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle on prior occasions, treating that evidence as “intrinsic” to the charged offense rather than as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. That matters because once evidence is labeled intrinsic, the normal safeguards associated with other-act evidence largely fall away.

The practical effect is significant. In gun cases, especially felon-in-possession prosecutions, the government often lacks direct proof of contemporaneous possession and instead builds its case from witness recollection, physical evidence, and inference. *Cutbank* confirms that in the Eighth Circuit, evidence of earlier possession of the same firearm can be a powerful and relatively easy route around Rule 404(b) whenever the government can frame it as showing ownership, control, or “continuous possession.”

## The court’s reasoning

The key evidentiary ruling came before trial, when Cutbank sought to bar D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had seen him with the sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. He argued the testimony was impermissible propensity evidence. The district court disagreed, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing its prior decision in *United States v. Battle*, which held that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

That is the core move. The panel did not treat the witnesses’ testimony as evidence of a defendant’s bad character or general gun familiarity. Instead, it viewed the prior sightings of the same rifle as part of the proof of possession itself. If the jury is deciding whether the defendant possessed this particular gun on the night in question, evidence that he had repeatedly possessed that same gun before is said to illuminate a fact in issue, not merely his disposition.

The opinion also frames the evidence as relevant to “continuous possession” before the incident. That phrasing is important. It suggests that the government is not limited to a single snapshot in time. If it can show that the firearm was the same weapon, and that the defendant had it before and during the charged episode, prior sightings become part of the narrative of ongoing possession rather than a separate bad act.

## A broad label with broad consequences

The opinion reflects a broader trend in firearm cases: once the government can tie an uncharged episode to the charged weapon, “intrinsic evidence” becomes a very flexible category. That flexibility helps prosecutors because it avoids the formal Rule 404(b) analysis: no need to identify a permissible nonpropensity purpose, no need to litigate whether the evidence is too remote or too prejudicial in relation to that purpose, and less pressure to give limiting instructions.

That does not mean the testimony escapes all scrutiny. Rule 403 still applies, and courts can exclude evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. But in practice, once a court concludes the evidence goes to identity, ownership, or possession of the same gun, Rule 403 usually does not do much work. The probative value is treated as obvious.

What is striking about *Cutbank* is how little the panel pauses over the risk that the “intrinsic” label can swallow the 404(b) rule in firearm cases. The witnesses’ testimony was not about a discrete event on the night of the offense; it concerned prior occasions when they had seen Cutbank with the same rifle. That is certainly probative of possession. But it is also the kind of evidence that can invite the jury to reason, consciously or not, that a defendant who habitually carried the gun must be the kind of person who possessed it again. The court never really engages that danger.

## What this means for defense lawyers

For defense counsel, *Cutbank* underscores the difficulty of drawing a line between evidence of possession and evidence of other wrongdoing in firearm prosecutions. If the government can connect prior conduct to the same firearm, it will often characterize that proof as direct evidence of the charged offense, not as a separate “other act.” That makes motions in limine harder, especially where witnesses can say they saw the weapon before and during the charged period.

The most promising defense strategy may be to attack the connective tissue. Was it really the same gun? How reliable is the witness’s identification? How close in time were the prior observations? Is the government really proving possession, or just trying to make the defendant look like a gun person? Those are the questions that matter if the defense wants to force the court to confront prejudice rather than accept the intrinsic-evidence label at face value.

There is also a broader litigation lesson here: the more the government can frame a case as one of “continuous possession,” the easier it is to admit prior sightings and prior handling of the firearm. That may be especially true in cases involving modified weapons, where witnesses can identify distinctive features like a sawed-off barrel or stock. The more distinctive the gun, the easier it is for the prosecution to argue that earlier sightings are not just relevant but confirmatory.

## The doctrinal takeaway

*Cutbank* is not doctrinally revolutionary, but it is revealing. The Eighth Circuit is comfortable treating prior possession of the same weapon as direct proof of a felon-in-possession charge. That is a sensible evidentiary principle in the abstract: possession is often proved circumstantially, and prior familiarity with the weapon can be probative. But the opinion also shows how quickly “intrinsic” evidence can become a catchall for uncharged conduct that bolsters the government’s story.

For now, defendants in the Eighth Circuit will face an uphill battle when they try to exclude testimony that they were seen with the same gun before the charged offense. The decision suggests that, at least in this circuit, the line between propensity evidence and proof of possession is thin indeed. And in practice, that may make Rule 404(b) less of a gatekeeping rule than a label the government can often avoid altogether.



# Context, Confrontation, and Harmless Error: Why *Cutbank* Shows How Hard It Is to Win Evidentiary Appeals

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Cutbank* is not a headline-grabber in the constitutional sense, but it is a useful reminder of how appellate courts actually treat trial-level evidence disputes in criminal cases. The panel rejected a cluster of objections involving hearsay, limiting instructions, impeachment, and Rule 403, and it did so in a way that will sound familiar to anyone who has watched evidentiary appeals run into the same wall: even if a defendant can identify a debatable ruling, reversal is hard when the government’s proof is strong and the challenged evidence is cumulative or contextual.

The opinion also reinforces a practical point about the Confrontation Clause after *Crawford*: not every out-of-court statement triggers serious Sixth Amendment concern, especially when the statement is introduced to explain police conduct and the declarant later takes the stand. Add the court’s insistence on ordinary evidentiary rules—authentication, relevance, prejudice balancing—and *Cutbank* reads less like a doctrinal breakthrough than a tidy example of appellate deference in action.

## The court’s approach to “context” evidence

The most interesting issue in the opinion was the defense effort to keep the jury from hearing D.F.’s statements to police until she testified. Cutbank framed the problem as hearsay and Confrontation Clause risk, but the panel saw the statements as context for the officers’ actions and knowledge, not as substantive proof of guilt. That framing mattered a great deal. Once the court accepted that the statements were being used to explain what the officers did, the constitutional and hearsay objections weakened substantially.

And in the end, the Confrontation Clause argument largely evaporated because D.F. did testify. The panel cited the familiar rule that confrontation concerns are satisfied when the declarant appears in court and is subject to cross-examination. That is not a new proposition, but *Cutbank* shows how often it does the real work in cases involving out-of-court statements. If the witness eventually takes the stand, the Sixth Amendment challenge usually becomes much harder to sustain, absent some separate problem with the way the statement was used.

The broader doctrinal point is that “context” evidence remains a durable category for prosecutors. Courts are often willing to let officers explain why they acted as they did, so long as the government is not obviously smuggling in testimonial accusations under a different label. Defense counsel can still push back when “context” looks like a pretext for accusatory hearsay, but *Cutbank* shows how difficult it is to win that fight on appeal once the trial record is built and the declarant testifies.

## Limiting instructions and trial management discretion

Cutbank also asked for a contemporaneous instruction telling the jury not to treat D.F.’s statements as substantive evidence until she testified. The panel rejected that argument as well, emphasizing that the district court had discretion over the timing of limiting instructions and could give an appropriate instruction later if needed.

That part of the opinion matters because it reflects a recurring appellate theme: trial judges get broad leeway in managing the sequence in which evidence comes in. The Eighth Circuit did not suggest that limiting instructions are unimportant. Instead, it stressed that the district court had not admitted hearsay in the first place and that the usual practice does not require an immediate instruction for every item of potentially sensitive evidence.

For defendants, this is a tough standard to overcome. Appellate courts are generally reluctant to second-guess the on-the-ground pacing of a trial unless there is a clear misuse of discretion and a real risk of prejudice. In *Cutbank*, the panel saw neither. The takeaway is less about any one instruction and more about the uphill climb defendants face when they argue that the timing of an instruction itself skewed the trial.

## Prior possession and the limits of Rule 404(b)

The opinion also approved testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. The district court treated that evidence as intrinsic to the possession count, not as forbidden propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), and the Eighth Circuit agreed. The panel relied on its earlier precedent that prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession because it helps establish ownership or control.

That reasoning is doctrinally familiar, but it has real practical consequences. In firearm-possession prosecutions, prior sighting evidence can be extremely powerful because it shortens the inferential distance between the defendant and the weapon. It is not just “he is the kind of person who has guns”; it is “this is the same gun, and witnesses saw him with it before.” Once the court views the evidence as part of the possession narrative rather than a separate bad-act story, the defense loses much of the leverage Rule 404(b) would otherwise provide.

The panel’s treatment of this issue also hints at a broader trend: courts often collapse “propensity” disputes into relevance disputes when the prior act involves the same weapon or closely connected conduct. That does not eliminate prejudice concerns under Rule 403, but it does make exclusion harder.

## Rule 403 and cumulative prejudice

Cutbank’s Rule 403 challenge to testimony about D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun fared no better. The panel saw that statement as responsive to cross-examination into why D.F. had made prior inconsistent statements and why she was uneasy around firearms. In other words, the defense opened the door, and the witness’s explanation was permitted as context.

That is a familiar appellate move, but it highlights how often evidentiary objections fail when the challenged testimony is tethered to the defense’s own impeachment strategy. Once the witness is impeached as inconsistent or evasive, courts tend to allow explanatory material unless it is truly inflammatory or unfairly misleading. Here, the panel found no obvious prejudice or confusion, especially since a similar reference came in through another witness without objection.

The same logic helps explain why the court ultimately treated any missteps as harmless. The government had substantial testimonial and physical evidence: the victim’s account, corroborating witnesses, DNA on the firearm, the recovery of the gun along the likely flight path, and Cutbank’s conduct with police. Against that backdrop, even if a particular ruling had been questionable, the panel thought it unlikely to have swayed the jury.

## Authentication matters, even on impeachment

Cutbank’s effort to use a police report to impeach C.F. also ran into a basic but important evidentiary requirement: authentication. The panel agreed that the district court could insist on proper authentication before allowing the defense to read portions of the report into evidence. That is unglamorous doctrine, but it is often decisive in practice. Impeachment by prior inconsistent statement is not a free pass to wave around any document that appears useful.

This part of the opinion is a good reminder that appellate courts are usually unsympathetic to arguments that collapse into “the judge blocked my impeachment” when the trial court was simply enforcing foundational requirements. If the document was not authored by the witness, counsel still has to do the work of establishing what it is, where it came from, and why it is admissible for the particular purpose offered.

## The real lesson: harmless error does most of the work

The deepest lesson of *Cutbank* is not about any one evidentiary doctrine. It is about appellate posture. The panel repeatedly noted the strength of the government’s proof and then invoked harmless-error principles to make clear that, even assuming some evidentiary friction, reversal would not be warranted without substantial prejudice.

That is where many evidentiary appeals die. Trial lawyers often hope that a stack of small errors will add up to a new trial. Appellate courts, by contrast, are usually reluctant to aggregate minor rulings into reversible prejudice when the record includes robust direct and physical evidence. *Cutbank* is a good example of that pattern. Once the government has a strong case, evidentiary rulings that might look contestable in isolation are rarely enough to upset a conviction.

So while *Cutbank* does not break new doctrinal ground, it is a useful case study in how evidentiary appeals are actually decided: context evidence is often admitted, declarants who testify blunt Confrontation Clause concerns, trial judges get latitude on limiting instructions and authentication, and harmless error looms over everything. For defendants looking to turn evidentiary objections into reversals, that is a sobering combination.



# When the Firearm Guideline Beats ACCA: Sentencing Logic in *United States v. Cutbank*

## Why this case matters

The sentencing question in *United States v. Cutbank* is a useful reminder that “career criminal” cases do not always turn on the Armed Career Criminal Act’s own guideline. Sometimes, the ordinary firearm guideline, plus its enhancements, produces a higher offense level than the Armed Career Criminal Guideline does on its own. When that happens, the Guidelines instruct the court to use the higher number. That is what happened here, and the Eighth Circuit had little trouble affirming.

The opinion is also a neat illustration of a recurring theme in federal sentencing appeals: once the district court has correctly calculated the advisory range, arguments framed as attacks on the Guidelines often collapse into the more familiar claim that the sentence is too high under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The panel treated Cutbank’s “parsimony principle” objection in exactly that way.

## The guideline math

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and was also found by the jury to qualify for ACCA’s enhanced penalties. The Presentence Investigation Report started with USSG § 2K2.1, the ordinary firearm guideline, and arrived at a total offense level of 35 after several enhancements: the sawed-off firearm base offense level, the altered serial number increase, the reckless-endangerment increase for fleeing police, and—most importantly here—a four-level bump under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm “in connection with another felony offense.”

That level was then compared with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4. The opinion notes that the ACCA guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34. Because the Guidelines direct courts to apply the greater offense level, the PSR and district court used 35. That is the core of the sentencing issue: ACCA status did not cap the range at the armed-career-criminal number. It only supplied one possible benchmark. The firearm guideline, with enhancements, beat it.

That result is not exotic; it is built into the structure of the Guidelines. But it matters because defendants often assume ACCA is the ceiling, when in fact it can be overtaken by a more heavily enhanced offense-level calculation under § 2K2.1.

## The real fight: guideline error or reasonableness challenge?

Cutbank argued on appeal that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) created a sentence that conflicted with § 3553(a)’s command that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The panel effectively answered: that is not a Guidelines-calculation argument at all, at least not once the district court’s factual findings and the PSR are accepted.

Why? Because the enhancement applies if the firearm was used or possessed “in connection with another felony offense,” and the commentary makes clear that this is so “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The district court adopted the PSR’s factual account without objection, and the Eighth Circuit therefore saw no clear error in the application of the enhancement. Once that step was complete, there was no calculation error left to correct.

At that point, Cutbank’s complaint became what it really was: a claim that the resulting within-range sentence was too severe. The panel dealt with that under the usual appellate framework, presuming a within-Guidelines sentence reasonable and asking whether the district court had meaningfully considered the § 3553(a) factors. The court had done so.

## What the panel’s reasoning signals

The most interesting feature of the opinion is its quiet insistence on the distinction between Guidelines law and sentencing policy. A defendant can certainly argue that a guideline mechanically ratchets up punishment in a way that overstates culpability. But that argument belongs in the district court’s § 3553(a) analysis, not in a claim that the court “misapplied” the Guidelines, unless there is a genuine calculation error.

That distinction matters because appellate review is far more deferential once the range is correctly calculated. The Eighth Circuit quoted the usual line: a district court must begin by correctly calculating the range, and only then may it consider whether the statutory factors warrant a variance. If the court does that, a within-range sentence comes to the court of appeals with a presumption of reasonableness. *Cutbank* shows how hard it is to dislodge that presumption when the offense conduct is violent and the record is aggravating.

## The limited force of the parsimony principle

Cutbank’s invocation of the “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” principle was not frivolous, but the opinion shows its limits in practice. The parsimony clause is a command to the sentencing judge, not a free-standing appellate veto. Once the district court has correctly calculated the range and explained that it considered the relevant factors, appellate review is narrow. The court need not give a rote recitation of every factor, and the Eighth Circuit will presume that arguments presented to the sentencing judge were considered and rejected absent evidence to the contrary.

In other words, the parsimony principle does real work at sentencing, but it rarely functions as a stand-alone reason to reverse a sentence that falls within the correctly calculated range—especially where, as here, the defendant’s conduct included domestic terror, assault, kidnapping-like confinement, and violence against officers.

## Bottom line

*Cutbank* is not a major sentencing-law case, but it is a clean example of how ACCA and § 2K2.1 can interact. The ordinary firearm guideline, enhanced for possession in connection with another felony offense, can outpace the Armed Career Criminal Guideline. When that happens, the “sentencing issue” on appeal is often less about guideline mechanics than about whether the district court should have varied downward. Here, the Eighth Circuit had little difficulty saying no.



# A Quiet Appellate-Practice Lesson in *Cutbank*: Undeveloped Arguments, Judicial Frustration, and Issue Inflation

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Cutbank* is, on the surface, a routine affirmance in a felon-in-possession case with ugly facts and a long criminal history. But beneath the black-letter holdings sits a useful appellate-practice lesson: how courts respond when an appellant gives them almost nothing to work with, and when the appellee, sensing weakness, tries to turn a sparse appeal into a broader battlefield than the appellant actually chose.

Judge Loken’s opinion is notable less for doctrinal innovation than for tone. Again and again, the court emphasizes that Cutbank offered “no cases and almost no analysis” to support his claims. And when the government attempted to frame a supposed additional issue that Cutbank had not actually pressed, the court swatted it away with a bit of dry impatience: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” That line says a lot about how appellate judges manage their docket, their briefing, and their patience.

## Thin briefing rarely gets a generous doctrinal response

The opinion’s treatment of Cutbank’s evidentiary arguments is a familiar appellate pattern. The court did not need to write a long discussion about the nuances of Rule 404(b), limiting instructions, or impeachment with extrinsic evidence because Cutbank did not do the work required to earn one. The opinion repeatedly notes the absence of authority and analysis, and that matters. Appellate courts are not research assistants for underdeveloped briefs. When a party fails to engage the governing standard, cite relevant authority, or explain why a ruling mattered in context, the court usually resolves the issue at a high level of generality and moves on.

That is exactly what happened here. The court found the prior firearm observations intrinsic to the possession charge, treated the statements to police as either contextual or cured by the witness’s actual testimony, and viewed the challenged “sister was killed by a gun” testimony as responsive to cross-examination and not unfairly prejudicial. None of that is especially novel, but the opinion’s real point is procedural: once the appellant has not built a serious argument, the court is unlikely to do that work for him.

Practitioners should read this as a reminder that appellate persuasion is cumulative. A weak issue can still be won, but only if the brief gives the panel a reason to see it as more than a complaint. Here, the court’s brisk treatment suggests that Cutbank’s briefing choices left the panel with little incentive to linger.

## Preservation and harmlessness do a lot of quiet work

A second lesson is the extent to which preservation and harmless-error principles narrow the field before merits analysis even begins. Several of Cutbank’s objections were either weakly preserved, addressed in real time by the district court, or undermined by the fact that the challenged witnesses testified in court. The opinion also leans heavily on harmlessness: even if some evidentiary rulings were questionable, the government’s proof was “substantial,” and the verdict was not meaningfully likely to have turned on the challenged pieces of testimony.

This is the kind of appellate posture that often makes unpublished-or-routine opinions feel terse. Once the court decides that any arguable error did not affect substantial rights, there is little reason to excavate the fine points. In practical terms, that means counsel who want meaningful review need to do more than identify a technical issue; they need to preserve it cleanly, explain why it is prejudicial, and situate it within the trial record in a way that gives the court a path to reversal.

## The court’s brief rebuke of the government is just as instructive

If the appellant’s briefing was thin, the appellee’s instincts were a little too expansive. The opinion pauses to note that the government’s brief tried to recast an unraised matter as if it were a distinct appellate claim. The court’s response is unusually pointed for a routine criminal appeal: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.”

That sentence deserves attention because it captures an often-overlooked appellate dynamic. Appellees are sometimes tempted to overbrief. If the appellant raises one weak issue, the instinct is to answer not only that issue but also a constellation of adjacent complaints, maybe in hopes of preempting trouble or signaling confidence. But overdoing that can backfire. It can make a narrow appeal look broader than it is, invite the court to notice questions it otherwise might not have addressed, and irritate a panel that prefers parties to keep the case within its actual bounds.

There is a strategic discipline here: respond fully to what the appellant actually raised, but don’t manufacture additional terrain. Appellate judges notice when briefs are trying to litigate the case the way counsel wishes it had been presented, rather than the way it was.

## Sentencing: little doctrinal movement, but a reminder about guideline layering

The sentencing discussion is more conventional, but it reinforces another practice point. Cutbank argued that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement somehow conflicted with the “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” language of § 3553(a). The court did not indulge that framing. It relied instead on the standard sequence: calculate the Guidelines correctly, assess the PSR’s factual findings, and then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a variance. The district court did that, imposed a within-Guidelines sentence, and received the usual appellate presumption of reasonableness.

What matters here is not only that the court affirmed, but how little room there was for a sentencing challenge once the record was set. The PSR’s facts were adopted without objection, the Guidelines calculation was internally coherent, and the district court said enough to show it had considered the statutory factors. In that setting, appellate arguments about the “core principle” of § 3553(a) tend to sound abstract rather than error-identifying.

The broader doctrinal point is that appellate sentencing review is often record-driven in the most literal sense. If counsel wants to contest the interaction between a specific enhancement and the overall sentence, the argument has to be developed at sentencing, preserved, and tied to concrete facts. Otherwise, the court will default to procedural regularity and defer to the district judge’s discretion.

## The bigger takeaway

*Cutbank* is not a case that changes the law. But it does reveal something important about appellate decisionmaking: briefing quality shapes not only the outcome, but the court’s willingness to engage. Sparse arguments tend to get sparse answers. Weak preservation narrows the field. And appellees who try to turn a thin appeal into a sprawling one may find the panel less interested, not more.

For practitioners, that is the lesson hiding in Judge Loken’s tone. Appellate litigation is not just about having the right issue; it is about presenting it with enough structure, authority, and precision that the court sees a reason to devote serious attention to it. In *Cutbank*, the appellant did not do that, and the appellee tried to go beyond the lines. The court, for its part, simply drew the lines back where they belonged.
