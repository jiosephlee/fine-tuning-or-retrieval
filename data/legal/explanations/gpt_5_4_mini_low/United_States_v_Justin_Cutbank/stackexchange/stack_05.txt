Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How can the sentencing court use 'another felony offense' for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) when the defendant wasn't separately convicted of that felony?
Question:
At sentencing, the court applied the four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm "in connection with another felony offense," apparently based on the underlying conduct involving domestic abuse/kidnapping/assault, even though the opinion doesn't mention a separate conviction for those offenses in this federal case. The commentary says the enhancement applies regardless of whether a charge was brought or a conviction obtained. Is that common across the Guidelines? What standard of proof applies when the sentencing judge finds that separate felony conduct occurred, and what evidence can the court rely on? The opinion also notes that the PSR's factual statements were adopted without objection—does failing to object effectively concede those facts for Guidelines purposes?
Answer:
Yes. Under the Guidelines, a sentencing court may apply § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) even if the defendant was never separately charged with, or convicted of, the “other felony offense.” The opinion says exactly that: the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” USSG § 2K2.1 cmt. n.14(C).

### 1) How can the court use uncharged conduct?
Because the Guidelines routinely allow sentencing courts to consider relevant conduct proved at sentencing, not just conduct that resulted in a conviction. This is not unique to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The application note for this enhancement expressly contemplates uncharged conduct, and the Eighth Circuit relied on that note in Cutbank.

Here, the district court used the underlying domestic-abuse/kidnapping/assault facts described in the PSR to find the firearm was possessed “in connection with another felony offense.” The Eighth Circuit held that was proper because the PSR’s factual statements were adopted without objection.

### 2) What standard of proof applies?
The opinion does not explicitly recite the burden, but in federal sentencing the usual standard for guideline facts is **preponderance of the evidence**. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s factual finding only for **clear error**. See the court’s citation to *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929–30 (8th Cir. 2013).

So the sequence is:

- **At sentencing:** the judge finds the enhancement facts by a preponderance of the evidence.
- **On appeal:** the finding is reviewed for clear error.

### 3) What evidence can the court rely on?
The court can rely on the **PSR**, so long as its factual assertions are supported and not objected to. That is exactly what happened here: “the district court adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements.” The Eighth Circuit then treated those facts as establishing the necessary felony conduct.

That is a standard sentencing practice: courts may rely on the PSR, trial evidence, and other reliable information at sentencing. In this opinion, the key point is that the PSR’s account was enough because Cutbank did not dispute it.

### 4) Does failing to object concede the facts?
For Guidelines purposes, **yes, effectively**. If the defendant does not object to the PSR’s factual assertions, the sentencing court may accept them as true. Cutbank’s failure to object meant the district court could rely on those facts to apply the enhancement, and the Eighth Circuit said there was no error, “much less clearly err[or].”

So in practical terms:

- **No objection to PSR factual statements = those facts are treated as admitted/accepted for sentencing.**
- A defendant who wants to contest a guideline enhancement must object specifically to the PSR facts supporting it.

### Bottom line
The enhancement did not require a separate conviction. The Guidelines expressly allow reliance on uncharged felony conduct, the sentencing court could find that conduct by a preponderance of the evidence, and the unobjected-to PSR supplied the factual basis.
