Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why isn't testimony that the defendant previously had the same gun barred by Rule 404(b) as propensity evidence?
Question:
I'm confused about the court's treatment of D.F.'s and C.F.'s testimony that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. My instinct was that evidence of prior acts is usually restricted by Rule 404(b), especially if it risks inviting the jury to think "he had a gun before, so he must have had it this time too." But the Eighth Circuit calls this "intrinsic evidence" and says it shows continuous possession and control of the same weapon. What exactly makes prior possession of the same firearm "intrinsic" rather than Rule 404(b) evidence? Is this basically an exception whenever the government can tie the prior act to the identical gun, or is there a narrower doctrinal rule here?
Answer:
Because the testimony was not being used to show **“he’s the kind of person who carries guns”**; it was being used to show **he possessed this same gun**.

That is the key distinction.

## 1) What Rule 404(b) covers
Rule 404(b) bars evidence of **other acts** when the point of the evidence is to prove propensity — i.e., “because he did it before, he likely did it again.”

So if the government were offering evidence that Cutbank once owned some unrelated firearm just to suggest he is a gun person, that would raise a classic 404(b) problem.

## 2) Why this testimony was treated as intrinsic
The Eighth Circuit accepted the district court’s view that D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony about seeing Cutbank with the **same sawed-off Marlin .22** before the charged incident was **intrinsic** to the possession charge.

The opinion relies on the doctrine that prior possession of the **same firearm** is directly probative of the charged possession because it tends to show **ownership, control, and continuing possession** of that weapon. The court quotes *United States v. Battle* for the proposition that:

> “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

So the relevance is not “he did a similar bad thing before.” It is “this is the same weapon, and the earlier sightings help prove he possessed the weapon on the charged date.”

## 3) Why that makes it “intrinsic” rather than 404(b)
“Intrinsic” evidence is evidence that is part of the charged offense itself, or so intertwined with it that it helps prove the offense without requiring a separate 404(b) analysis.

Here, the prior sightings of the same rifle were treated as part of the proof of the possession count because they helped establish a **continuous chain of possession** of the identical firearm. The opinion expressly says the district court viewed the testimony as:

- “intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge,” and
- evidence of “continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.”

That means the testimony was not being offered as a separate “other act” at all; it was offered as direct proof of the charged possession.

## 4) Is this a blanket exception for the same gun?
Not quite, but it is a **narrow and recurring rule** in firearm-possession cases.

The important limiting principle is that the evidence must be tied to the **same firearm** and must genuinely help prove the charged possession — usually by showing ownership, control, identification, or continuity of possession. That is what makes it probative of the offense rather than merely prejudicial character evidence.

So the rule is narrower than “any prior gun evidence is intrinsic if the government says it’s the same gun.” The government still has to show that the earlier possession meaningfully relates to the charged possession. But when the testimony is about the **same weapon**, and especially when it helps establish the defendant’s continuous possession or control, the Eighth Circuit treats it as outside Rule 404(b).

## 5) Bottom line
The testimony was not barred by Rule 404(b) because it was not offered to prove Cutbank’s propensity to possess guns. It was offered to prove he possessed **this specific gun**, and the court treated that as **intrinsic evidence** of the charged § 922(g) offense.

So the doctrinal move is:

- **other gun, or prior gun use offered for character** → likely 404(b);
- **same gun, prior possession offered to prove ownership/control/continuous possession** → often intrinsic, and admissible subject to ordinary relevance and Rule 403 limits.
