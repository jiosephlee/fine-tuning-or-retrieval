Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What does the harmless-error analysis mean when the court says the evidentiary rulings 'did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict'?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly brushes aside the evidentiary issues and ends by saying any error would have been harmless because the government's evidence was substantial. I understand harmless error in the abstract, but I'm confused about how searching the review actually is in a criminal case. Does the appellate court ask whether the verdict surely would have been the same, whether there was overwhelming evidence, or whether the challenged evidence likely affected the jury? And when the defendant argues cumulative error, how do courts decide whether a series of individually minor rulings adds up to substantial prejudice?
Answer:
The court is applying the ordinary **harmless-error** standard for nonconstitutional evidentiary errors: an evidentiary mistake does **not** require reversal unless it **substantially influenced the verdict**.

In the opinion, the Eighth Circuit quotes its usual formulation:

> “we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.”

And then, in the familiar shorthand from *DeMarce*, it says the challenged rulings are harmless if they:

> “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.”

So the court is **not** asking whether the evidence was merely relevant or whether the ruling was technically wrong. It is asking a practical appellate question: **was the mistake important enough that it probably affected the jury’s decision?**

### How searching is the review?

In a criminal case, for ordinary evidentiary error, the Eighth Circuit does **not** require the government to prove beyond all doubt that the verdict would have been the same. But the court does look at the **whole record** and asks whether the properly admitted proof was so strong that the error could only have had a minor effect.

That is exactly what the panel does here. After discussing the challenged rulings, it says:

> “The government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial. Thus, the district court’s evidentiary rulings ‘did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.’”

So the court’s reasoning is:

1. even assuming error,
2. the remaining evidence of guilt was substantial,
3. therefore the disputed rulings were not likely to have mattered in a way that would justify reversal.

That is the same as saying the error was **harmless in light of the record as a whole**.

### “Overwhelming evidence” vs. “likely affected the jury”

Those ideas are closely related, but they are not quite the test.

- **“Overwhelming evidence”** is a way of describing why an error was harmless.
- The actual question is whether the error **may have substantially swayed the jury** or had more than a slight influence.
- If the properly admitted evidence is strong enough, the court will usually conclude the error was harmless because the jury likely would have reached the same result anyway.

That is what the opinion means when it emphasizes the government’s “substantial” testimonial and physical evidence.

### Cumulative error

The opinion also states the Eighth Circuit rule on cumulative error:

> “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.”

This means the defendant cannot win by simply stacking several weak complaints together. The court asks whether the combined effect of the errors, taken together, created **substantial prejudice** — in other words, whether the overall fairness of the trial was materially undermined.

So:

- if the alleged errors are minor or not errors at all, they usually do **not** add up to reversible prejudice;
- if there are multiple actual errors, the court looks at their **combined impact** on the verdict.

In *Cutbank*, the court found no such cumulative prejudice because the evidentiary rulings were either proper or, at most, had only a slight effect in the face of strong evidence.

### Bottom line

In this opinion, “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict” means the court concluded that any evidentiary error was **harmless** because the jury was not likely to have been substantially swayed by it. And on cumulative error, the defendant must show more than a collection of minor issues — he must show **substantial prejudice** from the combined effect of actual errors.
