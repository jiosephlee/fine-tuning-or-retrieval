Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How did the Confrontation Clause issue disappear just because the declarant later testified at trial?
Question:
Before trial, the defense wanted an instruction telling the jury not to treat D.F.'s out-of-court statements to police as substantive evidence until D.F. herself testified. The court denied that request, and the opinion says there was no Confrontation Clause problem because D.F. later testified in person. I'm struggling with the timing issue. If officers repeat an accuser's statements before the accuser testifies, doesn't the jury still hear testimonial hearsay at that point? Does the later appearance of the declarant automatically cure any Sixth Amendment issue, or only if the defendant has a full opportunity to cross-examine her? Also, how do courts distinguish between testimony offered merely to explain police conduct and testimony that effectively conveys the truth of the accusation?
Answer:
The short answer is: **the Confrontation Clause problem disappears only if the declarant actually testifies and is subject to cross-examination on the relevant statement**. That is what the Eighth Circuit is relying on in *Cutbank* when it says:

> “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person.”  
> *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010).

So the timing point is not that the out-of-court statement was magically constitutional at the moment the officer repeated it. Rather, under the doctrine the court is applying, **the Sixth Amendment concern is cured once the declarant later comes to court and can be cross-examined**. The Constitution guarantees the defendant an opportunity to confront the witness, not a particular order of proof.

That said, there is an important caveat: **the witness must be available for meaningful cross-examination about the statement**. If the declarant testifies but refuses to answer, claims privilege, or otherwise cannot be questioned about the substance of the accusation, the confrontation problem may remain. *Cutbank* does not address such a failure; the opinion says simply that **D.F. testified in person**, which was enough for the court.

On your hearsay-timing concern: yes, the jury may hear the statement before the declarant takes the stand. But that does not automatically create a Confrontation Clause violation if:

1. the statement is **not admitted for its truth** at that stage, or
2. the declarant later testifies and is available for cross-examination.

That is why the court here also emphasized that it “**did not admit hearsay during the trial**” and that D.F.’s statements were used to provide **context for police conduct and knowledge**, not as substantive proof of Cutbank’s guilt.

### “Explain police conduct” vs. “backdoor accusation”
Courts draw this line by asking whether the testimony is genuinely offered for a non-hearsay purpose—such as explaining why officers acted as they did—or whether, in substance, it is just a way of conveying the accusation to the jury for its truth.

- **Permissible**: “We went to that location because we received a report of a disturbance.”
- **Problematic**: “We went there because D.F. said Cutbank pointed a gun at her.”

The second example is functionally accusatory; it risks being used substantively even if labeled “context.” The Eighth Circuit in *Cutbank* avoided that issue by saying the record showed the court **did not admit hearsay** and by pointing to the fact that **D.F. later testified**.

So the doctrinal answer is:

- **Later testimony can cure the Confrontation Clause issue**, but only if the declarant actually testifies and is subject to cross-examination.
- **If the statement was admitted only for context**, there may be no hearsay or confrontation problem in the first place.
- **If the statement was effectively offered for its truth before the declarant testified**, the later appearance usually cures the Sixth Amendment concern so long as the defense gets a real cross-examination opportunity.

In *Cutbank*, the court treated both points as satisfied.
