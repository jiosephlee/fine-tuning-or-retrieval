Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why did the court treat Cutbank's § 3553(a) argument as a substantive-reasonableness issue instead of a Guidelines-calculation error?
Question:
Cutbank argued that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produced a sentence greater than necessary under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), especially because the ACCA guideline would have yielded a lower total offense level than the otherwise-applicable calculations. The Eighth Circuit responded that the district court first had to correctly calculate the Guidelines, then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors justified a variance, and it presumed the within-Guidelines sentence was reasonable. I'm having trouble seeing the boundary here. If the defendant's real complaint is that the enhancement overstates the seriousness of the conduct in light of the ACCA framework, is that purely a substantive reasonableness argument? Or could there be a doctrinal argument that the enhancement should not apply at all because it double counts conduct already captured elsewhere in the sentence?
Answer:
Because, on the record in *Cutbank*, the complaint was not that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was legally unavailable, but that its application made the advisory range too high as a matter of sentencing policy under § 3553(a).

That distinction matters.

## 1. Guidelines calculation vs. § 3553(a) variance

The court followed the ordinary sentencing sequence:

1. **Correctly calculate the Guidelines range**  
   The district court adopted the PSR’s facts and applied the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The Eighth Circuit said that was not clearly erroneous because the facts supported that the firearm was used “in connection with another felony offense.”

2. **Then consider whether § 3553(a) calls for a variance**  
   Cutbank’s argument that the sentence was “greater than necessary” because the ACCA framework would have produced a lower offense level was treated as a request for a lower sentence **after** the Guidelines were correctly calculated. That is a classic **substantive-reasonableness** argument, not a Guidelines-calculation error.

The opinion says exactly that: after citing *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*, the court explains that the district court must first calculate the range, and only then consider whether § 3553(a) factors justify departure or variance.

## 2. Why the ACCA comparison did not make the enhancement erroneous

Cutbank’s real point was: “If the ACCA guideline would have yielded offense level 34, why should I get 35 with the enhancement?”

But the opinion answers that directly: the Guidelines themselves resolve the comparison. **USSG § 4B1.4(b) says the greater offense level applies.** So the existence of a lower ACCA offense level does not invalidate the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. It just means that once all applicable provisions are calculated, the higher one governs.

So the argument is not:

- “the enhancement cannot be applied under the Guidelines”; it is
- “even if the enhancement applies, the resulting sentence is too severe.”

That is a § 3553(a) argument.

## 3. What about double counting?

A double-counting argument could be doctrinally viable **if** the defendant could show that the Guidelines prohibit using the same conduct twice, or that the same facts are being counted in a way the Guidelines do not allow.

But that is not how the Eighth Circuit framed Cutbank’s claim here. The opinion treats the enhancement as independently supported by the facts and authorized by the commentary to § 2K2.1. It does **not** identify any guideline provision saying the conduct underlying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) cannot also coexist with the ACCA calculation.

In other words, nothing in the opinion suggests impermissible double counting. The issue was not whether the enhancement was barred; it was whether the resulting within-Guidelines sentence was too high in light of § 3553(a).

## 4. Why the court presumed reasonableness

Once the district court correctly calculated the range, the Eighth Circuit applied the normal presumption that a **within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable**. Because the district court noted its review of the PSR and the § 3553(a) factors, the appellate court treated Cutbank’s point as an argument the district court considered and rejected—not as a calculation mistake.

## Bottom line

Cutbank’s ACCA comparison did not show that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was wrongly applied. It showed only that he thought the resulting range was too harsh. That makes it a **substantive reasonableness** argument, not a **procedural Guidelines error**.
