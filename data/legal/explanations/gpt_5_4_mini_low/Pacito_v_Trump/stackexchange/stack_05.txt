Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 actually require refugee services, or does it merely authorize them?
Question:
This seems to be the core disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee, and I'm struggling with the statutory interpretation. The majority relies on phrases like the Director "shall" provide certain things "to the extent of available appropriations," plus other subsections requiring action for unaccompanied minors and support projects. Judge Lee responds that the section is titled as an "Authorization," that subsections (b)-(e) repeatedly say the Director is "authorized" to provide assistance, and that subsection (a) only supplies conditions if the government chooses to fund programs. In statutes like this, how do courts decide whether "shall" creates a mandatory duty when surrounding text sounds discretionary?
Answer:
Short answer: **the panel’s majority reads § 1522 as creating a real statutory duty, not just a permission**, but only **“to the extent of available appropriations.”** Judge Lee reads the same structure as authorization-only. The difference is not that “shall” magically overrides everything else; it’s that courts interpret **“shall” in context**, harmonizing it with the rest of the section.

The opinion gives you the key moves:

1. **Section 1522(a)(1)(A) is framed as a mandatory command**
   The majority relies on the language that the Director **“shall”** do certain things **“to the extent of available appropriations.”** In ordinary statutory reading, “shall” is presumptively mandatory. The qualifier does not turn it into a mere option; it means the duty exists **so far as appropriations permit**.

2. **Subsections (b)–(e) use “authorized,” but that does not necessarily make the whole section discretionary**
   Judge Lee emphasizes that the section is titled **“Authorization for programs …”** and that various subsections say the Director is **“authorized”** to provide assistance. The majority’s answer is structural: those provisions describe the **means and programmatic mechanisms** by which refugee services are delivered, while subsection (a) states the **conditions and considerations** that govern how those services must be provided.

3. **Courts do not read the title as controlling**
   A statutory heading can help confirm meaning, but it cannot override operative text. So “Authorization” in the title is relevant, but it cannot defeat a subsection that says the Director **shall** act.

4. **The court harmonizes the provisions rather than treating them as inconsistent**
   The majority appears to read the section as: Congress **authorized programs** in subsections (b)–(e), but once those programs exist and funds are available, subsection (a) imposes **mandatory requirements** for how refugees are to be served. That reading preserves both the “authorized” language and the “shall” language.

5. **Later-specific obligations reinforce the majority’s reading**
   The order points to § 1522(d)(2)(B) and § 1522(e)(7)(A), which use mandatory language like **“shall assume legal responsibility”** and **“shall develop and implement”**. The majority treats those as evidence that Congress was not speaking purely in discretionary terms throughout the section.

So, in doctrinal terms, the question is a classic **text-in-context** problem: when a statute contains both permissive and mandatory language, courts ask whether the mandatory terms impose enforceable duties or merely condition a discretionary program. Here, the majority reads the operative “shall” provisions as imposing duties subject to appropriations; Judge Lee reads them as conditions attached to optional funding programs.

If you want the cleanest takeaway from the opinion: **§ 1522 is not read as a pure “may fund refugee services if it wants” statute by the majority; it is read as a statute that authorizes programs but also requires certain services and protections once the program is operating and funds are available.**
