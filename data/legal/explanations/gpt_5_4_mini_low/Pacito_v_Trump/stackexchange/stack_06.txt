Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Why did the panel order reinstatement of cooperative agreements instead of just requiring the government to provide services by some other means?
Question:
The opinion says § 1522 services may be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements, and then directs the government to reinstate whatever cooperative agreements are necessary to provide the required services. But if the statute only requires services to be provided, why can the court effectively tell the executive what contracting mechanism to use? Is this because the government admitted it had no replacement provider ready, so reinstatement was the only practical way to preserve compliance?
Answer:
Because the court was not ordering the Executive to use **cooperative agreements as a matter of policy**; it was ordering the government to take the **specific step necessary to avoid violating § 1522** on the record then before it.

A few points from the order make that clear:

1. **The statutory duty identified by the panel is to provide services.**  
   The panel says that under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government “must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States.”

2. **The statute allows several mechanisms, but the government had terminated the existing ones.**  
   The order notes that these services “may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements,” and that the government had terminated all of the relevant cooperative agreements.

3. **The remedy is driven by the government’s uncertainty about replacement providers.**  
   The key sentence is: “In light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services…”

So the answer is yes, **practical necessity matters here**. The panel did not say “the executive must always use cooperative agreements.” It said, in effect: **because the government has cut off the existing vehicle for providing the services, and has not shown that another vehicle is ready, reinstating the agreements is the way to preserve compliance with the statute.**

That is a classic equitable move in a preliminary-injunction setting: the court tailors relief to the record before it and to the need to maintain the statutory status quo. The order even limits the remedy to “such cooperative agreements necessary” and only for “the services described in § 1522,” which shows it is not a broad intrusion into procurement discretion.

So your instinct is basically right: the panel’s choice of remedy turns on the government’s inability, at least on the record then presented, to show that it had a substitute provider lined up. The court was not picking a preferred contracting form in the abstract; it was ordering the reinstatement of the existing agreements because, on this record, that was the mechanism most clearly capable of keeping the government in compliance with § 1522.
