Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Why is the funding issue framed as an APA claim instead of just a direct statutory violation under 8 U.S.C. § 1522?
Question:
The opinion says the plaintiffs challenged the defunding of USRAP under the Administrative Procedure Act, and then the panel analyzes whether § 1522 requires the government to provide certain services. I'm not understanding why the APA is the vehicle here. If the statute itself allegedly mandates reception and placement services, why isn't the claim simply that the government violated the Refugee Act? Is the idea that terminating the cooperative agreements is final agency action that is unlawful because it is contrary to law under the APA?
Answer:
Because § 1522 is the **substantive law the plaintiffs rely on**, but the **APA is the procedural cause of action and waiver of sovereign immunity** that lets a court review the agency’s conduct.

A few doctrinal points explain the framing:

1. **The APA is not just about procedure; it is the standard vehicle for challenging agency action.**  
   Under 5 U.S.C. §§ 702 and 704, a plaintiff may sue the federal government for relief from “final agency action” that is “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” A claim that agency action violates another statute—here, the Refugee Act / 8 U.S.C. § 1522—is typically brought as an APA claim alleging the agency acted “not in accordance with law.”

2. **The plaintiffs are not merely saying “the statute says X.” They are challenging agency implementation.**  
   In *Pacito*, the challenged conduct was the State Department’s **defunding and termination of cooperative agreements** that were being used to carry out refugee reception and placement services. That is classic agency action. The opinion specifically notes that “the defunding of USRAP” was challenged “under the Administrative Procedure Act,” and then the panel analyzes whether § 1522 requires the government to provide certain services.

3. **Section 1522 supplies the legal rule; the APA supplies the remedy.**  
   The panel’s discussion of § 1522 is doing the merits work: it asks whether the statute imposes a duty to provide reception and placement services, and concludes the government is likely not to prevail on at least one APA challenge because the statute requires certain services “to the extent of available appropriations” and uses mandatory language in relevant provisions. That is exactly how APA statutory-violation claims work: the court asks whether the agency’s action is contrary to the governing statute.

So yes—your instinct is right: the idea is that the **termination of the cooperative agreements is the final agency action**, and the claim is that this action is unlawful **because it is contrary to § 1522 under the APA**.

Why not just sue for a “direct” Refugee Act violation? Usually because, absent a separate cause of action, plaintiffs need the APA’s waiver of sovereign immunity and review provisions to obtain judicial relief against federal agencies. The Refugee Act provides the alleged duty; the APA provides the path to enforce it in court.

That is also why the panel’s remedy is framed the way it is: it doesn’t announce a free-standing refugee-law injunction in the abstract; it orders reinstatement of agreements because, on the present record, the government is not likely to prevail against the APA challenge to its failure to provide services required by § 1522.
