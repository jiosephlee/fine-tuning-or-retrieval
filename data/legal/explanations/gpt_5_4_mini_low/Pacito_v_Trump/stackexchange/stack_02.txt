Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How does 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) let the President suspend refugee admissions if Congress already created a refugee admissions scheme?
Question:
The court seems to say Trump v. Hawaii basically controls the challenge to the executive order suspending refugee admissions, because § 1182(f) gives the President broad power to suspend the entry of a class of aliens. But the plaintiffs argued this conflicts with the Refugee Act of 1980, which appears to establish an ongoing statutory refugee system. I'm having trouble understanding the interaction between those two laws. Why doesn't the more specific refugee statute limit the President's general § 1182(f) power, at least as to refugees?
Answer:
The short answer is: **because the court read the Refugee Act as regulating the refugee program, not as withdrawing the President’s separate § 1182(f) authority to suspend entry.** On this panel’s view, there is no direct statutory conflict, so the general-specific canon never really gets off the ground.

Here is how the court framed it.

### 1. Section 1182(f) is treated as a freestanding, very broad entry-suspension power
The panel relied on **Trump v. Hawaii**, which said § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and gives the President “ample power to impose entry restrictions” beyond those elsewhere in the INA. The opinion quotes Hawaii for the proposition that the only textual prerequisite is that the President find entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Once that finding is made, the statute authorizes him to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens.”

So, under the panel’s reading, § 1182(f) is not a vague gap-filler. It is an express delegation of suspension power.

### 2. The Refugee Act does not say the President cannot do that
The plaintiffs’ argument was essentially: “Congress created a refugee admissions scheme, so that scheme should control over the President’s more general § 1182(f) authority.”

But the opinion does not read the Refugee Act as an express limitation on § 1182(f). Instead, it treats the Refugee Act as establishing the refugee program and, separately, § 1522 as governing **post-admission** reception and resettlement services. The discussion of the Refugee Act in the order is about services for refugees already admitted, not a provision saying refugees must be admitted regardless of a presidential suspension under § 1182(f).

### 3. Specific-over-general only helps if the statutes actually conflict
A specific statute can limit a general one, but only if both apply to the same subject in a way that cannot be reconciled. The panel did not find that here.

Its reasoning was essentially:

- § 1182(f) specifically authorizes the President to suspend entry of a class of aliens when he makes the statutory finding;
- the Refugee Act and § 1522 impose obligations regarding the refugee program, especially once refugees are in the United States;
- those statutes can coexist.

So the court saw **no irreconcilable conflict**. In the panel’s view, the Refugee Act does not say, “notwithstanding § 1182(f), refugees must continue to be admitted.”

### 4. The court’s practical point: refugee services are different from refugee admissions
The order actually draws a line between the two:

- It stayed the injunction against the **suspension of refugee admissions** because the government was likely to succeed under § 1182(f) and Hawaii.
- It declined to adopt the same reasoning for **domestic reception and placement services**, because § 1522 separately requires certain services for refugees already admitted.

That distinction matters. The panel is not saying Congress had no refugee policy. It is saying Congress’s refugee policy, as the court read it, regulates **what happens to refugees after admission** and how the program operates, but it does not displace the President’s statutory power to **suspend entry** under § 1182(f).

### Bottom line
The court’s answer is: **§ 1182(f) still applies because the Refugee Act does not expressly repeal or limit it, and the two statutes operate in different spheres.** Under *Hawaii*, the President’s entry-suspension authority is broad enough to cover refugees unless Congress has clearly withdrawn that power. On this panel’s reading, Congress did not do so here.
