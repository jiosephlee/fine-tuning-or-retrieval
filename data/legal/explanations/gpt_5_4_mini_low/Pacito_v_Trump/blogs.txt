Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Pacito v. Trump and the Two Faces of Refugee Law: Broad Presidential Entry Power, Narrower Executive Control Over Resettlement Services

## Why this order matters

The Ninth Circuit’s latest order in *Pacito v. Trump* is important not just because it keeps the litigation alive, but because it cleanly separates two questions that are often lumped together in refugee policy: who may enter the United States, and what the government must do once refugees are already here. On the first question, the panel treats the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as exceptionally broad, echoing *Trump v. Hawaii*. On the second, it suggests that the executive’s discretion is substantially narrower, because Congress has separately enacted a domestic resettlement scheme in 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

That separation is the order’s central doctrinal move. It may end up being the most consequential part of the case, because it frames refugee law as a two-stage system governed by different legal rules: near-plenary executive power at the border, but statutory obligations after admission. If that distinction holds, it could constrain future efforts to treat a suspension of refugee admissions as a de facto suspension of the domestic refugee-resettlement program.

## The panel’s split approach

The order is, in form, a stay ruling. But it does more than simply preserve the status quo. The panel says the government is likely to succeed in challenging the injunction as it applies to the suspension of refugee admissions, because § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and authorizes the President to suspend the entry of a class of aliens upon the requisite finding of detriment. Quoting *Hawaii*, the panel emphasizes that the judiciary cannot engage in a “searching inquiry” into the President’s justifications, and that the statutory text contains no relevant limit the executive here exceeded.

That part of the order is unsurprising after *Hawaii*. What is more notable is that the panel refuses to carry that same deference over to the post-admission side of the case. The government’s challenge to the district court’s injunctions regarding refugee resettlement services is not, in the panel’s view, likely to succeed across the board. The order points to § 1522’s detailed provisions on reception and placement services, employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and support for unaccompanied minors. Congress also appropriated billions for migration and refugee assistance. In the panel’s view, those provisions create at least some mandatory obligations for refugees already admitted to the United States.

The practical result is a split order: stay the district court’s injunction against the entry suspension, but preserve a carveout requiring the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements needed to provide ordinary reception and placement services to admitted refugees.

## Why § 1182(f) and § 1522 are doing different work

The order’s statutory architecture matters. Section 1182(f) is an immigration-filtering provision. It allows the President to suspend entry whenever he finds entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The panel reads that grant as capacious enough to cover a refugee admissions moratorium. Once the President makes the requisite finding, the court says, there is no room for a more searching merits inquiry.

Section 1522, by contrast, is a domestic-resettlement statute. It does not answer who gets admitted; it addresses what happens after refugees are admitted. That distinction is crucial. The order suggests that even if the executive may shut off the pipeline at the border, it cannot simply read the domestic resettlement system out of the statute for refugees who have already arrived.

The panel’s reasoning here is textually grounded. It does not say § 1522 creates an unlimited entitlement to services. The opinion is careful to anchor the government’s obligations in the statutory language, especially the provisions directing the responsible officials to make available certain resources “to the extent of available appropriations,” to provide support for unaccompanied children, and to implement alternative projects for refugees in the country less than 36 months. The reference to appropriations is important: the court is not purporting to require uncabined spending. It is holding, instead, that the government cannot disclaim the statutory program altogether while Congress has still funded it and the statute still speaks in mandatory terms.

## Judge Lee’s dissent and the harder statutory question

Judge Lee agrees that the President can suspend refugee admissions, but dissents from the limited carveout for resettlement services. His view is that § 1522 is best read as authorization, not mandate. He points to the title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and to the repeated use of “is authorized” in the operative subsections. On that reading, the government may create and fund resettlement programs, but is not required to operate them.

That argument is not frivolous. In many spending statutes, “authorized” does mean discretionary. And Lee is on firmer ground than the panel may have fully acknowledged in emphasizing the statute’s structure: § 1522 contains a mix of mandatory-sounding language and permissive language, which makes it a less-than-clean fit for a simple command-to-fund theory.

But the majority’s response is also strong. Titles can inform statutory meaning, but they do not override operative text. More importantly, Congress sometimes writes programs in a way that gives the executive discretion over implementation while still imposing mandatory duties once the program exists. The panel seems to think § 1522 fits that pattern. That is especially plausible where the statute uses “shall” in connection with particular responsibilities and where Congress has appropriated funds for refugee assistance. The order does not resolve every ambiguity; it does, however, signal that the government cannot avoid all domestic obligations simply by invoking the same refugee policy rationale that supports the entry suspension.

## The doctrinal stakes

This order could matter for at least three reasons.

First, it sharpens the distinction between immigration exclusion and domestic administration. That distinction is not new, but the order gives it practical force. Courts may be highly deferential when reviewing entry restrictions under § 1182(f), yet still willing to police whether the executive has complied with separate statutory duties toward people already admitted.

Second, the decision suggests that refugee litigation may turn less on constitutional theory than on statutory framing. If a challenge is packaged as an attack on the President’s exclusion authority, *Hawaii* looms large. If it is framed as a challenge to the executive’s refusal to administer a congressionally funded domestic program, the analysis changes. The statutory text of § 1522 becomes the battleground.

Third, the order may limit the government’s ability to use admissions policy to achieve a broader shutdown of refugee resettlement. The panel appears to view the domestic resettlement system as legally distinct from the admissions program, not merely derivative of it. That could matter in future disputes over funding, agency contracts, and the use of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations.

## Bottom line

*Pacito* is less a final answer than a signpost. The Ninth Circuit is leaning hard on *Trump v. Hawaii* to uphold a refugee-entry suspension under § 1182(f), but it is not treating that deference as a blank check to dismantle the separate statutory framework Congress created for refugees who have already been admitted. If that distinction survives the merits decision, the case could become an important reminder that refugee law has two faces: one governing entry, and another governing settlement. The President may have wide berth at the border, but the executive branch may find far less room to maneuver once Congress has already brought refugees inside the statutory gate.



## Authorization or Obligation? The Statutory Fight Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522 in Judge Lee’s Partial Dissent

## Why this order matters

The most immediate significance of *Pacito v. Trump* is not the familiar one—the scope of presidential power to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—but the quieter, more institutional question lurking in the background: when Congress funds a refugee-resettlement scheme, does the executive branch merely get permission to operate it, or does it incur a legal duty to do so?

That question matters because it draws the line between political discretion and judicially enforceable statutory obligation. In the panel’s interim order, the court treated the admissions suspension as likely lawful under *Trump v. Hawaii*, but it also concluded that the government was not likely to prevail against at least one APA challenge because § 1522 appears to require continued provision of reception and placement services for refugees already admitted. Judge Lee’s partial dissent takes direct aim at that premise. In his view, the statute is “most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate.”

## The majority’s reading of § 1522

The majority’s analysis, though brief in this order, is built around the structure of § 1522 and the statute’s repeated use of mandatory language. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) says that, “to the extent of available appropriations,” the Director “shall” make resources available for refugee employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and equal access for women. The order also points to subsection (d)(2)(B), which says that during an interim period for unaccompanied minor refugees, the Director “shall assume legal responsibility” if necessary, and subsection (e)(7)(A), which says the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects” for certain recently arrived refugees.

That “shall” language matters. The majority appears to read § 1522 not as a menu of optional programs but as a set of congressionally imposed obligations, subject only to the availability of appropriations. The point is reinforced by Congress’s FY 2025 funding for migration and refugee assistance, which the order treats as evidence that the program is supposed to continue operating rather than be switched off by executive fiat. On that view, the government may have discretion over how to administer the programs, but not whether to administer them at all.

## Judge Lee’s textual counterargument

Judge Lee’s dissent is classic statutory-structure textualism. He begins with the title: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” To him, that title is not decorative; it signals the provision’s basic character. “Authorization,” he notes, is permissive, not mandatory.

He then points to the operative subsections. Sections 1522(b) through (e) repeatedly say that the Director “is authorized” to provide various forms of assistance. In ordinary statutory drafting, that is the language of permission. And Lee reads § 1522(a)’s use of “shall” differently than the majority does. Because § 1522(a) is framed as “conditions and considerations” “[i]n providing assistance under this section,” he sees it as regulating how discretionary programs must be run if the executive branch chooses to fund them, not as independently compelling the government to keep those programs open.

That is a plausible structural argument, and one that will resonate with readers who think courts too often convert appropriations-based statutory frameworks into hard mandates. Lee’s critique is especially forceful on subsection (d)(2)(B): even there, he says, the duty arises only “if necessary,” and the provision is saturated with administrative discretion. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A)’s “as needed” qualifier leaves the executive room to decide the contours of the program.

## The deeper interpretive divide

What makes this disagreement interesting is that both opinions are doing modern textualism, but they are weighting different signals. The majority treats “shall” and Congress’s appropriations as enough to create an enforceable obligation. Judge Lee treats the title, the “authorized” language, and the statute’s discretion-laden qualifiers as decisive.

This is a familiar tension in spending-statute interpretation. Congress often writes in a hybrid way: authorizing programs, conditioning them on appropriations, and then using mandatory-sounding verbs to direct implementation. The hard question is whether those verbs create a judicially enforceable duty or simply describe the terms on which an authorized program should operate if the political branches choose to run it.

The majority’s approach is more functional. If Congress appropriated billions for refugee assistance and wrote mandatory language about how refugees are to be served, the executive cannot lawfully wind the program down to zero while leaving the statutory scheme formally intact. Judge Lee’s approach is more formal. If Congress meant to require operation, it knew how to say so more directly; the statute’s title and repeated “authorized” phrasing matter, and courts should not infer a duty from a funding statute that looks optional on its face.

## Doctrinal implications

The stakes extend beyond refugees. Many federal social-service programs are written in this in-between register: authorized, appropriated, conditioned, and administered by executive agencies through contracts or cooperative agreements. If the majority’s reading prevails, plaintiffs may have a stronger basis for arguing that once Congress funds a program and uses mandatory language, agencies cannot simply suspend operations by policy choice. That would give courts a more active role in policing executive nonfeasance.

If Judge Lee’s view gains traction, by contrast, agencies may enjoy broader latitude to slow-walk, pause, or effectively disable programs so long as the underlying statute can be characterized as permissive. That would be a significant victory for executive control over implementation, especially in areas where Congress has blended authorization and direction in the same statutory section.

The order also hints at a broader administrative-law point: the government’s uncertainty about whether it could even stand up a replacement system mattered. The majority was not simply reading a statute in the abstract; it was reacting to a practical vacuum. That is often where courts become less willing to accept the government’s “authorization not obligation” framing.

## Bottom line

Judge Lee’s dissent underscores a recurring problem in modern statutory interpretation: Congress frequently writes programs in language that is simultaneously enabling and directive. In *Pacito*, the panel split over how to read that mix. The majority leaned toward enforceable obligation, especially for refugees already admitted and for services Congress appears to have funded. Judge Lee saw discretion all the way down.

However the merits opinion ultimately comes out, this dispute is likely to matter well beyond refugee law. It asks a basic question about the administrative state: when Congress funds compassion, does it merely permit it—or require it?



# Stays First, Merits Later: What the Ninth Circuit’s Interim Order Signals About Emergency Appellate Decisionmaking

## Why this order matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* is a good reminder that, in modern public-law litigation, emergency appellate orders are often doing more than merely freezing the status quo. Here, the panel issued a stay pending appeal before its full merits opinion, but the order still managed to resolve the dispute in highly consequential ways: it effectively restored the government’s suspension of refugee admissions, while at the same time preserving, and then affirmatively enforcing, a statutory obligation to provide certain resettlement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. In practical terms, the order shaped both the architecture of the refugee program and the near-term lives of the people affected by it.

That is significant not just because the case is politically charged, but because the panel used the stay posture to preview the merits. The order is styled as temporary, but the reasoning is not tentative. It leans hard on the Supreme Court’s deferential approach in *Trump v. Hawaii* for the admissions question, while signaling that the refugee-resettlement funding issue is different because it turns on a statutory duty under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. That combination makes the order a useful example of how emergency appellate management increasingly doubles as substantive lawmaking.

## The panel’s two-track approach

The order divides the case into two distinct questions. On refugee admissions, the panel says the government is likely to prevail because § 1182(f) gives the President broad authority to suspend entry of covered aliens when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the national interest. Quoting *Hawaii*, the panel emphasizes that courts may not engage in a “searching inquiry” into the President’s stated reasons. On that theory, the stay factors point strongly in the government’s favor.

But on refugee services for people already admitted, the panel draws a sharp distinction. It indicates that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge because § 1522 imposes obligations to provide reception and placement services, and because Congress appropriated substantial funding for refugee assistance in FY 2025. The order then goes further than a typical stay ruling: it directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the statutorily required services, including “the usual and customary services” previously afforded to refugees.

That move is notable. A stay order usually preserves things as they are; here, the panel partly preserves the district court’s relief and partly rewrites the operative arrangements to fit what the panel sees as the statutory baseline. It is a reminder that “interim” appellate orders can become the effective law of the case for months, sometimes longer.

## The use of Nken as a merits proxy

The order also shows how the *Nken* stay factors can function as a proxy for merits adjudication. In theory, a stay pending appeal asks only whether the movant has made a strong showing of likely success, whether irreparable harm is likely, and how the equities and public interest balance. In practice, when the underlying dispute is urgent and institutionally charged, those factors often force a court to decide much of the merits in abbreviated form.

That is exactly what happened here. The panel’s discussion of likelihood of success on the refugee-admissions claim is essentially a merits conclusion in miniature. It does not just say the government has raised serious arguments; it says the government is likely to prevail, and it anchors that conclusion in a reading of *Hawaii* that all but resolves the legal issue. Conversely, the statutory-service portion is framed as a likelihood-of-success analysis, but the order also identifies specific statutory provisions that appear to impose affirmative obligations and then uses that analysis to require reinstatement of cooperative agreements.

This is the kind of doctrinal compression that emergency litigation encourages. The stay posture does not eliminate the need to decide legal questions; it just accelerates them and often truncates the explanation.

## The split within the panel

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent underscores the stakes of that compression. She agrees that the President can impose a moratorium on refugee admissions, but she rejects the conclusion that § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty to provide the services the majority preserves. In her view, the statute is best read as authorization rather than command: the title says “authorization,” the operative subsections use discretionary language, and the provisions the majority cites are, in her reading, qualified by phrases like “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” and “as needed.”

That disagreement is doctrinally important because it highlights a recurring question in federal spending and administrative law: when does statutory language that permits or structures a program become a legal mandate to continue operating it at a particular level? The majority appears to read § 1522 as creating something closer to a statutory floor once Congress has appropriated money for refugee assistance. Judge Lee sees no such floor. The difference is not merely semantic; it determines whether the executive branch may pause a program as a matter of policy or whether it remains legally bound to maintain the infrastructure of resettlement for already admitted refugees.

## What this signals about emergency appellate practice

This order fits a broader trend in high-stakes public-law cases: emergency rulings are increasingly substantive, and substantive rulings increasingly arrive in emergency form. Courts are under pressure to act quickly when a district court injunction has immediate nationwide or programwide effects. The result is that stay orders, administrative stays, and partial stays are often where the real action is.

There are advantages to that system. It gives appellate courts flexibility to prevent chaos, especially where foreign affairs, immigration, and large federal programs are involved. It also allows courts to preserve claims and defenses without forcing a rushed final opinion.

But there are costs. Interim orders can create uncertainty, especially when they are detailed enough to govern implementation but not final enough to provide durable precedent. They can also blur the line between emergency relief and merits adjudication, leaving litigants and lower courts to infer doctrinal commitments from language that is, at least nominally, provisional. And because the practical effect of a stay order can last for months, the distinction between “temporary” and “decisive” becomes hard to maintain.

## The downstream implications

The immediate downstream effect is clear enough: refugee admissions remain largely suspended, while the government is under pressure to restore services for refugees already admitted. More broadly, the order may encourage litigants and courts to treat emergency appellate proceedings as a venue for previewing the eventual merits rather than merely preserving the status quo. That is especially likely in cases involving executive power, where appellate courts may view early intervention as necessary to avoid irreversible policy changes.

The other implication is doctrinal. If the merits opinion tracks the stay order, the Ninth Circuit may reinforce a strong distinction between the President’s authority under § 1182(f) and the executive branch’s obligations under § 1522. That would preserve broad immigration exclusion power while also recognizing that Congress has imposed affirmative administrative duties once refugees are admitted. If, by contrast, the final opinion narrows the statutory-duty theory, Judge Lee’s reading may prove more influential: the refugee-resettlement statutes may be treated as programmatic authorizations rather than enforceable commands.

Either way, *Pacito* shows that stay orders in major public-law cases are no longer just procedural footnotes. They are often the first, and sometimes the most consequential, statement of the court’s view of the law.



# Pacito and the limits of “defunding by executive action”

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* is about more than refugee admissions. It goes to a recurring separation-of-powers question: can the executive branch effectively dismantle a congressionally created program by suspending funding and terminating contracts, even when Congress has kept the program on the books, appropriated money for it, and imposed operational duties on the responsible agencies?

That question has immediate consequences for refugee resettlement. But the broader stakes are much larger. If the executive can nullify statutory programs by cutting off the contractual machinery that makes them work, then formal appropriations and statutory commands may matter far less than Congress thinks. *Pacito* is therefore best understood not just as an immigration case, but as a case about whether the President and agencies may accomplish through procurement and funding decisions what they may not be able to do directly through statutory repeal.

## The panel’s bottom line in the stay order

The order does two things at once. First, it stays the district court’s injunction against the suspension of refugee admissions. On that question, the panel leans heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii* and the broad authority Congress gave the President in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That part of the order is unsurprising: the Supreme Court has already said that § 1182(f) confers sweeping discretion, and the panel treats the President’s finding that refugee entry would be detrimental as enough to satisfy the statute.

Second, however, the panel signals that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge to the suspension of domestic resettlement services. Here the court focuses on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which it reads as imposing statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel is especially troubled by the government’s inability to explain what would replace the cooperative-agreement structure after the State Department terminated those agreements. In other words, the government did not just stop funding a program; it also could not say how it intended to satisfy the programmatic duties Congress enacted.

That concern drives the order’s most striking directive: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted. For now, the panel is not resolving the merits. But it is clearly signaling skepticism about the executive’s attempt to shut down the operating infrastructure of a statutory program while leaving the statute itself formally intact.

## The real fight is over § 1522

Judge Lee’s partial dissent shows how the legal question is framed. In his view, § 1522 is mostly an authorization statute, not a mandate. He emphasizes the title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated use of the phrase “is authorized.” On that reading, the executive retains discretion to decide whether to run the program, so long as appropriations are available.

The majority’s interim order points in the opposite direction. It reads several provisions of § 1522 as imposing affirmative duties: the Director “shall” make resources available for employment training, “shall” ensure that certain support is provided, and “shall” develop and implement alternative projects for refugees under 36 months in the United States. The panel also relies on reporting requirements and the fact that Congress appropriated billions for migration and refugee assistance. Taken together, those features suggest a statutory scheme that is not merely permissive, but operational.

This is a classic statutory interpretation fight, but it has a constitutional shadow. If the executive’s reading were accepted, then a statute could be functionally neutralized by nonuse: the program would remain in the U.S. Code, but the federal government could simply refuse to staff it, contract for it, or fund the service-delivery network it requires. The panel’s concern is that such an approach would allow executive policy reversal to substitute for legislative repeal.

## Why the cooperative-agreement issue is so important

The most interesting part of the order is not the refugee-admissions question. It is the government’s handling of the domestic-service apparatus.

Congress often implements social programs through grants, contracts, and cooperative agreements with nonprofit or state partners. That structure matters because the statute does not operate by itself; it depends on a web of administrative and contractual relationships. If the executive terminates all of those relationships at once, the program may cease to function even if the underlying statute remains in place.

That is why the panel’s emphasis on the government’s inability to identify a replacement mechanism is so consequential. The court is not merely asking whether the executive can reallocate resources within the bounds of appropriations. It is asking whether the executive can extinguish the operating capacity of a statutory scheme without Congress’s authorization. That question sounds in impoundment, agency implementation, and perhaps even nondelegation in reverse: not whether Congress gave too much power, but whether the executive is trying to take back power Congress already exercised.

The panel’s reasoning suggests a judicial instinct to look past formal labels. Calling something a “funding suspension” or a “contract termination” does not end the inquiry if the practical effect is to disable statutory duties. Courts have long been wary of end-runs around statutory commands, and *Pacito* fits squarely within that tradition.

## The separation-of-powers backdrop

There is a deeper doctrinal debate here about executive discretion over appropriated funds. The Constitution’s Appropriations Clause gives Congress the power of the purse, and the impoundment debates of the 1970s were fundamentally about whether the President can refuse to spend money Congress has made available. Modern administrative law adds another layer: agencies have discretion, but not unlimited discretion, to decide how to implement statutes, especially when Congress has specified duties.

*Pacito* sits at the intersection of those doctrines. The government’s position, as reflected in Judge Lee’s dissent and the stay posture, is that the executive has wide latitude to suspend programs it views as inconsistent with current policy priorities. The panel’s contrary instinct is that where Congress has imposed concrete operational obligations—particularly when it has appropriated funds and required reporting—the executive cannot simply turn the program off.

That distinction matters because it marks the line between administration and repeal. If the executive can effectively repeal through procurement decisions, then Congress’s policy choices become precarious. If the executive cannot, then courts may be asked to police the boundary between lawful reprioritization and unlawful dismantling.

## What comes next

Because this is an interim stay order, the court has not yet finally resolved the merits. The panel expressly says an opinion will follow. But the order already reveals where the case is headed: strong deference to the President on refugee admissions under § 1182(f), coupled with significant skepticism about the government’s ability to nullify domestic refugee-resettlement obligations through funding cuts and contract terminations.

The downstream effects could extend beyond refugee law. Any statutory program run through cooperative agreements, grants, or contracts may now prompt the same question: does the executive have discretion to pause or terminate the program as a matter of policy, or must it continue the operational arrangements Congress required until Congress says otherwise? *Pacito* suggests that at least some panels will look closely at the practical consequences of executive “defunding,” not just the formal authority invoked to justify it.
