Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# 1. The Refugee Suspension Order and the Litigation’s Procedural Path

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Entry Under USRAP

This case began with President Trump’s January 20, 2025 executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Acting under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, or USRAP, would be detrimental to the interests of the United States and therefore suspended refugee entry pending further findings. The key statutory provision is § 1182(f), which allows the President, after making a finding of detriment, to suspend the entry of aliens or a class of aliens for as long as he deems necessary. That statutory authority became the centerpiece of the later stay dispute because the government argued that it likely had the power to halt refugee admissions in this way.

## The Related Funding Suspension Under Executive Order 14169

Just four days later, on January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended all funding for USRAP, saying it was acting under a separate executive order, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” That funding suspension swept in domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. In other words, the government did not just stop future refugee entry; it also cut off funding for the machinery that helps refugees after arrival. That distinction mattered because the plaintiffs challenged the entry suspension and the funding suspension on different legal grounds.

## Who the Plaintiffs Are: Refugees Abroad, Refugees Already Admitted, and Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs fell into two groups. First were individual refugees: some had already been admitted to the United States, while others had been approved for resettlement but were still outside the country. Second were three organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to process refugees overseas and to provide resettlement services inside the United States. These organizational plaintiffs were important because they were directly affected by the funding cutoffs and the termination of their government agreements.

## The Claims Asserted: Refugee Act Challenge to the Suspension and APA Challenges to the Defunding

In February 2025, the plaintiffs sued in the Western District of Washington. They claimed that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980, which Congress enacted as part of the Immigration and Nationality Act. They also claimed that the defunding of USRAP violated the Administrative Procedure Act. The opinion treats these as distinct challenges: one aimed at the legality of the suspension of refugee entry, and another aimed at the legality of stopping the funding and services that Congress had provided for refugees.

## The District Court’s February 25 Oral Injunction and February 28 Written Order

The district court moved quickly. On February 25, it orally issued a preliminary injunction barring enforcement or implementation of several provisions of Executive Order 14163. Two days later, on February 28, the court issued a written order explaining the injunction and clarifying that it also prohibited suspension of USRAP funding. That written order is important because it expanded the practical scope of the first injunction beyond the executive order’s text and into the funding decision.

## The Government’s February 26 Termination of Cooperative Agreements

Before the written order, but after the oral injunction, the government terminated every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States and nearly all cooperative agreements for overseas processing support. Those agreements were the practical mechanism by which refugee services had been delivered. The terminations made the dispute more urgent because they put actual service delivery at risk while the litigation was still in its early stages.

## The District Court’s March 24 Injunction Requiring Reinstatement of Cooperative Agreements

The district court then issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, requiring the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements it had terminated after the February 25 ruling. This second injunction reached beyond the original challenge to the executive order itself and directly compelled the government to restore the contractual structure used to deliver refugee services. That expanded the scope of the dispute and set up the later stay proceedings in the Ninth Circuit.

## The Government’s Appeals and Prior Ninth Circuit Stay Orders

The government appealed the February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions. It also sought a stay pending appeal. In March, a Ninth Circuit motions panel granted the stay in part. The panel relied on the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that § 1182(f) gives the President very broad authority over entry restrictions and requires substantial judicial deference. The panel nevertheless refused to stay the injunction entirely: it carved out one category of refugees from the stay—those who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025.

That limited carveout mattered because it preserved relief for a subset of refugees whom the court treated differently from the larger class of people whose entry had been suspended. The panel later issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9 clarifying that carveout.

## The Earlier Carveout for Refugees Conditionally Approved Before January 20, 2025

The carveout shows how the court was trying to balance deference to the executive branch with the plaintiffs’ interests. Even while recognizing the President’s likely authority to suspend new refugee admissions, the motions panel allowed the district court’s order to remain effective as to refugees who had already been conditionally approved before the executive order took effect. That meant the stay was not absolute at first. The later order in this chapter vacates that limited protection, at least temporarily, while the appeal proceeds.

## The District Court’s July 14 Enforcement Framework and Magistrate Oversight

After briefing was underway but before oral argument, the district court took another step. On July 14, it issued an order creating a framework to enforce the earlier carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. This reflected continuing disputes over how the earlier stay and injunctions should operate in practice. The district court was no longer just issuing broad preliminary relief; it was managing implementation and enforcement.

## The Government’s Emergency Motion and the Ninth Circuit’s Administrative Stay

The government responded on July 17 with an emergency motion asking the Ninth Circuit to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order. The next day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court’s enforcement order. That administrative stay was temporary, designed to preserve the status quo while the court considered the parties’ positions. The panel then expedited oral argument, which was heard on September 3, 2025.

## Why This Order Is Interlocutory: Merits Opinion to Follow, But Immediate Relief Needed Now

The September 12, 2025 order is not the final merits decision. It is an interim order resolving whether the district court’s preliminary injunctions should remain in effect while the appeal is pending. The panel expressly says that a full opinion will issue later. For now, the court vacates both its own earlier administrative stay and the earlier stay, and it stays the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions in full except as specifically noted. In other words, the court is making a temporary appellate ruling to control the status quo during the appeal, not deciding the entire case on the merits. That is why the opinion is structured as a stay order rather than a final judgment.

# 2. The Stay-Pending-Appeal Framework and the Court’s Interim Role

## The function of a stay pending appeal in federal appellate practice

A stay pending appeal is an appellate court’s way of preserving the status quo while the appeal is being resolved. It does not decide the merits finally. Instead, it temporarily suspends the effect of the district court’s order so that the challenged relief does not take hold, or does not continue, before the appellate court has an opportunity to review the case. In a case like *Pacito v. Trump*, where the district court had entered preliminary injunctions affecting both the President’s refugee-admissions order and the government’s funding and contracting decisions, the stay question was urgent because the practical consequences of the injunctions were immediate and ongoing.

That is why the Ninth Circuit’s order does more than simply say “stay granted” or “stay denied.” It is an interim management order. It vacates earlier stay orders, restructures the immediate relief, and tells the parties what remains operative while the appeal proceeds. The panel is using its stay authority to control the case’s posture before a full merits opinion is issued.

## Nken v. Holder’s four factors for a stay

The governing framework comes from *Nken v. Holder*. The court recites the familiar four factors: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury to the stay applicant absent relief, injury to the other parties if a stay is granted, and where the public interest lies. These factors are balanced, but they are not all treated as equally important.

The opinion emphasizes that the first two factors are the “most critical.” That is an important cue for reading stay orders. A stay applicant must do more than show that a stay would be convenient or that the equities are mixed; it must make a strong showing that it is likely to win and that it will suffer harm if the district court’s order remains in place. The remaining factors still matter, but they often operate as part of the court’s equitable judgment once the merits and harm showings are addressed.

## Why likelihood of success and irreparable injury are the “most critical” factors

The Ninth Circuit’s analysis follows the conventional *Nken* structure but reveals how heavily the merits can dominate the stay inquiry. On the refugee-admissions issue, the panel says the government is likely to prevail because Supreme Court precedent, especially *Trump v. Hawaii*, gives the President broad deference under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The panel quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that the President need only “find” that entry would be detrimental to the national interest and that courts may not conduct a searching inquiry into the President’s reasons. That conclusion largely resolves the first stay factor in the government’s favor.

The panel then notes that the other factors also favor the United States. That is a typical stay analysis move: once the court sees a strong likelihood of success on a matter involving immigration and national security, the public-interest and harm factors often align with the government’s position because the district court’s injunction would constrain executive action in an area where the political branches have substantial authority.

## How appellate courts can grant interim relief before issuing a full merits opinion

A key procedural feature of this order is that the Ninth Circuit is not issuing its final merits opinion yet. It expressly says that an opinion will issue “in due course,” but that, in the meantime, the order itself will constitute the operative stay. This is a useful example of how appellate courts can act quickly when a case requires immediate stabilization.

The court has already heard expedited oral argument. It has briefing from both sides. It therefore has enough of a record to make a provisional judgment on whether the district court’s injunctions should remain active during the rest of the appeal. But it does not yet purport to resolve every legal question definitively. Instead, it gives a preliminary assessment sufficient to justify interim relief. This is especially important where the case implicates government operations, refugee admissions, and ongoing funding decisions that cannot easily be undone later.

## The significance of vacating prior administrative and partial stays before issuing a new comprehensive stay

The order begins by vacating several prior stay-related orders: the administrative stay entered on July 18 and the earlier stay from March 25, as amended in April and May. That matters because the court is not merely layering a new order on top of old ones; it is replacing the prior stay structure with a new, comprehensive arrangement.

Earlier in the litigation, the motions panel had granted only a partial stay, leaving a narrow carveout for refugees who had been conditionally approved before January 20, 2025. The district court later entered additional orders trying to implement that carveout and to ensure compliance. By the time of the September 2025 interim order, the appellate panel had determined that the stay posture needed to be reset. Vacating the earlier stays clears away the prior piecemeal treatment and allows the court to announce a single, operative stay pending final resolution of the appeal.

This is procedurally significant because stay orders are often time-sensitive and may need revision as the case evolves. The appellate court is not locked into its first emergency response. It can refine or replace earlier interim relief when later briefing and argument show that a different arrangement is warranted.

## How the court treats the stay motion as requiring provisional merits assessment rather than final adjudication

A stay pending appeal requires the court to make a provisional merits assessment. That is exactly what the panel does here. It says the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the refugee-admissions suspension, but it also says the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge to the defunding of refugee reception and placement services. The order therefore reflects a split evaluation: one side of the injunction is likely to be stayed because the government appears strong on the merits; another side remains vulnerable because the plaintiffs may have a stronger statutory argument.

This provisional approach is central to understanding stay practice. The appellate court is not deciding the case as if on full appeal. It is asking whether, in light of the current record and the relevant legal standards, the district court’s orders should remain in effect while the appeal is pending. That allows the court to protect the integrity of appellate review without prematurely foreclosing the merits.

The panel also makes clear that its merits observations are not the final word. For the APA issues, it says “for reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow.” That language signals that the court is willing to rely on an incomplete merits assessment for interim relief, while preserving its ability to write a fuller and possibly more nuanced opinion later.

## The relationship between stay analysis, public interest, and separation-of-powers concerns

The stay analysis in this case is tightly connected to separation-of-powers concerns. On the refugee-admissions issue, the panel relies on the President’s broad statutory authority under § 1182(f) and on *Trump v. Hawaii*’s recognition of substantial deference in immigration matters. That means the public-interest factor is not treated as an abstract policy balance. It is tied to the constitutional and statutory allocation of authority over immigration and foreign affairs.

At the same time, the court’s discussion of the APA claims shows the other side of the separation-of-powers equation. Congress may have imposed statutory obligations regarding refugee services, and if so, the executive branch may not simply ignore them. Thus, the public interest is served not only by giving the President room to act in immigration matters, but also by ensuring the government complies with statutory commands. The stay order reflects that tension. It preserves executive authority where the court sees strong statutory backing for the government, but it also acknowledges that Congress may have required certain services and that those statutory obligations may limit executive discretion.

## Why the panel denies the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot after restructuring the stay

The order concludes by denying the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot. That is a practical consequence of the court’s decision to issue a new stay order. Once the panel has vacated the old stays and imposed a new, broader stay pending appeal, there is no separate emergency motion left to decide. The relief the government sought has effectively been superseded by the court’s own interim order.

“Moot” here does not mean the motion lacked importance when filed. It means the panel’s new order has resolved the immediate procedural need that the motion was trying to address. This is another example of how appellate courts manage live disputes in real time: they can absorb pending motions into a broader interim ruling, thereby streamlining the case and avoiding redundant rulings.

Taken together, the order shows how a stay pending appeal operates as a provisional but powerful appellate tool. It preserves the court’s ability to decide the merits later, while immediately controlling the effect of the district court’s injunctions in a case involving immigration authority, refugee services, and the balance between executive discretion and statutory limits.

# 3. Presidential Power Over Refugee Entry: Why the Government Is Likely to Prevail on the Executive Order

## 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the President’s power to suspend entry of aliens or classes of aliens

This part of the order addresses the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under Executive Order No. 14163. The Ninth Circuit begins from the statute the President relied on: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That provision authorizes the President, when he “finds” that the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend entry “for such period as he shall deem necessary,” or to impose other restrictions on entry. The panel treats this statute as the central source of authority for the refugee-entry suspension.

The order also references 8 U.S.C. § 1185(a), which the President cited along with § 1182(f). In this portion of the order, however, § 1182(f) does the real work. The court’s discussion focuses on whether the President made the kind of finding the statute requires, and whether the executive order fits within the text of the statutory grant.

## The President’s finding that refugee entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests

The Executive Order stated that entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and suspended that entry pending further findings. The panel treats that finding as enough to satisfy the statutory trigger in § 1182(f). The opinion does not require a separate judicial assessment of whether the President’s prediction was wise, accurate, or well supported as a matter of policy.

That point matters because the plaintiffs argued that the refugee suspension violated the Refugee Act and sought to enjoin implementation. At the stay stage, the Ninth Circuit’s question was not whether the plaintiffs ultimately might prevail on every theory, but whether the government had shown a likelihood of success sufficient to justify pausing the district court’s injunction.

## Trump v. Hawaii as the controlling precedent on deference under § 1182(f)

The panel relies heavily on Trump v. Hawaii. That case is treated as the controlling statement of how much deference courts owe the President when he acts under § 1182(f). Quoting Hawaii, the order says § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and gives him “ample power” to impose entry restrictions beyond those elsewhere listed in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

The court also quotes Hawaii for the proposition that the judiciary cannot engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” In other words, once the President has made the statutory finding of detriment, the court will not second-guess the strength of the policy judgment behind it. That is a highly deferential standard, and it strongly favors the government at the stay stage.

## The court’s emphasis that it cannot conduct a “searching inquiry” into presidential justifications

This language is crucial to understanding why the panel concludes the government is likely to win on the entry-suspension issue. The court does not say that the President’s reasons are immune from all legal scrutiny in every context. Rather, it says that under § 1182(f) and Hawaii, the court may not probe deeply into the persuasiveness of the President’s reasons for finding refugee entry detrimental.

That is why the order states that “[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’” Because the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here,” the panel concludes that the government is likely to succeed on the merits of this issue.

## The panel’s conclusion that the statutory prerequisite was satisfied

The Ninth Circuit’s merits prediction on the entry-suspension issue is narrow but decisive. The panel says the President made the required finding, and that is enough. It does not identify any additional procedural step or substantive constraint in § 1182(f) that the executive order failed to satisfy. The court therefore concludes that the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the refugee-entry suspension.

This reasoning is tied to the stay standard. A stay applicant must show a strong likelihood of success on the merits. By holding that the government is likely to prevail on the legality of the suspension, the court effectively resolves the first and most important stay factor in the government’s favor for this issue.

## Why the court says the executive order does not exceed any textual limit in § 1182(f)

The panel also emphasizes text. Quoting Hawaii again, it says that “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” That means the court sees the order as fitting within the statute’s broad language allowing the President to suspend entry of a class of aliens.

The phrase “any class of aliens” is especially important here. Refugees under USRAP can be treated as a class for purposes of entry restriction. Because the statute expressly permits the suspension of “all aliens or any class of aliens,” the order views the refugee admissions program as falling within the statutory text rather than beyond it.

## How foreign affairs and national-interest considerations affect irreparable harm and public interest

After finding that the government is likely to succeed on the merits, the panel turns briefly to the remaining stay factors. It says those factors “touch[] upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States” and favors the government as determined by the “broad discretion” conferred on the President. This reflects a traditional judicial concern in immigration and foreign-affairs cases: executive decisions about who may enter the country often implicate international relations, national security, and foreign policy.

The order does not separately spell out every injury the government claimed, but its treatment of the second, third, and fourth stay factors indicates that the panel believed the balance of harms and the public interest favored maintaining the suspension while the appeal proceeds. In stay analysis, that means the government showed enough potential harm from being unable to enforce the President’s entry decision, and the court saw no countervailing interest strong enough to outweigh the executive’s asserted national interests at this stage.

## Why the district court’s injunction against implementing the refugee-entry suspension is stayed

Because the government is likely to prevail on the legality of the entry suspension, the Ninth Circuit stays the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions “in their entirety,” except for one separate carveout dealing with domestic resettlement services. As to the refugee-entry issue itself, the effect is straightforward: the district court’s order blocking implementation of the suspension is paused.

A stay does not finally decide the appeal, but it does suspend the district court’s command during appellate review. Here, the stay means the executive order’s refugee-entry suspension can remain in effect while the Ninth Circuit considers the case on the merits.

## How this part of the order signals likely reversal or narrowing of the district court on the entry-suspension issue

This section of the order strongly suggests that the Ninth Circuit is skeptical of the district court’s injunction on the refugee-entry issue. The panel’s language is not tentative about the merits of § 1182(f): it says the government is “likely to prevail,” and it relies directly on Hawaii’s deferential framework. That is a strong indication that the district court’s merits ruling on the entry-suspension question may be reversed or at least narrowed in the court’s eventual opinion.

At the same time, the order carefully limits its present holding to the stay context. It does not issue the full merits opinion yet. But for the refugee-entry suspension, the practical message is clear: the Ninth Circuit viewed the President’s action as well supported by § 1182(f), and it was willing to let that policy remain in place while the appeal continues.

# 4. The APA and the Statutory Duty to Provide Post-Admission Refugee Services

## The distinction between suspending future refugee admissions and cutting off services for refugees already admitted

The panel’s order separates two very different government actions. The first is the President’s suspension of new refugee admissions into the United States. On that issue, the panel says the government is likely to prevail because 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) gives the President broad authority to suspend entry of covered aliens when he finds their entry would be detrimental to the national interest. The second action is the government’s termination of funding and service arrangements for refugees who were already admitted, or who were otherwise already in the process of resettlement. That second issue is where the panel sees a serious APA problem.

This distinction matters because the statutory authorization to control entry does not automatically answer whether the government can stop providing domestic resettlement services to refugees who are already in the country. The plaintiffs’ APA theory attacks the funding and implementation decisions, not just the admission moratorium itself.

## 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as the statutory framework for domestic refugee resettlement assistance

The key statute for the post-admission issue is 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The panel treats this provision as the federal framework for domestic resettlement assistance, sometimes called “reception and placement” services. It is the statute that governs what the government must do for refugees after they arrive, including employment training, language help, cash assistance, medical support, and case management.

The panel also notes that Congress has appropriated substantial funding for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025. That matters because many of the statute’s duties are qualified by available appropriations. The existence of appropriated funds supports the view that the statutory scheme is not merely aspirational; it is meant to operate in practice.

## Section 1522(a)(1)(A): the Director’s duties regarding employment, English training, cash assistance, and equal access

The majority emphasizes § 1522(a)(1)(A), which directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make resources available for employment training and placement, ensure access to English-language training, arrange cash assistance in a way that does not undermine self-sufficiency, and ensure women have equal training opportunities.

The panel reads these provisions as mandatory duties, not just optional goals. Even though the section is framed with the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations,” it still uses “shall” language. The court views that wording as creating an obligation to provide the listed services when funding exists. In other words, the availability of appropriations limits the duty, but does not eliminate it.

## Section 1522(d)(2)(B): legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children

The panel also relies on § 1522(d)(2)(B), which says that during any interim period while an unaccompanied refugee child is in the United States or in transit, the Director “shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)” for the child if necessary and is authorized to make necessary decisions for the child’s immediate care.

This provision is especially strong evidence of a statutory obligation because it uses mandatory language and directly addresses responsibility for vulnerable children. The majority uses it to show that Congress was not simply giving the agency permission to provide services; in at least some situations, it required the agency to assume responsibility.

## Section 1522(e)(7)(A): alternative projects providing interim support, medical services, support services, and case management

Section 1522(e)(7)(A) reinforces the same point. It says the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects” for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which they receive interim support, medical services, support services, and case management as needed.

Again, the majority sees “shall” as signaling a required program. The phrase “as needed” affects the scope of the services, but not the fact that the Secretary must develop and implement the program. That language strengthens the conclusion that post-admission refugee assistance is part of the statutory scheme Congress imposed.

## The significance of the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations”

The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is the main textual qualifier in the statute. It means the duty to provide services is limited by what Congress has funded. The panel does not read that phrase as converting the statute into a mere grant of discretion. Instead, it treats the phrase as a limitation on the amount and availability of services, not on the existence of the obligation itself.

That reading is central to the panel’s provisional APA analysis. If the government had no appropriated funds at all, the statute might not require performance beyond available resources. But the panel points out that Congress did appropriate funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025. So the appropriations qualifier does not appear to justify a wholesale termination of the statutory services.

The dissent reads the same language differently. Judge Lee argues that § 1522 is primarily an authorization statute and that its mandatory language describes conditions for programs the executive may choose to fund, not commands that the programs must operate. The split between the majority and the dissent is therefore largely a dispute about statutory interpretation: whether § 1522 creates a duty or merely permits action.

## Congressional appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025

The panel notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, later reduced by $800 million in a rescissions act. This factual detail matters because it undermines any suggestion that the executive was operating in a funding vacuum.

The court does not need to decide the full appropriations question at this stage, but the existence of a significant appropriation supports the majority’s view that the agency was still expected to carry out the refugee resettlement system in some form. It also helps explain why the plaintiffs’ APA claim remains viable despite the administration’s policy change.

## Section 1523’s reporting requirement as evidence of statutory accountability

The panel also cites § 1523, which requires the Secretary to submit a detailed report to Congress on refugee program activities after each fiscal year. This reporting obligation is not the main source of the duty to provide services, but it helps show that Congress created an accountable administrative program rather than a wholly discretionary set of foreign-policy choices.

In context, the reporting requirement supports the majority’s view that Congress expected continuing implementation of the refugee assistance framework. A program that must be reported to Congress is less likely to be a purely optional executive initiative.

## Why the court treats cooperative agreements as a permissible mechanism for delivering required services

The statute allows refugee services to be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. The panel uses this point to explain why the government’s use of cooperative agreements is legally important. The agreements are not the source of the statutory duty; they are the mechanism by which the government has historically carried it out.

That distinction matters for the APA claim. If the statute requires services, then the government cannot avoid the duty simply by terminating the agreements that help it perform the duty. The court’s provisional remedy therefore directs reinstatement of the cooperative agreements needed to provide the required services to admitted refugees.

## The factual importance of the State Department’s mass termination of cooperative agreements

The State Department terminated all cooperative agreements for reception and placement services and nearly all agreements for overseas processing support. The panel treats this as a practical shutdown of the refugee resettlement apparatus. Even if the executive retained some abstract authority to reorganize the program, eliminating the contracts and agreements necessary to deliver services made the statutory duty impossible to carry out.

That factual setting is crucial. The APA issue is not an abstract challenge to policy preference; it is a challenge to concrete administrative action that apparently left the government without a functioning delivery system for services that the statute appears to require.

## The government’s representation that it was seeking a replacement provider and its later uncertainty at oral argument

The government told the district court in March 2025 that it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency. But by the time of oral argument, the panel says the government was unsure whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement providers.

That uncertainty is legally significant because it goes directly to whether the government can realistically comply with § 1522. If the government cannot identify a working replacement system, then the mass termination of agreements looks less like a temporary reorganization and more like a failure to perform a statutory obligation.

## Why that uncertainty matters to likelihood of success on the APA claim

At the stay stage, the court does not decide the merits finally. It asks whether the government is likely to succeed on appeal. Here, the panel concludes that the government is “not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA” because the record suggests the government may have stopped providing services Congress required it to provide.

This is why the uncertainty about replacement providers matters. If the government does not know whether it can fulfill the statutory services at all, then it is harder to say the plaintiffs will lose their APA claim. The panel therefore treats the government’s inability to show an available substitute as supporting the plaintiffs’ likelihood of success.

## The panel’s provisional conclusion that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge

The court’s final provisional step is not a definitive merits ruling. It says only that, “for reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow,” the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA theory. Based on that showing, the panel orders reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the usual and customary reception and placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States.

So the key takeaway is this: the panel is willing to stay the district court’s injunction insofar as it blocks the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, but it is not prepared to let the government dismantle the statutory machinery for post-admission refugee assistance while the appeal is pending. The majority views § 1522 as creating a real obligation, and the State Department’s termination of the service network as likely inconsistent with that obligation. Judge Lee disagrees, reading § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory, but the panel order itself preserves at least part of the district court’s protection for already admitted refugees.

# 5. The Modified Stay, the Ordered Relief, and Judge Lee’s Partial Dissent

## The operative disposition: district court injunctions stayed in their entirety except for the services carveout

This Ninth Circuit filing is not the court’s full merits opinion. It is an interim order entered after expedited oral argument that functions as the operative appellate ruling until the panel issues its later opinion. The court first vacated its earlier administrative stay and its prior stay orders, then replaced them with a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal. The practical effect is that the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions are stayed “in their entirety,” except for one important exception the panel spells out at the end of the order.

That exception matters because it narrows the district court’s relief while preserving a limited obligation on the government. The panel is largely aligned with the government on the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), but it does not give the government a complete win. Instead, it leaves in place a requirement that the government reinstate certain cooperative agreements so that statutory refugee resettlement services can continue for refugees already admitted to the United States.

## The panel’s directive that the government reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 reception and placement services

The most concrete command in the order is this: the government is “directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” This is not a broad injunction about all refugee policy. It is a targeted directive tied to the statutory resettlement regime Congress created in 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

The court’s reasoning is driven by its concern that, after terminating the cooperative agreements, the government was uncertain whether it could still provide the services that federal law requires for admitted refugees. The panel notes that the government had represented to the district court that it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency, but at oral argument it could not say whether any progress had actually been made. That uncertainty is what leads the panel to order reinstatement of the cooperative agreements needed to keep the service structure functioning.

## The scope of the required services: “usual and customary services” previously afforded under the agreements

The order does not attempt to draft a new refugee program from scratch. It limits the reinstated services to the “usual and customary services” that refugees had received under the prior cooperative agreements. That phrase is important because it signals continuity rather than expansion. The panel is not requiring the government to create new benefits beyond what the existing system historically provided; it is requiring restoration of the baseline reception and placement services that had been furnished through the now-terminated agreements.

For a law student, this is a common appellate technique in interim relief: the court tries to preserve the statutory status quo rather than adjudicate every disputed detail immediately. Here, the panel’s remedy reflects a preliminary conclusion that the government is likely to lose on at least part of the APA challenge if the services disappear entirely.

## Why the panel’s order protects refugees already admitted to the United States, not prospective entrants abroad

A critical feature of the order is its distinction between two groups of refugees. The panel is willing to stay the district court’s injunction as to the suspension of refugee admissions, meaning it accepts that the government is likely to prevail on the legality of pausing entry into the United States. But the court draws a different line for refugees already admitted.

That distinction tracks the structure of the statutes at issue. Section 1182(f) concerns the President’s power to suspend entry. By contrast, § 1522 concerns domestic resettlement and assistance after admission. The panel’s relief therefore protects admitted refugees who need reception and placement services inside the United States, while leaving intact the government’s ability to prevent further entry of refugees abroad. Put differently, the order separates the law of admission from the law of resettlement.

## The practical consequence of the order for resettlement agencies and already-admitted refugees

In practical terms, the order forces the government back into the cooperative-agreement framework that had long supported refugee resettlement. For the organizations that contract with the government, the order means reinstatement of agreements that fund overseas processing support and domestic reception and placement services. For already-admitted refugees, it means the continuation of the services that help them transition into life in the United States: placement, support, and related resettlement assistance.

The court’s order is significant because it treats those services not as optional humanitarian assistance but as part of a statutory program that must remain operational, at least to the extent required by available appropriations and the structure of § 1522. The government’s inability to confirm that an alternative provider system was in place helped drive the panel’s decision to require reinstatement.

## Judge Lee’s agreement that the President may suspend refugee admissions

Judge Lee agrees with the panel on one major point: the district court should not have blocked the President’s decision to suspend refugee admissions. In his view, the President has “vast discretion and deference” in immigration matters, and the district court wrongly enjoined the executive branch from implementing that policy choice. So on the admissions issue, he would keep the stay in place across the board.

That agreement is important because it shows that the disagreement within the panel is not about the existence of presidential power under § 1182(f). All members of the panel accept, at least at this stage, that the President can suspend refugee admissions. The fight is over what happens after refugees are already in the system.

## Judge Lee’s disagreement over whether § 1522 is mandatory or merely permissive

Judge Lee’s partial dissent turns on a statutory interpretation question: does § 1522 require the government to provide resettlement services, or does it merely authorize the executive branch to do so if it chooses? The majority treats the statute as creating a legal duty to provide certain reception and placement services to admitted refugees. Judge Lee reads it as discretionary.

That difference matters because if § 1522 is mandatory, then the government cannot simply shut down the cooperative-agreement system without violating federal law. If it is permissive, then the executive branch has room to scale back or terminate programs so long as it remains within appropriations and other statutory limits. Judge Lee believes the latter is the better reading, and so he would stay the district court’s injunctions entirely.

## His reliance on the title “Authorization for programs” and repeated uses of “is authorized” in § 1522(b)-(e)

Judge Lee begins with the structure and wording of § 1522. He emphasizes that the statute’s title is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” In his view, “authorization” signals permission, not command. He then points to the repeated use of “is authorized” in subsections (b) through (e), which govern different categories of refugee assistance. To him, those phrases show that Congress gave the executive branch discretion to fund and operate the programs, rather than imposing an enforceable duty to do so.

This is a classic textual move: the dissent reads the statute’s verbs and heading as permissive indicators, while the majority reads the same statutory scheme as imposing operational obligations. The disagreement is not about whether Congress permitted refugee assistance; it is about whether Congress required it.

## His reading of § 1522(a) as imposing conditions only if the government chooses to provide assistance

Judge Lee treats § 1522(a), which uses “shall” and refers to “the extent of available appropriations,” as conditional rather than mandatory in the way the majority reads it. In his view, subsection (a) does not itself create a freestanding duty to operate refugee programs. Instead, it sets conditions that apply if the government elects to provide assistance under the discretionary program provisions in the rest of § 1522.

That is why he sees subsection (a) as a kind of instruction manual for how to administer programs the executive has chosen to fund, not as an independent requirement that those programs exist. On this view, “shall” describes how assistance must be provided if provided, but it does not compel the government to provide it in the first place.

## His treatment of subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) as too qualified to create enforceable mandates

Judge Lee also addresses the two provisions the majority relies on most heavily to support a mandatory-duty reading. He thinks those provisions are too qualified to do the work the majority assigns them.

As to subsection (d)(2)(B), he emphasizes the qualifying phrase “if necessary” and the authorization to make “necessary decisions” for the child’s immediate care. To him, that language leaves too much discretion to the executive branch to count as a clear mandate. He also notes that the record does not make clear whether unaccompanied refugee children are actually at issue.

As to subsection (e)(7)(A), he focuses on the phrase “as needed.” In his view, that wording means the scope and operation of the support projects remain within executive judgment. He therefore regards that subsection as another example of discretionary program authority, not a direct command enforceable through injunction.

## The core interpretive split between the majority and dissent: statutory duty versus executive discretion

The central divide in the panel is straightforward: is § 1522 a statutory duty or a statutory permission? The majority says the government must maintain enough of the cooperative-agreement system to provide the reception and placement services Congress required for admitted refugees. Judge Lee says Congress merely authorized the executive to create and fund such services, leaving the decision whether to keep them operating to the political branches.

That split explains why the panel’s order is so limited and why Judge Lee parts company with it. The majority sees the government’s shutdown of the cooperative agreements as inconsistent with the statute. Judge Lee sees the shutdown as a lawful policy choice that the courts should not second-guess. The difference is not over refugee policy in the abstract; it is over the meaning and legal force of § 1522.

## What the order suggests about the issues likely to dominate the forthcoming merits opinion and possible further review

Because this is only a stay order, the panel repeatedly says that a full merits opinion will follow. But the order strongly suggests the issues that will dominate that later opinion: the scope of presidential power under § 1182(f), the meaning of § 1522, and whether the government’s termination of cooperative agreements unlawfully eliminated services Congress required for admitted refugees.

The order also hints at the likely shape of further appellate or Supreme Court review. The panel is relying heavily on precedent such as Trump v. Hawaii for deference on admission decisions, while reserving the statutory question about resettlement services for later explanation. That division makes the case especially important because it separates immigration exclusion power from domestic statutory administration. The forthcoming merits opinion will likely need to resolve that statutory split directly, and the partial dissent shows that the court is already divided over how to read the refugee resettlement provisions.
