Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Pacito and the limits of “defunding by executive action”

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* is about more than refugee admissions. It goes to a recurring separation-of-powers question: can the executive branch effectively dismantle a congressionally created program by suspending funding and terminating contracts, even when Congress has kept the program on the books, appropriated money for it, and imposed operational duties on the responsible agencies?

That question has immediate consequences for refugee resettlement. But the broader stakes are much larger. If the executive can nullify statutory programs by cutting off the contractual machinery that makes them work, then formal appropriations and statutory commands may matter far less than Congress thinks. *Pacito* is therefore best understood not just as an immigration case, but as a case about whether the President and agencies may accomplish through procurement and funding decisions what they may not be able to do directly through statutory repeal.

## The panel’s bottom line in the stay order

The order does two things at once. First, it stays the district court’s injunction against the suspension of refugee admissions. On that question, the panel leans heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii* and the broad authority Congress gave the President in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That part of the order is unsurprising: the Supreme Court has already said that § 1182(f) confers sweeping discretion, and the panel treats the President’s finding that refugee entry would be detrimental as enough to satisfy the statute.

Second, however, the panel signals that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge to the suspension of domestic resettlement services. Here the court focuses on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which it reads as imposing statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel is especially troubled by the government’s inability to explain what would replace the cooperative-agreement structure after the State Department terminated those agreements. In other words, the government did not just stop funding a program; it also could not say how it intended to satisfy the programmatic duties Congress enacted.

That concern drives the order’s most striking directive: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted. For now, the panel is not resolving the merits. But it is clearly signaling skepticism about the executive’s attempt to shut down the operating infrastructure of a statutory program while leaving the statute itself formally intact.

## The real fight is over § 1522

Judge Lee’s partial dissent shows how the legal question is framed. In his view, § 1522 is mostly an authorization statute, not a mandate. He emphasizes the title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated use of the phrase “is authorized.” On that reading, the executive retains discretion to decide whether to run the program, so long as appropriations are available.

The majority’s interim order points in the opposite direction. It reads several provisions of § 1522 as imposing affirmative duties: the Director “shall” make resources available for employment training, “shall” ensure that certain support is provided, and “shall” develop and implement alternative projects for refugees under 36 months in the United States. The panel also relies on reporting requirements and the fact that Congress appropriated billions for migration and refugee assistance. Taken together, those features suggest a statutory scheme that is not merely permissive, but operational.

This is a classic statutory interpretation fight, but it has a constitutional shadow. If the executive’s reading were accepted, then a statute could be functionally neutralized by nonuse: the program would remain in the U.S. Code, but the federal government could simply refuse to staff it, contract for it, or fund the service-delivery network it requires. The panel’s concern is that such an approach would allow executive policy reversal to substitute for legislative repeal.

## Why the cooperative-agreement issue is so important

The most interesting part of the order is not the refugee-admissions question. It is the government’s handling of the domestic-service apparatus.

Congress often implements social programs through grants, contracts, and cooperative agreements with nonprofit or state partners. That structure matters because the statute does not operate by itself; it depends on a web of administrative and contractual relationships. If the executive terminates all of those relationships at once, the program may cease to function even if the underlying statute remains in place.

That is why the panel’s emphasis on the government’s inability to identify a replacement mechanism is so consequential. The court is not merely asking whether the executive can reallocate resources within the bounds of appropriations. It is asking whether the executive can extinguish the operating capacity of a statutory scheme without Congress’s authorization. That question sounds in impoundment, agency implementation, and perhaps even nondelegation in reverse: not whether Congress gave too much power, but whether the executive is trying to take back power Congress already exercised.

The panel’s reasoning suggests a judicial instinct to look past formal labels. Calling something a “funding suspension” or a “contract termination” does not end the inquiry if the practical effect is to disable statutory duties. Courts have long been wary of end-runs around statutory commands, and *Pacito* fits squarely within that tradition.

## The separation-of-powers backdrop

There is a deeper doctrinal debate here about executive discretion over appropriated funds. The Constitution’s Appropriations Clause gives Congress the power of the purse, and the impoundment debates of the 1970s were fundamentally about whether the President can refuse to spend money Congress has made available. Modern administrative law adds another layer: agencies have discretion, but not unlimited discretion, to decide how to implement statutes, especially when Congress has specified duties.

*Pacito* sits at the intersection of those doctrines. The government’s position, as reflected in Judge Lee’s dissent and the stay posture, is that the executive has wide latitude to suspend programs it views as inconsistent with current policy priorities. The panel’s contrary instinct is that where Congress has imposed concrete operational obligations—particularly when it has appropriated funds and required reporting—the executive cannot simply turn the program off.

That distinction matters because it marks the line between administration and repeal. If the executive can effectively repeal through procurement decisions, then Congress’s policy choices become precarious. If the executive cannot, then courts may be asked to police the boundary between lawful reprioritization and unlawful dismantling.

## What comes next

Because this is an interim stay order, the court has not yet finally resolved the merits. The panel expressly says an opinion will follow. But the order already reveals where the case is headed: strong deference to the President on refugee admissions under § 1182(f), coupled with significant skepticism about the government’s ability to nullify domestic refugee-resettlement obligations through funding cuts and contract terminations.

The downstream effects could extend beyond refugee law. Any statutory program run through cooperative agreements, grants, or contracts may now prompt the same question: does the executive have discretion to pause or terminate the program as a matter of policy, or must it continue the operational arrangements Congress required until Congress says otherwise? *Pacito* suggests that at least some panels will look closely at the practical consequences of executive “defunding,” not just the formal authority invoked to justify it.
