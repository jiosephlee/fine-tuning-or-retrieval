Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Stays First, Merits Later: What the Ninth Circuit’s Interim Order Signals About Emergency Appellate Decisionmaking

## Why this order matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* is a good reminder that, in modern public-law litigation, emergency appellate orders are often doing more than merely freezing the status quo. Here, the panel issued a stay pending appeal before its full merits opinion, but the order still managed to resolve the dispute in highly consequential ways: it effectively restored the government’s suspension of refugee admissions, while at the same time preserving, and then affirmatively enforcing, a statutory obligation to provide certain resettlement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. In practical terms, the order shaped both the architecture of the refugee program and the near-term lives of the people affected by it.

That is significant not just because the case is politically charged, but because the panel used the stay posture to preview the merits. The order is styled as temporary, but the reasoning is not tentative. It leans hard on the Supreme Court’s deferential approach in *Trump v. Hawaii* for the admissions question, while signaling that the refugee-resettlement funding issue is different because it turns on a statutory duty under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. That combination makes the order a useful example of how emergency appellate management increasingly doubles as substantive lawmaking.

## The panel’s two-track approach

The order divides the case into two distinct questions. On refugee admissions, the panel says the government is likely to prevail because § 1182(f) gives the President broad authority to suspend entry of covered aliens when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the national interest. Quoting *Hawaii*, the panel emphasizes that courts may not engage in a “searching inquiry” into the President’s stated reasons. On that theory, the stay factors point strongly in the government’s favor.

But on refugee services for people already admitted, the panel draws a sharp distinction. It indicates that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge because § 1522 imposes obligations to provide reception and placement services, and because Congress appropriated substantial funding for refugee assistance in FY 2025. The order then goes further than a typical stay ruling: it directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the statutorily required services, including “the usual and customary services” previously afforded to refugees.

That move is notable. A stay order usually preserves things as they are; here, the panel partly preserves the district court’s relief and partly rewrites the operative arrangements to fit what the panel sees as the statutory baseline. It is a reminder that “interim” appellate orders can become the effective law of the case for months, sometimes longer.

## The use of Nken as a merits proxy

The order also shows how the *Nken* stay factors can function as a proxy for merits adjudication. In theory, a stay pending appeal asks only whether the movant has made a strong showing of likely success, whether irreparable harm is likely, and how the equities and public interest balance. In practice, when the underlying dispute is urgent and institutionally charged, those factors often force a court to decide much of the merits in abbreviated form.

That is exactly what happened here. The panel’s discussion of likelihood of success on the refugee-admissions claim is essentially a merits conclusion in miniature. It does not just say the government has raised serious arguments; it says the government is likely to prevail, and it anchors that conclusion in a reading of *Hawaii* that all but resolves the legal issue. Conversely, the statutory-service portion is framed as a likelihood-of-success analysis, but the order also identifies specific statutory provisions that appear to impose affirmative obligations and then uses that analysis to require reinstatement of cooperative agreements.

This is the kind of doctrinal compression that emergency litigation encourages. The stay posture does not eliminate the need to decide legal questions; it just accelerates them and often truncates the explanation.

## The split within the panel

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent underscores the stakes of that compression. She agrees that the President can impose a moratorium on refugee admissions, but she rejects the conclusion that § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty to provide the services the majority preserves. In her view, the statute is best read as authorization rather than command: the title says “authorization,” the operative subsections use discretionary language, and the provisions the majority cites are, in her reading, qualified by phrases like “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” and “as needed.”

That disagreement is doctrinally important because it highlights a recurring question in federal spending and administrative law: when does statutory language that permits or structures a program become a legal mandate to continue operating it at a particular level? The majority appears to read § 1522 as creating something closer to a statutory floor once Congress has appropriated money for refugee assistance. Judge Lee sees no such floor. The difference is not merely semantic; it determines whether the executive branch may pause a program as a matter of policy or whether it remains legally bound to maintain the infrastructure of resettlement for already admitted refugees.

## What this signals about emergency appellate practice

This order fits a broader trend in high-stakes public-law cases: emergency rulings are increasingly substantive, and substantive rulings increasingly arrive in emergency form. Courts are under pressure to act quickly when a district court injunction has immediate nationwide or programwide effects. The result is that stay orders, administrative stays, and partial stays are often where the real action is.

There are advantages to that system. It gives appellate courts flexibility to prevent chaos, especially where foreign affairs, immigration, and large federal programs are involved. It also allows courts to preserve claims and defenses without forcing a rushed final opinion.

But there are costs. Interim orders can create uncertainty, especially when they are detailed enough to govern implementation but not final enough to provide durable precedent. They can also blur the line between emergency relief and merits adjudication, leaving litigants and lower courts to infer doctrinal commitments from language that is, at least nominally, provisional. And because the practical effect of a stay order can last for months, the distinction between “temporary” and “decisive” becomes hard to maintain.

## The downstream implications

The immediate downstream effect is clear enough: refugee admissions remain largely suspended, while the government is under pressure to restore services for refugees already admitted. More broadly, the order may encourage litigants and courts to treat emergency appellate proceedings as a venue for previewing the eventual merits rather than merely preserving the status quo. That is especially likely in cases involving executive power, where appellate courts may view early intervention as necessary to avoid irreversible policy changes.

The other implication is doctrinal. If the merits opinion tracks the stay order, the Ninth Circuit may reinforce a strong distinction between the President’s authority under § 1182(f) and the executive branch’s obligations under § 1522. That would preserve broad immigration exclusion power while also recognizing that Congress has imposed affirmative administrative duties once refugees are admitted. If, by contrast, the final opinion narrows the statutory-duty theory, Judge Lee’s reading may prove more influential: the refugee-resettlement statutes may be treated as programmatic authorizations rather than enforceable commands.

Either way, *Pacito* shows that stay orders in major public-law cases are no longer just procedural footnotes. They are often the first, and sometimes the most consequential, statement of the court’s view of the law.
