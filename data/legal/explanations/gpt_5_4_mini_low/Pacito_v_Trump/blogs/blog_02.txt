Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Authorization or Obligation? The Statutory Fight Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522 in Judge Lee’s Partial Dissent

## Why this order matters

The most immediate significance of *Pacito v. Trump* is not the familiar one—the scope of presidential power to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—but the quieter, more institutional question lurking in the background: when Congress funds a refugee-resettlement scheme, does the executive branch merely get permission to operate it, or does it incur a legal duty to do so?

That question matters because it draws the line between political discretion and judicially enforceable statutory obligation. In the panel’s interim order, the court treated the admissions suspension as likely lawful under *Trump v. Hawaii*, but it also concluded that the government was not likely to prevail against at least one APA challenge because § 1522 appears to require continued provision of reception and placement services for refugees already admitted. Judge Lee’s partial dissent takes direct aim at that premise. In his view, the statute is “most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate.”

## The majority’s reading of § 1522

The majority’s analysis, though brief in this order, is built around the structure of § 1522 and the statute’s repeated use of mandatory language. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) says that, “to the extent of available appropriations,” the Director “shall” make resources available for refugee employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and equal access for women. The order also points to subsection (d)(2)(B), which says that during an interim period for unaccompanied minor refugees, the Director “shall assume legal responsibility” if necessary, and subsection (e)(7)(A), which says the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects” for certain recently arrived refugees.

That “shall” language matters. The majority appears to read § 1522 not as a menu of optional programs but as a set of congressionally imposed obligations, subject only to the availability of appropriations. The point is reinforced by Congress’s FY 2025 funding for migration and refugee assistance, which the order treats as evidence that the program is supposed to continue operating rather than be switched off by executive fiat. On that view, the government may have discretion over how to administer the programs, but not whether to administer them at all.

## Judge Lee’s textual counterargument

Judge Lee’s dissent is classic statutory-structure textualism. He begins with the title: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” To him, that title is not decorative; it signals the provision’s basic character. “Authorization,” he notes, is permissive, not mandatory.

He then points to the operative subsections. Sections 1522(b) through (e) repeatedly say that the Director “is authorized” to provide various forms of assistance. In ordinary statutory drafting, that is the language of permission. And Lee reads § 1522(a)’s use of “shall” differently than the majority does. Because § 1522(a) is framed as “conditions and considerations” “[i]n providing assistance under this section,” he sees it as regulating how discretionary programs must be run if the executive branch chooses to fund them, not as independently compelling the government to keep those programs open.

That is a plausible structural argument, and one that will resonate with readers who think courts too often convert appropriations-based statutory frameworks into hard mandates. Lee’s critique is especially forceful on subsection (d)(2)(B): even there, he says, the duty arises only “if necessary,” and the provision is saturated with administrative discretion. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A)’s “as needed” qualifier leaves the executive room to decide the contours of the program.

## The deeper interpretive divide

What makes this disagreement interesting is that both opinions are doing modern textualism, but they are weighting different signals. The majority treats “shall” and Congress’s appropriations as enough to create an enforceable obligation. Judge Lee treats the title, the “authorized” language, and the statute’s discretion-laden qualifiers as decisive.

This is a familiar tension in spending-statute interpretation. Congress often writes in a hybrid way: authorizing programs, conditioning them on appropriations, and then using mandatory-sounding verbs to direct implementation. The hard question is whether those verbs create a judicially enforceable duty or simply describe the terms on which an authorized program should operate if the political branches choose to run it.

The majority’s approach is more functional. If Congress appropriated billions for refugee assistance and wrote mandatory language about how refugees are to be served, the executive cannot lawfully wind the program down to zero while leaving the statutory scheme formally intact. Judge Lee’s approach is more formal. If Congress meant to require operation, it knew how to say so more directly; the statute’s title and repeated “authorized” phrasing matter, and courts should not infer a duty from a funding statute that looks optional on its face.

## Doctrinal implications

The stakes extend beyond refugees. Many federal social-service programs are written in this in-between register: authorized, appropriated, conditioned, and administered by executive agencies through contracts or cooperative agreements. If the majority’s reading prevails, plaintiffs may have a stronger basis for arguing that once Congress funds a program and uses mandatory language, agencies cannot simply suspend operations by policy choice. That would give courts a more active role in policing executive nonfeasance.

If Judge Lee’s view gains traction, by contrast, agencies may enjoy broader latitude to slow-walk, pause, or effectively disable programs so long as the underlying statute can be characterized as permissive. That would be a significant victory for executive control over implementation, especially in areas where Congress has blended authorization and direction in the same statutory section.

The order also hints at a broader administrative-law point: the government’s uncertainty about whether it could even stand up a replacement system mattered. The majority was not simply reading a statute in the abstract; it was reacting to a practical vacuum. That is often where courts become less willing to accept the government’s “authorization not obligation” framing.

## Bottom line

Judge Lee’s dissent underscores a recurring problem in modern statutory interpretation: Congress frequently writes programs in language that is simultaneously enabling and directive. In *Pacito*, the panel split over how to read that mix. The majority leaned toward enforceable obligation, especially for refugees already admitted and for services Congress appears to have funded. Judge Lee saw discretion all the way down.

However the merits opinion ultimately comes out, this dispute is likely to matter well beyond refugee law. It asks a basic question about the administrative state: when Congress funds compassion, does it merely permit it—or require it?
