Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Updating “Ongoing”: What *Farahi v. FBI* Says About Staleness and FOIA Exemption 7(A)

## Why the case matters

*Farahi v. FBI* is a useful reminder that FOIA litigation moves slowly, but Exemption 7(A) does not freeze time. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion holds that an agency can still withhold records under Exemption 7(A) even after years of litigation, so long as it updates the court with a record showing that the relevant law-enforcement proceeding remains pending or reasonably anticipated. That may sound unremarkable at first glance. But in practice, it matters a great deal: FOIA plaintiffs often file when an investigation appears old or dormant, only to face declarations that were drafted years earlier. *Farahi* shows that courts may require a freshness check, but they may also accept a relatively terse update if it sits atop a more detailed record and the district court has already vetted the underlying claim.

The opinion therefore sits at the intersection of two familiar D.C. Circuit themes: deference to law-enforcement agencies in national-security-related FOIA cases, and the insistence that Exemption 7(A) be tied to a concrete, not hypothetical, enforcement proceeding. The court’s answer here is pragmatic rather than rigid. Agencies do not need to start over every time the calendar advances. But they do need to show that their “ongoing” investigation is still ongoing.

## The staleness problem

The district court saw the problem clearly. The FBI’s first summary-judgment papers were filed in 2019, but the court did not reach the merits until 2022. That lag mattered because Exemption 7(A) is inherently time-sensitive: it protects records only if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” If the proceeding has ended, or if the agency can no longer plausibly say one is expected, the exemption falls away.

So the district court asked for updated declarations. That was the right move. Exemption 7(A) is not a permanent seal; it is keyed to the status of an investigation or prosecution. A court asked to rule years later should not simply assume that conditions remain unchanged.

What makes *Farahi* interesting is how the D.C. Circuit handled the update. The supplemental Bender declaration was not, standing alone, a model of elaboration. The court openly acknowledged that it was “surprisingly terse.” But it refused to treat that terseness as fatal because the declaration did not come into a vacuum. The earlier Hardy declarations had already explained, in both public and ex parte form, the nature and scope of the FBI’s investigation. The district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” carried its burden, and the Bender filing was framed as a confirmation that nothing material had changed.

That sequencing matters. The panel did not say a bare “still ongoing” assertion is always enough. Quite the opposite: it distinguished *CREW*, where a vague assertion of ongoing investigations was insufficient. Instead, the court treated the update as part of a cumulative showing. The lesson is not that agencies can be conclusory; it is that the adequacy of a supplemental declaration depends on the record it supplements.

## How *Farahi* fits with *CREW* and *Juarez*

The court’s analysis is best understood as an application of *CREW* and *Juarez*, not a departure from them. In *CREW*, the D.C. Circuit said Exemption 7(A) requires a “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceeding, and that an ongoing criminal investigation can qualify. In *Juarez*, the court emphasized that if an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and premature release would jeopardize that case, the exemption applies.

*Farahi* reinforces both propositions. The panel had little difficulty concluding that the FBI’s investigation of Farahi fit within the law-enforcement prong, especially given the national-security and terrorism context. More importantly, it treated the agency’s public and sealed declarations as sufficient to show that there was still a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” That phrase is doing work. It signals that the agency must identify an actual investigative process, not just an abstract interest in the subject.

At the same time, the opinion reads *CREW* as requiring enough specificity to avoid boilerplate, but not so much that agencies must expose the very details that Exemption 7(A) is designed to protect. That is the familiar FOIA tension. The government must give a court enough to test the claim, but not enough to compromise the investigation. *Farahi* lands on the side of allowing that balance to be struck with a combination of public and ex parte declarations.

## What agencies must do when years pass

The most practical takeaway from *Farahi* is procedural. When a FOIA case lingers, agencies should expect to refresh their declarations. A declaration that was adequate in 2019 may look stale in 2022 or 2025. But an update need not be a full re-litigation of the exemption. If the agency can show that the same investigation continues and the earlier declarations already described the relevant law-enforcement context with specificity, a supplemental affidavit may suffice.

That said, the opinion also suggests a cautionary note: courts are not obliged to accept a “still ongoing” statement at face value if the surrounding record is thin. The FBI benefited here from several features absent in many FOIA cases: a long-running national-security investigation, a district court that had already scrutinized the exemption, and ex parte material that the panel could rely on without disclosing sensitive details. Agencies whose initial showing is weaker should not assume they can patch in a one-line update years later.

There is also a broader institutional point. The district court did what good FOIA courts often do: it demanded an updated record when time had passed. The D.C. Circuit did not punish that insistence; it endorsed it. That may encourage district judges to keep pressing agencies for current declarations rather than deciding stale records on autopilot. But *Farahi* also reassures agencies that meeting that request does not require them to reveal operational details or produce a mini-trial on the status of an investigation.

## Segregability and ex parte review

The segregability discussion is shorter, but it tracks the same basic logic. Farahi argued that, given the huge volume of responsive records, some nonexempt material must be releasable. The court disagreed, emphasizing that FOIA protects information, not random words and phrases, and that where nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material, wholesale withholding is permissible.

The opinion is also notable for its comfort with ex parte review in this setting. That is not new law, but it is a reminder that in sensitive cases, ex parte declarations remain a central tool for resolving segregability disputes. For plaintiffs, that is a hard pill to swallow. For courts, the alternative may be to force disclosure of the very information the exemption protects. *Farahi* accepts that tradeoff and treats it as an inherent feature of some FOIA disputes.

## Bottom line

*Farahi* is not a doctrinal reset. It is a careful reaffirmation that Exemption 7(A) is dynamic, not static. Agencies cannot rest forever on old declarations, especially when litigation has dragged on. But if they update the court, tie the records to a real and continuing investigation, and provide enough detail—sometimes in camera—to satisfy the judge, the D.C. Circuit will continue to give them substantial room.

For FOIA practitioners, the message is straightforward: in Exemption 7(A) cases, time matters, but context matters too. A stale record can sink an exemption claim. A well-supported update can revive it.



# Deference, National Security, and the FBI File: *Farahi*’s Reinforcement of FOIA Exceptionalism

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Farahi v. FBI* is a reminder that FOIA’s promise of disclosure is often at its weakest when the FBI invokes national security and counterterrorism. The panel had little trouble affirming the withholding of most of Farahi’s FBI file under Exemption 7(A), which protects law-enforcement records whose release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. What makes the opinion notable is not that the FBI won, but how it won: by layering the ordinary deference courts give the Bureau under Exemption 7 onto the even greater solicitude the D.C. Circuit has long afforded in cases touching terrorism and national security.

The result is a fairly high bar for FOIA plaintiffs. Public declarations can be terse if the real detail appears in sealed or ex parte submissions. And once the agency can credibly say that an investigation remains “ongoing,” the court is willing to treat that as enough, especially where the underlying subject matter involves terrorism-related allegations.

## The court’s path through Exemption 7(A)

The statutory framework is familiar. Exemption 7(A) applies to records compiled for law-enforcement purposes if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The D.C. Circuit has long read that to require pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings, but it also has consistently recognized that ongoing criminal investigations qualify.

On the first requirement, the panel had no difficulty concluding that Farahi’s file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes. That conclusion rested not only on the FBI’s public declarations, which described the records as connected to national-security and international-terrorism investigations, but also on the broader context of Farahi’s immigration litigation, where the government had previously alleged ties to terrorist organizations and individuals. The court’s analysis here is unsurprising, but it reinforces the practical reality that once the FBI frames a file as terrorism-related, the law-enforcement nexus is rarely hard to establish.

The more interesting part is the second requirement: interference. The agency said release could tip off targets about the scope of an ongoing investigation. That rationale is standard Exemption 7(A) fare, and the court treated it as enough. Farahi tried to resist by emphasizing the passage of time and arguing that the agency’s later declaration—stating only that the investigation “remain[ed] ongoing”—was too conclusory. The panel largely sidestepped that objection by reading the later declaration in context: the earlier Hardy declaration, plus the sealed submission, had already supplied the needed detail, and the Bender declaration functioned as an update rather than a stand-alone justification.

That contextual move matters. It suggests that a declaration that might look thin in isolation can still carry the day if the court is satisfied, from sealed material or prior submissions, that the agency has told a coherent story about an active investigation.

## Deference on top of deference

*Farahi* is best understood as an intensification of an already deferential line of cases. The D.C. Circuit has long said that the FBI’s Exemption 7 claims deserve deference because the Bureau is a law-enforcement agency. But the opinion goes further by invoking the court’s separate tradition of deference when national security is implicated, citing *Center for National Security Studies* and related cases. That is a powerful combination. It means the agency is not only entitled to some room to define its investigative needs; it also benefits from a judicial instinct to avoid forcing public disclosure in counterterrorism matters.

The practical consequence is that the public-facing justification may become less demanding when sealed submissions fill in the gaps. That is not a formal abandonment of FOIA’s requirement that agencies justify withholdings. But it does mean the adversarial balance shifts. A plaintiff sees only a thin public explanation and has limited ability to test the sealed one. The court, in turn, may be satisfied by a record that is persuasive in the aggregate even if little of that persuasiveness is visible to the requester.

## Segregability gets the same treatment

The opinion’s treatment of segregability is similarly restrained. FOIA requires release of any reasonably segregable nonexempt information, and in theory that duty applies even when Exemption 7(A) covers much of a file. But the panel accepted the FBI’s explanation that meaningful segregation was not feasible because nonexempt material was too intertwined with exempt content. The court’s discussion leans heavily on the idea that FOIA concerns information, not stray words or phrases.

That is doctrinally orthodox, but it is also an area where secrecy compounds itself. Once the agency persuades the court—again, with help from ex parte material—that the file is heavily intertwined with sensitive investigative information, the segregability inquiry risks becoming a formality. *Farahi* does not change the law on this point, but it illustrates how easily a segregability argument can lose force when the court cannot discuss the underlying records publicly.

## The larger takeaway

The broader lesson from *Farahi* is that FOIA exceptionalism in national-security cases remains very much alive in the D.C. Circuit. The court did not relax the doctrinal requirements for Exemption 7(A); instead, it applied them in a way that gives substantial weight to agency assurances, especially where sealed declarations supply the missing context. For agencies, that is a welcome signal that updated, carefully layered declarations can preserve withholdings even after significant delay. For requesters, it is a warning that challenging an FBI file tied to terrorism or counterterrorism will be uphill unless the public record itself reveals a serious flaw.

Whether this is the right balance is a harder question. FOIA depends on judicial scrutiny, but judicial scrutiny is necessarily blunted when the decisive facts are sealed away. *Farahi* does not resolve that tension so much as embody it.



# How Much Can Be Hidden in Secret? Ex Parte Declarations and Segregability After *Farahi*

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Farahi v. FBI* is a reminder that the most important battles in FOIA litigation are often fought in the dark. The court upheld the FBI’s use of Exemption 7(A) to withhold large swaths of Farahi’s file, but the more interesting part of the opinion is procedural: the panel approved the district court’s reliance on sealed, ex parte declarations both to sustain the exemption and to reject the plaintiff’s segregability challenge.

That matters because FOIA is built around disclosure, yet many of its most consequential disputes are resolved on submissions the requester never sees. *Farahi* doesn’t break new doctrinal ground so much as it reinforces a familiar but uneasy point: when disclosure itself could undermine an investigation, courts are willing to accept secret evidence from the government, and they will often do so with considerable deference. The opinion also shows how hard it is for plaintiffs to press segregability objections when they lack access to the very material needed to prove their point.

## The court’s comfort with secrecy

Exemption 7(A) protects law-enforcement records whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The FBI made that case here with a public declaration, a sealed supplemental declaration, and later an updated declaration confirming that the investigation remained ongoing. Judge Katsas’s opinion treats that package as enough, and the court is explicit that ex parte material can play a legitimate role in that analysis.

That is hardly surprising in D.C. Circuit FOIA law. The court has long accepted that agencies may need to submit classified or sensitive details ex parte to avoid disclosing the very information they seek to protect. *Farahi* fits comfortably within that tradition. What is notable is how the panel handled the plaintiff’s argument that the later Bender declaration was too conclusory, given the passage of time. The court’s answer was essentially contextual: the update was not meant to establish everything from scratch, but to confirm that the earlier, more detailed showing was still current. In other words, the ex parte record did real work, and the public declaration only makes sense when read alongside it.

That approach is doctrinally orthodox, but it also illustrates the asymmetry embedded in FOIA litigation. The agency can rely on confidential detail to demonstrate the existence and scope of an investigation; the plaintiff is left to attack the showing only at a high level, often without knowing what the key facts are. The court does not pretend this is ideal. It treats it as the price of secrecy.

## Segregability gets the same treatment

The segregability holding may be even more significant. FOIA requires disclosure of any reasonably segregable nonexempt portion of a record. That obligation is supposed to prevent agencies from withholding entire documents just because some part is exempt. In practice, however, segregability often becomes a judgment call about whether the remaining material would convey anything meaningful once the sensitive portions are removed.

Here, the district court reviewed Bender’s supplemental ex parte declaration and concluded that the FBI had adequately explained why no reasonably segregable material remained. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, emphasizing that isolated words or phrases are not enough if they are “randomly interspersed” and do not amount to meaningful information. The opinion leans on *Mead Data* for the proposition that FOIA protects information, not words.

That is a sensible statement of the law, but it leaves a lot of room for agency discretion. Once a court accepts the premise that the exempt portions are thoroughly intertwined with the rest, segregability can become almost collapsible into the merits of the exemption itself. If the file is deemed sensitive enough, then even visible fragments may be treated as worthless. *Farahi* shows how that can happen without any public explanation at all, so long as the court is satisfied in camera.

## The importance of not objecting

The procedural wrinkle that may matter most for future litigants is Farahi’s failure to oppose the FBI’s request to file the supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. The court uses that omission to blunt his appellate argument that the district court leaned too heavily on sealed material.

That is a cautionary tale. FOIA plaintiffs often want to preserve maximal access to the public record while still resisting agency secrecy, but the time to object is early and explicitly. If a requester does not challenge ex parte procedure in the district court, the D.C. Circuit is unlikely to be sympathetic later. The panel’s reasoning suggests that silence can be treated not merely as a strategic choice but as a forfeiture of procedural complaint.

That does not mean an objection would necessarily succeed. But it matters a great deal to preserve the issue. In a case like this, where the court is already inclined to defer to the FBI’s law-enforcement judgments, a plaintiff cannot afford to give away the procedural argument too.

## The broader tension

The deeper tension in *Farahi* is familiar: FOIA’s transparency mission depends on judicial review, but judicial review sometimes depends on secrecy. Ex parte declarations are a practical solution to a real problem. If the government had to reveal operational details of an ongoing investigation just to justify withholding the records that would compromise it, Exemption 7(A) would be self-defeating.

Still, *Farahi* is a useful reminder that secret judicial review is not costless. It limits adversarial testing, reduces the plaintiff’s ability to challenge overbreadth, and makes segregability determinations especially opaque. The doctrine survives because courts trust agency affidavits and because the alternative may be to disclose sensitive law-enforcement material. But the price of that compromise is that a lot of FOIA law gets made in chambers rather than in the open.

In that sense, *Farahi* is not a dramatic departure from settled doctrine. It is a polished reaffirmation of how that doctrine works in practice: when the FBI can show an ongoing investigation, a court may accept sealed evidence, defer to agency expertise, and conclude that even the remaining scraps are not reasonably segregable. For FOIA litigants, the lesson is simple but sobering: if you want to challenge secrecy, you need to object early, press segregability hard, and recognize that the most important record may be the one you never get to see.



# Category-by-Category Withholding and the Cost of Underdeveloped Appellate Arguments

## Why this part of the case matters

The headline holding in *Farahi v. FBI* is familiar enough: Exemption 7(A) can shield FBI records tied to ongoing or reasonably anticipated law-enforcement proceedings. But the more interesting piece, for FOIA lawyers and repeat litigants alike, is the panel’s brief treatment of the FBI’s categorical withholding approach. The D.C. Circuit reaffirmed that agencies cannot hide behind overly broad buckets of documents; categories must be specific enough for a court to test whether the claimed exemption actually fits. Yet the court declined to probe that issue further because Farahi never concretely explained why the FBI’s categories were deficient. That may sound like a modest procedural point. In practice, it is a reminder that modern FOIA litigation is increasingly won or lost not just on the substantive scope of an exemption, but on how precisely the requester preserves and develops objections to the agency’s Vaughn-style showing.

## The court’s doctrinal baseline

The panel restated a settled but important principle: when an agency withholds records on a category-by-category basis, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to let a court determine whether the exemption applies to each one. That requirement matters because FOIA does not permit agencies to defeat judicial review by lumping together disparate documents and asserting a single, generalized rationale.

At the same time, the court did not suggest that categorical withholding is disfavored per se. To the contrary, the D.C. Circuit has long accepted that agencies may describe and justify withholdings by category, especially where the volume of records is large and line-by-line description would itself reveal exempt information. The real question is whether the categories are sharp enough to support meaningful review.

## Why Farahi lost the point

What is striking here is not that the court accepted the FBI’s categories, but that it essentially refused to entertain the challenge because Farahi did not do the work. The opinion says he offered no specific explanation for why the categories were overbroad, and therefore forfeited the argument.

That is a familiar move in appellate practice, but it carries real force in FOIA cases. Requesters often object in broad terms — that the agency’s categories are “generic,” “vague,” or “overinclusive” — without explaining how a narrower category could have been drawn or why the agency’s grouping obscures exemption-specific differences among records. The D.C. Circuit is signaling that this sort of abstract objection will not be enough. If the requester wants a court to second-guess the agency’s taxonomy, the requester has to identify what is wrong with the taxonomy and why it matters to the exemption analysis.

That is a demanding standard, especially where the agency’s detail comes partly in ex parte or sealed declarations. But it is also consistent with how FOIA cases are actually litigated: the district court is not expected to redo the agency’s record review from scratch, and the burden shifts meaningfully only when the requester gives the court a concrete reason to doubt the categorization.

## The deeper procedural lesson

The practical lesson of *Farahi* is that FOIA doctrine has become highly process-sensitive. Courts remain willing to police categorical withholding, segregability, and the adequacy of agency declarations. But they will not do that work in the abstract. A requester must identify the defect with enough precision to make it litigable.

That has downstream effects. First, it rewards careful issue framing at the district court level. If a requester thinks the categories are too broad, the objection should not stop at “overinclusive.” Counsel needs to explain whether the defect is that the category mixes documents with different exemption statuses, collapses temporally distinct records, or describes files at a level too general to test the agency’s assertion.

Second, it highlights the importance of the Vaughn process itself. Agencies increasingly rely on category-based descriptions because they are administratively efficient and, in sensitive cases, reduce the risk of disclosure through description. *Farahi* suggests that unless the requester can expose the weakness in that category structure, appellate courts may simply accept it as a practical necessity.

Third, the opinion reinforces a recurring asymmetry in FOIA litigation: agencies can often satisfy their burden with declarations that are broad but contextually plausible, while requesters need to make targeted, record-based attacks to force deeper scrutiny. That asymmetry is especially pronounced where the underlying records concern national security or counterterrorism, as they did here.

## Segregability in the background

The same dynamic appears in the court’s segregability analysis. The panel accepted the FBI’s position that there was no meaningful nonexempt material to release, despite the large volume of responsive records. The court emphasized that FOIA protects information, not isolated words or phrases, and it rejected the notion that the mere size of the file implies that something must be releasable.

That part of the opinion matters because it shows how closely intertwined segregability is with the agency’s categorical presentation. If the categories are accepted, and if the declaration explains why disclosure within those categories would compromise the investigation, then segregability challenges become much harder to press unless the requester can point to a specific, releasable kind of information.

## Bottom line

*Farahi* is not a groundbreaking FOIA opinion, but it is a useful one. The panel reaffirmed the principle that agencies must define withholding categories with enough precision to permit review. But it also made clear that courts will not rescue a requester who attacks those categories only in generalities. In FOIA litigation, precision is now a two-way street: agencies must be specific, but so must challengers. The cost of underdeveloped appellate arguments is not just forfeiture of a point; it is often the loss of the only path to meaningful judicial scrutiny of how the agency has organized its exemption claims.
