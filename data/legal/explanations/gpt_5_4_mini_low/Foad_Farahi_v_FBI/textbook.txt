Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# 1. The FOIA Request, the FBI File, and the Litigation’s Procedural Path

## Farahi’s background and the immigration context

This case begins with Foad Farahi’s long-running immigration dispute with the federal government. Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings based on a visa overstay. Over the years, Farahi sought several forms of relief from removal, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.

Those immigration proceedings matter because they explain why Farahi’s FBI file was so important and why the government treated it as sensitive. In opposing relief, DHS relied on documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who said Farahi had ties to “high-level terrorists.” The opinion does not resolve the merits of those allegations, but it makes clear that terrorism-related assertions had already become part of the record in Farahi’s immigration case. That background helps explain why a request for his FBI file raised law-enforcement and national-security concerns from the government’s perspective.

## The FOIA request and the size of the FBI’s response

Farahi submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act for his FBI file. FOIA generally requires federal agencies to disclose records to the public upon request, unless an exemption applies. Here, the FBI located a very large set of potentially responsive materials: about 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. That volume is important because it shows the request was not for a single document or a narrow file fragment, but for a substantial law-enforcement record system.

The FBI did not produce all of those materials right away. Farahi sued after the agency missed FOIA’s statutory response deadline. That is a common FOIA posture: once the deadline passes without a final agency response, the requester may sue to force disclosure. The filing of the lawsuit shifted the dispute from the administrative process to federal court, where the agency had to justify any withholding under a statutory exemption.

## The FBI’s staged review and partial disclosure

After the lawsuit was filed, the FBI undertook a lengthy review of the responsive records. Over the next several years, the agency reviewed the records, disclosed some of them to Farahi, and withheld most of the rest. The opinion does not suggest that the FBI refused to process the request; instead, it portrays a staged production in which the agency continued to examine a very large file and release only the portions it deemed disclosable.

This kind of gradual production is often seen in FOIA litigation, especially where the records may contain exempt law-enforcement material mixed with nonexempt information. The core question then becomes whether the agency can justify withholding under a FOIA exemption and whether it has released everything reasonably segregable from the exempt material.

## The first summary-judgment motion and the Hardy declarations

Once the FBI finished its review, it moved for summary judgment in October 2019. In FOIA cases, summary judgment is the usual vehicle for resolving whether an agency properly withheld records. The agency supported its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section, often referred to as RIDS. The Hardy declaration asserted, among other things, that the withheld records had been compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that releasing them could interfere with enforcement proceedings that were then pending or reasonably anticipated.

That declaration was central because FOIA cases often turn on agency affidavits. The declarations are meant to explain what was withheld, why it fits within an exemption, and why nonexempt portions could not be segregated and released. The FBI also sought leave to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. “Ex parte” means the submission was made to the court without being shown to Farahi. Courts permit this in FOIA cases when disclosing the declaration itself would reveal the very information the agency is trying to protect. Farahi did not oppose the request, and the district court allowed the sealed, ex parte filing.

## The district court’s first look and its concern about stale evidence

The district court did not immediately rule for either side. In November 2022, it addressed the FBI’s summary-judgment motion and concluded that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes. It also said the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden to show that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

But the court was concerned about timing. A significant amount of time had passed since the FBI had submitted the original declarations, and the court wanted to know whether the relevant enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated as of the time of its decision. That concern reflects a practical point in FOIA litigation: an agency’s declaration can become stale if circumstances change while the case is pending. The court also wanted more information on segregability, meaning whether any nonexempt material could be separated from exempt material and released.

For those reasons, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice. That phrase means the denial was not on the merits in a way that barred the FBI from trying again. Instead, it left the door open for the agency to supplement the record and renew its request for judgment.

## The updated Bender declarations

The FBI responded by submitting updated declarations and renewing its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency relied on Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration.

In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi’s file, and that agent confirmed the investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration supplied more detail about the investigation and about why the records could not be segregated in a way that would permit meaningful disclosure. In other words, the FBI used the renewed motion to address exactly the concerns the district court had identified: whether the law-enforcement proceedings were still alive and whether any releasable information could be separated out.

## The final district court ruling and the appeal

The district court granted the renewed motion. It credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant proceedings were ongoing and accepted the supplemental explanation that the protected material was not reasonably segregable. That ruling resolved the case in the FBI’s favor at the trial level.

Farahi then appealed to the D.C. Circuit. The appellate opinion that follows reviews the district court’s grant of summary judgment, focusing on whether the FBI properly invoked FOIA Exemption 7(A) and whether it adequately addressed segregability. But the procedural path matters because it explains why the appellate record included both public and ex parte declarations, why the district court asked for updated evidence, and why the court ultimately ruled only after the agency refreshed its showing.

# 2. The Governing FOIA Framework: Exemption 7(A), Segregability, and Standards of Review

## FOIA’s baseline rule of disclosure and the agency’s burden to justify withholding

The starting point in any FOIA case is the statute’s broad disclosure command. FOIA requires federal agencies to release records to a requester unless a specific statutory exemption applies. That means the default rule is disclosure, not secrecy. But once an agency decides to withhold records, the burden shifts to the government to justify that withholding under one of FOIA’s enumerated exemptions.

This allocation of burdens matters throughout the Farahi opinion. The FBI could not simply say the records were sensitive or part of a law-enforcement file. It had to show, with record evidence, that the particular exemption it invoked actually covered the withheld material. The court’s analysis therefore turns on whether the FBI satisfied the elements of Exemption 7(A), and whether it adequately addressed segregability.

## The text of Exemption 7(A): records compiled for law enforcement purposes whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings

Exemption 7(A) is one of FOIA’s law-enforcement exemptions. It protects records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The opinion treats this as a two-part inquiry. First, the agency must show that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes. Second, it must show that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.

The court also uses its prior cases to clarify that Exemption 7(A) is not triggered by any law-enforcement record in the abstract. The exemption is tied to a concrete enforcement setting. The records must relate to proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated. That gloss comes from earlier D.C. Circuit precedent and is essential to understanding why the court scrutinized the FBI’s declarations here.

## The D.C. Circuit rule that enforcement proceedings must be pending or reasonably anticipated

Under D.C. Circuit law, Exemption 7(A) applies only if the relevant enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” This rule prevents the government from using Exemption 7(A) to withhold records based on a vague possibility of some future enforcement action. There has to be an identifiable proceeding or at least a concrete prospective one.

In Farahi, this requirement became central because the district court had initially been concerned that the FBI’s declarations were stale. By the time the court considered the renewed motion, several years had passed since the FBI first claimed that disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings. The court therefore needed updated information showing that the relevant proceedings were still ongoing or otherwise reasonably anticipated.

## Why ongoing criminal investigations qualify as enforcement proceedings

The opinion makes clear that “enforcement proceedings” are not limited to filed indictments or formal adjudications. Ongoing criminal investigations count too, so long as they are part of the process of gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case. In that setting, disclosure can interfere by tipping off the targets, revealing the scope of the investigation, or exposing investigative methods and leads.

That point is important because the FBI’s declarations focused on an ongoing investigation rather than a completed prosecution. The court accepted that posture as sufficient under Exemption 7(A). Once the agency showed that the investigation was continuing and that disclosure could undermine it, the exemption could apply even though no criminal case had yet been filed.

## The agency’s segregability duty under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b)

FOIA does not allow agencies to withhold an entire record just because some portion of it is exempt. Agencies must take reasonable steps to segregate and release any nonexempt information. The statute states both that agencies must take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt material and that “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion” of a record must be disclosed after deletion of the exempt portions.

This duty is separate from the exemption analysis itself. Even if a record falls within Exemption 7(A), the agency still has to consider whether any portion of the record can be reasonably separated and released. In Farahi, the district court required the FBI to explain not only why the records were exempt, but also why no meaningful nonexempt material could be released separately. The court ultimately accepted the FBI’s segregability showing, including its ex parte submission.

## Summary judgment in FOIA cases and de novo appellate review

FOIA cases are commonly resolved on summary judgment because the dispute usually turns on the adequacy of agency declarations rather than live factfinding. On appeal, the court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo. That means the appellate court gives no deference to the district court’s legal conclusions and independently assesses whether the government met its burden.

Even with de novo review, however, the agency’s declarations play a central role. Because FOIA disputes often involve classified or sensitive material, courts frequently decide them on the basis of sworn declarations, Vaughn-style explanations, and, where necessary, ex parte submissions. The D.C. Circuit in this case evaluated whether the declarations provided enough detail to support Exemption 7(A) and segregability.

## The role of agency declarations in carrying the government’s burden

Agency declarations are the main evidentiary vehicle in FOIA litigation. They must be sufficiently detailed and specific to permit judicial review, but they need not disclose the very information the agency claims is exempt. In a case like this one, the declarations serve two functions: they explain why the exemption applies, and they show why the material cannot be meaningfully segregated.

The opinion demonstrates how the court tests those declarations. It looks for a rational connection between the records and a law-enforcement purpose, an explanation of how disclosure could interfere with proceedings, and enough detail to assess segregability. When the district court thought the original declarations had become stale, it required updated declarations before ruling. That reflects how important the declaration record is in FOIA cases.

## Deference to the FBI as a law-enforcement agency

The opinion also emphasizes that the FBI receives a measure of deference when it invokes Exemption 7 because it is a law-enforcement agency. That deference does not eliminate judicial review, and the FBI still must satisfy the statutory requirements. But the court recognizes that the FBI has institutional expertise in identifying law-enforcement risks and explaining why disclosure might interfere with investigations.

In Farahi, the court found that deference especially apt because the records concerned national security and terrorism-related investigations. The opinion thus treats the FBI’s law-enforcement judgment as entitled to respect, while still requiring enough factual support to show that the exemption fits the records at issue.

## Heightened judicial deference where national security and counterterrorism concerns are implicated

A notable feature of the opinion is its emphasis on national security. The court states that it has consistently deferred to the executive in FOIA cases where national security concerns are implicated, including Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigations. That context affects how the court reads the FBI’s declarations and why it was willing to accept updated, partly ex parte submissions.

This does not mean the court abandons scrutiny. Instead, it means that in areas involving terrorism, intelligence, or other sensitive security matters, the court is more willing to credit the agency’s judgment that disclosure could harm ongoing investigations. That background helps explain why the court was satisfied that the FBI’s public and sealed declarations established the required connection between the records, the investigation, and the risk of interference.

## The rule permitting categorical withholding if categories are sufficiently distinct

FOIA litigation often proceeds record category by record category rather than line-by-line document by document. The agency may group documents into categories and explain why each category is exempt. But those categories must be sufficiently distinct to let the court assess whether the claimed exemption properly applies to each one.

Farahi briefly argued that the FBI had not adequately justified how it defined its categories. The court rejected that argument largely because Farahi did not identify a concrete problem with the categories used. The lesson for a law student is that categorical withholding is permissible, but only if the agency’s groupings are coherent enough to support judicial review.

## The principle that records may be withheld in full when nonexempt information is inextricably intertwined with exempt material

Finally, the opinion explains why the FBI could withhold records in full. If exempt and nonexempt material are inextricably intertwined, and the nonexempt portions cannot reasonably be separated out, the agency may withhold the entire record. The touchstone is not whether the record contains some nonexempt words or phrases, but whether any meaningful segregable information can be released without undermining the exemption.

That principle is what resolved Farahi’s segregability challenge. The court accepted the district court’s conclusion, based on the declarations, that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable. The fact that isolated words or phrases might be removed did not require disclosure if the remaining material would still reveal protected law-enforcement information or if the exempt and nonexempt material were too intertwined to separate in any meaningful way.

Taken together, these doctrines explain the court’s ultimate holding. FOIA begins with a presumption of disclosure, but Exemption 7(A) allows withholding when the FBI shows that records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes, that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings, and that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material can be released. The court concluded that the FBI met that burden here.

# 3. Why the File Was “Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes”

## The first statutory requirement under Exemption 7(A)

Exemption 7(A) does not protect every FBI record automatically. It protects only records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. In this part of the opinion, the court addresses only the first of those two requirements. The question is whether the FBI file Farahi requested was gathered in the course of a law-enforcement investigation, as opposed to some other administrative or informational activity.

That requirement matters because FOIA exemptions are construed against withholding, and the government bears the burden of proving that an exemption applies. So the FBI could not simply say that the file involved a sensitive person or a difficult case. It had to show that the records were created or collected for a law-enforcement purpose.

## The Pratt/Campbell nexus test for law-enforcement purposes

Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, the court does not demand the same level of explanation it might require from a non-law-enforcement agency. Even so, the agency must still establish a rational connection between the records and a law-enforcement mission. The opinion relies on the familiar Pratt/Campbell formulation: the declarations must show a nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law-enforcement duties, and a connection between the individual or incident at issue and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.

This is a functional test. The court is asking whether the records were generated as part of an investigation into possible legal violations, not whether the investigation resulted in charges or conviction. That distinction is important in FOIA cases because Exemption 7 is often invoked before an investigation has ripened into an enforcement action. Here, the court treated the FBI’s showing as enough because the file related to investigations of national security and international terrorism, both classic law-enforcement concerns for the FBI.

## How deference operates when the FBI asserts a law-enforcement justification

The opinion repeatedly notes that the FBI receives a “fair measure of deference” on this issue. That does not mean the court rubber-stamps the agency’s claim. It means that when the FBI, as a specialized law-enforcement agency, explains that records were compiled in connection with an investigation, courts are more willing to credit the agency’s assessment than they would be with an agency whose ordinary business is unrelated to criminal or security enforcement.

The deference is especially pronounced where national security is involved. The court cites prior D.C. Circuit precedent emphasizing executive expertise in FOIA cases touching counterterrorism and similar matters. In practice, this deference makes the agency’s declarations particularly important: if they are specific and credible, they can carry the government’s burden without the court needing more.

## The Hardy public declaration’s statement about national security and international terrorism

The public Hardy declaration did most of the work on this element. It stated that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and that those investigations included pending law-enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals involved in such violations. That language is exactly the sort of explanation that fits the Pratt/Campbell nexus requirement.

The court treated this as more than a bare conclusion. The declaration identified the type of investigation, the subject matter of the suspected violations, and the law-enforcement purpose of the file. That was enough to show that the records were not gathered casually or for some administrative reason; they were compiled for investigative use in a law-enforcement setting.

## The supporting role of the ex parte Hardy declaration

The public declaration was reinforced by an ex parte Hardy declaration. The court could not describe that sealed submission in full, but it says enough to show its function: it supplied “considerable detail” about the nature of the investigation. In FOIA litigation, ex parte declarations are sometimes used when public explanation would itself reveal sensitive material that the agency seeks to protect.

Here, the ex parte submission did not change the legal standard. It simply helped the court confirm the FBI’s public assertions. The court’s point is that the public declaration was already adequate, and the sealed declaration made the showing even stronger. For a student reading the opinion, the key lesson is that ex parte material can support a FOIA exemption when public disclosure would undercut the very interest the exemption protects.

## Farahi’s concessions in district court about the nature of the FBI investigation

Farahi’s own litigation position also undercut any argument that the file lacked a law-enforcement character. In the district court, he conceded that the FBI had investigated him for ties to organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations and for ties to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Those concessions matter because they confirm that the FBI’s file was not about some vague or purely speculative concern.

The opinion uses Farahi’s concessions as corroboration. They do not independently establish the exemption, but they support the reasonableness of the FBI’s explanation. When the requester’s own filings acknowledge the existence of terrorism-related investigations, it becomes much harder to argue that the file was not compiled for law-enforcement purposes.

## The significance of the immigration-record context in corroborating the FBI’s explanation

The opinion also situates the dispute in Farahi’s immigration proceedings. DHS had placed him in removal proceedings, and the government opposed relief by presenting FBI-related evidence about his alleged connections to terrorists. That background helps explain why the FBI file existed and why it would naturally contain investigative materials tied to law enforcement and national security.

This context is not the legal test itself, but it makes the FBI’s explanation more plausible. The court is not deciding whether the allegations about Farahi were true; it is deciding whether the FBI’s records were compiled in the course of a law-enforcement investigation. The immigration history shows that the FBI’s file was part of a broader governmental effort centered on security-sensitive allegations, not merely routine recordkeeping.

## Why the court had no difficulty concluding the file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes

Putting the pieces together, the court had little trouble concluding that the first Exemption 7(A) requirement was satisfied. The FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its declarations described national-security and terrorism investigations, the sealed declaration filled in additional details, and Farahi’s own submissions confirmed that the FBI had investigated him for alleged terrorist connections. On that record, the court said it had “no doubt” that the file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes.

That conclusion is important because it narrows the rest of the appeal. Once the court accepts that the file falls within the law-enforcement category, the real dispute shifts to the second Exemption 7(A) requirement: whether disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The segregability issue also remains, because even exempt records must be disclosed to the extent nonexempt material can be reasonably separated out. So this first element does not end the case, but it clears the threshold question and frames the remaining analysis.

# 4. Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Enforcement Proceedings

## The second Exemption 7(A) requirement: likely interference with enforcement proceedings

The key dispute in this part of the opinion is not whether the FBI’s records were gathered for law-enforcement purposes, but whether releasing them would interfere with enforcement proceedings that were still live. FOIA Exemption 7(A) does not protect law-enforcement records automatically. The agency must also show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” In this circuit, that means the proceedings must be pending or reasonably anticipated. The court’s task here was to decide whether the FBI’s declarations, read in context, adequately established that requirement.

The opinion makes clear that the government can satisfy this burden with affidavits or declarations describing an ongoing investigation or prosecution, but those submissions must do more than recite conclusions in the abstract. The question is whether the declarations give the court enough factual basis to understand that disclosure would genuinely risk interfering with a concrete enforcement effort.

## The accepted theory of interference: tipping off targets about the scope of an investigation

The court accepted a familiar Exemption 7(A) theory: disclosure can interfere with an investigation by tipping off its targets about its scope, direction, or evidence. That can allow subjects to hide evidence, coordinate stories, intimidate witnesses, or otherwise frustrate law-enforcement efforts. The FBI’s public declaration said that release of information from an ongoing investigation could reveal what the agency was looking at and thereby interfere with the investigation. The court treated that as a widely accepted and sufficient theory of harm under Exemption 7(A).

This matters because the FBI did not need to prove a specific, already-occurring obstruction. It was enough to show that disclosure of the records could reasonably be expected to interfere with a live investigation. In Exemption 7(A) cases, that forward-looking prediction is central.

## The Hardy declaration’s assertion that the investigation was ongoing and disclosure would interfere

The FBI’s initial public showing came through the Hardy declaration. Hardy stated that the withheld records had been compiled in furtherance of investigations into national-security and international-terrorism violations, including pending law-enforcement proceedings and Farahi’s associations with individuals involved in those violations. Hardy also stated that disclosure could interfere with the ongoing investigative process.

For the court, that declaration did two important things. First, it connected the records to law-enforcement activity. Second, it identified a concrete risk from disclosure: the records would expose the scope and direction of the investigation. The court emphasized that the agency had not merely asserted that the records were sensitive; it had tied the sensitivity to the existence of a real, ongoing enforcement effort.

## The ex parte Hardy declaration’s role in elaborating on the nature and scope of the investigation

The public Hardy declaration did not tell the whole story. The FBI also submitted an ex parte, sealed supplement. Because that filing was not disclosed to Farahi, the court could use it in camera to evaluate the FBI’s claims without revealing sensitive law-enforcement information.

The opinion treats that sealed declaration as important context. While it cannot quote the details, the court says the ex parte submission supplied “considerable detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation. That detail mattered because it helped the court assess whether the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7(A) was grounded in a real investigation rather than a generic law-enforcement claim. In other words, the sealed declaration strengthened the public declaration by giving the court confidence that the public statements were not overstated or conclusory.

## The district court’s concern that time had passed and its request for an updated status assessment

The district court did not immediately grant summary judgment. By the time it first considered the FBI’s motion, several years had passed since the agency’s initial declarations. That passage of time raised a natural concern: even if proceedings were pending in 2019, were they still pending in 2022?

So the district court asked the FBI to update its account of whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or reasonably anticipated. This was a practical move. Exemption 7(A) depends on present or reasonably imminent proceedings, so a stale declaration can become less persuasive if circumstances may have changed. The district court also wanted more information on segregability, but the time problem was central to the 7(A) analysis.

## The Bender declaration as an update confirming the investigation remained ongoing in 2023

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The public Bender declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for Farahi’s file, and that agent confirmed the investigation was still ongoing. The sealed Bender declaration supplied more detail, again outside the public record.

The court treated Bender’s declaration as an update, not as a standalone replacement for the Hardy materials. That distinction was important. The question was not whether Bender’s few public lines could, by themselves, prove everything. Rather, the question was whether the updated submission, viewed against the earlier declarations and the district court’s prior ruling, confirmed that the investigation had not gone stale. The court concluded it did.

## Farahi’s reliance on CREW to argue that vague claims of ongoing investigations are insufficient

Farahi argued that the FBI’s proof was too thin, relying on the D.C. Circuit’s statement in CREW that vague assertions of ongoing investigations may not be enough. That is a real limit on Exemption 7(A): an agency cannot simply say “the investigation is ongoing” and expect automatic deference if the claim is unsupported or indeterminate.

The court did not reject that principle. Instead, it held that the principle did not help Farahi on these facts. The problem with Farahi’s argument was that it treated Bender’s declaration in isolation, as if the case turned only on those few public words. The court said that approach ignored the broader record.

## Why the D.C. Circuit refused to read the Bender declaration in isolation

The court’s main response was contextual. Bender’s declaration did not come out of nowhere. It was submitted after the district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden on the merits, but wanted an updated status check because of the time elapsed. And Bender’s declaration expressly functioned as that update.

So the court read Bender alongside the earlier Hardy declarations, which had already given a public explanation that the records were compiled for active national-security and terrorism-related investigations, plus the ex parte detail that supported those assertions. In that setting, Bender’s statement that the investigation remained ongoing was not a naked conclusory assertion; it was a confirmation that the earlier showing was still current.

This is the key methodological point in the opinion. A declaration that may seem too brief if viewed alone can be sufficient when it is supplementing a more detailed earlier showing and when the court has already seen enough to understand the underlying investigation.

## The significance of the district court’s earlier finding that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden

The district court’s earlier description of the FBI’s showing as “seemingly” sufficient also mattered. That meant the court had already been persuaded, at least preliminarily, that the FBI’s declarations established the right kind of law-enforcement context. The remaining issue was whether that context had become stale.

The D.C. Circuit treated the Bender declaration as answering that narrower question. It was not the first and only evidence of an ongoing investigation; it was a refreshed confirmation after the court asked for one. That procedural posture made Farahi’s “vagueness” objection less persuasive.

## How public allegations from Farahi’s immigration proceedings made continued investigation plausible

The court also relied on broader public context to show that the FBI’s claims of ongoing investigation were plausible. The opinion refers to public allegations from Farahi’s immigration proceedings that he had ties to terrorist organizations and individuals, including claims that he recruited for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, was connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other Al Qaeda members, and supported charities alleged to have financed terrorism.

The court did not rely on those allegations as independent findings of fact in this case. Rather, it used them as context confirming that the FBI’s investigation was serious and far-reaching enough that it could realistically remain open over time. Because the underlying subject matter involved national security and terrorism, the court was less skeptical of the agency’s updated claim that the investigation was still active.

## The court’s conclusion that the FBI showed a concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding still existed

Putting the public Hardy declaration, the sealed Hardy submission, the Bender update, and the surrounding context together, the court concluded that the FBI had shown a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” remained in existence. That phrase captures the core Exemption 7(A) requirement as the court understood it: there must be a real enforcement effort, not an abstract possibility.

The court was satisfied that the agency had met that burden here. The investigation was not only serious; it had continuing operational significance. Disclosure could reasonably be expected to reveal investigative scope and interfere with the agency’s work. That was enough to sustain the exemption.

## The rejection of Farahi’s underdeveloped challenge to the FBI’s document categories as forfeited

Farahi also tried to argue that the FBI had not adequately justified the categories of documents it used to explain its withholding decisions. In FOIA cases, agencies sometimes withhold records by category rather than document-by-document, but those categories must be specific enough for the court to assess whether the exemption applies.

The court declined to consider this argument because Farahi did not develop it. He did not identify which categories were overbroad or explain why the classification failed. Under ordinary appellate principles, an undeveloped argument is forfeited. So although this objection briefly appears in the opinion, it does not alter the court’s Exemption 7(A) analysis.

# 5. Segregability, Ex Parte Review, and the Case’s Final Disposition

## Segregability remains a separate FOIA obligation

The court’s final stage of analysis reminds students that winning an exemption is not the end of a FOIA case. Even when an agency establishes that a record falls within an exemption, FOIA still requires the agency to release any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt material. That duty appears in both the statute’s general disclosure command and in the specific segregation language requiring release of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion” after exempt portions are deleted. In other words, the government cannot withhold an entire record just because some part of it is exempt, unless the exempt and nonexempt information are so closely connected that segregation would not be meaningful.

Here, that issue became especially important because the FBI was withholding a large file. Farahi argued that with so many pages and CDs, there must be something releasable. The district court did not accept that assumption. Instead, it asked the FBI to give a more detailed explanation of why segregability was not possible, because the earlier record left the court with unanswered questions.

## Why the district court asked for more before ruling

The district judge initially denied summary judgment without prejudice, not because the FBI had necessarily lost on the merits, but because the court wanted a better updated record and more information on segregation. That procedural move matters. It shows that courts do not simply accept an agency’s assertion that nothing can be segregated. They may require the agency to explain, with enough specificity, why disclosure of isolated material would not leave meaningful nonexempt information.

The court also had concerns about age. Several years had passed since the FBI’s original declarations, and the judge wanted to know whether the enforcement proceedings that justified Exemption 7(A) were still pending or reasonably anticipated. But on the segregability issue, the court’s request was narrower: it needed a fuller explanation of why the exempt material could not be separated from the rest in a way that would produce useful disclosure.

## Bender’s supplemental ex parte declaration

The FBI responded with updated submissions, including a new declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. This declaration was crucial because it addressed both the staleness concern and the segregability concern. Bender’s public declaration confirmed that the relevant investigations remained ongoing, but his ex parte declaration contained additional details the court could consider privately.

That ex parte material mattered for segregability because the FBI explained, in more detail than it could safely place on the public record, why the withheld records were not reasonably divisible into releasable and nonreleasable parts. The district court reviewed that private submission and concluded that the FBI had met its burden. The appellate court agreed, emphasizing that the district court had received a “detailed justification” for the FBI’s segregability position.

## Why isolated words or snippets are not enough

Farahi argued that some information must be releasable simply because the volume of withheld material was so large. The court rejected that reasoning. FOIA does not require disclosure of every individual word or fragment when the fragments do not amount to meaningful information. The relevant question is not whether a document contains some nonexempt words, but whether those words can be separated and disclosed in a way that leaves a reasonably useful nonexempt portion.

The court relied on older FOIA precedent explaining that if nonexempt material is scattered throughout a document line by line, it may not be reasonably segregable. This is the key practical point: segregation is not required when the nonexempt material is so intertwined with exempt material that extraction would leave only disconnected scraps. The court invoked Mead Data for the proposition that FOIA focuses on “information,” not merely on “words” written on a page. That distinction allowed the court to reject Farahi’s argument that the presence of some releasable content in the file necessarily meant the FBI had to disclose it.

## The Mead Data principle and “meaningful” disclosure

The Mead Data principle is central to understanding the court’s reasoning. It teaches that segregability is not a mechanical exercise in redaction. A court asks whether the nonexempt material would convey meaningful information after redaction, or whether it would just leave behind isolated terms, phrases, or snippets that do not provide real informational value.

That is why the court could say that individual words or phrases might technically survive redaction but still not be “reasonably segregable.” If the remaining material would be incomprehensible, misleading, or trivial, the agency is not required to produce it. This is especially true where the withheld records are part of a counterterrorism or national security investigation, and where even fragments could reveal sensitive investigative structure, targets, or methods.

## Why the court accepted ex parte review

Farahi also challenged the court’s reliance on the FBI’s ex parte declaration. The appellate court rejected that challenge as well. In FOIA litigation, ex parte submissions are sometimes necessary when the very facts needed to justify withholding cannot be disclosed without revealing the protected information itself. If the agency had to publicly explain every detail of why material was inseparable, that explanation might itself disclose the contents or structure of the sensitive file.

The court described that problem as inherent in certain FOIA cases. Here, the ex parte procedure was permissible because it allowed the court to evaluate segregability without forcing the FBI to reveal the very material it claimed was exempt. The opinion also notes a procedural point: Farahi had not opposed the earlier motion to use ex parte declarations, so he could not later complain that the district court relied on them. That is a reminder that objections must often be made at the district court stage to be preserved.

## The appellate court’s conclusion on segregability

After reviewing the record, the D.C. Circuit agreed with the district court that the FBI had satisfied its segregability burden. The court did not require the agency to prove that every single word in the file was sensitive. Instead, it accepted the FBI’s explanation that no meaningful information could be separated from the exempt material without revealing protected law-enforcement information. In the court’s view, the combination of the public declarations, the ex parte elaboration, and the district court’s own review was enough to support that conclusion.

Farahi’s reliance on the sheer size of the file did not change the analysis. A large volume of responsive records does not itself establish that some meaningful portion must be releasable. The controlling question remains whether the nonexempt material can be reasonably separated and disclosed without undermining the exemption’s purpose.

## Final disposition and practical significance

Having rejected Farahi’s segregability and ex parte arguments, the court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI. The disposition is simple: the records stayed withheld under Exemption 7(A), and the FBI had no further obligation to release meaningful segregable material.

Practically, the opinion shows how FOIA cases involving counterterrorism or other sensitive law-enforcement investigations often turn on the quality of agency declarations and the court’s willingness to examine classified or sealed support. It also illustrates a recurring FOIA problem: when declarations become stale during long litigation, courts may demand updated evidence before accepting an exemption. But once the agency supplies a current and sufficiently specific explanation, and shows that the withheld material is not meaningfully separable, the court may affirm withholding even after years of delay.
