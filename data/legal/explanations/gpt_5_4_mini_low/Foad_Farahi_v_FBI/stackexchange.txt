Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How can an FBI investigation count as an "enforcement proceeding" under FOIA Exemption 7(A) if no charges have been filed?
Question:
I'm confused about the court's statement that ongoing criminal investigations themselves can qualify as "enforcement proceedings" for purposes of Exemption 7(A). I thought an enforcement proceeding meant something more formal, like an indictment, trial, or at least an administrative adjudication. Here, the FBI seems to have relied largely on declarations saying the investigation was still ongoing. Why is that enough under D.C. Circuit precedent, and what limits are there to stop agencies from calling any open-ended investigation a pending proceeding?
Answer:
Because in D.C. Circuit FOIA doctrine, an “enforcement proceeding” under Exemption 7(A) is **not limited to formal adjudications** like indictments, trials, or administrative hearings.

The key point is that Exemption 7(A) protects records whose disclosure could interfere with **pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings**, and the court in *Farahi* reiterates that **ongoing criminal investigations can themselves be those proceedings**. The opinion cites *CREW v. DOJ*, which held that “ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes,” and *Juarez v. DOJ*, which explains the test this way: so long as the investigation is still gathering evidence for a **possible future criminal case**, and premature disclosure would jeopardize that case, Exemption 7(A) applies.

So the FBI did not need to show that charges had already been filed. It only needed to show that there was a concrete law-enforcement matter still in progress and that disclosure could interfere with it. In *Farahi*, the court accepted that through:

- the Hardy declarations, which said the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations of national security and international terrorism, and  
- the Bender declaration, which confirmed the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.”

### What prevents agencies from using this to shield any open file?

The limits are the ones the opinion emphasizes:

1. **There must be a real law-enforcement nexus.**  
   The agency must show the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes, with a rational connection to an investigation of possible federal law violations.

2. **There must be a concrete, pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding.**  
   A vague assertion that something is “ongoing” is not enough by itself. The court in *Farahi* acknowledges this concern and notes that a “vague nature” assertion may be insufficient, quoting *CREW*.

3. **The agency must explain how disclosure would interfere.**  
   Here, the FBI said release could tip off targets about the scope of the investigation. That is a classic 7(A) rationale, and the court found it plausible in light of the declarations and the terrorism context.

4. **The court still examines the declaration’s specificity and context.**  
   In *Farahi*, the Bender declaration standing alone was “surprisingly terse,” but the court treated it as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations, not as a standalone assertion.

So the answer is: under D.C. Circuit precedent, an FBI investigation can be an “enforcement proceeding” because the statute is aimed at protecting **the enforcement process before charges are filed**, not just formal litigation after they are filed. The agency still has to show a **specific, ongoing investigative proceeding** and a **reasonable risk of interference**; it cannot rely on a bare label alone.

### What does it mean for records to be "compiled for law enforcement purposes" when the FBI is also dealing with national security matters?
Question:
The opinion says the FBI gets some deference because it is a law-enforcement agency, but it still has to show a "rational nexus" between the investigation and a law-enforcement duty, plus a connection to a possible security risk or violation of law. I'm having trouble understanding how demanding that test really is in practice. If the investigation involves terrorism or national security, does that automatically satisfy the "law enforcement purposes" requirement, or does the agency still need to tie the file to a specific possible violation of federal law?
Answer:
No — “national security” or “terrorism” does **not automatically** satisfy Exemption 7(A)’s “compiled for law enforcement purposes” requirement, even for the FBI.

What the D.C. Circuit says is that the FBI gets **deference** because it is a law-enforcement agency, but it still must make a **threshold showing** that there is a **rational nexus** between the investigation and one of the FBI’s law-enforcement duties, plus a connection between the person or incident and a **possible security risk or violation of federal law**. The court quotes its prior cases for exactly that point:

> the FBI’s declarations must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’”

In *Farahi*, the FBI met that burden because its public declaration said the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and tied those investigations to Farahi’s association with individuals involved in such violations. The court treated that as enough, especially given the ex parte declaration and the broader record.

So the practical standard is:

- **Not automatic**: the agency must still connect the file to a law-enforcement function and a possible legal/security violation.
- **But not demanding in the ordinary sense**: for the FBI, and particularly where counterterrorism/national-security investigations are involved, courts give substantial deference to the agency’s sworn declarations.

In other words, “national security” is not a magic word, but in an FBI terrorism case it is often enough **if the declaration ties the records to an actual investigative purpose and a possible violation of law**.

### Why did the court treat the newer Bender declaration as an "update" instead of requiring a full fresh showing for Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The district court denied summary judgment without prejudice because the earlier Hardy declarations might have become stale. Then the FBI came back with a pretty short Bender declaration saying the investigation remained ongoing, plus a sealed ex parte submission. On appeal, Farahi argued that this was too conclusory. I'm confused about why the D.C. Circuit didn't require the FBI to fully re-establish the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings as of 2023, instead of letting it rely on the earlier Hardy declarations plus a brief update.
Answer:
Because the FBI was **not starting over from zero**.

Under Exemption 7(A), the government must show that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that disclosure **could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings**. The key point in this opinion is that the FBI had already made that showing in the earlier Hardy declarations, and the district court had already said the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden before asking for an update because time had passed.

So when the FBI later filed the Bender declaration, the D.C. Circuit read it in context:

- **Hardy (public + ex parte)** had already established the nature and scope of the investigation and asserted that the relevant proceedings were ongoing.
- The district court’s 2022 order did **not** reject that showing on the merits; it only questioned whether it had become stale.
- **Bender’s declaration** was therefore treated as a **status update** confirming that the investigation was still ongoing as of March 2023, not as an attempt to prove the entire Exemption 7(A) case anew.

That is why the court said Bender’s “surprisingly terse” statement was enough **in context**. The panel expressly said the declaration should not be read in isolation; it was “an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document.”

This fits the usual FOIA practice. Agencies often may rely on declarations to describe ongoing investigations, and courts give special deference to the FBI in law-enforcement and national-security matters. Here, the combination of:

1. the earlier detailed Hardy declarations,
2. the district court’s prior assessment that the FBI had largely met its burden,
3. Bender’s confirmation that nothing had changed, and
4. the breadth of the underlying terrorism/national-security investigation,

was enough for the court to conclude that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” still existed.

So the short answer is: **the court did not require a fresh full showing because the FBI had already made the substantive Exemption 7(A) showing; Bender only updated the record to show that the earlier showing remained current.**

### When are ex parte and sealed declarations allowed in FOIA cases, and does failing to object waive challenges to them?
Question:
A lot of the court's reasoning seems to depend on sealed ex parte declarations that the requester could not see. I understand the practical reason for that in sensitive cases, but I'm not sure about the procedural rules. Is it generally acceptable in FOIA litigation for the government to make key showings ex parte, especially on issues like Exemption 7(A) and segregability? Also, the opinion says Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he could not later challenge reliance on them. Is that a true waiver/forfeiture rule, and how specific would an objection have needed to be?
Answer:
Yes—but only as a limited FOIA procedure, not as the default.

In **Farahi**, the D.C. Circuit treated sealed ex parte declarations as permissible because they were used to support claims that could not safely be laid out on the public record. The court noted that the FBI filed a supplemental Hardy declaration “under seal and ex parte,” and later that Bender’s ex parte submission supplied more detail on both **Exemption 7(A)** and **segregability**. The panel then explained that FOIA plaintiffs are indeed “handicap[ped]” by ex parte declarations, but that this is “inherent” in cases where an ex parte submission is “the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” That is the governing idea.

## 1) Are ex parte declarations generally allowed in FOIA cases?

Yes, in the D.C. Circuit they are allowed when necessary to protect sensitive information and the court needs more detail than can be placed in a public affidavit.

FOIA summary judgment usually rests on agency affidavits, and those affidavits must be reasonably specific. But when the public declaration cannot safely disclose the details—common in national security, law-enforcement, or ongoing-investigation cases—the court may review a sealed ex parte declaration in camera. **Farahi** fits that pattern: the FBI’s public declarations gave the basic theory, while the sealed declarations supplied the operational detail the district court needed.

That is especially common where disclosure itself would defeat the exemption, which is exactly the concern with **Exemption 7(A)**: the government must show that release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” In **Farahi**, the court relied on sealed material to confirm the nature and scope of the ongoing investigation and to address why disclosure would interfere.

## 2) Can the government use ex parte declarations on segregability?

Yes. The opinion says the district court reviewed Bender’s ex parte submission and found it adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit approved that, citing the principle that segregability can be shown by affidavits with “reasonable specificity,” and that a court need not put its analysis of a sealed declaration on the public record.

So, even on segregability, ex parte material may be used if the explanation itself would reveal protected information. The court still has to make the segregability determination; it just may do so based on sealed support.

## 3) Does failing to object waive later challenges?

Yes, at least in the posture reflected here. The panel was explicit: because Farahi “did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.”

That is a standard forfeiture rule. If a party does not raise an objection in the district court, the issue is ordinarily forfeited on appeal. The court also cited **Khine** for forfeiture in the FOIA context when a party fails to make a specific argument below.

## 4) How specific would the objection need to be?

Specific enough to alert the district court to the procedural objection you want preserved. A bare failure to respond is not enough. If Farahi had wanted to preserve the issue, he would have needed to object to the motion to file the declaration ex parte/sealed, or at least argue that:

- the court should require a public version or more detailed public affidavit,
- the sealed submission was unnecessary,
- the court should limit reliance on sealed material,
- or the court should use some less restrictive procedure.

A generalized objection to the merits of the exemption would not necessarily preserve a distinct objection to the **use of sealed ex parte evidence**. In other words, you need to object to the **procedure** if you want to challenge the procedure later.

## Bottom line

**Farahi** confirms two familiar FOIA principles in the D.C. Circuit:

1. **Ex parte/sealed declarations are allowed when necessary**, especially in sensitive Exemption 7(A) and segregability disputes.
2. **If you do not object in the district court to the use of those declarations, you generally forfeit the argument on appeal.**

So the opinion is not saying ex parte declarations are always okay; it is saying they are okay **when the court needs them to resolve the case without disclosing the secret material**, and a party who silently accepts that procedure cannot later complain about it.

### How can an agency satisfy FOIA's segregability requirement when it withholds almost an entire investigative file?
Question:
The FBI found over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs, disclosed some material, and withheld most of the rest. Farahi argued that there had to be at least some non-exempt information buried in a file that large. The court responded that isolated words or phrases are not necessarily "reasonably segregable" if they are interspersed line-by-line and don't convey meaningful information. I'm struggling with how courts determine what counts as a "reasonably segregable portion" versus meaningless fragments. At what point does withholding nearly everything become inconsistent with FOIA's general disclosure rule?
Answer:
FOIA’s segregability rule does **not** require an agency to release every scrap of non-exempt text. It requires release only of a **“reasonably segregable portion”** of the record. If the non-exempt material is so intertwined with exempt material that disclosure would leave only fragments with no meaningful informational value, the agency may withhold the whole record.

That is exactly how the D.C. Circuit treated it here.

### The basic doctrine
Under FOIA, even when an exemption applies, the agency must still take reasonable steps to segregate and release any non-exempt information. But segregation is not “reasonable” if the remaining material would be meaningless or would require disclosing exempt material to make sense of it.

The court quoted the classic rule from **Mead Data**:

- if the non-exempt material is “randomly **interspersed line-by-line throughout the document**,” it is not likely to be reasonably segregable; and
- FOIA focuses on **information**, “not simply words” on a page.

The opinion also cites **Juarez** for the related principle that an entire record may be withheld when non-exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt portions.

### How courts decide
Courts look for whether the agency has given a **reasonable, specific explanation** showing why further segregation is not possible. The agency does not have to prove mathematically that literally no word could be released. It must show that any remaining non-exempt material would not form a meaningful releaseable portion without compromising the exemption.

So the question is not:

> “Can I find some innocent words somewhere in the file?”

It is:

> “Can the agency reasonably separate out meaningful non-exempt information without exposing exempt information or leaving only isolated words and phrases?”

### Why Farahi lost the segregability argument
Farahi argued that a file of 10,750 pages and 80 CDs had to contain something releasable. The court rejected that inference. It said that even if individual words or phrases could be disclosed, that “is neither here nor there” if they are merely fragments scattered through exempt material. In other words, **isolated snippets are not necessarily ‘reasonably segregable’**.

The court accepted the FBI’s explanation that no **meaningful information** remained segregable, and it upheld the district court’s reliance on the FBI’s declarations, including the ex parte submission.

### Bottom line
Withholding nearly everything is consistent with FOIA **if** the agency shows that the remaining non-exempt material is either:

1. **inextricably intertwined** with exempt material, or
2. merely isolated words/phrases that would not yield **meaningful information** if disclosed.

So the key line is not volume, but **functional separability**. A huge file may still be mostly or entirely non-segregable if the exempt and non-exempt material are too tightly intertwined.

### Why did the court say Farahi forfeited his challenge to the FBI's document categories?
Question:
The opinion briefly mentions that when an agency withholds records by category, the categories must be sufficiently distinct for the court to test whether the exemptions were properly applied. But then it says Farahi forfeited this argument because he did not specifically explain why the FBI's categories were overbroad. What exactly would a FOIA plaintiff need to argue to preserve this kind of issue on appeal? Is a general objection that the categories are too vague not enough?
Answer:
The court treated this as a **waived/forfeited appellate argument because Farahi did not develop it with any specificity**.

The relevant rule is that, in a category-by-category FOIA withholding, the agency’s categories must be “**sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied**” to each category. The court cited **CREW** for that proposition. But Farahi’s problem was not that the rule was wrong — it was that he **didn’t actually explain why the FBI’s particular categories failed that standard**. The panel therefore said he “**provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad**,” and so he “**forfeited his arguments on this score**,” citing **Khine v. DHS**.

### What would have preserved the issue?
A FOIA plaintiff would need to do more than say “the categories are vague” or “overbroad” in the abstract. He would need to identify, with some precision:

- **which categories** are being challenged;
- **why those categories are not sufficiently distinct**;
- **how the categories lump together documents that should have been separated**; and
- **why that lumping prevents the court from testing the exemption claim category by category**.

In other words, the plaintiff has to give the court a concrete reason to think the agency’s grouping masks nonexempt material or makes meaningful judicial review impossible.

### Is a general objection enough?
No. A bare objection that the categories are “too vague” is usually not enough to preserve the point for appeal. Appellate courts generally will not entertain an argument that was only mentioned in passing below or not supported by a developed explanation. That is exactly what the panel meant here: **without a specific explanation of overbreadth, there was nothing for the court to review.**

So the takeaway is: **preservation requires a targeted, reasoned challenge to the actual category definitions, not just a generalized complaint that the agency used categories at all.**

### How much deference do courts really give the FBI in Exemption 7(A) cases involving terrorism or national security?
Question:
The court repeatedly emphasizes deference to the FBI as a law-enforcement agency, and says that deference is especially appropriate where national security is implicated. I'm trying to understand the doctrinal significance of that. Does this deference affect only how the court reads the agency's declarations, or does it effectively lower the government's burden of proof? In other words, if the case did not involve terrorism-related allegations, would the same public declarations have been viewed as too conclusory?
Answer:
The deference is real, but it does **not** eliminate the government’s burden under Exemption 7(A). The opinion says the opposite in so many words:

> “The government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies.”

So deference does **not** mean “the FBI wins if it says so.” It means the court gives the FBI a **fair measure of leeway in evaluating its declarations**, especially where the records concern law enforcement and national security.

## What the deference does
The court explains that because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, “its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference,” and that this is “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.” In practical terms, that affects **how the court reads the agency’s affidavits/declarations**:

- the court is more willing to credit agency representations about the nature of the investigation;
- it may accept less public detail when the ex parte declaration fills in the gaps;
- it will assess the declarations in **context**, not in isolation.

That is exactly how the panel treated the terse Bender declaration. The court said that, “considered in isolation,” it looked “surprisingly terse,” but it was acceptable because it was an **update** to the earlier Hardy declarations and was backed by the broader record.

## What the deference does not do
It does **not lower the legal standard** for Exemption 7(A). The FBI still had to show:

1. the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and  
2. disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.”

The court still insists on a “rational nexus” between the investigation and a law-enforcement duty, and it still notes that vague assertions are not enough. The opinion expressly cites **CREW** for the proposition that a “vague nature” of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may be insufficient.

## So would the declarations have been too conclusory without the terrorism/national-security context?
Possibly, yes — or at least **harder to sustain**.

The opinion’s logic is that the FBI’s public declarations were not judged in a vacuum. They were strengthened by:

- the ex parte Hardy declaration;
- the district court’s prior conclusion that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden;
- and the broader context showing that the investigation involved alleged ties to terrorist organizations and other terrorism-related matters.

That context made the representation that the investigation remained “ongoing” more plausible. Without that context, the same public language might well have looked more like the kind of conclusory statement **CREW** warns against.

## Bottom line
The deference in this opinion is best understood as **deference in evidentiary appraisal**, not a reduced burden of proof. National-security and terrorism context makes the FBI’s declarations easier to credit, but the agency still has to make a legally sufficient showing under Exemption 7(A).
