Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Updating “Ongoing”: What *Farahi v. FBI* Says About Staleness and FOIA Exemption 7(A)

## Why the case matters

*Farahi v. FBI* is a useful reminder that FOIA litigation moves slowly, but Exemption 7(A) does not freeze time. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion holds that an agency can still withhold records under Exemption 7(A) even after years of litigation, so long as it updates the court with a record showing that the relevant law-enforcement proceeding remains pending or reasonably anticipated. That may sound unremarkable at first glance. But in practice, it matters a great deal: FOIA plaintiffs often file when an investigation appears old or dormant, only to face declarations that were drafted years earlier. *Farahi* shows that courts may require a freshness check, but they may also accept a relatively terse update if it sits atop a more detailed record and the district court has already vetted the underlying claim.

The opinion therefore sits at the intersection of two familiar D.C. Circuit themes: deference to law-enforcement agencies in national-security-related FOIA cases, and the insistence that Exemption 7(A) be tied to a concrete, not hypothetical, enforcement proceeding. The court’s answer here is pragmatic rather than rigid. Agencies do not need to start over every time the calendar advances. But they do need to show that their “ongoing” investigation is still ongoing.

## The staleness problem

The district court saw the problem clearly. The FBI’s first summary-judgment papers were filed in 2019, but the court did not reach the merits until 2022. That lag mattered because Exemption 7(A) is inherently time-sensitive: it protects records only if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” If the proceeding has ended, or if the agency can no longer plausibly say one is expected, the exemption falls away.

So the district court asked for updated declarations. That was the right move. Exemption 7(A) is not a permanent seal; it is keyed to the status of an investigation or prosecution. A court asked to rule years later should not simply assume that conditions remain unchanged.

What makes *Farahi* interesting is how the D.C. Circuit handled the update. The supplemental Bender declaration was not, standing alone, a model of elaboration. The court openly acknowledged that it was “surprisingly terse.” But it refused to treat that terseness as fatal because the declaration did not come into a vacuum. The earlier Hardy declarations had already explained, in both public and ex parte form, the nature and scope of the FBI’s investigation. The district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” carried its burden, and the Bender filing was framed as a confirmation that nothing material had changed.

That sequencing matters. The panel did not say a bare “still ongoing” assertion is always enough. Quite the opposite: it distinguished *CREW*, where a vague assertion of ongoing investigations was insufficient. Instead, the court treated the update as part of a cumulative showing. The lesson is not that agencies can be conclusory; it is that the adequacy of a supplemental declaration depends on the record it supplements.

## How *Farahi* fits with *CREW* and *Juarez*

The court’s analysis is best understood as an application of *CREW* and *Juarez*, not a departure from them. In *CREW*, the D.C. Circuit said Exemption 7(A) requires a “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceeding, and that an ongoing criminal investigation can qualify. In *Juarez*, the court emphasized that if an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and premature release would jeopardize that case, the exemption applies.

*Farahi* reinforces both propositions. The panel had little difficulty concluding that the FBI’s investigation of Farahi fit within the law-enforcement prong, especially given the national-security and terrorism context. More importantly, it treated the agency’s public and sealed declarations as sufficient to show that there was still a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” That phrase is doing work. It signals that the agency must identify an actual investigative process, not just an abstract interest in the subject.

At the same time, the opinion reads *CREW* as requiring enough specificity to avoid boilerplate, but not so much that agencies must expose the very details that Exemption 7(A) is designed to protect. That is the familiar FOIA tension. The government must give a court enough to test the claim, but not enough to compromise the investigation. *Farahi* lands on the side of allowing that balance to be struck with a combination of public and ex parte declarations.

## What agencies must do when years pass

The most practical takeaway from *Farahi* is procedural. When a FOIA case lingers, agencies should expect to refresh their declarations. A declaration that was adequate in 2019 may look stale in 2022 or 2025. But an update need not be a full re-litigation of the exemption. If the agency can show that the same investigation continues and the earlier declarations already described the relevant law-enforcement context with specificity, a supplemental affidavit may suffice.

That said, the opinion also suggests a cautionary note: courts are not obliged to accept a “still ongoing” statement at face value if the surrounding record is thin. The FBI benefited here from several features absent in many FOIA cases: a long-running national-security investigation, a district court that had already scrutinized the exemption, and ex parte material that the panel could rely on without disclosing sensitive details. Agencies whose initial showing is weaker should not assume they can patch in a one-line update years later.

There is also a broader institutional point. The district court did what good FOIA courts often do: it demanded an updated record when time had passed. The D.C. Circuit did not punish that insistence; it endorsed it. That may encourage district judges to keep pressing agencies for current declarations rather than deciding stale records on autopilot. But *Farahi* also reassures agencies that meeting that request does not require them to reveal operational details or produce a mini-trial on the status of an investigation.

## Segregability and ex parte review

The segregability discussion is shorter, but it tracks the same basic logic. Farahi argued that, given the huge volume of responsive records, some nonexempt material must be releasable. The court disagreed, emphasizing that FOIA protects information, not random words and phrases, and that where nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material, wholesale withholding is permissible.

The opinion is also notable for its comfort with ex parte review in this setting. That is not new law, but it is a reminder that in sensitive cases, ex parte declarations remain a central tool for resolving segregability disputes. For plaintiffs, that is a hard pill to swallow. For courts, the alternative may be to force disclosure of the very information the exemption protects. *Farahi* accepts that tradeoff and treats it as an inherent feature of some FOIA disputes.

## Bottom line

*Farahi* is not a doctrinal reset. It is a careful reaffirmation that Exemption 7(A) is dynamic, not static. Agencies cannot rest forever on old declarations, especially when litigation has dragged on. But if they update the court, tie the records to a real and continuing investigation, and provide enough detail—sometimes in camera—to satisfy the judge, the D.C. Circuit will continue to give them substantial room.

For FOIA practitioners, the message is straightforward: in Exemption 7(A) cases, time matters, but context matters too. A stale record can sink an exemption claim. A well-supported update can revive it.
