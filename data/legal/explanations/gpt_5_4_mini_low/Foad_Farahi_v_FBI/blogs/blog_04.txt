Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Category-by-Category Withholding and the Cost of Underdeveloped Appellate Arguments

## Why this part of the case matters

The headline holding in *Farahi v. FBI* is familiar enough: Exemption 7(A) can shield FBI records tied to ongoing or reasonably anticipated law-enforcement proceedings. But the more interesting piece, for FOIA lawyers and repeat litigants alike, is the panel’s brief treatment of the FBI’s categorical withholding approach. The D.C. Circuit reaffirmed that agencies cannot hide behind overly broad buckets of documents; categories must be specific enough for a court to test whether the claimed exemption actually fits. Yet the court declined to probe that issue further because Farahi never concretely explained why the FBI’s categories were deficient. That may sound like a modest procedural point. In practice, it is a reminder that modern FOIA litigation is increasingly won or lost not just on the substantive scope of an exemption, but on how precisely the requester preserves and develops objections to the agency’s Vaughn-style showing.

## The court’s doctrinal baseline

The panel restated a settled but important principle: when an agency withholds records on a category-by-category basis, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to let a court determine whether the exemption applies to each one. That requirement matters because FOIA does not permit agencies to defeat judicial review by lumping together disparate documents and asserting a single, generalized rationale.

At the same time, the court did not suggest that categorical withholding is disfavored per se. To the contrary, the D.C. Circuit has long accepted that agencies may describe and justify withholdings by category, especially where the volume of records is large and line-by-line description would itself reveal exempt information. The real question is whether the categories are sharp enough to support meaningful review.

## Why Farahi lost the point

What is striking here is not that the court accepted the FBI’s categories, but that it essentially refused to entertain the challenge because Farahi did not do the work. The opinion says he offered no specific explanation for why the categories were overbroad, and therefore forfeited the argument.

That is a familiar move in appellate practice, but it carries real force in FOIA cases. Requesters often object in broad terms — that the agency’s categories are “generic,” “vague,” or “overinclusive” — without explaining how a narrower category could have been drawn or why the agency’s grouping obscures exemption-specific differences among records. The D.C. Circuit is signaling that this sort of abstract objection will not be enough. If the requester wants a court to second-guess the agency’s taxonomy, the requester has to identify what is wrong with the taxonomy and why it matters to the exemption analysis.

That is a demanding standard, especially where the agency’s detail comes partly in ex parte or sealed declarations. But it is also consistent with how FOIA cases are actually litigated: the district court is not expected to redo the agency’s record review from scratch, and the burden shifts meaningfully only when the requester gives the court a concrete reason to doubt the categorization.

## The deeper procedural lesson

The practical lesson of *Farahi* is that FOIA doctrine has become highly process-sensitive. Courts remain willing to police categorical withholding, segregability, and the adequacy of agency declarations. But they will not do that work in the abstract. A requester must identify the defect with enough precision to make it litigable.

That has downstream effects. First, it rewards careful issue framing at the district court level. If a requester thinks the categories are too broad, the objection should not stop at “overinclusive.” Counsel needs to explain whether the defect is that the category mixes documents with different exemption statuses, collapses temporally distinct records, or describes files at a level too general to test the agency’s assertion.

Second, it highlights the importance of the Vaughn process itself. Agencies increasingly rely on category-based descriptions because they are administratively efficient and, in sensitive cases, reduce the risk of disclosure through description. *Farahi* suggests that unless the requester can expose the weakness in that category structure, appellate courts may simply accept it as a practical necessity.

Third, the opinion reinforces a recurring asymmetry in FOIA litigation: agencies can often satisfy their burden with declarations that are broad but contextually plausible, while requesters need to make targeted, record-based attacks to force deeper scrutiny. That asymmetry is especially pronounced where the underlying records concern national security or counterterrorism, as they did here.

## Segregability in the background

The same dynamic appears in the court’s segregability analysis. The panel accepted the FBI’s position that there was no meaningful nonexempt material to release, despite the large volume of responsive records. The court emphasized that FOIA protects information, not isolated words or phrases, and it rejected the notion that the mere size of the file implies that something must be releasable.

That part of the opinion matters because it shows how closely intertwined segregability is with the agency’s categorical presentation. If the categories are accepted, and if the declaration explains why disclosure within those categories would compromise the investigation, then segregability challenges become much harder to press unless the requester can point to a specific, releasable kind of information.

## Bottom line

*Farahi* is not a groundbreaking FOIA opinion, but it is a useful one. The panel reaffirmed the principle that agencies must define withholding categories with enough precision to permit review. But it also made clear that courts will not rescue a requester who attacks those categories only in generalities. In FOIA litigation, precision is now a two-way street: agencies must be specific, but so must challengers. The cost of underdeveloped appellate arguments is not just forfeiture of a point; it is often the loss of the only path to meaningful judicial scrutiny of how the agency has organized its exemption claims.
