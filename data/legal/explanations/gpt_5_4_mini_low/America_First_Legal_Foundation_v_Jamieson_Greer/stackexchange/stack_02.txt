Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How do 5 U.S.C. 7 1216 and FOIA's disciplinary provision in 5 U.S.C. 7 552(a)(4)(F) fit together?
Question:
The opinion discusses two different routes for OSC involvement in alleged FOIA misconduct: section 1216(a)(3), which says OSC 'shall' investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding, and FOIA's own provision in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which requires OSC to initiate a proceeding only after a court orders production, awards fees, and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. OSC seems to have treated the FOIA-specific provision as limiting its authority under section 1216, while the district court rejected that view. What is the best way to understand the relationship between these statutes? Does section 552(a)(4)(F) create a mandatory minimum trigger for OSC action, while section 1216 independently authorizes broader investigations, or is there a stronger argument that the more specific FOIA provision should control?
Answer:
The best way to read the two provisions is as **overlapping but not identical** enforcement mechanisms:

- **5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** is a **narrow, mandatory trigger**. If a court:
  1. orders disclosure,
  2. awards fees,
  3. and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously,

  then OSC **“shall promptly initiate”** a disciplinary proceeding. That is a **required follow-on step** once those predicates are met.

- **5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)** is a **separate grant of authority/duty**. It says OSC **“shall … conduct an investigation”** of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding. On the face of the statute, that language is broader: it is not limited to cases where a court has already made the findings described in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

That is essentially how the district court understood the relationship in the opinion: **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power under § 1216**. The D.C. Circuit did **not** decide the merits; it resolved the case on standing. But the opinion’s statutory description supports the view that the two provisions serve different functions: § 552(a)(4)(F) gives OSC a mandatory duty in a specific FOIA-judgment setting, while § 1216 independently authorizes OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings more generally.

So the cleanest reading is:

> **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is a mandatory minimum trigger for OSC action in a defined class of cases, but it is not the exclusive source of OSC authority over FOIA misconduct.**

There is, of course, a possible **specific-over-general** argument that the FOIA-specific provision should control where the statutes overlap. But the opinion as summarized here reflects the opposite view: because Congress enacted a separate FOIA-related authority in § 1216 and used mandatory language there too, the better reading is that **both provisions can coexist**, with § 552(a)(4)(F) adding a mandatory enforcement mechanism rather than displacing § 1216.
