Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why didn't the relaxed standing rules for procedural injuries help AFL here?
Question:
The opinion says that when a plaintiff alleges violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest, courts relax imminence and redressability somewhat. But the D.C. Circuit still says AFL fails even under that more forgiving framework. I'm having trouble seeing why. If AFL's concrete interest is access to records or access to investigative information, why isn't an order requiring OSC to perform the allegedly mandatory investigation enough to satisfy the reduced redressability standard? Is the key point that even the relaxed doctrine does not eliminate the need for a non-speculative link between the requested procedure and the concrete benefit?
Answer:
Because the D.C. Circuit thought AFL was trying to do two different things, and neither worked even under the more forgiving procedural-injury doctrine.

### 1) The relaxed rule does **not** eliminate redressability
The court quotes the standard correctly: when a plaintiff alleges violation of a procedural right tied to a concrete interest, courts “relax” imminence and redressability, “but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” The key point is that the plaintiff still must show that the procedure, if properly provided, would likely protect some concrete interest in a non-speculative way.

That is exactly where AFL failed.

---

### 2) On AFL’s “records” theory, redressability was too speculative
AFL said: OSC should investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy; if OSC investigates, it might seek corrective or disciplinary action; that might lead MSPB and then DOJ to release records.

The court found that chain too attenuated because it depends on:

- OSC deciding to investigate and enforce,
- MSPB deciding to sanction,
- DOJ deciding to change its behavior and disclose records.

That is more than the relaxed procedural-injury doctrine permits. The opinion expressly says the chain “requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit” and about “how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion.” So even under relaxed redressability, AFL could not show that an order requiring OSC to investigate would “very likely spur the next steps necessary” to get the records.

In other words: **an order forcing a procedure is not enough if the concrete benefit depends on multiple discretionary and speculative steps by others.**

---

### 3) On AFL’s “investigatory information” theory, AFL lacked an actual entitlement
AFL also tried to say it had an informational injury from not receiving notices and information that would come with an OSC investigation.

But the court rejected that because section 1216 does **not** itself give AFL a right to receive that information. AFL was trying to import section 1214’s notice provisions into a section 1216 investigation, but the opinion says AFL “does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations,” and it cites authority treating that choice as discretionary.

So even if OSC had to investigate, AFL still had no statutory right to the information it claimed to be missing. Without a statutory entitlement to that information, there is no concrete informational injury to redress.

---

### Bottom line
Yes — your intuition is right. The court’s point is that **the relaxed standing doctrine for procedural injuries still requires a non-speculative link between the missing procedure and a concrete benefit to the plaintiff**.

Here, AFL could not show that:
1. compelling OSC to investigate would likely produce the records it wanted, or
2. AFL had a legal entitlement to the investigative notices/information it sought.

So the “relaxed” doctrine did not save standing. It softens the redressability inquiry, but it does not erase it.
