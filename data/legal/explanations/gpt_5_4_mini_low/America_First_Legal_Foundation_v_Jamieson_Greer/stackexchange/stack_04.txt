Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the court say AFL's FOIA-related injury was not fairly traceable to OSC?
Question:
The court says AFL's inability to get the requested records is traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the causal chain through OSC is too speculative. But if OSC had a mandatory duty to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholding, and if that investigation could lead to corrective or disciplinary proceedings, doesn't that at least indirectly affect DOJ's incentives to comply? How do courts decide when an enforcement-related chain of causation becomes too attenuated for standing purposes, especially where the plaintiff is trying to force one agency to police another agency's conduct?
Answer:
Because standing requires more than “OSC might, in theory, influence DOJ someday.” The court held that AFL’s alleged injury — not getting the FOIA records — was caused immediately by DOJ’s own refusal to produce them, while OSC’s noninvestigation was only an upstream omission whose effect depended on a long chain of discretionary decisions by third parties.

The court’s causation analysis is very much in line with ordinary Article III doctrine:

1. **The injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant.**  
   Under *Lujan* and the cases the court cites, the plaintiff has to show that the defendant’s conduct—not someone else’s—is the source of the injury.

2. **A chain with multiple independent actors is often too speculative.**  
   Here, AFL’s theory was:  
   - OSC investigates DOJ;  
   - OSC might decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action;  
   - the MSPB might impose sanctions;  
   - DOJ might then change course and release the records.  
   
   The court said that this requires speculation about the choices of **two nonparty decisionmakers**: OSC/MSPB and DOJ. It quoted *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the point that causation cannot rest on speculation about the “unfettered choices” of independent actors.

3. **OSC’s duty to investigate does not make DOJ’s withholding traceable to OSC.**  
   Even assuming OSC had a mandatory duty under § 1216(a)(3), the court treated that as an obligation to investigate, not as a direct cause of record production. The injury AFL complains of is the lack of records from DOJ, and that is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.”

4. **The court also emphasized enforcement discretion.**  
   Even if OSC had to investigate, the court noted that it would still be speculative whether OSC would choose to pursue enforcement, and courts generally may not compel that kind of discretionary enforcement decision. That makes the causal link even weaker.

So the key point is this: **an indirect incentive effect is not enough for standing when the plaintiff’s injury depends on a series of contingent, discretionary steps by third parties.** Courts will usually find the chain too attenuated unless the plaintiff can show a much more direct and likely path from the defendant’s conduct to the injury.

Here, the court thought AFL’s theory was exactly the kind of “maybe this investigation would pressure DOJ” argument that Article III does not treat as fairly traceable causation.
