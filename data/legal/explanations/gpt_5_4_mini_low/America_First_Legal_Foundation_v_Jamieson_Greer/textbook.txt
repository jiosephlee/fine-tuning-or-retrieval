Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# 1. The Dispute and the Statutory Setting

## America First Legal Foundation’s effort to obtain DOJ emails concerning judicial recusals

This case begins with a FOIA request. America First Legal Foundation, or AFL, was conducting an investigation into judicial recusals and sought from the Department of Justice certain emails between an outside attorney and DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL believed those emails would shed light on the subject of its broader inquiry. DOJ did not initially produce the records in the manner AFL wanted, and AFL challenged the agency’s response in a FOIA lawsuit.

According to AFL, DOJ denied the request on a questionable ground: it claimed that it does not search employee emails for responsive records unless the employee consents. That alleged policy became the focus of AFL’s broader complaint. AFL viewed the policy as an unlawful way to withhold records that FOIA requires agencies to search for and disclose unless an exemption applies.

## DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails without the employee’s consent

The legal significance of DOJ’s alleged policy is that it was not just a one-off response to a single request. AFL characterized it as an agency-wide practice of refusing to search employee email accounts without first obtaining the employee’s permission. If true, AFL thought the policy might reflect a systemic approach to FOIA processing, rather than an isolated error.

That distinction mattered because AFL was not only seeking the requested records; it also wanted some mechanism to examine and potentially correct the alleged policy itself. A single FOIA case might force disclosure of records, but it would not necessarily compel broader internal review of DOJ’s practices. AFL therefore looked beyond ordinary FOIA litigation.

## AFL’s two-track strategy: a direct FOIA suit against DOJ and a separate effort to trigger OSC investigation

AFL pursued a two-track strategy. First, it sued DOJ directly under FOIA to obtain the emails it had requested. That lawsuit proceeded separately, and the opinion notes that DOJ had already produced, and was continuing to produce, responsive records. The adequacy of that production was not the issue in this appeal.

Second, AFL asked the Office of Special Counsel, or OSC, to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent. AFL’s theory was that OSC had a statutory duty to investigate arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings, and that DOJ’s alleged policy fit that description. In effect, AFL wanted OSC to use its investigative authority to look into whether DOJ had been improperly withholding information in a way that violated FOIA.

This second track is what ultimately generated the jurisdictional dispute in the court of appeals. The central question in the appeal was not whether DOJ had complied with FOIA in the underlying request, but whether AFL could sue to force OSC to investigate.

## The role of the Office of Special Counsel as an investigative and prosecutorial civil-service agency

To understand the statutory backdrop, it helps to know what OSC is. The Office of Special Counsel is an investigative and prosecutorial agency created to help maintain a fair and lawful civil service. The opinion describes OSC as an agency empowered to investigate misconduct by federal employees and, when appropriate, to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board, or MSPB.

OSC is not a general-purpose FOIA enforcement office. Its mission comes from the Civil Service Reform Act, which gives it authority over certain types of federal employee misconduct. That structure matters because AFL’s theory tried to connect a FOIA dispute to OSC’s civil-service enforcement powers.

## OSC authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 to investigate prohibited personnel practices and seek corrective or disciplinary action

One major OSC power comes from 5 U.S.C. § 1214. Under that provision, OSC may investigate “prohibited personnel practices” and, if warranted, seek corrective action or disciplinary action through proceedings before the MSPB. The opinion notes that § 1214 also includes procedures for notifying the complaining party about the status of an investigation.

This provision is important because it establishes the model for OSC investigations. In the ordinary prohibited-personnel-practice setting, OSC can investigate, decide whether to pursue action, and communicate with the complainant using the procedures Congress specified. AFL later argued that similar procedures should apply when OSC investigates an alleged FOIA withholding under a different statutory provision.

## OSC authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate other misconduct, including arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings

The key provision in this case is 5 U.S.C. § 1216. Section 1216 expands OSC’s authority beyond prohibited personnel practices. As relevant here, it directs OSC to conduct an investigation of any allegation involving the arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under FOIA. In other words, if an agency is alleged to have improperly withheld records in violation of FOIA, § 1216 says OSC “shall” investigate.

Section 1216 also provides that OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That wording is what made AFL think OSC had to take up its complaint about DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. AFL read the statute as creating a mandatory investigative obligation, and perhaps as importing the usual § 1214 enforcement machinery into the FOIA context.

## FOIA’s separate OSC-related enforcement provision in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

FOIA itself contains a separate OSC-related enforcement mechanism. Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), if a court in a FOIA case orders production of records, awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records, then OSC must promptly begin a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the responsible officer or employee.

This provision is narrower than § 1216. It is triggered only after a court has made particular findings in a FOIA lawsuit. By contrast, § 1216 appears, at least on its face, to authorize OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding more generally. The relationship between these two provisions became the central statutory puzzle in the case.

## Why the apparent overlap between § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) created the legal controversy

The controversy arose because the two statutes seem to point in different directions. AFL argued that § 1216 gives OSC an independent duty to investigate alleged FOIA violations whenever a person alleges arbitrary or capricious withholding. OSC, by contrast, took the position that it lacked authority to investigate unless the preconditions in FOIA’s own enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), had been satisfied.

That disagreement mattered for two reasons. First, it determined whether AFL could force OSC to act on its complaint about DOJ’s alleged policy. Second, it raised the question whether an alleged FOIA requester has any judicially enforceable right to make OSC investigate in the first place. The district court and then the court of appeals had to sort out that statutory relationship, and the appellate court ultimately resolved the case on standing grounds before reaching the merits of the statutory interpretation dispute.

The result is that this case sits at the intersection of FOIA enforcement and civil-service oversight. AFL tried to use OSC as a supplementary enforcement tool for an alleged FOIA policy, while OSC resisted being drawn into a role it believed Congress had not assigned it.

# 2. Procedural History and the District Court’s Merits Ruling

## AFL’s Request that OSC Investigate DOJ Under § 1216(a)(3)

The dispute began with AFL’s attempt to use the Office of Special Counsel as an enforcement avenue for an alleged FOIA violation. AFL had submitted a FOIA request to the Department of Justice seeking certain emails. It believed DOJ had denied the request on an improper policy ground—namely, that DOJ would not search employee emails for responsive records unless the employee consented. AFL then asked OSC to investigate that alleged policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”

That request matters because AFL was not merely seeking records from DOJ through its separate FOIA lawsuit. It was also trying to trigger OSC’s investigative machinery against DOJ, presumably to pressure the agency and potentially individual employees responsible for the withholding. The case thus involved two different tracks: the ordinary FOIA suit against DOJ, and the attempted OSC investigation under the Civil Service Reform Act and FOIA’s special enforcement provisions.

## OSC’s Position that It Lacked Authority Absent the FOIA-Specific Predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

OSC declined to investigate. Its position was that the statutory conditions for a FOIA-based investigation were not met. OSC relied on 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which provides that when a court orders production in a FOIA case, awards attorneys’ fees, and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records, OSC “shall promptly initiate” a disciplinary proceeding against the responsible officer or employee.

In OSC’s view, those three predicates functioned as the trigger for its authority to act on an alleged FOIA withholding. Because AFL’s situation did not satisfy those predicates, OSC concluded it lacked authority to investigate. AFL disagreed, reading § 1216(a)(3) as an independent mandate that required OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings even when the separate FOIA-enforcement provision in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) was not triggered.

This was the central statutory disagreement in the district court. The question was not simply whether DOJ had violated FOIA. It was whether OSC had a duty—or even authority—to investigate the alleged policy under § 1216 without the special conditions described in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

## AFL’s Argument that § 1216 Independently Required an Investigation

AFL’s theory was that § 1216 gave OSC a freestanding obligation to investigate “any allegation” of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding. On that reading, § 1216 was not limited by FOIA’s narrower disciplinary trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL therefore argued that OSC had unlawfully refused to investigate its allegation about DOJ’s email-search policy.

This distinction between the two provisions was important. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) speaks to a specific post-judgment disciplinary process after a court has already found a FOIA withholding and made certain additional findings. Section 1216, by contrast, appears to authorize OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings more broadly. AFL’s position was that OSC had wrongly collapsed the broader § 1216 authority into the narrower § 552 mechanism.

## The District Court’s Conclusion that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) Does Not Limit OSC’s Investigative Power Under § 1216

The district court agreed with AFL on the statutory interpretation question. It concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216. In other words, the court read § 1216 as independently authorizing OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholdings, regardless of whether the FOIA-specific disciplinary provision had been triggered.

That ruling was favorable to AFL on the legal issue. It meant the court accepted AFL’s core claim that OSC had misread the statute. The district court therefore treated OSC’s refusal to investigate as resting on a legal error rather than a correct understanding of the statute.

## The District Court’s Determination that OSC’s Refusal Rested on Legal Error

After concluding that OSC had erred in thinking it lacked authority, the district court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was based on the wrong legal premise. The court’s reasoning was straightforward: if § 1216 authorizes investigations independent of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), then OSC could not justify its refusal by pointing to the absence of the three FOIA-specific predicates.

But that did not end the case. Even after finding legal error, the district court declined to order OSC to investigate.

## The District Court’s Further Conclusion that, Under § 1216(c), Whether to Investigate a FOIA Policy Was Committed to OSC’s Discretion

The district court held that although OSC had misread the scope of its authority, the decision whether to investigate a particular FOIA policy was committed to OSC’s discretion under § 1216(c). That subsection says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action” and disciplinary action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The district court read that language as leaving the actual decision to pursue an investigation to OSC.

This is the key move that explains the posture of the case when it reached the court of appeals. The district court did not enter an order compelling OSC to investigate. Instead, it concluded that OSC had made a legal mistake but that the remedy was not mandatory judicial compulsion. The court therefore stopped short of ordering the agency to act.

## The Remand to OSC for Reconsideration Rather Than an Order Compelling Investigation

Instead of requiring an investigation, the district court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration in light of its statutory interpretation. In practical terms, the court told OSC to reconsider its position now that the court had determined § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not restrict § 1216 authority.

That remand is significant because it shows the district court viewed the case as one in which the agency should get another chance to decide whether to investigate, but not one in which the court could itself compel the investigation. The district court’s choice of remand rather than direct relief reflects its view that the statute left room for agency discretion.

## OSC’s Renewed Refusal to Investigate and AFL’s Motion for Entry of Final Judgment

After the remand, OSC again declined to investigate. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. At that point, the litigation reached a posture in which there was a final district court disposition from which AFL could appeal.

The sequence matters for understanding the appellate opinion. The district court had already decided the statutory interpretation issue, but it had not granted the remedy AFL wanted. OSC’s second refusal, followed by the final dismissal, created the final judgment that brought the case to the D.C. Circuit.

## The Appeal to the D.C. Circuit and the Court’s De Novo Review

AFL appealed, and the D.C. Circuit reviewed the case de novo. De novo review means the court of appeals did not defer to the district court’s legal conclusions; it independently examined the issues. Because the district court had dismissed the case on the merits, the appeal initially looked like a straightforward review of statutory interpretation and remedy.

But the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits first.

## Why the Court of Appeals Addressed Standing Before the Merits Despite the District Court’s Merits-Based Disposition

Before deciding whether the district court correctly interpreted § 1216 and § 552, the D.C. Circuit asked whether AFL had Article III standing. This step was necessary because standing goes to subject-matter jurisdiction. A federal court cannot decide the merits if the plaintiff lacks standing, even if the district court already ruled on the merits.

The court therefore treated standing as a threshold issue. It explained that Article III limits federal courts to actual “Cases” and “Controversies,” and that standing is the doctrine that enforces that limitation. Because neither party had initially addressed standing, the court ordered supplemental briefing on the issue. Only after concluding that AFL lacked standing did the court vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

In other words, the district court’s statutory ruling became irrelevant once the court of appeals determined that AFL could not invoke federal judicial power at all. The merits discussion in the district court was overtaken by the jurisdictional defect.

# 3. Article III Standing and the Court’s Procedural-Injury Framework

## Article III’s Case-or-Controversy Requirement as the Source of Standing Doctrine

The court began with a threshold question that is easy to miss if one reads straight to the merits: did AFL have constitutional standing to sue at all? Article III allows federal courts to decide only “Cases” and “Controversies,” and standing doctrine is the main way courts enforce that limit. Before a federal court may interpret a statute, determine whether an agency acted unlawfully, or order relief, the plaintiff must show that it is the proper party to invoke the judicial power.

That is why the D.C. Circuit paused to examine standing even though neither party had initially focused on it. Standing is not a technicality; it is a subject-matter-jurisdiction requirement. If standing is missing, the federal courts have no authority to resolve the dispute on the merits. The court therefore treated standing as antecedent to the district court’s statutory rulings and, ultimately, to any appellate review of those rulings.

## The Three Familiar Standing Elements: Injury in Fact, Causation, and Redressability

The court recited the familiar tripartite standing test from Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife. A plaintiff must show: first, an injury in fact; second, that the injury was caused by the defendant; and third, that a favorable judicial decision would likely redress the injury. Injury in fact means an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete and particularized and actual or imminent, not merely conjectural or hypothetical.

These elements work together. A plaintiff cannot rely on an abstract objection to government conduct. The plaintiff must identify a personal stake in the outcome, connect the claimed harm to the defendant’s conduct, and show that the court can meaningfully remedy the harm. In this case, AFL tried to do that by arguing that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under § 1216 harmed AFL in two ways: it allegedly deprived AFL of records DOJ should have produced under FOIA, and it deprived AFL of the investigatory notices and information AFL says OSC should have supplied.

## The Pleading Burden at the Motion-to-Dismiss Stage

Because the case was being reviewed after dismissal, AFL had the burden of alleging facts that plausibly supported each standing element. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, standing is not proven with evidence, but it must be plausibly alleged. The court cited D.C. Circuit authority for the proposition that the plaintiff’s complaint and briefing must set out facts showing injury, causation, and redressability.

That matters because AFL’s standing theory depended on a chain of future events, not on a direct injury caused immediately by OSC. The court therefore asked whether AFL had plausibly shown that OSC’s refusal to investigate actually caused a concrete harm that a judicial order could redress. The court concluded it had not.

## Why Federal Courts Must Examine Standing Before Reaching the Merits

The panel emphasized that standing had to be addressed before the court could decide whether OSC had misread § 1216. That ordering follows from the basic separation-of-powers function of standing doctrine. A court cannot assume jurisdiction and then interpret a statute to justify it. As the panel framed it, the standing question was not separate from the case; it was the gateway to deciding whether there was a case at all.

This is why the court did not simply affirm or reverse the district court’s statutory interpretation. Even if AFL were right about the meaning of § 1216, the federal courts still could not grant relief unless AFL had a constitutionally sufficient injury that was traceable to OSC and redressable by a court order.

## AFL’s Theory That OSC Violated a Statutory Procedural Right to an Investigation Under § 1216

AFL’s central theory was framed as a procedural-right claim. AFL argued that § 1216 gave it a right to have OSC investigate an allegedly arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding, and that OSC violated that procedural entitlement by refusing to investigate DOJ’s claimed policy of not searching employee emails without consent.

That framing mattered because procedural-right cases sometimes receive a somewhat more forgiving standing analysis. The plaintiff does not have to show that the procedure would certainly have produced a different substantive outcome. But the plaintiff still must show that the procedural violation affected some concrete interest of its own. A bare failure to follow procedure, standing alone, is not enough.

## The Rule from Summers v. Earth Island Institute That a Procedural Right in the Abstract Is Insufficient

The court invoked Summers v. Earth Island Institute for a key limitation: the deprivation of a procedural right “in vacuo” does not create Article III standing. In other words, a plaintiff cannot establish standing merely by pointing to a statutory procedure that was allegedly not followed. There must be some concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect, and the violation of the procedure must bear on that interest.

That principle was fatal to AFL’s attempt to treat OSC’s refusal to investigate as a standalone injury. Even if § 1216 uses mandatory language, the constitutional question is whether AFL itself suffered a concrete injury from the failure to comply with that statutory command. The court held that AFL had not shown such an injury.

## The D.C. Circuit’s Relaxed Standing Approach for Procedural-Rights Cases

The court then explained the more lenient standing principles that sometimes apply when a plaintiff alleges violation of a procedural right. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, courts relax—though do not eliminate—the imminence and redressability requirements when the plaintiff claims that a statutory procedure designed to protect a threatened concrete interest was denied. The reason for the relaxation is practical: if the plaintiff’s concrete interest is threatened by a failure to follow procedure, the harm may be more indirect, and the plaintiff may not be able to show with certainty what would have happened had the procedure been followed.

But the relaxation is limited. The plaintiff still must show a concrete, particularized interest and some causal connection between the violation and that interest. The court made clear that even under this more forgiving framework, AFL had not met the necessary showing.

## What Remained Required Even Under the Relaxed Standard: A Concrete, Particularized Interest and Adequate Causation

Applying that framework, the court held that AFL did not satisfy either the ordinary standing test or the more forgiving procedural-injury version. AFL’s first asserted injury was that DOJ had not produced the FOIA records it requested. But that injury, the court said, was caused by DOJ’s own conduct, not by OSC’s refusal to investigate. OSC’s inaction might, at most, be one step in a speculative chain that could someday influence DOJ, but standing cannot rest on speculation about how independent actors will respond.

The court also rejected AFL’s attempt to connect the dots through OSC enforcement. The chain required assuming that OSC would investigate, then choose to pursue corrective or disciplinary action, then the MSPB would sanction employees, and then DOJ would respond by producing the records. That is too attenuated. The court relied on the general principle that causation and redressability cannot depend on speculation about independent actors not before the court, and it also noted that OSC’s own enforcement choices are discretionary in important respects.

AFL’s second alleged injury—its asserted interest in information that would have been generated by the OSC investigation—also failed. The court explained that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive the information at issue. Even assuming § 1216 requires OSC to investigate certain FOIA violations, AFL identified no entitlement to receive notices or other information from that investigation. AFL tried to rely on the notice provisions associated with § 1214, but it conceded that those requirements are discretionary, not mandatory, in a § 1216 investigation. Without an entitlement to those notices, there was no concrete informational injury.

## The Court’s Choice Not to Classify AFL’s Asserted Injuries as Definitively Procedural or Substantive

An important feature of the opinion is that the panel did not finally decide whether AFL’s claimed injuries should be labeled “procedural” or “substantive.” Instead, it concluded that AFL failed even under the relaxed procedural-injury approach. That allowed the court to avoid a harder classification question and focus on the bottom line: whatever label one uses, AFL had not shown the kind of concrete injury and redressable causation Article III demands.

This move is pedagogically useful. In standing analysis, labels are often less important than the actual constitutional showing. Calling something “procedural” does not automatically lower the bar enough to save a case. The plaintiff must still connect the alleged statutory violation to a real-world interest that the law protects.

## How the Standing Inquiry Reframed the Case Away from Statutory Entitlement and Toward Constitutional Justiciability

The standing analysis changed the entire orientation of the case. AFL had argued as though the main question was whether § 1216 required OSC to investigate. The district court had even addressed that statutory question. But the court of appeals reframed the dispute: before asking what § 1216 means, the court had to ask whether AFL had any constitutionally sufficient stake in that meaning.

Once the court answered no, the statutory dispute became irrelevant for jurisdictional purposes. The opinion therefore demonstrates a standard move in federal litigation: a plaintiff may invoke a statute and even allege that an agency misread it, but federal courts still cannot act unless the plaintiff satisfies Article III. Here, the standing inquiry did not just narrow the issues; it disposed of the case. Because AFL lacked standing, the proper disposition was vacatur of the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# 4. Why AFL’s Two Standing Theories Failed

## AFL’s first theory: OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from obtaining the FOIA records it sought from DOJ

The court first addressed AFL’s argument that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused AFL to remain without the records it had requested from DOJ under FOIA. This theory was important because AFL tried to link OSC’s inaction to a concrete harm: the continued nonproduction of documents. But the court held that the theory did not satisfy Article III standing because the injury AFL pointed to was not fairly traceable to OSC, and a favorable judgment against OSC would not likely redress it.

The court began with the basic point that the immediate source of AFL’s injury was DOJ, not OSC. If AFL was injured by not receiving records, that injury came from DOJ’s alleged refusal to disclose them. OSC’s failure to investigate was only one step removed from that harm. The court therefore treated any direct informational injury as attributable to the agency that withheld the documents, not to the separate agency that declined to investigate the withholding.

## Why the court treated any immediate informational injury regarding records as traceable to DOJ rather than OSC

This distinction mattered because standing requires causation. A plaintiff cannot simply identify a government failure somewhere in the background and then attribute all downstream consequences to the defendant. Here, the court reasoned that the records injury was “plainly traceable” to DOJ’s alleged FOIA denial. OSC’s role was secondary and contingent. The court was unwilling to say that the refusal to investigate itself caused the loss of records when DOJ was the actor that actually withheld them.

The court also explained that AFL’s theory depended on a chain of hypothetical events, not on a direct causal link. That kind of chain is often fatal in standing analysis, especially when it relies on several independent decisions by other actors.

## The speculative causal chain identified by the court: OSC investigation, possible OSC enforcement action, possible MSPB sanction, and possible DOJ production

The court laid out AFL’s theory step by step. If OSC had investigated, it might have chosen to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. Then the Merit Systems Protection Board might have sanctioned the responsible employees. After that, DOJ might have decided to produce the requested records. Each link in that sequence was uncertain.

That uncertainty made the theory too speculative for Article III. Standing does not permit a plaintiff to rely on a “maybe-if-then” chain stretching across multiple discretionary decisions and different agencies. The court emphasized that AFL had not shown that a favorable ruling would likely cause the records to be released. At best, the ruling might prompt some investigative activity, which might or might not lead to enforcement, which might or might not change DOJ’s behavior.

## The significance of independent actors not before the court, especially MSPB and DOJ

The presence of independent actors was central to the court’s reasoning. MSPB and DOJ were not parties to the lawsuit, yet AFL’s theory assumed that each would react in a way that would eventually benefit AFL. The court treated that as a classic red flag for causation and redressability. When relief depends on the choices of entities outside the court’s control, especially nonparties, the causal link becomes too uncertain.

This is why the court said the theory required speculation about “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” The problem was not just that those actors might act differently than AFL hoped; it was that the court could not presume they would act at all, or act in the expected way, based on a judgment against OSC.

## The court’s use of FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine on speculation about third-party choices

To reinforce that point, the court cited FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine. That case stands for the proposition that causation cannot rest on speculation about the choices of independent third parties. The court used that principle here to show that AFL’s causal chain was too remote. Even if OSC had investigated, the court could not assume that MSPB would sanction anyone, or that DOJ would then disclose records.

This citation helped the court frame the standing problem in familiar terms: when relief depends on the voluntary actions of others, especially those not joined in the suit, the plaintiff usually cannot show the kind of concrete causal connection Article III requires.

## The court’s invocation of Heckler v. Chaney and the problem of agency enforcement discretion

The court also relied on Heckler v. Chaney to underscore that OSC’s own enforcement choices are discretionary. That matters because AFL’s theory assumed the court could predict, and perhaps effectively compel, OSC to take enforcement steps once an investigation began. But the court reminded readers that agencies often have broad discretion in deciding whether to pursue enforcement.

In other words, even before the theory reached MSPB or DOJ, it already depended on a discretionary decision by OSC. The court would not treat an uncertain enforcement response as a reliable step toward redressing AFL’s injury. This made the theory even more speculative.

## Why a favorable judgment would not “very likely” redress AFL’s inability to obtain records

The redressability inquiry asks whether a court order is likely to make the plaintiff’s injury go away. The court concluded that AFL could not make that showing. A judgment requiring OSC to investigate would not itself produce the requested records. It would only perhaps start a process that might eventually lead to records disclosure.

The court therefore held that AFL had not shown that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its injury. That phrase captures the key point: Article III demands more than a hope that litigation might trigger a series of favorable events. The chain must be sufficiently likely, and here it was not.

## The court’s reliance on Haaland v. Brackeen to stress that redressability generally depends on relief binding the parties, not persuasive effect on outsiders

The court added another important redressability point from Haaland v. Brackeen: redressability generally depends on what the judgment binds, not on whether the court’s reasoning might influence people who are not parties. This mattered because AFL’s theory implicitly assumed that a court ruling against OSC might persuade DOJ to release the records.

The court rejected that approach. A judgment that merely exerts persuasive pressure on outsiders is not the same as a judgment that legally binds them. Since DOJ was not the defendant in this case, a ruling against OSC would not directly require DOJ to disclose anything. That made the requested relief too weak to satisfy redressability.

## AFL’s second theory: OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of notices and information associated with an OSC investigation

AFL’s second standing theory focused not on the records themselves, but on the process of investigation. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of the notices and information that would accompany an OSC investigation. In effect, AFL attempted to turn the absence of an investigation into a separate informational injury.

The court rejected that theory as well. Its reasoning was more doctrinal than factual: AFL had to show not just that it wanted information, but that a statute entitled it to receive that information. Without such an entitlement, there was no concrete informational injury.

## The doctrine of informational standing: denial of information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive

Informational standing is a narrow doctrine. A plaintiff suffers a concrete injury when it is denied information that a statute gives it a right to obtain. The court cited cases such as Public Citizen, Akins, and ASCPA v. Feld to explain that the injury arises from the withholding of information the law requires to be disclosed.

That framing is crucial. A plaintiff does not get standing merely because it would like to know more. The statute must create an entitlement to the specific information. Otherwise, the alleged injury is not concrete in the Article III sense.

## Why § 1216(a)(3), even if mandatory as to investigation, does not itself create an entitlement to information

Applying that doctrine, the court held that § 1216(a)(3) did not help AFL. Even if that provision requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, it says nothing about providing AFL with investigative notices or other information. A mandate to investigate is not the same thing as a right to receive information from the investigation.

That was the decisive gap in AFL’s second theory. The statute may, on AFL’s reading, require OSC to act, but it does not automatically require OSC to inform AFL of the details of that action. Without a statutory right to receive the information, AFL could not establish informational standing.

## AFL’s attempt to rely on § 1214 notice provisions through § 1216 investigations

AFL tried to bridge that gap by pointing to the notice provisions in § 1214. Its argument was that if OSC investigates under § 1216, it may use the same procedures that apply to prohibited personnel practice investigations under § 1214, including notice requirements. If so, AFL argued, it should receive the kind of updates and information that § 1214 provides.

But the court did not accept that move. AFL was effectively asking the court to import the § 1214 notice scheme into § 1216 investigations, even though the statutory text does not clearly do that by itself. The court found that AFL had not established a legal entitlement to those procedural disclosures.

## Why that argument failed because AFL did not contend that § 1214 notice requirements must apply in § 1216 investigations

A key reason AFL lost was the way it framed its own argument. The court noted that AFL did not actually contend that § 1214’s notice provisions are mandatory in § 1216 investigations. Instead, AFL acknowledged that those procedures may be optional. That concession undermined its claim to a concrete injury.

If the notices are discretionary, then their absence cannot be an injury caused by the denial of a statutory entitlement. At most, AFL could say that OSC might have chosen to provide more information. But a hoped-for discretionary benefit is not enough for informational standing.

## The importance of Carson v. Office of Special Counsel and the discretionary nature of using § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation

The court relied on Carson v. Office of Special Counsel to support that point. Carson held that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. That precedent was fatal to AFL’s second theory because it showed there was no mandatory right to the notices AFL wanted.

Once the court accepted that § 1214 procedures were discretionary in this context, AFL could not claim that OSC’s failure to provide them deprived it of information the statute guaranteed. Without a guarantee, there was no concrete informational injury.

## Why the absence of a statutory entitlement defeated concrete injury and also undermined redressability for the second theory

The absence of a statutory entitlement mattered in two ways. First, it meant AFL had not shown injury in fact: there was no legally protected interest in the information it claimed to have lost. Second, it meant redressability was weak as well. Even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, that would not necessarily require OSC to provide § 1214-style notices or disclosures.

So the second theory failed on both sides of the standing analysis. AFL could not show that it was deprived of information the law required OSC to give it, and it could not show that a judicial order would likely supply that information. That left AFL without Article III standing on either theory.

# 5. Holding, Disposition, and the Opinion’s Significance

## The Court’s Holding: No Article III Standing

The D.C. Circuit’s bottom-line holding was not about the meaning of the Civil Service Reform Act or FOIA. It was about jurisdiction. The court held that America First Legal Foundation lacked Article III standing to bring this suit. That meant AFL could not proceed in federal court, even if its statutory theory about the Office of Special Counsel’s investigative duties were correct.

This is an important distinction. A plaintiff can have what looks like a strong argument on the merits and still lose if it cannot show the concrete injury, causation, and redressability required by Article III. Here, the court concluded that AFL’s complaint was really an effort to force OSC to investigate DOJ and possibly trigger further enforcement steps. But AFL could not show that OSC’s failure to investigate caused a legally cognizable injury that a court order against OSC would likely remedy.

## The Disposition: Vacatur and Remand to Dismiss for Lack of Jurisdiction

Because standing is a threshold requirement, the court could not leave the district court’s merits judgment in place. Instead, the D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

That disposition matters procedurally. “Vacate” means the appellate court nullified the district court’s judgment. The merits ruling below therefore does not stand as a binding adjudication of AFL’s statutory claims. The remand instruction tells the district court what to do next: dismiss the case because the federal courts lack power to decide it on the merits.

## Why the Court Did Not Decide the Statutory Merits

A federal court may not reach the merits unless Article III standing exists. That is why the opinion begins its jurisdictional analysis by citing the constitutional case-or-controversy requirement and then applying the familiar standing elements from Lujan: injury in fact, causation, and redressability.

The court’s reasoning shows why standing can be fatal even when a plaintiff has identified a statutory provision that appears to support its requested relief. AFL argued that § 1216 required OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, and the district court had accepted part of that reading. But the D.C. Circuit never had authority to resolve whether that interpretation was right, because AFL still had to show that it personally suffered a concrete injury traceable to OSC and likely to be fixed by a court order. Without that showing, the court had no jurisdiction to answer the statutory question.

## What Remains Unresolved About § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

The opinion leaves open the substantive relationship between § 1216 and FOIA’s separate enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That relationship was the statutory dispute at the center of the case. AFL wanted the court to hold that OSC had to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy under § 1216 even though the specific triggers in FOIA’s own OSC-enforcement provision were not met.

The D.C. Circuit did not decide whether that interpretation is correct. Nor did it decide whether OSC has broad or narrow authority to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings under § 1216. Those issues remain undecided because the case was resolved on standing grounds alone.

So the opinion is best read as a jurisdictional decision, not a definitive construction of the statutory scheme. That matters for future litigants: the court’s holding does not finally settle the scope of OSC’s authority under these provisions.

## A Favorable Statutory Theory Is Not the Same as a Justiciable Case

One of the clearest lessons from the opinion is that a plaintiff can lose even if it has a plausible legal argument on the statute. AFL may have believed that the statute required OSC to act. But standing asks a different question: did the plaintiff suffer a concrete injury that the court can likely redress?

The court’s answer was no. AFL’s claimed harms were too indirect. Its first theory depended on a chain of events: OSC would investigate; OSC might pursue corrective or disciplinary action; the MSPB might act; DOJ might then change course and disclose records. Its second theory depended on OSC providing investigatory notices and information that AFL had no independent entitlement to receive. Neither theory established the kind of direct, judicially redressable injury Article III requires.

In other words, it is not enough to show that an agency may have violated a statute. The plaintiff must also show that the violation injured the plaintiff in a concrete way and that a court order against the defendant will probably fix the injury.

## The Limits of Suits to Force Agency Investigation or Enforcement

This case is a strong example of the limits on lawsuits that try to compel agency investigation or enforcement activity. The court was especially skeptical of AFL’s attempt to turn OSC’s discretionary or intermediate enforcement decisions into a basis for standing. The problem was not only injury, but also the uncertainty created by multiple decisionmakers.

OSC is one actor. The Merit Systems Protection Board is another. DOJ is a third. AFL’s requested relief would not itself force disclosure of the documents it wanted. At most, it might prompt OSC to begin an investigation that could, through several additional discretionary steps by other actors, eventually lead to a change in DOJ’s behavior. That is too speculative for Article III standing.

The opinion also reflects a familiar principle from enforcement cases: courts are reluctant to assume that one agency’s decision to investigate or prosecute will automatically produce the downstream result a plaintiff wants. The mere possibility that an investigation might cause later action does not make the injury redressable.

## Informational Injury Requires a Real Statutory Entitlement

AFL tried to recast its case as an informational injury case, but the court rejected that theory as well. The opinion draws a sharp line: informational standing exists only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive specific information and the plaintiff is denied it.

That is why the court found AFL’s first theory insufficient. AFL had a claim to records under FOIA against DOJ, but the alleged failure by OSC to investigate was not the source of the informational injury. And AFL’s second theory also failed because § 1216, even if it required an investigation, did not itself give AFL a right to the notices and information that accompany a § 1214 investigation. In short, a statute that authorizes or requires an agency to investigate is not the same thing as a statute that requires the agency to disclose information to the requester.

This part of the opinion is a useful reminder that “informational injury” is not a free-floating concept. The plaintiff must point to an actual statutory entitlement to the information allegedly withheld.

## Causation and Redressability When Relief Depends on Third Parties

The opinion also illustrates the modern standing problem posed by chains of independent decisionmaking. The court emphasized that AFL’s alleged injury depended on speculative choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ. That kind of chain weakens both causation and redressability.

Causation is weak because the injury comes immediately from DOJ’s alleged refusal to disclose records, not from OSC’s failure to investigate. Redressability is weak because a favorable ruling against OSC would not itself require DOJ to release documents. At best, it might influence behavior indirectly. But standing doctrine generally does not accept “maybe it will persuade someone else” as enough.

The court’s redressability discussion is especially important. It stressed that what matters is whether the judgment binds the defendant in a way that likely remedies the plaintiff’s injury, not whether the court’s reasoning might persuade outsiders to act differently. That is a recurring theme in standing cases and a central reason AFL lost here.

## The Case in Modern Standing Doctrine

This opinion fits comfortably within recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit standing precedent. It applies the standard Lujan framework, follows Summers in rejecting a procedural-right claim disconnected from a concrete interest, and relies on modern cases stressing that causation and redressability cannot rest on speculation about independent actors.

It also reflects the D.C. Circuit’s careful policing of Article III in cases seeking government action rather than direct relief. Even where a plaintiff frames the dispute as one about statutory procedure, the court still asks whether the alleged violation actually injured the plaintiff in a concrete way. If the answer is no, the federal courts must dismiss.

The larger lesson is straightforward: a plaintiff may have a colorable argument that a statute imposes duties on an agency, but unless the plaintiff can show a direct, personal, and judicially redressable injury from the agency’s failure to act, the federal courts cannot hear the case.
