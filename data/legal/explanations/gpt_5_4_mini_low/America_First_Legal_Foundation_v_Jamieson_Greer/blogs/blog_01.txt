Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedure Without Payoff: The D.C. Circuit’s Standing Limit on Efforts to Compel OSC FOIA Investigations

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is less about FOIA than about the limits of statutory process claims in federal court. America First Legal Foundation wanted to force the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged FOIA-withholding policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, which says OSC “shall” investigate certain arbitrary withholding allegations. But the court never reached the merits of that statutory argument. Instead, Judge Katsas held that AFL lacked Article III standing because the claimed right to an OSC investigation was not tied to a concrete injury the court could redress.

That matters well beyond this dispute. Litigants increasingly try to convert mandatory-sounding statutory procedures into standalone causes of action. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion is a reminder that “shall” is not enough. Even where Congress has created a procedural entitlement, a plaintiff still has to show a concrete interest affected by the violation, plus causation and redressability sufficient to satisfy Article III.

## The court’s standing analysis

The court began with the familiar basics from *Lujan*: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. AFL said OSC violated its statutory right to an investigation under § 1216. But as the panel noted, a “procedural right in vacuo” does not create standing. That phrasing, drawn from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and D.C. Circuit procedural-injury cases, does a lot of work here. A plaintiff cannot sue simply because an agency failed to follow a process Congress prescribed; the plaintiff must connect that procedural failure to some concrete, particularized harm.

AFL tried to do that in two ways. First, it argued that if OSC had investigated DOJ, the investigation might have led to enforcement action, which might have induced DOJ to produce the withheld records. The panel found that chain far too speculative. It depended on discretionary choices by OSC, possible action by the MSPB, and then a further possibility that DOJ would respond by disclosing records. That is not the kind of direct redressability Article III requires, especially where the alleged remedy turns on choices by multiple independent actors not before the court. The opinion also stressed that courts generally cannot compel OSC’s enforcement discretion, invoking *Heckler v. Chaney*.

Second, AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of information it would have received in the course of an investigation, including notice and updates. But the panel rejected that theory because § 1216 does not itself entitle a complainant to the information AFL wanted. AFL tried to borrow the notice provisions from § 1214, which govern prohibited personnel practice investigations, but the court said that was a stretch. Even if OSC must investigate under § 1216, AFL did not show it had a statutory right to the particular disclosures it claimed.

## What the court is really saying

The opinion is an important limit on a familiar litigating move: identify a mandatory statutory command, frame its violation as procedural injury, and then argue that the process itself is the protected interest. The D.C. Circuit did not deny that Congress can create statutory obligations for agencies. It said something narrower but constitutionally significant: not every statutory obligation creates judicially cognizable standing for every disappointed complainant.

That distinction is especially sharp here because AFL’s real grievance was not the absence of OSC process in the abstract. It wanted either more records from DOJ or pressure on DOJ through an OSC investigation. The court treated both as too remote from the injury alleged. That is a classic standing analysis, but it also reflects a broader skepticism toward efforts to use one agency’s enforcement machinery to redress a dispute with another agency.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

The decision strengthens the D.C. Circuit’s already demanding procedural-standing doctrine. By citing *Summers*, *Fla. Audubon*, and *Center for Law and Education*, the panel reinforced the proposition that a procedural right must protect a concrete interest, not just create a freestanding administrative entitlement. That will likely matter in future suits seeking to compel investigations, referrals, reports, or other agency follow-on actions that are only indirectly connected to a plaintiff’s substantive complaint.

The opinion also suggests that courts will scrutinize redressability especially closely when the requested relief depends on a chain of discretionary decisions across multiple agencies. That is a meaningful constraint in regulatory and oversight litigation, where plaintiffs often ask courts to order one agency to trigger another agency’s processes. The D.C. Circuit is signaling that such claims will have a hard time clearing Article III unless the plaintiff can show a direct legal entitlement to the information or action sought.

## Bottom line

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a clean application of standing doctrine, but it carries a broader message: mandatory language in a statute does not automatically translate into a justiciable right to compel agency action. If the only alleged injury is the denial of a procedure, the plaintiff still has to show that the procedure protects a concrete interest and that a favorable decision will likely redress the harm. Here, the court found AFL could show neither.
