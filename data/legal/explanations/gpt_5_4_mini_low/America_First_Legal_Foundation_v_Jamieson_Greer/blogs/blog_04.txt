Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Informational Injury After *Akins*: Why Not Every Statutory Information Ecosystem Creates Standing

## Why the D.C. Circuit’s decision matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a useful reminder that “informational injury” is not a magic incantation. Plaintiffs often try to reframe a complaint about agency inaction as a denial of information, because informational standing can be easier to plead than more traditional injuries. But Judge Katsas’s opinion draws an important line: a statute that requires an agency to investigate does not necessarily create a right to receive information from that investigation.

That distinction matters well beyond this case. If courts treated any statutory enforcement or oversight mechanism as an “information entitlement,” then plaintiffs could transform disappointed petitions for agency action into Article III cases simply by pointing to the downstream possibility that an investigation might uncover something useful. The panel refused to go there, and in doing so it tightened the doctrinal boundary between true disclosure statutes and statutes that merely set an administrative process in motion.

## The statutory setup and the plaintiff’s theory

AFL wanted the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. It pointed to 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations concerning arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. AFL argued that OSC had a mandatory duty to investigate even if FOIA itself would not have triggered the separate disciplinary mechanism in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

But before the panel reached that statutory dispute, it held that AFL lacked standing. That holding is the real doctrinal contribution of the case. AFL tried to ground standing in two alleged informational injuries: first, DOJ still had not given it the records it sought; second, OSC had not given it the notices and information that would accompany an investigation under § 1214. The court rejected both theories.

## Why the court refused to treat an investigation right as an information right

The first theory was always the harder sell. AFL said that if OSC had investigated, disciplined, or pressured DOJ employees, DOJ might have produced the requested records. The panel had little trouble seeing the problem: the injury from nondisclosure was caused by DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain ran through multiple layers of discretionary decisions by agencies and officials not before the court.

That analysis tracks the Supreme Court’s recent emphasis in *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Brackeen* on the limits of causation and redressability when a plaintiff relies on how third parties might react. A favorable judgment here would not itself produce the records; it would at most set in motion a sequence of speculative enforcement choices. That is not enough.

The second theory is more interesting. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of information that would have come out of the investigation itself. But the panel answered the key question in a very disciplined way: what information does the statute actually entitle the plaintiff to receive? On that point, the answer was none. Section 1216 may require OSC to investigate, but it does not, by its own force, create a right in the requester to receive notice, updates, findings, or other investigatory materials. AFL tried to import the procedural and notification features of § 1214, but the court noted that AFL itself conceded that those requirements are discretionary in the § 1216 context, citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*.

That is the crucial move. *Akins* and *Public Citizen* recognize informational standing where Congress has granted access to specific information and the plaintiff is denied it. They do not recognize standing whenever Congress creates an administrative process that might, incidentally, surface information. The panel’s formulation keeps the doctrine anchored to the statutory entitlement itself, rather than to the plaintiff’s hoped-for informational byproducts.

## The court’s line-drawing is doctrinally sound

The opinion’s distinction between a disclosure right and an investigative regime is persuasive. Informational standing works best when the statutory text is concrete: the plaintiff “is entitled to receive” particular data, records, or disclosures, and the government withholds them. That makes the injury easy to identify and trace.

By contrast, an investigation statute does something different. It tells an agency to look into allegations, not necessarily to disclose what it learns to the complainant. The whole point of such a scheme may be to enable internal oversight, not private access. If the mere existence of an investigative obligation were enough to create informational standing, then virtually every disappointed enforcement request would become a disclosure case in disguise.

That would be a serious expansion of *Akins*. It would also blur the line between private informational rights and public-interest objections to executive enforcement discretion. The Supreme Court has repeatedly resisted that move, and the D.C. Circuit’s opinion fits comfortably within that resistance.

## Implications for administrative-law plaintiffs

There is a broader lesson here for plaintiffs who try to challenge agency nonenforcement by invoking standing theories that sound informational but really seek oversight. After *Greer*, a litigant will have a harder time arguing that “the statute says the agency must investigate” automatically means “I am entitled to the information the investigation might generate.” Courts will likely ask a more precise question: what, exactly, did Congress require the agency to give the plaintiff?

That matters in FOIA-adjacent disputes, election-law complaint systems, ethics investigations, and other administrative schemes where Congress has created complaint processes rather than disclosure mandates. Plaintiffs may still have standing if the statute expressly requires the agency to provide notices, reports, or records to the complainant. But if the claimed injury is only that the agency failed to investigate and therefore failed to create or uncover information, *Greer* suggests that is not enough.

The opinion also helps police an important boundary in Article III doctrine. Informational standing is real, but it is bounded by statutory text. Otherwise, courts would end up converting generalized grievances about agency oversight into cognizable personal injuries. That would be especially problematic in cases like this one, where the plaintiff is effectively asking a court to supervise how an executive-branch enforcement office uses its discretion.

## A modest but important narrowing

This is not a dramatic retreat from *Akins* or *Public Citizen*. Those cases remain good law. But *Greer* narrows their reach in a practical way by insisting on a direct link between the statute and the information allegedly withheld. A statutory duty to investigate is not, by itself, a statutory duty to inform.

That may sound technical, but it is exactly the sort of technicality that keeps informational standing from swallowing the rest of Article III. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion is careful, and perhaps deliberately so: it leaves room for genuine disclosure rights while refusing to let plaintiffs bootstrap standing from the possibility that agency oversight might someday produce useful facts.
