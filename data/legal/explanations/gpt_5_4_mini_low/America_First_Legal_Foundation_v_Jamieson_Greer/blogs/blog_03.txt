Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Section 1216, Section 552(a)(4)(F), and the Vanishing Merits: What the Standing Dismissal Leaves Unresolved About OSC’s FOIA Authority

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is notable less for what it decided than for what it left undecided. The panel held that America First Legal lacked Article III standing to force the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged arbitrary FOIA withholding. That jurisdictional holding wiped away the district court’s merits ruling and sent the case back for dismissal.

But the real significance lies in the statutory question the court did not reach: Does OSC’s broad investigative authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 operate independently of FOIA’s narrower disciplinary trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), or does the FOIA-specific provision cabin OSC’s power to act on alleged arbitrary withholdings? The district court had answered that question one way, OSC another, and the court of appeals sidestepped it entirely. That leaves FOIA requesters, agencies, and OSC itself with an important unresolved issue about when, if ever, a requester can compel OSC attention to an alleged FOIA violation.

## The jurisdictional move

Judge Katsas’s opinion is a straightforward standing opinion in the modern Article III style: identify the asserted injury, test causation and redressability, and insist on something more concrete than a procedural grievance in the abstract. AFL argued that OSC violated a statutory duty to investigate DOJ’s alleged “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of FOIA records. But the panel concluded that AFL could not convert that alleged violation into standing by pointing to two downstream informational harms.

The first theory was that OSC’s failure to investigate kept AFL from getting the requested DOJ records. The court rejected that as too speculative. Any injury from nondisclosure was traceable to DOJ, not OSC; and redress would depend on a long chain of contingencies involving OSC’s enforcement choice, potential MSPB action, and DOJ’s response. The second theory—that AFL had a right to information generated by the OSC investigation itself—failed because AFL could not identify any statutory entitlement to the notices and information it sought. The panel emphasized that even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate, it does not follow that OSC must import § 1214’s notice regime into a § 1216 inquiry.

That is all doctrinally orthodox. The court was careful not to stretch procedural standing beyond the plaintiff’s concrete interests, and it treated redressability with appropriate skepticism. The opinion also fits comfortably with the Supreme Court’s recent emphasis on causation and redressability when relief depends on nonparty choices.

## What the court did not decide

What makes the case interesting is that the panel vacated without resolving the statutory structure underneath the dispute. The district court had held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—FOIA’s more specific disciplinary mechanism—does not limit OSC’s authority under § 1216. In other words, the district court read § 1216 as an independent grant of investigative power over arbitrary FOIA withholdings, even outside the circumstances described in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). But it also held that any follow-on enforcement choice remained discretionary, which is why it refused to order OSC to investigate in the way AFL wanted.

That combination of holdings would have been doctrinally consequential. It would have suggested that requesters might be able to invoke OSC earlier, before a FOIA case culminates in a production order, fee award, and judicial finding that the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. In practical terms, that would create a second enforcement lane for FOIA grievances, one not tied to the remedial trigger Congress wrote into § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

The D.C. Circuit’s standing dismissal leaves that possibility unresolved. The panel did not say whether the district court was right to read § 1216 broadly, nor whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is best understood as a floor, a ceiling, or simply a separate enforcement track. It only said AFL was not the right party, at least on these facts, to force that question into court.

## The likely doctrinal tension

The unresolved statutory question is not trivial. Section 1216 is cast in mandatory language: OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” That sounds broad enough to cover at least some FOIA-withholding allegations even when no court has yet made the findings required by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

But FOIA’s own special OSC provision is much narrower and more structured. Congress tied mandatory disciplinary action to a judicial order, a fee award, and a specific judicial question about arbitrariness. That structure suggests a deliberate choice to condition OSC disciplinary proceedings on a completed FOIA litigation record, rather than on any requester’s bare allegation.

Those two provisions can be read together, but not easily. If § 1216 is self-executing in every alleged FOIA withholding, then § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) looks less like a trigger and more like a duplicative backstop. If, on the other hand, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is the specific FOIA enforcement mechanism, then § 1216 may be better understood as reaching some other category of misconduct, or at least as not overriding the more particular FOIA scheme.

The panel’s standing decision leaves that interpretive tension in place. And because the court did not reach the merits, it also left the district court’s more expansive reading without appellate approval.

## What this means for future litigants

For FOIA requesters, the immediate lesson is that a suit to compel OSC action will face a serious standing problem unless the requester can identify a concrete entitlement that OSC’s inaction itself defeats. A generalized interest in having an agency investigate is not enough. Nor is it enough to argue that a successful OSC investigation might indirectly pressure the agency to release records. The D.C. Circuit treated that causal chain as too contingent.

That means the most plausible path for future plaintiffs may be to anchor their injury in an actual statutory entitlement to information, not in the mere existence of an investigation. But *Greer* makes clear that § 1216, standing alone, may not supply that entitlement. The court’s emphasis on the absence of a statutory right to receive the notice and information AFL wanted suggests that many requester-driven suits will founder before they ever reach the statutory interpretation question.

For OSC, the practical consequence is flexibility. The agency avoided an appellate ruling that would either confirm or foreclose its ability to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings under § 1216 absent the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates. That uncertainty may be attractive to the agency in the short term, but it also invites more litigation over the boundaries of its authority.

For agencies subject to FOIA, the case preserves the status quo for now. There is no appellate holding that OSC can or must investigate any alleged arbitrary withholding upon request. But neither is there a decision foreclosing that possibility. The statutory cloud remains.

## Bottom line

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a reminder that standing can be outcome-determinative not just for the parties, but for the development of the law itself. The D.C. Circuit’s jurisdictional ruling cleaned the case off the merits docket, but in doing so it postponed a significant question about the relationship between § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That question will matter whenever a FOIA requester tries to bypass the ordinary FOIA enforcement path and enlist OSC before a court has entered the kind of order FOIA itself specifies.

For now, the panel has told us only that AFL was not entitled to a judicial answer. It has not told us what the answer would have been.
