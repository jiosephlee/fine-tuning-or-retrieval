Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the D.C. Circuit vacate for lack of standing instead of just affirming the district court's merits dismissal?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The district court seems to have reached the merits and concluded that OSC made a legal error about its authority, but still ultimately dismissed AFL's claims. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit says AFL lacked Article III standing and remands with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Why does the standing problem require vacating the district court's judgment rather than simply affirming the result? Is this because a federal court that lacks Article III jurisdiction is not allowed to decide the merits at all?
Answer:
Yes. The short answer is: **a federal court cannot validly decide the merits if Article III standing is absent**, so once the D.C. Circuit concluded AFL lacked standing, it could not simply leave the merits dismissal in place.

A few points explain the procedural move:

1. **Standing is jurisdictional.**  
   Article III standing is part of subject-matter jurisdiction. The court says exactly that: standing is “a question bearing on whether the district court had subjectmatter jurisdiction,” citing *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*.

2. **A court without jurisdiction has no authority to reach the merits.**  
   If the plaintiff lacked standing, then the district court’s merits discussion was made without constitutional power to adjudicate the dispute. Under *Steel Co.*, federal courts must confirm jurisdiction before reaching the merits.

3. **So the proper appellate disposition is to vacate, not affirm on the merits.**  
   If the appellate court affirmed the district court’s judgment, it would be leaving intact a decision that rested on a merits determination made without jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit instead vacated because the merits judgment cannot stand when the court lacked power to enter it.

4. **The remand instruction reflects the proper dismissal basis.**  
   The appellate court remanded “with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.” That is the correct disposition when standing is absent: the case is dismissed **without prejudice to the merits**, because no merits judgment should have been entered at all.

So your instinct is right: **the standing problem means the federal court was not allowed to decide the merits in the first place**. That is why the D.C. Circuit vacated rather than simply affirming the district court’s dismissal on different grounds.

### How do 5 U.S.C. 7 1216 and FOIA's disciplinary provision in 5 U.S.C. 7 552(a)(4)(F) fit together?
Question:
The opinion discusses two different routes for OSC involvement in alleged FOIA misconduct: section 1216(a)(3), which says OSC 'shall' investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding, and FOIA's own provision in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which requires OSC to initiate a proceeding only after a court orders production, awards fees, and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. OSC seems to have treated the FOIA-specific provision as limiting its authority under section 1216, while the district court rejected that view. What is the best way to understand the relationship between these statutes? Does section 552(a)(4)(F) create a mandatory minimum trigger for OSC action, while section 1216 independently authorizes broader investigations, or is there a stronger argument that the more specific FOIA provision should control?
Answer:
The best way to read the two provisions is as **overlapping but not identical** enforcement mechanisms:

- **5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** is a **narrow, mandatory trigger**. If a court:
  1. orders disclosure,
  2. awards fees,
  3. and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously,

  then OSC **“shall promptly initiate”** a disciplinary proceeding. That is a **required follow-on step** once those predicates are met.

- **5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)** is a **separate grant of authority/duty**. It says OSC **“shall … conduct an investigation”** of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding. On the face of the statute, that language is broader: it is not limited to cases where a court has already made the findings described in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

That is essentially how the district court understood the relationship in the opinion: **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power under § 1216**. The D.C. Circuit did **not** decide the merits; it resolved the case on standing. But the opinion’s statutory description supports the view that the two provisions serve different functions: § 552(a)(4)(F) gives OSC a mandatory duty in a specific FOIA-judgment setting, while § 1216 independently authorizes OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings more generally.

So the cleanest reading is:

> **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is a mandatory minimum trigger for OSC action in a defined class of cases, but it is not the exclusive source of OSC authority over FOIA misconduct.**

There is, of course, a possible **specific-over-general** argument that the FOIA-specific provision should control where the statutes overlap. But the opinion as summarized here reflects the opposite view: because Congress enacted a separate FOIA-related authority in § 1216 and used mandatory language there too, the better reading is that **both provisions can coexist**, with § 552(a)(4)(F) adding a mandatory enforcement mechanism rather than displacing § 1216.

### Why wasn't AFL's loss of a 'mandatory investigation' enough to count as an Article III injury by itself?
Question:
AFL argued that section 1216 gave it a procedural right to have OSC investigate its allegation, and OSC refused. My instinct was that if Congress says an agency 'shall' do something in response to my complaint, then the agency's failure to do it injures me. But the court says a 'procedural right in vacuo' is not enough and relies on Summers. What exactly is missing here? Is the problem that the procedure was not tied closely enough to a separate concrete interest of AFL's, or that the statute created a duty owed to the public at large rather than to this particular complainant?
Answer:
The short answer is: **a statutory command to investigate is not automatically a concrete Article III injury unless the denial of that procedure affects a concrete, particularized interest of the plaintiff.** That is what the court means by a “procedural right in vacuo.”

The D.C. Circuit is applying the rule from **Summers v. Earth Island Institute**:  
> “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation” is not enough for standing.

So even if § 1216 says OSC **“shall … conduct an investigation,”** AFL still had to show more than “OSC failed to follow the statute.” It had to show that the missed investigation itself invaded some concrete interest of AFL’s.

## What was missing here?

The court says AFL could not identify a concrete injury tied to the missed procedure:

1. **No concrete informational entitlement from the investigation itself.**  
   AFL argued that if OSC investigated, it might receive notices and information. But the court rejected that because § 1216 does **not** itself entitle AFL to receive those materials. The only notice provisions it identified are in § 1214, and the court noted AFL did not have a right to have those § 1214 procedures imported into a § 1216 investigation.

2. **No sufficiently direct link to a concrete harm.**  
   AFL tried to connect the failure to investigate to DOJ’s refusal to produce FOIA records. But the court held that chain was too speculative: OSC would have to investigate, then maybe pursue corrective or disciplinary action, then MSPB might act, then DOJ might produce records. That is too dependent on the choices of third parties.

## Is the problem that the duty was owed to the public rather than AFL?

Not exactly. The court’s reasoning is **not** simply that § 1216 is a public-duty statute. The key point is narrower: **even if § 1216 creates a duty to investigate AFL’s complaint, AFL still needed to show that the denial of that procedure injured a concrete interest of AFL.**

In other words:

- **A duty alone is not enough for standing.**
- A plaintiff must show that the lost procedure was tied to some **independent concrete interest**—for example, a statutory right to information, or a procedural protection designed to safeguard a concrete, personal interest.

That is why the court distinguishes between:
- a bare right to have the agency do something, and
- a procedural right that protects a concrete interest and whose denial causes real harm.

## Bottom line

The court is saying that **“mandatory investigation” by itself is only a procedural entitlement, not automatically a constitutional injury.** AFL needed to show that the failure to investigate caused a concrete injury to AFL—such as a denial of information it was legally entitled to receive—but it could not do that on the facts or the statute the court analyzed.

### Why did the court say AFL's FOIA-related injury was not fairly traceable to OSC?
Question:
The court says AFL's inability to get the requested records is traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the causal chain through OSC is too speculative. But if OSC had a mandatory duty to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholding, and if that investigation could lead to corrective or disciplinary proceedings, doesn't that at least indirectly affect DOJ's incentives to comply? How do courts decide when an enforcement-related chain of causation becomes too attenuated for standing purposes, especially where the plaintiff is trying to force one agency to police another agency's conduct?
Answer:
Because standing requires more than “OSC might, in theory, influence DOJ someday.” The court held that AFL’s alleged injury — not getting the FOIA records — was caused immediately by DOJ’s own refusal to produce them, while OSC’s noninvestigation was only an upstream omission whose effect depended on a long chain of discretionary decisions by third parties.

The court’s causation analysis is very much in line with ordinary Article III doctrine:

1. **The injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant.**  
   Under *Lujan* and the cases the court cites, the plaintiff has to show that the defendant’s conduct—not someone else’s—is the source of the injury.

2. **A chain with multiple independent actors is often too speculative.**  
   Here, AFL’s theory was:  
   - OSC investigates DOJ;  
   - OSC might decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action;  
   - the MSPB might impose sanctions;  
   - DOJ might then change course and release the records.  
   
   The court said that this requires speculation about the choices of **two nonparty decisionmakers**: OSC/MSPB and DOJ. It quoted *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the point that causation cannot rest on speculation about the “unfettered choices” of independent actors.

3. **OSC’s duty to investigate does not make DOJ’s withholding traceable to OSC.**  
   Even assuming OSC had a mandatory duty under § 1216(a)(3), the court treated that as an obligation to investigate, not as a direct cause of record production. The injury AFL complains of is the lack of records from DOJ, and that is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.”

4. **The court also emphasized enforcement discretion.**  
   Even if OSC had to investigate, the court noted that it would still be speculative whether OSC would choose to pursue enforcement, and courts generally may not compel that kind of discretionary enforcement decision. That makes the causal link even weaker.

So the key point is this: **an indirect incentive effect is not enough for standing when the plaintiff’s injury depends on a series of contingent, discretionary steps by third parties.** Courts will usually find the chain too attenuated unless the plaintiff can show a much more direct and likely path from the defendant’s conduct to the injury.

Here, the court thought AFL’s theory was exactly the kind of “maybe this investigation would pressure DOJ” argument that Article III does not treat as fairly traceable causation.

### Why didn't the relaxed standing rules for procedural injuries help AFL here?
Question:
The opinion says that when a plaintiff alleges violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest, courts relax imminence and redressability somewhat. But the D.C. Circuit still says AFL fails even under that more forgiving framework. I'm having trouble seeing why. If AFL's concrete interest is access to records or access to investigative information, why isn't an order requiring OSC to perform the allegedly mandatory investigation enough to satisfy the reduced redressability standard? Is the key point that even the relaxed doctrine does not eliminate the need for a non-speculative link between the requested procedure and the concrete benefit?
Answer:
Because the D.C. Circuit thought AFL was trying to do two different things, and neither worked even under the more forgiving procedural-injury doctrine.

### 1) The relaxed rule does **not** eliminate redressability
The court quotes the standard correctly: when a plaintiff alleges violation of a procedural right tied to a concrete interest, courts “relax” imminence and redressability, “but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” The key point is that the plaintiff still must show that the procedure, if properly provided, would likely protect some concrete interest in a non-speculative way.

That is exactly where AFL failed.

---

### 2) On AFL’s “records” theory, redressability was too speculative
AFL said: OSC should investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy; if OSC investigates, it might seek corrective or disciplinary action; that might lead MSPB and then DOJ to release records.

The court found that chain too attenuated because it depends on:

- OSC deciding to investigate and enforce,
- MSPB deciding to sanction,
- DOJ deciding to change its behavior and disclose records.

That is more than the relaxed procedural-injury doctrine permits. The opinion expressly says the chain “requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit” and about “how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion.” So even under relaxed redressability, AFL could not show that an order requiring OSC to investigate would “very likely spur the next steps necessary” to get the records.

In other words: **an order forcing a procedure is not enough if the concrete benefit depends on multiple discretionary and speculative steps by others.**

---

### 3) On AFL’s “investigatory information” theory, AFL lacked an actual entitlement
AFL also tried to say it had an informational injury from not receiving notices and information that would come with an OSC investigation.

But the court rejected that because section 1216 does **not** itself give AFL a right to receive that information. AFL was trying to import section 1214’s notice provisions into a section 1216 investigation, but the opinion says AFL “does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations,” and it cites authority treating that choice as discretionary.

So even if OSC had to investigate, AFL still had no statutory right to the information it claimed to be missing. Without a statutory entitlement to that information, there is no concrete informational injury to redress.

---

### Bottom line
Yes — your intuition is right. The court’s point is that **the relaxed standing doctrine for procedural injuries still requires a non-speculative link between the missing procedure and a concrete benefit to the plaintiff**.

Here, AFL could not show that:
1. compelling OSC to investigate would likely produce the records it wanted, or
2. AFL had a legal entitlement to the investigative notices/information it sought.

So the “relaxed” doctrine did not save standing. It softens the redressability inquiry, but it does not erase it.

### What counts as 'informational standing,' and why didn't AFL qualify for it here?
Question:
The court distinguishes cases like Akins and Public Citizen, where denial of information required by statute was enough for standing, from AFL's claim here. AFL argued that if OSC conducted the investigation, it might receive notices and information associated with section 1214 investigations. The court rejected that because AFL had no statutory entitlement to that information in a section 1216 investigation. Is informational standing always dependent on a clear statutory right to receive specific information, as opposed to a possibility that information may be generated or disclosed during some agency process? If so, how specific does the statute have to be?
Answer:
Yes—at least in the D.C. Circuit’s formulation, **informational standing depends on the plaintiff being denied information that a statute actually entitles the plaintiff to receive**. A mere hope that information might later be generated, or might be disclosed as a byproduct of some agency process, is not enough.

That is exactly how the court distinguishes AFL’s case from **Akins** and **Public Citizen**. Those cases involved statutes that **required disclosure of specific information to the plaintiffs**. Because the plaintiffs were statutorily entitled to receive that information, the denial itself was a concrete injury.

Here, by contrast, the court says that even if § 1216(a)(3) required OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it **“creates no entitlement to receive information.”** AFL’s theory was only that, if OSC investigated under § 1216, OSC **might** choose to give it notices and updates of the sort associated with a § 1214 investigation. But the court rejected that because AFL did **not** allege a right to those § 1214 notices in a § 1216 investigation. The opinion even notes that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, citing **Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel**.

So the rule from this opinion is:

- **Informational standing requires a concrete statutory entitlement to specific information.**
- The entitlement must be **real and present**, not contingent on agency discretion or on some separate process that may or may not produce information.
- The statute need not spell out every detail with mathematical precision, but it must clearly give the plaintiff a right to receive the information at issue.

That is why AFL failed here: it had, at most, a claim to have OSC investigate. It did **not** have a statutory right to the information it said would flow from that investigation. Without that entitlement, there was no informational injury for Article III standing.

### What exactly was discretionary versus mandatory in OSC's response to an alleged FOIA violation?
Question:
I am struggling with the opinion's treatment of statutory discretion. Section 1216(a)(3) says OSC 'shall' conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding, which sounds mandatory. But the opinion also emphasizes OSC's enforcement discretion and cites Heckler v. Chaney, and it notes that under Carson the decision whether to use section 1214's notice procedures in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. So which parts of OSC's conduct are mandatory and which parts remain committed to agency discretion? Is the idea that even if opening some investigation is mandatory, the depth of the investigation, the use of section 1214 procedures, and any follow-on enforcement choices are still discretionary?
Answer:
The opinion draws a fairly sharp line between **whether OSC must start an investigation** and **how OSC conducts that investigation once it exists**.

## 1. What is mandatory?

The court reads **5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)** as using mandatory language:

> OSC “**shall** … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”

So, at least on the statute’s face, the **threshold decision to investigate an allegation** of arbitrary/capricious FOIA withholding is framed as mandatory.

That is why AFL argued OSC had no choice but to investigate its allegation, and why the district court thought OSC’s refusal rested on a legal mistake.

## 2. What remains discretionary?

The opinion makes clear that **not everything that might happen after an investigation is mandatory**.

### a. Whether OSC uses § 1214 procedures
Section 1216(c) says OSC **“may”** investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”

The court relies on this permissive wording, and on **Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel**, to explain that **the decision whether to apply § 1214’s notice/procedural framework in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary**.

That is the key point the court uses in rejecting AFL’s second standing theory: even if an investigation were mandatory, **AFL had no entitlement to the § 1214 notices and information**, because OSC was not required to follow § 1214’s procedures in the first place.

### b. Whether OSC seeks corrective or disciplinary action
The opinion also treats OSC’s follow-on enforcement choices as discretionary. It cites **Heckler v. Chaney** for the general principle that agency enforcement decisions are ordinarily committed to agency discretion and not judicially compellable.

So even if OSC had to investigate, the court says AFL’s claimed causal chain still depended on speculative choices OSC might make afterward:

- OSC might decide to pursue corrective action under § 1214,
- or disciplinary action under § 1215,
- or do neither.

Those are discretionary enforcement judgments.

## 3. What about “the depth” of the investigation?

The opinion does **not** expressly say “depth” in so many words, but its reasoning points the same way: the statute appears to mandate **an investigation**, not any particular **scope, manner, or result** of investigation. The court’s discussion of discretion, Heckler, and Carson all support the idea that **how OSC investigates** is not fully prescribed by § 1216(a)(3).

## 4. Bottom line

Yes—your understanding is basically right.

Under the opinion:

- **Mandatory:** OSC must conduct an investigation when § 1216(a)(3) is triggered by an allegation of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding.
- **Discretionary:** OSC’s use of § 1214 notice procedures, its decision whether to seek corrective or disciplinary action, and its broader enforcement choices remain committed to agency discretion.

So the opinion treats § 1216(a)(3) as imposing a mandatory duty to investigate, but **not** a mandatory duty to investigate in any particular way or to proceed to enforcement.
