Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # 3. Article III Standing and the Court’s Procedural-Injury Framework

## Article III’s Case-or-Controversy Requirement as the Source of Standing Doctrine

The court began with a threshold question that is easy to miss if one reads straight to the merits: did AFL have constitutional standing to sue at all? Article III allows federal courts to decide only “Cases” and “Controversies,” and standing doctrine is the main way courts enforce that limit. Before a federal court may interpret a statute, determine whether an agency acted unlawfully, or order relief, the plaintiff must show that it is the proper party to invoke the judicial power.

That is why the D.C. Circuit paused to examine standing even though neither party had initially focused on it. Standing is not a technicality; it is a subject-matter-jurisdiction requirement. If standing is missing, the federal courts have no authority to resolve the dispute on the merits. The court therefore treated standing as antecedent to the district court’s statutory rulings and, ultimately, to any appellate review of those rulings.

## The Three Familiar Standing Elements: Injury in Fact, Causation, and Redressability

The court recited the familiar tripartite standing test from Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife. A plaintiff must show: first, an injury in fact; second, that the injury was caused by the defendant; and third, that a favorable judicial decision would likely redress the injury. Injury in fact means an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete and particularized and actual or imminent, not merely conjectural or hypothetical.

These elements work together. A plaintiff cannot rely on an abstract objection to government conduct. The plaintiff must identify a personal stake in the outcome, connect the claimed harm to the defendant’s conduct, and show that the court can meaningfully remedy the harm. In this case, AFL tried to do that by arguing that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under § 1216 harmed AFL in two ways: it allegedly deprived AFL of records DOJ should have produced under FOIA, and it deprived AFL of the investigatory notices and information AFL says OSC should have supplied.

## The Pleading Burden at the Motion-to-Dismiss Stage

Because the case was being reviewed after dismissal, AFL had the burden of alleging facts that plausibly supported each standing element. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, standing is not proven with evidence, but it must be plausibly alleged. The court cited D.C. Circuit authority for the proposition that the plaintiff’s complaint and briefing must set out facts showing injury, causation, and redressability.

That matters because AFL’s standing theory depended on a chain of future events, not on a direct injury caused immediately by OSC. The court therefore asked whether AFL had plausibly shown that OSC’s refusal to investigate actually caused a concrete harm that a judicial order could redress. The court concluded it had not.

## Why Federal Courts Must Examine Standing Before Reaching the Merits

The panel emphasized that standing had to be addressed before the court could decide whether OSC had misread § 1216. That ordering follows from the basic separation-of-powers function of standing doctrine. A court cannot assume jurisdiction and then interpret a statute to justify it. As the panel framed it, the standing question was not separate from the case; it was the gateway to deciding whether there was a case at all.

This is why the court did not simply affirm or reverse the district court’s statutory interpretation. Even if AFL were right about the meaning of § 1216, the federal courts still could not grant relief unless AFL had a constitutionally sufficient injury that was traceable to OSC and redressable by a court order.

## AFL’s Theory That OSC Violated a Statutory Procedural Right to an Investigation Under § 1216

AFL’s central theory was framed as a procedural-right claim. AFL argued that § 1216 gave it a right to have OSC investigate an allegedly arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding, and that OSC violated that procedural entitlement by refusing to investigate DOJ’s claimed policy of not searching employee emails without consent.

That framing mattered because procedural-right cases sometimes receive a somewhat more forgiving standing analysis. The plaintiff does not have to show that the procedure would certainly have produced a different substantive outcome. But the plaintiff still must show that the procedural violation affected some concrete interest of its own. A bare failure to follow procedure, standing alone, is not enough.

## The Rule from Summers v. Earth Island Institute That a Procedural Right in the Abstract Is Insufficient

The court invoked Summers v. Earth Island Institute for a key limitation: the deprivation of a procedural right “in vacuo” does not create Article III standing. In other words, a plaintiff cannot establish standing merely by pointing to a statutory procedure that was allegedly not followed. There must be some concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect, and the violation of the procedure must bear on that interest.

That principle was fatal to AFL’s attempt to treat OSC’s refusal to investigate as a standalone injury. Even if § 1216 uses mandatory language, the constitutional question is whether AFL itself suffered a concrete injury from the failure to comply with that statutory command. The court held that AFL had not shown such an injury.

## The D.C. Circuit’s Relaxed Standing Approach for Procedural-Rights Cases

The court then explained the more lenient standing principles that sometimes apply when a plaintiff alleges violation of a procedural right. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, courts relax—though do not eliminate—the imminence and redressability requirements when the plaintiff claims that a statutory procedure designed to protect a threatened concrete interest was denied. The reason for the relaxation is practical: if the plaintiff’s concrete interest is threatened by a failure to follow procedure, the harm may be more indirect, and the plaintiff may not be able to show with certainty what would have happened had the procedure been followed.

But the relaxation is limited. The plaintiff still must show a concrete, particularized interest and some causal connection between the violation and that interest. The court made clear that even under this more forgiving framework, AFL had not met the necessary showing.

## What Remained Required Even Under the Relaxed Standard: A Concrete, Particularized Interest and Adequate Causation

Applying that framework, the court held that AFL did not satisfy either the ordinary standing test or the more forgiving procedural-injury version. AFL’s first asserted injury was that DOJ had not produced the FOIA records it requested. But that injury, the court said, was caused by DOJ’s own conduct, not by OSC’s refusal to investigate. OSC’s inaction might, at most, be one step in a speculative chain that could someday influence DOJ, but standing cannot rest on speculation about how independent actors will respond.

The court also rejected AFL’s attempt to connect the dots through OSC enforcement. The chain required assuming that OSC would investigate, then choose to pursue corrective or disciplinary action, then the MSPB would sanction employees, and then DOJ would respond by producing the records. That is too attenuated. The court relied on the general principle that causation and redressability cannot depend on speculation about independent actors not before the court, and it also noted that OSC’s own enforcement choices are discretionary in important respects.

AFL’s second alleged injury—its asserted interest in information that would have been generated by the OSC investigation—also failed. The court explained that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive the information at issue. Even assuming § 1216 requires OSC to investigate certain FOIA violations, AFL identified no entitlement to receive notices or other information from that investigation. AFL tried to rely on the notice provisions associated with § 1214, but it conceded that those requirements are discretionary, not mandatory, in a § 1216 investigation. Without an entitlement to those notices, there was no concrete informational injury.

## The Court’s Choice Not to Classify AFL’s Asserted Injuries as Definitively Procedural or Substantive

An important feature of the opinion is that the panel did not finally decide whether AFL’s claimed injuries should be labeled “procedural” or “substantive.” Instead, it concluded that AFL failed even under the relaxed procedural-injury approach. That allowed the court to avoid a harder classification question and focus on the bottom line: whatever label one uses, AFL had not shown the kind of concrete injury and redressable causation Article III demands.

This move is pedagogically useful. In standing analysis, labels are often less important than the actual constitutional showing. Calling something “procedural” does not automatically lower the bar enough to save a case. The plaintiff must still connect the alleged statutory violation to a real-world interest that the law protects.

## How the Standing Inquiry Reframed the Case Away from Statutory Entitlement and Toward Constitutional Justiciability

The standing analysis changed the entire orientation of the case. AFL had argued as though the main question was whether § 1216 required OSC to investigate. The district court had even addressed that statutory question. But the court of appeals reframed the dispute: before asking what § 1216 means, the court had to ask whether AFL had any constitutionally sufficient stake in that meaning.

Once the court answered no, the statutory dispute became irrelevant for jurisdictional purposes. The opinion therefore demonstrates a standard move in federal litigation: a plaintiff may invoke a statute and even allege that an agency misread it, but federal courts still cannot act unless the plaintiff satisfies Article III. Here, the standing inquiry did not just narrow the issues; it disposed of the case. Because AFL lacked standing, the proper disposition was vacatur of the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
