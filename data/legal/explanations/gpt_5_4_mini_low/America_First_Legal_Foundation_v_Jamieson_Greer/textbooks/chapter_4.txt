Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # 4. Why AFL’s Two Standing Theories Failed

## AFL’s first theory: OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from obtaining the FOIA records it sought from DOJ

The court first addressed AFL’s argument that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused AFL to remain without the records it had requested from DOJ under FOIA. This theory was important because AFL tried to link OSC’s inaction to a concrete harm: the continued nonproduction of documents. But the court held that the theory did not satisfy Article III standing because the injury AFL pointed to was not fairly traceable to OSC, and a favorable judgment against OSC would not likely redress it.

The court began with the basic point that the immediate source of AFL’s injury was DOJ, not OSC. If AFL was injured by not receiving records, that injury came from DOJ’s alleged refusal to disclose them. OSC’s failure to investigate was only one step removed from that harm. The court therefore treated any direct informational injury as attributable to the agency that withheld the documents, not to the separate agency that declined to investigate the withholding.

## Why the court treated any immediate informational injury regarding records as traceable to DOJ rather than OSC

This distinction mattered because standing requires causation. A plaintiff cannot simply identify a government failure somewhere in the background and then attribute all downstream consequences to the defendant. Here, the court reasoned that the records injury was “plainly traceable” to DOJ’s alleged FOIA denial. OSC’s role was secondary and contingent. The court was unwilling to say that the refusal to investigate itself caused the loss of records when DOJ was the actor that actually withheld them.

The court also explained that AFL’s theory depended on a chain of hypothetical events, not on a direct causal link. That kind of chain is often fatal in standing analysis, especially when it relies on several independent decisions by other actors.

## The speculative causal chain identified by the court: OSC investigation, possible OSC enforcement action, possible MSPB sanction, and possible DOJ production

The court laid out AFL’s theory step by step. If OSC had investigated, it might have chosen to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. Then the Merit Systems Protection Board might have sanctioned the responsible employees. After that, DOJ might have decided to produce the requested records. Each link in that sequence was uncertain.

That uncertainty made the theory too speculative for Article III. Standing does not permit a plaintiff to rely on a “maybe-if-then” chain stretching across multiple discretionary decisions and different agencies. The court emphasized that AFL had not shown that a favorable ruling would likely cause the records to be released. At best, the ruling might prompt some investigative activity, which might or might not lead to enforcement, which might or might not change DOJ’s behavior.

## The significance of independent actors not before the court, especially MSPB and DOJ

The presence of independent actors was central to the court’s reasoning. MSPB and DOJ were not parties to the lawsuit, yet AFL’s theory assumed that each would react in a way that would eventually benefit AFL. The court treated that as a classic red flag for causation and redressability. When relief depends on the choices of entities outside the court’s control, especially nonparties, the causal link becomes too uncertain.

This is why the court said the theory required speculation about “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” The problem was not just that those actors might act differently than AFL hoped; it was that the court could not presume they would act at all, or act in the expected way, based on a judgment against OSC.

## The court’s use of FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine on speculation about third-party choices

To reinforce that point, the court cited FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine. That case stands for the proposition that causation cannot rest on speculation about the choices of independent third parties. The court used that principle here to show that AFL’s causal chain was too remote. Even if OSC had investigated, the court could not assume that MSPB would sanction anyone, or that DOJ would then disclose records.

This citation helped the court frame the standing problem in familiar terms: when relief depends on the voluntary actions of others, especially those not joined in the suit, the plaintiff usually cannot show the kind of concrete causal connection Article III requires.

## The court’s invocation of Heckler v. Chaney and the problem of agency enforcement discretion

The court also relied on Heckler v. Chaney to underscore that OSC’s own enforcement choices are discretionary. That matters because AFL’s theory assumed the court could predict, and perhaps effectively compel, OSC to take enforcement steps once an investigation began. But the court reminded readers that agencies often have broad discretion in deciding whether to pursue enforcement.

In other words, even before the theory reached MSPB or DOJ, it already depended on a discretionary decision by OSC. The court would not treat an uncertain enforcement response as a reliable step toward redressing AFL’s injury. This made the theory even more speculative.

## Why a favorable judgment would not “very likely” redress AFL’s inability to obtain records

The redressability inquiry asks whether a court order is likely to make the plaintiff’s injury go away. The court concluded that AFL could not make that showing. A judgment requiring OSC to investigate would not itself produce the requested records. It would only perhaps start a process that might eventually lead to records disclosure.

The court therefore held that AFL had not shown that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its injury. That phrase captures the key point: Article III demands more than a hope that litigation might trigger a series of favorable events. The chain must be sufficiently likely, and here it was not.

## The court’s reliance on Haaland v. Brackeen to stress that redressability generally depends on relief binding the parties, not persuasive effect on outsiders

The court added another important redressability point from Haaland v. Brackeen: redressability generally depends on what the judgment binds, not on whether the court’s reasoning might influence people who are not parties. This mattered because AFL’s theory implicitly assumed that a court ruling against OSC might persuade DOJ to release the records.

The court rejected that approach. A judgment that merely exerts persuasive pressure on outsiders is not the same as a judgment that legally binds them. Since DOJ was not the defendant in this case, a ruling against OSC would not directly require DOJ to disclose anything. That made the requested relief too weak to satisfy redressability.

## AFL’s second theory: OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of notices and information associated with an OSC investigation

AFL’s second standing theory focused not on the records themselves, but on the process of investigation. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of the notices and information that would accompany an OSC investigation. In effect, AFL attempted to turn the absence of an investigation into a separate informational injury.

The court rejected that theory as well. Its reasoning was more doctrinal than factual: AFL had to show not just that it wanted information, but that a statute entitled it to receive that information. Without such an entitlement, there was no concrete informational injury.

## The doctrine of informational standing: denial of information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive

Informational standing is a narrow doctrine. A plaintiff suffers a concrete injury when it is denied information that a statute gives it a right to obtain. The court cited cases such as Public Citizen, Akins, and ASCPA v. Feld to explain that the injury arises from the withholding of information the law requires to be disclosed.

That framing is crucial. A plaintiff does not get standing merely because it would like to know more. The statute must create an entitlement to the specific information. Otherwise, the alleged injury is not concrete in the Article III sense.

## Why § 1216(a)(3), even if mandatory as to investigation, does not itself create an entitlement to information

Applying that doctrine, the court held that § 1216(a)(3) did not help AFL. Even if that provision requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, it says nothing about providing AFL with investigative notices or other information. A mandate to investigate is not the same thing as a right to receive information from the investigation.

That was the decisive gap in AFL’s second theory. The statute may, on AFL’s reading, require OSC to act, but it does not automatically require OSC to inform AFL of the details of that action. Without a statutory right to receive the information, AFL could not establish informational standing.

## AFL’s attempt to rely on § 1214 notice provisions through § 1216 investigations

AFL tried to bridge that gap by pointing to the notice provisions in § 1214. Its argument was that if OSC investigates under § 1216, it may use the same procedures that apply to prohibited personnel practice investigations under § 1214, including notice requirements. If so, AFL argued, it should receive the kind of updates and information that § 1214 provides.

But the court did not accept that move. AFL was effectively asking the court to import the § 1214 notice scheme into § 1216 investigations, even though the statutory text does not clearly do that by itself. The court found that AFL had not established a legal entitlement to those procedural disclosures.

## Why that argument failed because AFL did not contend that § 1214 notice requirements must apply in § 1216 investigations

A key reason AFL lost was the way it framed its own argument. The court noted that AFL did not actually contend that § 1214’s notice provisions are mandatory in § 1216 investigations. Instead, AFL acknowledged that those procedures may be optional. That concession undermined its claim to a concrete injury.

If the notices are discretionary, then their absence cannot be an injury caused by the denial of a statutory entitlement. At most, AFL could say that OSC might have chosen to provide more information. But a hoped-for discretionary benefit is not enough for informational standing.

## The importance of Carson v. Office of Special Counsel and the discretionary nature of using § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation

The court relied on Carson v. Office of Special Counsel to support that point. Carson held that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. That precedent was fatal to AFL’s second theory because it showed there was no mandatory right to the notices AFL wanted.

Once the court accepted that § 1214 procedures were discretionary in this context, AFL could not claim that OSC’s failure to provide them deprived it of information the statute guaranteed. Without a guarantee, there was no concrete informational injury.

## Why the absence of a statutory entitlement defeated concrete injury and also undermined redressability for the second theory

The absence of a statutory entitlement mattered in two ways. First, it meant AFL had not shown injury in fact: there was no legally protected interest in the information it claimed to have lost. Second, it meant redressability was weak as well. Even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, that would not necessarily require OSC to provide § 1214-style notices or disclosures.

So the second theory failed on both sides of the standing analysis. AFL could not show that it was deprived of information the law required OSC to give it, and it could not show that a judicial order would likely supply that information. That left AFL without Article III standing on either theory.
