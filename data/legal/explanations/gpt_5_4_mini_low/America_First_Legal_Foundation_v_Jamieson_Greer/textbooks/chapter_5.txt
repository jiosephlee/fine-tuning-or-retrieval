Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 5: # 5. Holding, Disposition, and the Opinion’s Significance

## The Court’s Holding: No Article III Standing

The D.C. Circuit’s bottom-line holding was not about the meaning of the Civil Service Reform Act or FOIA. It was about jurisdiction. The court held that America First Legal Foundation lacked Article III standing to bring this suit. That meant AFL could not proceed in federal court, even if its statutory theory about the Office of Special Counsel’s investigative duties were correct.

This is an important distinction. A plaintiff can have what looks like a strong argument on the merits and still lose if it cannot show the concrete injury, causation, and redressability required by Article III. Here, the court concluded that AFL’s complaint was really an effort to force OSC to investigate DOJ and possibly trigger further enforcement steps. But AFL could not show that OSC’s failure to investigate caused a legally cognizable injury that a court order against OSC would likely remedy.

## The Disposition: Vacatur and Remand to Dismiss for Lack of Jurisdiction

Because standing is a threshold requirement, the court could not leave the district court’s merits judgment in place. Instead, the D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

That disposition matters procedurally. “Vacate” means the appellate court nullified the district court’s judgment. The merits ruling below therefore does not stand as a binding adjudication of AFL’s statutory claims. The remand instruction tells the district court what to do next: dismiss the case because the federal courts lack power to decide it on the merits.

## Why the Court Did Not Decide the Statutory Merits

A federal court may not reach the merits unless Article III standing exists. That is why the opinion begins its jurisdictional analysis by citing the constitutional case-or-controversy requirement and then applying the familiar standing elements from Lujan: injury in fact, causation, and redressability.

The court’s reasoning shows why standing can be fatal even when a plaintiff has identified a statutory provision that appears to support its requested relief. AFL argued that § 1216 required OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, and the district court had accepted part of that reading. But the D.C. Circuit never had authority to resolve whether that interpretation was right, because AFL still had to show that it personally suffered a concrete injury traceable to OSC and likely to be fixed by a court order. Without that showing, the court had no jurisdiction to answer the statutory question.

## What Remains Unresolved About § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

The opinion leaves open the substantive relationship between § 1216 and FOIA’s separate enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That relationship was the statutory dispute at the center of the case. AFL wanted the court to hold that OSC had to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy under § 1216 even though the specific triggers in FOIA’s own OSC-enforcement provision were not met.

The D.C. Circuit did not decide whether that interpretation is correct. Nor did it decide whether OSC has broad or narrow authority to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings under § 1216. Those issues remain undecided because the case was resolved on standing grounds alone.

So the opinion is best read as a jurisdictional decision, not a definitive construction of the statutory scheme. That matters for future litigants: the court’s holding does not finally settle the scope of OSC’s authority under these provisions.

## A Favorable Statutory Theory Is Not the Same as a Justiciable Case

One of the clearest lessons from the opinion is that a plaintiff can lose even if it has a plausible legal argument on the statute. AFL may have believed that the statute required OSC to act. But standing asks a different question: did the plaintiff suffer a concrete injury that the court can likely redress?

The court’s answer was no. AFL’s claimed harms were too indirect. Its first theory depended on a chain of events: OSC would investigate; OSC might pursue corrective or disciplinary action; the MSPB might act; DOJ might then change course and disclose records. Its second theory depended on OSC providing investigatory notices and information that AFL had no independent entitlement to receive. Neither theory established the kind of direct, judicially redressable injury Article III requires.

In other words, it is not enough to show that an agency may have violated a statute. The plaintiff must also show that the violation injured the plaintiff in a concrete way and that a court order against the defendant will probably fix the injury.

## The Limits of Suits to Force Agency Investigation or Enforcement

This case is a strong example of the limits on lawsuits that try to compel agency investigation or enforcement activity. The court was especially skeptical of AFL’s attempt to turn OSC’s discretionary or intermediate enforcement decisions into a basis for standing. The problem was not only injury, but also the uncertainty created by multiple decisionmakers.

OSC is one actor. The Merit Systems Protection Board is another. DOJ is a third. AFL’s requested relief would not itself force disclosure of the documents it wanted. At most, it might prompt OSC to begin an investigation that could, through several additional discretionary steps by other actors, eventually lead to a change in DOJ’s behavior. That is too speculative for Article III standing.

The opinion also reflects a familiar principle from enforcement cases: courts are reluctant to assume that one agency’s decision to investigate or prosecute will automatically produce the downstream result a plaintiff wants. The mere possibility that an investigation might cause later action does not make the injury redressable.

## Informational Injury Requires a Real Statutory Entitlement

AFL tried to recast its case as an informational injury case, but the court rejected that theory as well. The opinion draws a sharp line: informational standing exists only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive specific information and the plaintiff is denied it.

That is why the court found AFL’s first theory insufficient. AFL had a claim to records under FOIA against DOJ, but the alleged failure by OSC to investigate was not the source of the informational injury. And AFL’s second theory also failed because § 1216, even if it required an investigation, did not itself give AFL a right to the notices and information that accompany a § 1214 investigation. In short, a statute that authorizes or requires an agency to investigate is not the same thing as a statute that requires the agency to disclose information to the requester.

This part of the opinion is a useful reminder that “informational injury” is not a free-floating concept. The plaintiff must point to an actual statutory entitlement to the information allegedly withheld.

## Causation and Redressability When Relief Depends on Third Parties

The opinion also illustrates the modern standing problem posed by chains of independent decisionmaking. The court emphasized that AFL’s alleged injury depended on speculative choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ. That kind of chain weakens both causation and redressability.

Causation is weak because the injury comes immediately from DOJ’s alleged refusal to disclose records, not from OSC’s failure to investigate. Redressability is weak because a favorable ruling against OSC would not itself require DOJ to release documents. At best, it might influence behavior indirectly. But standing doctrine generally does not accept “maybe it will persuade someone else” as enough.

The court’s redressability discussion is especially important. It stressed that what matters is whether the judgment binds the defendant in a way that likely remedies the plaintiff’s injury, not whether the court’s reasoning might persuade outsiders to act differently. That is a recurring theme in standing cases and a central reason AFL lost here.

## The Case in Modern Standing Doctrine

This opinion fits comfortably within recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit standing precedent. It applies the standard Lujan framework, follows Summers in rejecting a procedural-right claim disconnected from a concrete interest, and relies on modern cases stressing that causation and redressability cannot rest on speculation about independent actors.

It also reflects the D.C. Circuit’s careful policing of Article III in cases seeking government action rather than direct relief. Even where a plaintiff frames the dispute as one about statutory procedure, the court still asks whether the alleged violation actually injured the plaintiff in a concrete way. If the answer is no, the federal courts must dismiss.

The larger lesson is straightforward: a plaintiff may have a colorable argument that a statute imposes duties on an agency, but unless the plaintiff can show a direct, personal, and judicially redressable injury from the agency’s failure to act, the federal courts cannot hear the case.
