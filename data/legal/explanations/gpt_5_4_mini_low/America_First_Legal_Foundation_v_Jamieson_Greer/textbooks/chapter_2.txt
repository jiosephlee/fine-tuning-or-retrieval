Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # 2. Procedural History and the District Court’s Merits Ruling

## AFL’s Request that OSC Investigate DOJ Under § 1216(a)(3)

The dispute began with AFL’s attempt to use the Office of Special Counsel as an enforcement avenue for an alleged FOIA violation. AFL had submitted a FOIA request to the Department of Justice seeking certain emails. It believed DOJ had denied the request on an improper policy ground—namely, that DOJ would not search employee emails for responsive records unless the employee consented. AFL then asked OSC to investigate that alleged policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”

That request matters because AFL was not merely seeking records from DOJ through its separate FOIA lawsuit. It was also trying to trigger OSC’s investigative machinery against DOJ, presumably to pressure the agency and potentially individual employees responsible for the withholding. The case thus involved two different tracks: the ordinary FOIA suit against DOJ, and the attempted OSC investigation under the Civil Service Reform Act and FOIA’s special enforcement provisions.

## OSC’s Position that It Lacked Authority Absent the FOIA-Specific Predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

OSC declined to investigate. Its position was that the statutory conditions for a FOIA-based investigation were not met. OSC relied on 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which provides that when a court orders production in a FOIA case, awards attorneys’ fees, and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records, OSC “shall promptly initiate” a disciplinary proceeding against the responsible officer or employee.

In OSC’s view, those three predicates functioned as the trigger for its authority to act on an alleged FOIA withholding. Because AFL’s situation did not satisfy those predicates, OSC concluded it lacked authority to investigate. AFL disagreed, reading § 1216(a)(3) as an independent mandate that required OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings even when the separate FOIA-enforcement provision in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) was not triggered.

This was the central statutory disagreement in the district court. The question was not simply whether DOJ had violated FOIA. It was whether OSC had a duty—or even authority—to investigate the alleged policy under § 1216 without the special conditions described in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

## AFL’s Argument that § 1216 Independently Required an Investigation

AFL’s theory was that § 1216 gave OSC a freestanding obligation to investigate “any allegation” of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding. On that reading, § 1216 was not limited by FOIA’s narrower disciplinary trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL therefore argued that OSC had unlawfully refused to investigate its allegation about DOJ’s email-search policy.

This distinction between the two provisions was important. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) speaks to a specific post-judgment disciplinary process after a court has already found a FOIA withholding and made certain additional findings. Section 1216, by contrast, appears to authorize OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings more broadly. AFL’s position was that OSC had wrongly collapsed the broader § 1216 authority into the narrower § 552 mechanism.

## The District Court’s Conclusion that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) Does Not Limit OSC’s Investigative Power Under § 1216

The district court agreed with AFL on the statutory interpretation question. It concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216. In other words, the court read § 1216 as independently authorizing OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholdings, regardless of whether the FOIA-specific disciplinary provision had been triggered.

That ruling was favorable to AFL on the legal issue. It meant the court accepted AFL’s core claim that OSC had misread the statute. The district court therefore treated OSC’s refusal to investigate as resting on a legal error rather than a correct understanding of the statute.

## The District Court’s Determination that OSC’s Refusal Rested on Legal Error

After concluding that OSC had erred in thinking it lacked authority, the district court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was based on the wrong legal premise. The court’s reasoning was straightforward: if § 1216 authorizes investigations independent of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), then OSC could not justify its refusal by pointing to the absence of the three FOIA-specific predicates.

But that did not end the case. Even after finding legal error, the district court declined to order OSC to investigate.

## The District Court’s Further Conclusion that, Under § 1216(c), Whether to Investigate a FOIA Policy Was Committed to OSC’s Discretion

The district court held that although OSC had misread the scope of its authority, the decision whether to investigate a particular FOIA policy was committed to OSC’s discretion under § 1216(c). That subsection says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action” and disciplinary action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The district court read that language as leaving the actual decision to pursue an investigation to OSC.

This is the key move that explains the posture of the case when it reached the court of appeals. The district court did not enter an order compelling OSC to investigate. Instead, it concluded that OSC had made a legal mistake but that the remedy was not mandatory judicial compulsion. The court therefore stopped short of ordering the agency to act.

## The Remand to OSC for Reconsideration Rather Than an Order Compelling Investigation

Instead of requiring an investigation, the district court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration in light of its statutory interpretation. In practical terms, the court told OSC to reconsider its position now that the court had determined § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not restrict § 1216 authority.

That remand is significant because it shows the district court viewed the case as one in which the agency should get another chance to decide whether to investigate, but not one in which the court could itself compel the investigation. The district court’s choice of remand rather than direct relief reflects its view that the statute left room for agency discretion.

## OSC’s Renewed Refusal to Investigate and AFL’s Motion for Entry of Final Judgment

After the remand, OSC again declined to investigate. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. At that point, the litigation reached a posture in which there was a final district court disposition from which AFL could appeal.

The sequence matters for understanding the appellate opinion. The district court had already decided the statutory interpretation issue, but it had not granted the remedy AFL wanted. OSC’s second refusal, followed by the final dismissal, created the final judgment that brought the case to the D.C. Circuit.

## The Appeal to the D.C. Circuit and the Court’s De Novo Review

AFL appealed, and the D.C. Circuit reviewed the case de novo. De novo review means the court of appeals did not defer to the district court’s legal conclusions; it independently examined the issues. Because the district court had dismissed the case on the merits, the appeal initially looked like a straightforward review of statutory interpretation and remedy.

But the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits first.

## Why the Court of Appeals Addressed Standing Before the Merits Despite the District Court’s Merits-Based Disposition

Before deciding whether the district court correctly interpreted § 1216 and § 552, the D.C. Circuit asked whether AFL had Article III standing. This step was necessary because standing goes to subject-matter jurisdiction. A federal court cannot decide the merits if the plaintiff lacks standing, even if the district court already ruled on the merits.

The court therefore treated standing as a threshold issue. It explained that Article III limits federal courts to actual “Cases” and “Controversies,” and that standing is the doctrine that enforces that limitation. Because neither party had initially addressed standing, the court ordered supplemental briefing on the issue. Only after concluding that AFL lacked standing did the court vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

In other words, the district court’s statutory ruling became irrelevant once the court of appeals determined that AFL could not invoke federal judicial power at all. The merits discussion in the district court was overtaken by the jurisdictional defect.
