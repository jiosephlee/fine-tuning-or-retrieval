Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedure Without Payoff: The D.C. Circuit’s Standing Limit on Efforts to Compel OSC FOIA Investigations

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is less about FOIA than about the limits of statutory process claims in federal court. America First Legal Foundation wanted to force the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged FOIA-withholding policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, which says OSC “shall” investigate certain arbitrary withholding allegations. But the court never reached the merits of that statutory argument. Instead, Judge Katsas held that AFL lacked Article III standing because the claimed right to an OSC investigation was not tied to a concrete injury the court could redress.

That matters well beyond this dispute. Litigants increasingly try to convert mandatory-sounding statutory procedures into standalone causes of action. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion is a reminder that “shall” is not enough. Even where Congress has created a procedural entitlement, a plaintiff still has to show a concrete interest affected by the violation, plus causation and redressability sufficient to satisfy Article III.

## The court’s standing analysis

The court began with the familiar basics from *Lujan*: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. AFL said OSC violated its statutory right to an investigation under § 1216. But as the panel noted, a “procedural right in vacuo” does not create standing. That phrasing, drawn from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and D.C. Circuit procedural-injury cases, does a lot of work here. A plaintiff cannot sue simply because an agency failed to follow a process Congress prescribed; the plaintiff must connect that procedural failure to some concrete, particularized harm.

AFL tried to do that in two ways. First, it argued that if OSC had investigated DOJ, the investigation might have led to enforcement action, which might have induced DOJ to produce the withheld records. The panel found that chain far too speculative. It depended on discretionary choices by OSC, possible action by the MSPB, and then a further possibility that DOJ would respond by disclosing records. That is not the kind of direct redressability Article III requires, especially where the alleged remedy turns on choices by multiple independent actors not before the court. The opinion also stressed that courts generally cannot compel OSC’s enforcement discretion, invoking *Heckler v. Chaney*.

Second, AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of information it would have received in the course of an investigation, including notice and updates. But the panel rejected that theory because § 1216 does not itself entitle a complainant to the information AFL wanted. AFL tried to borrow the notice provisions from § 1214, which govern prohibited personnel practice investigations, but the court said that was a stretch. Even if OSC must investigate under § 1216, AFL did not show it had a statutory right to the particular disclosures it claimed.

## What the court is really saying

The opinion is an important limit on a familiar litigating move: identify a mandatory statutory command, frame its violation as procedural injury, and then argue that the process itself is the protected interest. The D.C. Circuit did not deny that Congress can create statutory obligations for agencies. It said something narrower but constitutionally significant: not every statutory obligation creates judicially cognizable standing for every disappointed complainant.

That distinction is especially sharp here because AFL’s real grievance was not the absence of OSC process in the abstract. It wanted either more records from DOJ or pressure on DOJ through an OSC investigation. The court treated both as too remote from the injury alleged. That is a classic standing analysis, but it also reflects a broader skepticism toward efforts to use one agency’s enforcement machinery to redress a dispute with another agency.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

The decision strengthens the D.C. Circuit’s already demanding procedural-standing doctrine. By citing *Summers*, *Fla. Audubon*, and *Center for Law and Education*, the panel reinforced the proposition that a procedural right must protect a concrete interest, not just create a freestanding administrative entitlement. That will likely matter in future suits seeking to compel investigations, referrals, reports, or other agency follow-on actions that are only indirectly connected to a plaintiff’s substantive complaint.

The opinion also suggests that courts will scrutinize redressability especially closely when the requested relief depends on a chain of discretionary decisions across multiple agencies. That is a meaningful constraint in regulatory and oversight litigation, where plaintiffs often ask courts to order one agency to trigger another agency’s processes. The D.C. Circuit is signaling that such claims will have a hard time clearing Article III unless the plaintiff can show a direct legal entitlement to the information or action sought.

## Bottom line

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a clean application of standing doctrine, but it carries a broader message: mandatory language in a statute does not automatically translate into a justiciable right to compel agency action. If the only alleged injury is the denial of a procedure, the plaintiff still has to show that the procedure protects a concrete interest and that a favorable decision will likely redress the harm. Here, the court found AFL could show neither.



# The Long Causal Chain Problem: How *America First Legal* Fits the Post-*Hippocratic Medicine* Standing Trend

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is important less for its immediate FOIA-and-OSC setting than for what it says about modern standing doctrine. The court held that AFL lacked Article III standing to force the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged FOIA practice, because the path from OSC’s refusal to investigate to AFL’s alleged injury ran through too many independent decisionmakers. That may sound like a familiar Article III move. But the opinion is a good example of how aggressively courts are now policing causal chains, especially after the Supreme Court’s decision in *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*.

The takeaway is straightforward: a plaintiff cannot manufacture standing by asking a court to order one agency to act in hopes that a second agency, or an adjudicatory body, will someday respond in the plaintiff’s favor. The D.C. Circuit treated that as a redressability and causation problem, but the deeper point is institutional. Article III does not permit litigation to become a proxy for supervisory control over a chain of executive discretion.

## The court’s standing analysis

AFL tried to frame the case as one about a procedural right to an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). That matters because procedural-injury cases get a somewhat relaxed standing analysis. But, as the court emphasized, even procedural plaintiffs still need a concrete underlying interest, plus causation and redressability that are more than speculative.

The court rejected two injury theories. First, AFL said OSC’s failure to investigate deprived it of the FOIA records it wanted from DOJ. But the court treated that as an injury caused by DOJ, not OSC. More importantly, the alleged chain of redress was far too attenuated: OSC would have to investigate; then maybe bring corrective or disciplinary action; then the MSPB would have to act; then DOJ might produce the records. That chain depended on the “unfettered choices” of multiple actors not before the court. The panel relied heavily on *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the proposition that Article III does not rest on speculation about how nonparties might respond to a judicial ruling.

Second, AFL argued that it had an informational injury because OSC had not given it the notices and information it would receive in a section 1214-style investigation. But the court said there is no statutory entitlement to that information here. Even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate, it does not automatically import the notice regime of § 1214. Citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, the court noted that whether OSC follows those procedures in a § 1216 matter is discretionary. Without a concrete statutory entitlement to the information, AFL could not bootstrap informational standing.

## The court’s use of recent Supreme Court doctrine

This opinion fits neatly within the Supreme Court’s current emphasis on concrete redressability. *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* is the obvious anchor. There, the Court stressed that standing cannot rest on speculation about the choices of independent third parties. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion is almost a textbook application of that principle: when a plaintiff needs one agency to act, another body to sanction, and a final agency to change course, the causal link becomes too fragile for Article III.

The panel also leaned on *Haaland v. Brackeen*, which reminds courts that redressability turns on whether the judgment binds the relevant party, not whether its reasoning might persuade outsiders. That distinction matters here. Even if an order compelling OSC to investigate might influence DOJ’s behavior indirectly, the court refused to count persuasion as redress. That is a meaningful tightening of the doctrine, because many administrative-law plaintiffs are really seeking leverage, not direct relief. The court is saying that leverage is not enough.

The invocation of *Heckler v. Chaney* reinforces the same point from a different angle. Courts are especially reluctant to compel enforcement choices. If the plaintiff’s theory depends on forcing an agency to launch a discretionary process, and then hoping that process produces downstream sanctions or compliance, standing becomes doubly unstable.

## Doctrinal implications

This decision is part of a broader trend in which the Court and the D.C. Circuit are narrowing opportunities for plaintiffs to challenge government inaction by chaining together multiple statutory steps. That trend has consequences beyond FOIA. It affects public-interest litigation whenever the requested relief is indirect: complaints about agency nonenforcement, oversight failures, referral regimes, and procedural statutes that rely on subsequent enforcement decisions by other actors.

The opinion also has an important structural message for administrative law. Congress often creates layered enforcement schemes, but Article III does not treat every layer as a usable injury. A plaintiff may identify a statutory duty at the first stage of the process, but if the practical benefit of enforcement depends on a second agency’s discretion or on an adjudicator’s later intervention, standing will often fail. In that sense, the court is separating statutory design from constitutional justiciability: Congress can create procedural mechanisms, but plaintiffs still need a direct, concrete connection between the violation and the relief sought.

For litigants, the lesson is that framing matters. A complaint that is really about getting government records, changing an agency policy, or inducing enforcement action will face serious standing problems if the requested judicial order does not itself redress the injury. Plaintiffs cannot solve that by invoking a “procedural right” label. The court was explicit that a procedural right “in vacuo” is not enough.

## Bottom line

*America First Legal* is a strong example of post-*Hippocratic Medicine* standing doctrine in action. The D.C. Circuit was unwilling to let AFL turn OSC into a gateway for coercing DOJ compliance through a speculative cascade of agency and board decisions. That reflects a broader judicial skepticism toward litigation strategies that depend on indirect influence rather than direct redress. If the current trend continues, plaintiffs will increasingly have to show that the court’s order itself, not a hoped-for chain reaction, is what cures the injury.



# Section 1216, Section 552(a)(4)(F), and the Vanishing Merits: What the Standing Dismissal Leaves Unresolved About OSC’s FOIA Authority

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is notable less for what it decided than for what it left undecided. The panel held that America First Legal lacked Article III standing to force the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged arbitrary FOIA withholding. That jurisdictional holding wiped away the district court’s merits ruling and sent the case back for dismissal.

But the real significance lies in the statutory question the court did not reach: Does OSC’s broad investigative authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 operate independently of FOIA’s narrower disciplinary trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), or does the FOIA-specific provision cabin OSC’s power to act on alleged arbitrary withholdings? The district court had answered that question one way, OSC another, and the court of appeals sidestepped it entirely. That leaves FOIA requesters, agencies, and OSC itself with an important unresolved issue about when, if ever, a requester can compel OSC attention to an alleged FOIA violation.

## The jurisdictional move

Judge Katsas’s opinion is a straightforward standing opinion in the modern Article III style: identify the asserted injury, test causation and redressability, and insist on something more concrete than a procedural grievance in the abstract. AFL argued that OSC violated a statutory duty to investigate DOJ’s alleged “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of FOIA records. But the panel concluded that AFL could not convert that alleged violation into standing by pointing to two downstream informational harms.

The first theory was that OSC’s failure to investigate kept AFL from getting the requested DOJ records. The court rejected that as too speculative. Any injury from nondisclosure was traceable to DOJ, not OSC; and redress would depend on a long chain of contingencies involving OSC’s enforcement choice, potential MSPB action, and DOJ’s response. The second theory—that AFL had a right to information generated by the OSC investigation itself—failed because AFL could not identify any statutory entitlement to the notices and information it sought. The panel emphasized that even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate, it does not follow that OSC must import § 1214’s notice regime into a § 1216 inquiry.

That is all doctrinally orthodox. The court was careful not to stretch procedural standing beyond the plaintiff’s concrete interests, and it treated redressability with appropriate skepticism. The opinion also fits comfortably with the Supreme Court’s recent emphasis on causation and redressability when relief depends on nonparty choices.

## What the court did not decide

What makes the case interesting is that the panel vacated without resolving the statutory structure underneath the dispute. The district court had held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—FOIA’s more specific disciplinary mechanism—does not limit OSC’s authority under § 1216. In other words, the district court read § 1216 as an independent grant of investigative power over arbitrary FOIA withholdings, even outside the circumstances described in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). But it also held that any follow-on enforcement choice remained discretionary, which is why it refused to order OSC to investigate in the way AFL wanted.

That combination of holdings would have been doctrinally consequential. It would have suggested that requesters might be able to invoke OSC earlier, before a FOIA case culminates in a production order, fee award, and judicial finding that the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. In practical terms, that would create a second enforcement lane for FOIA grievances, one not tied to the remedial trigger Congress wrote into § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

The D.C. Circuit’s standing dismissal leaves that possibility unresolved. The panel did not say whether the district court was right to read § 1216 broadly, nor whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is best understood as a floor, a ceiling, or simply a separate enforcement track. It only said AFL was not the right party, at least on these facts, to force that question into court.

## The likely doctrinal tension

The unresolved statutory question is not trivial. Section 1216 is cast in mandatory language: OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” That sounds broad enough to cover at least some FOIA-withholding allegations even when no court has yet made the findings required by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

But FOIA’s own special OSC provision is much narrower and more structured. Congress tied mandatory disciplinary action to a judicial order, a fee award, and a specific judicial question about arbitrariness. That structure suggests a deliberate choice to condition OSC disciplinary proceedings on a completed FOIA litigation record, rather than on any requester’s bare allegation.

Those two provisions can be read together, but not easily. If § 1216 is self-executing in every alleged FOIA withholding, then § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) looks less like a trigger and more like a duplicative backstop. If, on the other hand, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is the specific FOIA enforcement mechanism, then § 1216 may be better understood as reaching some other category of misconduct, or at least as not overriding the more particular FOIA scheme.

The panel’s standing decision leaves that interpretive tension in place. And because the court did not reach the merits, it also left the district court’s more expansive reading without appellate approval.

## What this means for future litigants

For FOIA requesters, the immediate lesson is that a suit to compel OSC action will face a serious standing problem unless the requester can identify a concrete entitlement that OSC’s inaction itself defeats. A generalized interest in having an agency investigate is not enough. Nor is it enough to argue that a successful OSC investigation might indirectly pressure the agency to release records. The D.C. Circuit treated that causal chain as too contingent.

That means the most plausible path for future plaintiffs may be to anchor their injury in an actual statutory entitlement to information, not in the mere existence of an investigation. But *Greer* makes clear that § 1216, standing alone, may not supply that entitlement. The court’s emphasis on the absence of a statutory right to receive the notice and information AFL wanted suggests that many requester-driven suits will founder before they ever reach the statutory interpretation question.

For OSC, the practical consequence is flexibility. The agency avoided an appellate ruling that would either confirm or foreclose its ability to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings under § 1216 absent the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates. That uncertainty may be attractive to the agency in the short term, but it also invites more litigation over the boundaries of its authority.

For agencies subject to FOIA, the case preserves the status quo for now. There is no appellate holding that OSC can or must investigate any alleged arbitrary withholding upon request. But neither is there a decision foreclosing that possibility. The statutory cloud remains.

## Bottom line

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a reminder that standing can be outcome-determinative not just for the parties, but for the development of the law itself. The D.C. Circuit’s jurisdictional ruling cleaned the case off the merits docket, but in doing so it postponed a significant question about the relationship between § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That question will matter whenever a FOIA requester tries to bypass the ordinary FOIA enforcement path and enlist OSC before a court has entered the kind of order FOIA itself specifies.

For now, the panel has told us only that AFL was not entitled to a judicial answer. It has not told us what the answer would have been.



# Informational Injury After *Akins*: Why Not Every Statutory Information Ecosystem Creates Standing

## Why the D.C. Circuit’s decision matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a useful reminder that “informational injury” is not a magic incantation. Plaintiffs often try to reframe a complaint about agency inaction as a denial of information, because informational standing can be easier to plead than more traditional injuries. But Judge Katsas’s opinion draws an important line: a statute that requires an agency to investigate does not necessarily create a right to receive information from that investigation.

That distinction matters well beyond this case. If courts treated any statutory enforcement or oversight mechanism as an “information entitlement,” then plaintiffs could transform disappointed petitions for agency action into Article III cases simply by pointing to the downstream possibility that an investigation might uncover something useful. The panel refused to go there, and in doing so it tightened the doctrinal boundary between true disclosure statutes and statutes that merely set an administrative process in motion.

## The statutory setup and the plaintiff’s theory

AFL wanted the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. It pointed to 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations concerning arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. AFL argued that OSC had a mandatory duty to investigate even if FOIA itself would not have triggered the separate disciplinary mechanism in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

But before the panel reached that statutory dispute, it held that AFL lacked standing. That holding is the real doctrinal contribution of the case. AFL tried to ground standing in two alleged informational injuries: first, DOJ still had not given it the records it sought; second, OSC had not given it the notices and information that would accompany an investigation under § 1214. The court rejected both theories.

## Why the court refused to treat an investigation right as an information right

The first theory was always the harder sell. AFL said that if OSC had investigated, disciplined, or pressured DOJ employees, DOJ might have produced the requested records. The panel had little trouble seeing the problem: the injury from nondisclosure was caused by DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain ran through multiple layers of discretionary decisions by agencies and officials not before the court.

That analysis tracks the Supreme Court’s recent emphasis in *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Brackeen* on the limits of causation and redressability when a plaintiff relies on how third parties might react. A favorable judgment here would not itself produce the records; it would at most set in motion a sequence of speculative enforcement choices. That is not enough.

The second theory is more interesting. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of information that would have come out of the investigation itself. But the panel answered the key question in a very disciplined way: what information does the statute actually entitle the plaintiff to receive? On that point, the answer was none. Section 1216 may require OSC to investigate, but it does not, by its own force, create a right in the requester to receive notice, updates, findings, or other investigatory materials. AFL tried to import the procedural and notification features of § 1214, but the court noted that AFL itself conceded that those requirements are discretionary in the § 1216 context, citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*.

That is the crucial move. *Akins* and *Public Citizen* recognize informational standing where Congress has granted access to specific information and the plaintiff is denied it. They do not recognize standing whenever Congress creates an administrative process that might, incidentally, surface information. The panel’s formulation keeps the doctrine anchored to the statutory entitlement itself, rather than to the plaintiff’s hoped-for informational byproducts.

## The court’s line-drawing is doctrinally sound

The opinion’s distinction between a disclosure right and an investigative regime is persuasive. Informational standing works best when the statutory text is concrete: the plaintiff “is entitled to receive” particular data, records, or disclosures, and the government withholds them. That makes the injury easy to identify and trace.

By contrast, an investigation statute does something different. It tells an agency to look into allegations, not necessarily to disclose what it learns to the complainant. The whole point of such a scheme may be to enable internal oversight, not private access. If the mere existence of an investigative obligation were enough to create informational standing, then virtually every disappointed enforcement request would become a disclosure case in disguise.

That would be a serious expansion of *Akins*. It would also blur the line between private informational rights and public-interest objections to executive enforcement discretion. The Supreme Court has repeatedly resisted that move, and the D.C. Circuit’s opinion fits comfortably within that resistance.

## Implications for administrative-law plaintiffs

There is a broader lesson here for plaintiffs who try to challenge agency nonenforcement by invoking standing theories that sound informational but really seek oversight. After *Greer*, a litigant will have a harder time arguing that “the statute says the agency must investigate” automatically means “I am entitled to the information the investigation might generate.” Courts will likely ask a more precise question: what, exactly, did Congress require the agency to give the plaintiff?

That matters in FOIA-adjacent disputes, election-law complaint systems, ethics investigations, and other administrative schemes where Congress has created complaint processes rather than disclosure mandates. Plaintiffs may still have standing if the statute expressly requires the agency to provide notices, reports, or records to the complainant. But if the claimed injury is only that the agency failed to investigate and therefore failed to create or uncover information, *Greer* suggests that is not enough.

The opinion also helps police an important boundary in Article III doctrine. Informational standing is real, but it is bounded by statutory text. Otherwise, courts would end up converting generalized grievances about agency oversight into cognizable personal injuries. That would be especially problematic in cases like this one, where the plaintiff is effectively asking a court to supervise how an executive-branch enforcement office uses its discretion.

## A modest but important narrowing

This is not a dramatic retreat from *Akins* or *Public Citizen*. Those cases remain good law. But *Greer* narrows their reach in a practical way by insisting on a direct link between the statute and the information allegedly withheld. A statutory duty to investigate is not, by itself, a statutory duty to inform.

That may sound technical, but it is exactly the sort of technicality that keeps informational standing from swallowing the rest of Article III. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion is careful, and perhaps deliberately so: it leaves room for genuine disclosure rights while refusing to let plaintiffs bootstrap standing from the possibility that agency oversight might someday produce useful facts.
