Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Erie Restraint and State-Law Prediction: Why the Fifth Circuit Refused to Certify to the Louisiana Supreme Court

## Why this case matters

*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* is, on one level, a fairly technical insurance-cancellation dispute. But it also highlights a recurring federalism problem: when a federal court sitting in diversity confronts an important but unsettled question of state law, how aggressively should it predict the answer itself, and how readily should it ask the state supreme court to do the talking?

The Fifth Circuit chose prediction over certification here. It held that GoAuto’s electronic cancellation process complied with Louisiana’s premium-finance statute and rejected the plaintiffs’ invitation to certify the interpretive questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. That move matters beyond the parties. Louisiana’s insurance-finance cancellation regime is a recurring and heavily regulated field, and the opinion gives insurers and finance companies some comfort that modern electronic workflows can satisfy a statute written in more traditional terms. But it also reinforces the Fifth Circuit’s longstanding reluctance to certify absent a “compelling reason,” even where state high court guidance is thin.

## The statutory question

The Louisiana statute at issue requires a premium finance company, after an uncured default, to send the insurer a copy of the cancellation notice together with a certification that certain conditions are met: a valid power of attorney, an uncured default, notice to the insured, and notice to others with an interest in the policy. The Louisiana courts have insisted on “strict adherence” to those procedures, because cancellation under a premium-finance arrangement can cut off coverage quickly and with serious consequences.

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s process failed on two fronts. First, APAC’s certification was not signed until after the insurer’s computer system had already canceled the policy. Second, they said GoAuto never truly “received” the cancellation request because the process involved an APAC employee later stamping the printed documents and placing them in a shared file cabinet.

The panel rejected both arguments. On the signature issue, Judge Southwick emphasized that the statute does not expressly require one. Louisiana cases had required a statement certifying compliance, but none had clearly held that a manual signature is indispensable. The court treated the certification requirement as functional rather than formalistic: if the company certifies the statutory predicates, the absence of a handwritten or stamped signature does not, by itself, defeat cancellation.

On receipt, the court took an even more modern view. It read “receipt” to mean taking possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request, and concluded that GoAuto’s computer system received the electronic notice when APAC’s system transmitted it. In other words, a company can “receive” a cancellation request through software. That may be a straightforward application of modern commercial reality, but it is also the move that most clearly reveals the court’s willingness to translate an older statute into contemporary terms rather than pause for Louisiana’s own highest court to do so.

## The certification question

The more interesting part of the opinion, for federal courts and state-court relationship watchers, is the panel’s refusal to certify. The plaintiffs argued that the meaning of “certifying” and “receipt” was important to Louisiana’s insurance scheme and that the absence of controlling Louisiana Supreme Court precedent counseled deference.

The Fifth Circuit was unmoved. Quoting *Jefferson v. Lead Industries Ass’n*, the panel reiterated that it is “chary” about certification and will not send a question up simply because it remains unresolved. That is classic Fifth Circuit doctrine: certification is a tool, not an obligation, and the court has long guarded against overuse.

There is a defensible logic to that stance. Certification can slow litigation, add cost, and invite state-court engagement only where the federal court truly cannot predict the answer with confidence. Here, the panel evidently thought existing Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions, the text of the statute, and the practical realities of electronic processing supplied enough guidance to make an Erie prediction.

Still, the refusal to certify is open to critique. This was not an ordinary contract dispute. It implicated a statutory cancellation scheme that Louisiana courts have repeatedly described as demanding strict compliance. The question was not merely how to read a generic term like “receipt,” but how to adapt a regulatory procedure to modern automated workflows in a way that affects coverage termination. That is exactly the kind of issue on which state courts often have the strongest institutional claim.

The counterargument, of course, is that federal courts are not required to defer simply because a question is important. If they can make a reasoned prediction from the available materials, Erie asks them to do so. On that view, the panel’s refusal to certify reflects not hostility to state-court primacy, but confidence that the state law was sufficiently discernible.

## What the opinion signals

The practical effect of the decision may be to normalize electronic cancellation procedures under Louisiana’s premium-finance statute, so long as the statutory predicates are documented and the insurer’s system actually receives and acts on the notice. The opinion reads the statute in a technologically neutral way, which will likely matter as insurers and finance companies continue automating notices, acknowledgments, and cancellations.

Doctrinally, the case also shows the Fifth Circuit’s preference for narrowing certification to genuine dead ends. The panel did not say Louisiana law was crystal clear; it said the absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court case is not enough, standing alone, to justify certification. That is a familiar and durable position in this circuit. But it also means that, in areas like insurance regulation where state law evolves unevenly and the stakes are high, the federal courts will often be the first to supply the controlling gloss.

Whether that is ideal depends on one’s view of Erie. If the goal is prediction and efficiency, the Fifth Circuit’s approach is hard to fault. If the goal is maximal respect for state courts in defining state regulatory policy, *Williams* may look like another example of federal courts deciding too much themselves. Either way, the opinion is a good reminder that certification doctrine is not just about comity in the abstract. It determines who gets to say what state law means when modern technology runs ahead of the statute’s text.
