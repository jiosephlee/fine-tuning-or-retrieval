Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Strict Compliance, Minimal Formality: The Fifth Circuit Reads Louisiana’s Premium-Finance Cancellation Statute for the Digital Age

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* is a useful reminder that “strict compliance” does not mean “judicial embellishment.” The Louisiana premium-finance cancellation statute has long been treated as unforgiving: if the finance company misses a statutory step, the cancellation fails. But the question in this case was more nuanced. Must the statute’s requirement that the finance company “certif[y]” certain facts be satisfied by a pre-cancellation, human-signed document? And must the insurer receive the cancellation request through some traditional, physical handoff?

The court’s answer was no. That matters well beyond this dispute. Premium-finance cancellations are common, and the mechanics increasingly run through automated systems. The opinion gives insurers and finance companies some breathing room to modernize their workflows without running afoul of Louisiana law—while also signaling that courts will not add formalities the statute itself does not contain.

## The statutory question: strict compliance, but with the statute as written

Louisiana’s statute requires a premium-finance company, after an uncured default, to send the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying several specified facts: the existence of a valid power of attorney, the uncured default, the sending of notice to the insured, and notice to others with an interest in the policy.

The plaintiffs argued that this framework demands a signature on the certification, and that the signature must exist before cancellation takes effect. That argument had intuitive force: if the finance company is “certifying” something, isn’t that the kind of formal act that usually requires a signature? But the Fifth Circuit refused to read that extra requirement into the text. The statute says “certifying,” but it does not say “signed certification,” nor does it identify a named employee who must execute it.

That distinction is the opinion’s core move. The court did not dilute Louisiana’s strict-compliance regime; instead, it confined strict compliance to the requirements the legislature actually imposed. In other words, strict compliance means every required statutory step must be followed, not that courts may layer on customary paper-based assumptions about how a business should evidence compliance.

That is a pretty orthodox textual move, but an important one in practice. If the legislature wanted a signed certification, it could have said so. The court’s point is not that signatures are irrelevant in the abstract, but that courts should not treat them as mandatory when the statutory text does not.

## “Receipt” in the era of e-mail and automated cancellation

The plaintiffs’ second major argument was that GoAuto never “received” the cancellation request in the sense contemplated by the statute. Here too the court took a functional but text-bound approach. Louisiana law says the insurer may cancel only “upon receipt” of the notice and statement from the finance company. The plaintiffs urged a physical-possession reading: someone at GoAuto had to actually take the papers into hand before cancellation could occur.

The Fifth Circuit accepted that “receipt” ordinarily connotes taking possession, custody, or delivery. But it then applied that understanding to the facts as they now exist, not as they might have existed decades ago. GoAuto’s system received the e-mailed notice, and its computer system then processed the cancellation automatically. In the court’s view, that was enough. The statute is not frozen in a paper-file world, and nothing in it requires that a human employee be the one to open the e-mail or click the button.

This is the opinion’s most forward-looking portion. It recognizes a basic point about modern commercial operations: a company can “receive” notice through its systems, not just through a receptionist or file clerk. That reading matters because many statutory schemes drafted in a pre-digital era now have to operate in a world of automated triggers, electronic records, and shared platforms. The court is not rewriting the statute for modernity; it is applying ordinary statutory terms to modern business processes.

## What the court did not do

Just as important, the panel declined to embrace the district court’s more creative theory that an APAC employee delivering the documents could count as a “private carrier.” The Fifth Circuit called that reading “imaginative and unconventional,” and then avoided it entirely by finding that e-mail receipt was enough.

That restraint is notable. The opinion shows an awareness that statutory interpretation in regulated insurance contexts can get slippery fast. Rather than stretching “private carrier” to fit a modern workflow, the court anchored its holding in the cleaner proposition that electronic transmission and automated receipt satisfy the statute’s language. That makes the decision more durable and less dependent on a novel gloss that future litigants could attack.

## Doctrinal implications

The biggest doctrinal takeaway is that “strict compliance” remains a demanding standard, but not an excuse for courts to impose unwritten procedural hurdles. That should resonate in other Louisiana cancellation cases and, more broadly, in insurance disputes where statutory notice mechanics are at issue. If the legislature specifies a list of required facts, courts will insist on those facts—but they are less likely to require a particular evidentiary format unless the statute says so.

The decision also nudges Louisiana law toward technological neutrality. A statute that speaks in terms of sending, receipt, and certification can be satisfied through electronic systems if those systems accomplish the same statutory functions. That has obvious consequences for insurers and premium-finance companies, who now have stronger arguments that automated cancellation workflows comply with state law so long as the required notices are actually sent and received.

For policyholders, the downside is equally obvious. This reading makes it harder to attack a cancellation on the basis of missing signatures, later-added stamps, or the internal routing of documents within a company. Future challenges will likely have to focus on whether the statutory notices were actually sent, whether the default had truly gone uncured, or whether the content of the certification was incomplete or inaccurate. Formal defects alone will be less fruitful unless they map onto an explicit statutory requirement.

## A practical reading, but not a lenient one

On balance, the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning is sensible. It preserves the statute’s structure while refusing to convert “strict adherence” into a judicial requirement of old-school paperwork. That said, the opinion also subtly shifts the battleground in these cases. The fight is no longer about whether a cancellation packet had the right signature stamp in the right place at the right time. It is about whether the insurer’s electronic systems received a statutorily sufficient notice, and whether the finance company’s certifications truly tracked the statute’s required facts.

That is probably where the law should be. Premium-finance cancellation rules are meant to protect insureds from surprise while allowing insurers and finance companies a workable method for dealing with default. A rule that demanded a human signature before any automated cancellation could occur might be easier to litigate, but it would be hard to justify from the text. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion keeps the statute faithful to its language—and makes clear that in Louisiana, strict compliance does not mean insisting on ritual where the legislature required procedure.
