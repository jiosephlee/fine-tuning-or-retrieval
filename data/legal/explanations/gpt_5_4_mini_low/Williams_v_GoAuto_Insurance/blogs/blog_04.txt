Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Benitez, Text, and the Limits of Adverse Inference: What *Williams* Means for Future Cancellation Challenges

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* is about a niche corner of Louisiana insurance law, but it has a broader lesson that reaches well beyond premium-finance cancellations: when a statute is detailed and a state court has not squarely added a requirement, federal courts are reluctant to read one in from hints, paraphrases, or stray language in intermediate appellate opinions. That is especially true where plaintiffs are trying to transform a procedural irregularity into a wholesale invalidation of a cancellation.

The practical stakes are real. Louisiana’s financed-policy cancellation regime is designed to protect insureds from surprise termination while allowing finance companies and insurers to unwind coverage quickly when premiums go unpaid. If a cancellation is ineffective, the insurer may still be on the risk. If it is effective, plaintiffs lose the ability to attack the cancellation process unless they can identify a concrete statutory defect. *Williams* makes that harder.

## The statute and the plaintiffs’ theory

The dispute centered on La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3), which allows a premium finance company to request cancellation after default by sending the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a certification that the agreement contains a valid power of attorney, the agreement is in default and uncured, notice was sent to the insured, and copies went to other interested parties.

The plaintiffs’ main move was to argue that “certifying” necessarily means a signed certification, and that because APAC did not affix a signature until after GoAuto’s computer system had already canceled the policy, the cancellation was defective. They also argued that GoAuto itself never truly “received” the request, because receipt required physical possession by GoAuto rather than an electronic trigger processed by its systems.

Those arguments had surface appeal because Louisiana courts have long said premium-finance cancellations demand “strict adherence.” But the Fifth Circuit refused to convert that phrase into a license to add words to the statute.

## How the panel read *Benitez*

The most interesting part of the opinion is its treatment of *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a Louisiana Fifth Circuit decision plaintiffs tried to use as a lever for a signature requirement. In *Benitez*, the court noted that the notice there did not identify any employee, was not signed, and did not “purport to ‘certify’” the statements. Plaintiffs argued that this language effectively made a signature mandatory.

Judge Southwick’s panel would have none of it. The opinion treats *Benitez* as descriptive, not doctrinal. The absence of a signature in *Benitez* was one defect among several, and the case did not hold that a signature alone is essential. That distinction matters. A lot of litigation turns on whether a court’s observation is a holding or merely an account of what made the document inadequate in that case. *Williams* takes a disciplined approach: unless the Louisiana courts have clearly made signature a statutory condition, federal courts should not infer one from an opinion that did not decide the question.

That is an important narrowing move. It means plaintiffs cannot simply cite *Benitez* for the proposition that an unsigned notice is void. They need something more—either clearer state authority or a statutory text that actually says what they want it to say.

## Text over implication

The panel’s strongest reasoning is textual. Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the finance company to send a copy of the notice and a statement certifying the listed facts. It does not say the certification must be signed, and it does not say that a named employee must personally execute it before cancellation can proceed. The court emphasized that the statute assigns the certification function to “the insurance premium finance company,” not to a particular human being.

That observation reflects a practical point about modern corporate operations. Firms act through systems, not always through hand-signed paper. The panel was willing to recognize that reality, especially where the notice and certification were generated through APAC’s automated process and transmitted to GoAuto’s system, which then canceled the policy.

There is a nice structural point here: “strict adherence” does not mean courts get to supplement the statute with extra formalities. Louisiana law demands compliance with the enumerated steps; it does not authorize a judicial rewrite of those steps to include a signature requirement that the legislature did not include.

## Receipt in the digital age

The second major issue was “receipt.” The statute says the insurer may cancel “upon receipt” of the notice of cancellation and statement from the finance company. Plaintiffs urged a physical-possession reading: no possession by a human employee, no receipt, no cancellation.

Again, the panel read the text more flexibly. Relying on ordinary dictionary definitions, it concluded that receipt includes taking possession, delivery, or custody of the request. That let the court treat GoAuto’s computer system as the recipient when the e-mail landed and the system automatically processed cancellation. The later stamping of paper documents by an APAC employee did not matter because cancellation had already occurred.

This is the part of the opinion most likely to shape future litigation. It suggests that courts will not insist on a paper-era model of notice where the statute does not clearly do so. If the insurer’s automated system gets the message and responds by canceling the policy, that may satisfy “receipt” even if no employee manually opens an envelope or signs for it.

## What this means for future plaintiffs

The immediate strategic consequence is that plaintiffs challenging financed-policy cancellations in Louisiana will have a harder time focusing on missing signatures, unnamed certifiers, or the mechanics of electronic transmission. Those arguments are not dead, but *Williams* makes them much weaker absent stronger Louisiana authority.

The battlefield may shift toward the statute’s expressly enumerated prerequisites: Was the policy actually in default? Was notice timely sent to the insured? Were copies sent to all interested parties? Did the finance company really have a valid power of attorney? Those are more promising avenues because they go to the statute’s express conditions rather than to implied formalities.

There is also a litigation lesson for defendants. If an insurer and finance company want to defend these cancellations cleanly, they should make the documentary record easy to defend: keep timestamps, preserve transmission logs, identify the chain of notice, and make sure the certification language tracks the statute. *Williams* is forgiving about the medium, but not about substance.

## The broader doctrinal signal

The opinion also reflects a familiar Fifth Circuit stance in diversity cases: it will not lightly certify a question to a state supreme court, and it will not use uncertainty in state law as an invitation to create new state-law requirements. The panel declined certification, emphasizing that the absence of controlling state supreme court precedent is not enough by itself.

That restraint has downstream effects. It means intermediate state appellate dicta will be treated cautiously, especially when plaintiffs ask a federal court to elevate them into hard-edged rules. It also means Louisiana litigants may continue to see federal courts favor a text-first approach when the state supreme court has not spoken.

In short, *Williams* does not abolish cancellation challenges. But it does narrow them. The opinion treats the statute as a checklist, not a trapdoor, and it makes clear that plaintiffs cannot turn the absence of a signature into a stand-in for a missing statutory requirement. For future cases, that is likely to matter more than any one insurance cancellation dispute.
