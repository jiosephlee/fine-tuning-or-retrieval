Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How did the court decide the harm was not "on account of" political opinion when the threats explicitly mentioned the Liberal Party?
Question:
The opinion says the nexus requirement failed because the main reason for the threats was Jimenez's involvement with Don Rafa's property and criminal operations, not the family's political opinion. But some of the threats literally said things like "death to snitches from Liberal Party," and the attackers also described them as people "in politics." I understand mixed-motive cases exist, but I'm having trouble seeing why the political component was only tangential rather than "one central reason." Is the idea that once there is a stronger non-protected motive (like retaliation for investigating criminal activity), the political motive becomes legally insufficient? How do courts evaluate evidence when the persecutor seems to invoke both political affiliation and anti-snitch retaliation?
Answer:
The key point is that **an express political reference is not enough by itself** if the record supports the agency’s finding that the **real reason** for the threats was something else.

Under the INA, an applicant must show that the protected ground was **“at least one central reason”** for the harm. The Tenth Circuit repeats the governing rule this way: the protected ground cannot be “**incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate**” to another motive. A persecutor can certainly have **mixed motives**, but one of those motives must still be central.

That is exactly how the court read the evidence here.

### Why the “Liberal Party” language did not control
The court did **not** ignore the brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party” or the later comment that Petitioners were “in politics.” Instead, it treated those statements as part of the overall record and asked what the threats were **really about**.

The court emphasized several facts showing the dominant motive was retaliation for Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa’s property and criminal operations:

- the escalated threats began **after** Jimenez became involved in the property dispute;
- one threat accused him of being a **“snitch” for the prosecutor’s office**;
- the men said he was responsible for the **searches and seizures** at the property;
- later, they again called him a **“snitch for the police”** and said the order came from **Don Rafa**.

On that record, the court concluded the political references were **secondary**. In the court’s words, Petitioners’ political opinion was “**merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm**.”

### So does a stronger non-protected motive automatically defeat nexus?
Not automatically. But if the record supports the finding that the non-protected motive was the **predominant** or **central** one, then the political motive will not satisfy the nexus requirement even if it is mentioned.

That is the important distinction in mixed-motive cases:  
- **Protected motive present** = not enough.  
- **Protected motive central** = enough.  
- **Protected motive only incidental to retaliation, revenge, or anti-snitch conduct** = not enough.

### How courts evaluate this kind of evidence
Courts look at the **whole context**, not isolated phrases. Here, the Tenth Circuit relied on the sequence of events and the persecutors’ repeated focus on Jimenez’s role as a “snitch” connected to the property, not on his partisan activity. The court also noted that Petitioners had already been involved in Liberal Party activity before the property dispute, yet the serious threats escalated only after the dispute and investigation began.

So the short answer is:

> The court did not say political language is irrelevant. It said the political references were outweighed by substantial evidence that the persecutors were mainly retaliating against Jimenez for interfering with Don Rafa’s criminal/property interests. Under the “one central reason” standard, a merely tangential political motive is legally insufficient.
