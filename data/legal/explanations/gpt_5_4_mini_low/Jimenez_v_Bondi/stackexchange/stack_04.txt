Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why was internal relocation discussed as if the burden was on the applicants, even though the opinion quotes the government's rebuttal standard?
Question:
The court says the government may rebut a presumption of future persecution by showing the applicant can relocate internally, citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i). But earlier the court already held there was no past persecution, so there was no presumption to rebut. Then in the relocation section the court says the petitioners 'have not demonstrated' a fear of future persecution, but 'even if they had,' the record supports relocation. I'm confused about who bears the burden on internal relocation when there is no finding of past persecution. Is the burden on the asylum applicant to prove relocation is unreasonable, or on DHS to prove relocation is reasonable? Did the court just discuss relocation as an alternative holding?
Answer:
Yes — the court was discussing **internal relocation as an alternative ground**, and the burden point changes depending on whether the applicant has already established **past persecution**.

## Short answer

- **If the applicant proves past persecution**, a **presumption** of future persecution arises.
  - Then DHS can rebut that presumption by showing, among other things, that the applicant **could safely and reasonably relocate** internally.
- **If the applicant does not prove past persecution**, there is **no presumption to rebut**.
  - In that situation, the **asylum applicant** bears the burden of proving a **well-founded fear of future persecution**, which ordinarily includes showing that safe internal relocation is not a reasonable way to avoid the harm.

So the court was not shifting the burden improperly. It was saying:

1. Petitioners **failed to show past persecution**, so no presumption attached; and
2. **Even assuming** they had shown a fear of future persecution, the record still supported denial because they could relocate within Colombia.

## How the opinion uses that framework

The key line is in Part F:

> “**Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate** to another part of Colombia.”

That is classic **alternative reasoning**. The court is saying relocation is enough to defeat the claim **even if** the applicants cleared the earlier hurdle.

## Why the opinion quotes the government rebuttal standard

The court quotes the rebuttal standard from **8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)** because that is the regulation governing the **presumption** scenario after past persecution. But the opinion does **not** rest its relocation discussion solely on that rebuttal framework.

Instead, the court has already held:
- no past persecution,
- no nexus,
- no showing that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them,
- no well-founded fear of future persecution.

Once the court gets there, the relocation discussion becomes **extra support**, not the sole basis for the result.

## Burden when there is no past persecution

The opinion itself states the general asylum rule:

> “To be eligible for a discretionary grant of asylum, **an applicant bears the burden of proving** by a preponderance of the evidence that he is a refugee.”

That means that absent past persecution, the **applicant** must establish the elements of future fear. Internal relocation is part of that future-fear analysis, so the applicant cannot simply rely on DHS to disprove it unless the regulatory presumption has been triggered.

## Bottom line

So the answer is:

- **No past persecution = no presumption = no DHS rebuttal burden.**
- The **asylum applicant** bears the burden of showing a well-founded fear of future persecution, which includes dealing with the feasibility of internal relocation.
- The court’s relocation discussion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is best read as **an alternative holding / alternative ground** supporting denial, not as a burden-shifting error.
