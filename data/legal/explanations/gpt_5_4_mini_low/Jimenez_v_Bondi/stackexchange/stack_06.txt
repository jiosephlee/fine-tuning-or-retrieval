Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why did the withholding of removal claim automatically fail just because the asylum claim failed?
Question:
At the end, the court says that because the petitioners failed to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution for asylum, they necessarily failed to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. I thought withholding and asylum overlap a lot, but are not identical, especially because asylum is discretionary and withholding is mandatory if the standard is met. Is the court saying withholding always fails whenever asylum fails, or only when asylum fails on certain merits elements like persecution/nexus/government protection rather than timing, discretion, or procedural bars?
Answer:
The court is **not** saying that withholding of removal always fails whenever asylum fails. It is saying something narrower: **when asylum fails because the applicant did not prove the underlying persecution-based elements, withholding will usually fail too, because withholding uses the same core factual predicates but a higher burden of proof.**

### Why that follows here

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit affirmed that petitioners failed to show:

- **past persecution**
- **nexus** to a protected ground
- **government unwillingness/unability** to protect them
- and, relatedly, a **well-founded fear of future persecution**

Those are the substantive elements that matter to both asylum and withholding. The court then cites the familiar rule that because asylum requires a **“well-founded fear”** and withholding requires the more demanding **“clear probability”** / **“more likely than not”** showing, a petitioner who cannot satisfy the lower asylum standard ordinarily cannot satisfy the higher withholding standard. That is why the opinion says they “necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.”

### The key distinction

You are right that asylum and withholding are **not identical**:

- **Asylum** is **discretionary**
- **Withholding** is **mandatory** if the standard is met
- They also differ in some **procedural bars** and eligibility rules

So the rule is **not**:

> “Any time asylum is denied, withholding automatically fails.”

Rather, it is:

> “If asylum is denied because the applicant failed to prove the merits of persecution/nexus/government protection/fear, then withholding generally fails too, because withholding requires an even stronger showing.”

### When asylum can fail but withholding still be available

That can happen when asylum is denied for reasons **not tied to the merits of persecution**, such as:

- the **one-year filing deadline**
- some **discretionary** denial of asylum
- certain asylum-specific statutory bars

In those situations, withholding may still be available because it is evaluated under a different framework and is not subject to every asylum limitation.

### Why the court’s phrasing makes sense in this opinion

Here, the denial was **not** based on a timing bar or a discretionary refusal. It was based on the petitioners’ failure to establish the basic substantive facts needed for refugee status in the first place. So once the court concluded they did not meet the asylum standard on the merits, it was correct to say they necessarily could not meet withholding’s higher standard.
