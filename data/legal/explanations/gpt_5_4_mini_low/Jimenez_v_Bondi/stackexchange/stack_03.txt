Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why did the court treat the 'unable or unwilling to protect' element as unmet when police officers told them to leave because police were part of the problem?
Question:
The family apparently spoke to at least two police officers, and one officer allegedly said nothing could be done and that police officers were contributing to the problem. That sounds to me like evidence of state inability or corruption. But the court says this was only a 'one-off conversation' and that the family made minimal efforts to seek help. What level of reporting or proof is usually required to show a government is unwilling or unable to control private persecutors? Also, if an applicant has reason to think the police are compromised, how much evidence do they need to show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous?
Answer:
The court was not saying that the officer’s statement was irrelevant. It was saying that, on this record, it was not enough to compel the conclusion that the *Colombian government* was unwilling or unable to protect Petitioners.

That distinction is important in asylum law. The “unable or unwilling to control” element asks whether the *state as a whole* failed to protect the applicant from private harm. A single unhelpful officer can be evidence, but it is usually not enough by itself. As the court put it, the inquiry looks to whether the government responded to the persecution, and “a key fact” is whether the applicant reported the harm. The reason is practical: without notice, “there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” *Jimenez v. Bondi* (citing *Singh*).

Here, the Tenth Circuit emphasized three things:

1. **Minimal reporting.** Petitioners spoke to two officers, but they did not report wrongdoing through more official channels or otherwise show a broader attempt to seek protection.
2. **A “one-off conversation” is not systemic proof.** The court relied on the idea that police apathy or corruption by one officer does not necessarily show the government is unwilling or unable to protect. It cited *Osorio-Morales* for the proposition that a single unhelpful encounter does not establish a government-wide failure.
3. **The record cut the other way.** The court noted that authorities had already attempted searches and seizures at the property. That made it harder to argue that the government was aligned with or indifferent to the persecutors.

So the court’s point was not “the officer’s statement proves nothing,” but rather: *standing alone, it does not compel a finding of state inability or unwillingness, especially when the applicants did little else to seek protection and the record contains evidence of government action against the criminal actors.*

## What level of proof is usually required?

There is no rigid reporting formula. But in practice, applicants generally need evidence showing one of the following:

- they **reported** the harm and the authorities failed to act;
- they attempted to report, but the authorities **refused, ignored, or participated** in the harm;
- reporting would have been **futile or dangerous**, supported by concrete evidence; or
- the country conditions and other record evidence show a **systemic inability or unwillingness** to protect similarly situated people.

The more an applicant can show that the failure was not isolated, but part of a broader pattern, the stronger the claim.

## If police are compromised, how much evidence of futility or danger is needed?

The applicant does **not** have to risk harm just to create a record. The Tenth Circuit expressly says a failure to report is not necessarily fatal if the applicant can show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. But the claim has to be supported by evidence, not just suspicion.

In *Jimenez*, the court found the futility/danger argument unsupported because:

- the Petitioners’ claim that officers were part of the problem rested mainly on the one conversation;
- they did not use available official channels, including Jimenez’s government contacts; and
- the record showed the state had already acted against the property-linked criminal activity.

So the lesson is: if an applicant believes police are compromised, they should try to produce **corroboration** — prior reports ignored by police, threats against complainants, country reports showing police collusion, testimony about widespread corruption, or similar evidence. A bare assertion that “reporting would have been dangerous” usually will not be enough unless the record backs it up.

In short, the court treated the element as unmet because it saw **insufficient evidence of a state-wide failure of protection**, not just an isolated discouraging remark by one officer.
