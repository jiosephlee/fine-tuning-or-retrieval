Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Chapter 1: Facts, Parties, and the Path to Federal Court

## The petitioners: a Colombian family from Bogota involved in a furniture business

The petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* are a family from Bogota, Colombia: Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. Before coming to the United States, the family owned and worked in a furniture factory in Bogota. That detail matters because the factory became one of the main places where the alleged threats and confrontations occurred. The case is not just about isolated political activity or a single private dispute; the court viewed it as a record made up of several connected events involving the family’s work, home life, and public activities.

## Their political activity: membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and support for Federico Gutierrez in the 2022 presidential election

The family had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012, and in the 2022 Colombian presidential election they supported Federico Gutierrez. They went door to door in local neighborhoods to seek favorable votes. This political activity is the factual basis for their claim that they were targeted because of political opinion. In asylum cases, the applicant must show not only harm, but harm linked to a protected ground such as political opinion. Here, the family’s campaign work was the first strand of facts the court had to consider.

## Initial politically tinged threats received during campaign activity

Around the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice a week about his involvement in the Liberal Party. Other party leaders and colleagues also received threats. In early June, a colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite reporting threats. The family continued campaigning anyway. These early threats are important because they are the strongest facts tying the case to politics. But the court later distinguishes between these threats and later events tied to the property dispute, which became central to the nexus analysis.

## Jimenez’s legal and prosecutorial background: law student, provisional lawyer, and intern in the National Prosecutor’s office

Jimenez was not only a political supporter; he also had legal experience. In 2022 he was a law student, held a provisional law license, and worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia. In that role, he helped victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. The opinion uses this background to explain why others may have viewed him as connected to law enforcement or the justice system. That connection later becomes important when the court evaluates whether the threats were motivated by politics or by Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute and perceived cooperation with authorities.

## The San Bernardo property dispute and Jimenez’s involvement on behalf of a client

In June 2022, a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez for help with a property dispute. She said she had not received rent from a tenant for over a year and wanted Jimenez to speak with him about restitution and leaving the property. Jimenez visited the property to negotiate, but the tenant told him not to “look for trouble” and refused to negotiate. The client chose not to proceed with a formal eviction and instead hoped the tenant would surrender the property after a lawyer intervened.

This episode matters because it introduced a separate source of conflict, one that the court later treated as potentially more important than the political threats. The opinion distinguishes the campaign-related threats from the property dispute because the two streams of events may have had different motives.

## Discovery that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros ('Don Rafa') and the property’s connection to trafficking, prostitution, kidnapping, and murder

After reviewing the title to the property, Jimenez learned that the tenant was actually the client’s ex-husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa.” The opinion describes him as a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Gustavo Petro. Jimenez also discovered that the property had been used as a day-rental for migrants and as a base for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The property was associated with kidnapping and murder, and the health department had sealed off the third floor. Law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures, but apparently without success.

This portion of the facts is central to the court’s later reasoning about motive and government protection. If the threats came from Don Rafa because Jimenez interfered with criminal activity or with a property operation, then the threats might not be “on account of” political opinion. And if the government had already attempted searches and seizures, that undercut the claim that the Colombian government was wholly unwilling or unable to respond.

## Escalation of events: threatening brochure, gunpoint confrontation at the factory, threats to employees, and the school incident involving the younger son

The conflict escalated after the property visit. Jimenez received a threatening brochure at the furniture factory that said, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He threw it away. Then, the next month, two men confronted him at night at the factory, held him at gunpoint, and told him to leave the neighborhood. One man accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures at the property.

About two months later, the same men returned to the factory while the family was away. They assaulted an employee, demanded to know where the petitioners were, and again called them “snitches for the police” who were in politics. They said Don Rafa had sent them and threatened to kill the petitioners if they were seen again.

Days later, Jimenez saw a man put his arm around his younger son as the boy left school, cover the boy’s mouth, and tell him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez ran over and pushed the man while the child escaped. These are the most alarming facts in the case, and they are the ones the family relied on to argue that they suffered persecution and had a well-founded fear of future harm.

## Temporary move to Jimenez’s mother’s home and the family’s decision to leave Colombia

After the school incident, the family moved to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. During that time, they decided to leave for the United States. The temporary move matters because it became part of the court’s safe-relocation analysis. If they could live safely, even for a short time, in another part of Colombia, that tended to support the conclusion that they could avoid future harm by relocating within the country.

## Contacts with police officers before departure and the officers’ alleged responses

Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez told a police patrol officer what had happened. According to Jimenez, the officer said nothing could be done, claimed police officers were contributing to the problem, and told the family they should leave. Earlier, a local police officer had also told Jimenez to stay away from the property and not seek trouble.

These conversations are important because asylum applicants claiming persecution by private actors generally must show that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them. Reporting to police, or explaining why reporting would have been futile or dangerous, is often relevant to that issue. Here, the petitioners relied on these officer conversations as evidence of government inability or unwillingness, but the court later found that the record showed only limited contact with police and did not establish a broader failure of state protection.

## Removal proceedings in the United States: Notices to Appear, conceded removability, and applications for asylum, withholding, and CAT protection

Three months after arriving in the United States without being admitted or paroled, the Department of Homeland Security served the family with individual Notices to Appear. The notices charged them as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act. In August 2023, they admitted the factual allegations. The immigration judge then charged them as inadmissible and ordered them removed to Colombia.

The family applied for asylum and withholding of removal, and the minor children proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application. They also sought protection under the Convention Against Torture. This posture is typical in immigration litigation: the government initiates removal, the noncitizens concede or contest removability, and then seek relief from removal based on fear of harm if returned.

## The IJ’s ruling, the BIA’s affirmance, and the narrowed issues presented to the Tenth Circuit

The immigration judge denied all forms of relief. Although the IJ found the petitioners credible, the IJ held that they failed to show past persecution, failed to show a nexus between the harm and a protected ground, failed to show that internal relocation was unreasonable, and failed to show that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The IJ also denied CAT protection.

The petitioners appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals, which affirmed and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected their due process arguments about the right to counsel. By the time the case reached the Tenth Circuit, however, the petitioners had narrowed their challenge. They asked only for review of the asylum and withholding rulings, not the CAT denial or the due process issue.

That narrowing is important for reading the rest of the opinion. The court’s analysis focuses on three core asylum questions: whether the petitioners suffered persecution, whether the persecution was on account of political opinion, and whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The court also briefly addresses internal relocation, because even if a fear of harm exists, asylum may still be denied if the applicant can safely relocate within the home country.

# Chapter 2: Standard of Review and the Asylum/Withholding Framework

## Jurisdiction and the Scope of Review

The Tenth Circuit begins by identifying its authority to hear the case. Here, the court exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which authorizes judicial review of final orders of removal. That matters because the petitioners are not asking the court to start the immigration case over; they are asking the court to review whether the agency legally and factually erred in denying relief.

The court also makes clear that its review is limited to the administrative record—the evidence that was before the agency when it decided the case. That means the court cannot consider new evidence or retry the facts. The petition for review is therefore an appellate review of the immigration record, not a fresh proceeding.

## Reviewing the BIA, While Looking to the IJ for Context

Because a single Board of Immigration Appeals member affirmed the immigration judge’s decision, the Tenth Circuit treats the BIA as the operative decisionmaker. That is the decision under review. At the same time, the court may consult the immigration judge’s reasoning when the BIA adopted or relied on the same grounds. In practical terms, the BIA’s decision is the focus, but the IJ’s opinion can help explain the factual and legal basis for the result.

This is an important feature of immigration appellate practice. The court does not simply substitute its own judgment for that of the agency. Instead, it asks whether the agency’s decision is supported by the record under the proper standard of review.

## Substantial-Evidence Review

The court repeatedly applies the substantial-evidence standard to the agency’s factual findings. Under that standard, the BIA’s findings are conclusive unless the record would compel any reasonable adjudicator to reach the opposite conclusion. That is a demanding standard for the petitioners. It is not enough to show that the evidence could support a different result; the record must require a different result.

This standard explains the court’s posture throughout the opinion. When the petitioners argue that threats, fear, and government inaction amount to persecution, the court is not asking whether it might have viewed the evidence differently in the first instance. It is asking whether the BIA’s contrary view was still reasonable in light of the record.

## Asylum as Discretionary Relief and the Burden of Proof

The opinion next sets out the basic asylum framework. Asylum is discretionary relief: even if an applicant meets the statutory definition of a refugee, the Attorney General still has discretion whether to grant asylum. But before discretion matters, the applicant must first prove eligibility.

The applicant bears the burden of showing by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a refugee. “Preponderance” means more likely than not. So the applicant must persuade the agency that the evidence makes refugee status more probable than not.

The INA defines a refugee as a person who is outside the country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return, or to avail himself or herself of that country’s protection, because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of one of five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. The protected-ground requirement is central. Harm alone is not enough; the harm must be connected to one of these statutory reasons.

## Past Persecution and the Presumption of Future Persecution

One common way to prove refugee status is to show past persecution. If an applicant proves past persecution, that generally creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. That presumption is important because it shifts some of the burden into the government’s court: the applicant no longer has to prove future fear from scratch unless the government rebuts the presumption.

But past persecution is not established merely by showing unpleasant or even frightening treatment. The applicant must satisfy three elements. First, there must be an incident or series of incidents rising to the level of persecution. Second, the persecution must be on account of a protected ground. Third, the persecution must be by the government or by private actors the government is unable or unwilling to control.

The court’s later analysis tracks these three requirements closely. It asks first whether the threats and related events were severe enough to count as persecution at all. It then asks whether the harm was inflicted because of political opinion or some other protected reason. Finally, it asks whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners from the people threatening them.

## Withholding of Removal and Its Relationship to Asylum

The petitioners also sought withholding of removal, but the court explains that their failure on asylum largely defeats withholding as well. That is because withholding generally imposes a higher standard than asylum. If an applicant cannot meet the lower asylum standard on the same set of facts and theory, the applicant usually cannot satisfy withholding either.

The opinion therefore treats asylum as the threshold claim. Once the petitioners fail to establish a well-founded fear of persecution for asylum purposes, they necessarily fail to meet the more demanding standard for withholding of removal on the same evidence.

## Claims Not Pursued on Appeal

Although the immigration judge also denied protection under the Convention Against Torture and rejected due process arguments related to the right to counsel, the petitioners did not pursue those issues in the Tenth Circuit. That means the court does not analyze them on the merits. For appellate purposes, abandoned issues are not before the court, so the opinion confines itself to asylum and withholding of removal.

Understanding what the petitioners did not appeal is as important as understanding what they did. It narrows the case to the legal questions the court actually decided: whether the record compels a finding of past persecution, whether the harm was connected to political opinion, and whether the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them.

# Chapter 3: Why the Court Found No Past Persecution

## The INA’s undefined term and the Tenth Circuit’s working standard

In the asylum context, one of the first questions is whether the applicant suffered “persecution.” The Immigration and Nationality Act does not define that term, so courts supply the content through case law. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit relied on its own precedent to explain that persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm” in a way that is more serious than ordinary difficulty, harassment, or isolated threats. The court’s formulation matters because it sets a threshold: an asylum applicant must show not just that events were frightening or unpleasant, but that they crossed the legal line into persecution.

The court also reminds readers that persecution is a fact-intensive concept. It is not enough to identify a bad event in the abstract. The question is whether, on the record as a whole, the treatment rises to the level the INA requires. That framing is important because the court’s review is deferential. It is not deciding the issue from scratch; it is asking whether the record compels a contrary conclusion to the agency’s finding.

## More than restrictions or threats

The Tenth Circuit repeated a familiar rule from its asylum cases: persecution must involve harm that is more than “mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” That language shows that not every serious-seeming threat qualifies. The court treated this case as involving threats, intimidation, and psychological distress, but no physical violence against Petitioners themselves.

That distinction drove the analysis. Petitioners described a series of menacing incidents: threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, armed confrontations at the factory, and the frightening schoolyard encounter involving one of the children. The court did not dismiss these events as trivial. Instead, it accepted that they were alarming and dangerous. But under Tenth Circuit precedent, fear alone is not automatically persecution. The court asked whether the combined effect of these incidents amounted to the type of harm the asylum statute recognizes.

## The cumulative-harm approach

The court did not analyze each incident in isolation. It expressly used the cumulative-harm approach, which asks whether the aggregate of all mistreatment amounts to persecution even if no single event would do so by itself. That matters in asylum cases because a series of lesser acts can, taken together, become legally significant.

Here, however, the cumulative picture still did not persuade the court. The threats, the brochure, the factory confrontations, and the incident involving the child were serious, but the court concluded they did not add up to the kind of sustained harm that compels a finding of persecution. This is where the appellate standard becomes crucial. The court did not ask whether it might have viewed the facts differently in the first instance. It asked whether any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to find persecution. The answer was no.

## Threats and the court’s insistence that threats alone are usually not enough

A central point in the court’s reasoning was its statement that threats alone are generally insufficient to constitute persecution. That rule is important because many asylum claims involve intimidation without physical injury. The court acknowledged that threats are relevant and must be considered, but it refused to treat them as automatically satisfying the persecution element.

The court used Petitioners’ case to illustrate why. Even though they received repeated threats and one of the threats was accompanied by a gunpoint confrontation, Petitioners conceded that they were not physically harmed. The court treated that concession as significant. It did not mean physical injury is required in every case, but it did reinforce that the record lacked one of the strongest markers of persecution. Without lasting physical harm, the court looked for other unusually severe facts that might still establish persecution. It found none.

## The significance of the no-lasting-physical-harm concession

Petitioners conceded at the merits hearing that they were not physically harmed and acknowledged in their briefing that they suffered no lasting physical harm. The court was careful to note that physical harm is not a necessary prerequisite to asylum. But the concession still mattered because it narrowed the case to threats and emotional injury.

That narrowing made Petitioners’ burden harder. Once the physical-harm component dropped out, the question became whether the remaining intimidation and distress were severe enough to qualify on their own. The court’s answer was that, on this record, they were not. The opinion therefore shows that while physical injury is not required, its absence can make it much more difficult to persuade a reviewing court that the agency was compelled to find persecution.

## Emotional and psychological harm

Petitioners tried to shift the focus to the emotional and psychological effects of what happened to them. Duarte said she could not sleep or rest, and their son developed panic attacks and stress. The court did not doubt that these reactions were real or that the family had experienced distress. But it treated those effects as consequences of the threats rather than as independent harm severe enough to amount to persecution.

That distinction is central to the court’s analysis. Psychological suffering can count in asylum law, but the Tenth Circuit made clear that distress caused by frightening threats is usually not enough by itself. The court described Petitioners’ experience as unfortunate and understandably upsetting, but still outside the legal definition of persecution. In other words, the fact that threats generate fear, sleep loss, or anxiety does not automatically convert the threats into persecution.

## Why the court thought the record fell short

The opinion also situates this case within the Tenth Circuit’s prior decisions. The court pointed to other cases involving more severe or more numerous threats, yet still found no persecution. Those comparisons were meant to show how demanding the circuit’s standard is. If applications involving telephone threats, vague in-person threats, detention, armed robbery, or threats tied to compliance demands did not compel a finding of persecution, then Petitioners’ record—though serious—did not either.

This use of precedent is doing real work. The court is not saying Petitioners’ case is unimportant. It is saying that, measured against the circuit’s existing asylum cases, the events here do not cross the line. That is why the opinion repeatedly emphasizes substantial evidence. The agency’s finding survives unless the record would force any reasonable adjudicator to conclude the opposite. The court concluded that this record did not force that result.

## The substantial-evidence standard and the court’s limited role

The substantial-evidence standard shaped the outcome of the persecution issue. Under that standard, the court did not reweigh the evidence or decide whether persecution might be a plausible conclusion. It asked a narrower question: did the evidence compel a finding of past persecution? That is a demanding standard for petitioners. A claimant can present a sympathetic story and still lose if the evidence leaves room for the agency’s conclusion.

That is exactly what happened here. The court accepted that the family suffered multiple frightening incidents and emotional stress. But because the law requires more than threats and distress, and because the record did not contain physical harm or other exceptionally severe mistreatment, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s conclusion.

## The holding on past persecution

The court’s bottom line on this element was straightforward: the family’s experiences, considered together, did not amount to past persecution under the INA. The threats, psychological distress, and other hardships were real, but they did not compel a contrary finding. As a result, the court upheld the BIA on the persecution element and moved on to the separate questions of nexus and government protection.

# Chapter 4: Nexus to Political Opinion and the Court’s Mixed-Motive Analysis

## The petitioners narrowed their nexus theory on appeal

On appeal, the petitioners did not press every theory they may have mentioned below. Although the opinion notes that they had also invoked a particular social group theory, they did not brief any meaningful argument about that group or their membership in it. The Tenth Circuit therefore treated that theory as abandoned for purposes of its review and focused on the only preserved causation theory: that the harm they suffered was “on account of” their political opinion. For law students, this is an important appellate move. Courts generally limit themselves to arguments actually developed in the briefs, and here that narrowing mattered because it left the petitioners with one route to asylum nexus rather than two.

## The asylum statute requires a protected ground to be at least one central reason

The court framed nexus under the asylum statute’s “one central reason” requirement. It is not enough that an applicant’s political opinion played some role in the harm. The applicant must show that the protected ground was at least one central reason for the persecution. In practical terms, this means the political motive has to do real work in explaining why the persecutor acted.

The opinion emphasizes that a protected ground cannot be merely incidental, tangential, superficial, subordinate, or minor. That language matters because it shows the court was not asking whether politics was mentioned somewhere in the story. It was asking whether politics was a primary explanatory cause of the threats and intimidation. Under this standard, a protected ground can coexist with other motives, but it cannot be pushed to the sidelines.

## Mixed motives do not defeat nexus, but they do not make it easy to prove

The court acknowledged the basic mixed-motive principle: a persecutor can have more than one reason for targeting a person. That means the existence of a personal, economic, or criminal motive does not automatically defeat asylum. But mixed motives help the applicant only if a protected ground remains central among them. The court’s analysis therefore turned on relative importance. It asked whether political opinion was a central reason, or whether it was merely a label attached to conduct that was really driven by other concerns.

That distinction is the key to the court’s reasoning. The petitioners tried to show that the threats were politically charged because they had supported a rival candidate and were active in the Liberal Party. The court did not deny that politics was part of the background. But background politics is not the same thing as causation under the statute. The question was whether the persecutors targeted them because of their political views, or because of something else.

## The evidence supporting a political-opinion theory

The petitioners had some evidence that could support a political-opinion narrative. They were members of the Colombian Liberal Party, they campaigned for Federico Gutiérrez, and they went door-to-door seeking votes. Jimenez also received early threatening calls related to his party involvement. One threatening brochure said “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” which on its face connects the threat to party affiliation. There was also evidence that another Liberal Party colleague had been threatened and later kidnapped and murdered.

Taken together, this evidence could suggest that political activity created danger. The problem for the petitioners was not that the record contained no political references at all. The problem was that the court saw those political references as part of an earlier and different phase of events, not as the main driver of the specific threats that gave rise to their asylum claim.

## The evidence cutting against nexus was stronger

The court found that the later and more serious threats began only after Jimenez became involved in the property dispute involving Don Rafa’s operation. That timing mattered. The escalation coincided with Jimenez’s work as a provisional lawyer and his investigation of the property used for criminal activity, not merely with his campaign activity.

The opinion repeatedly highlights how the persecutors described Jimenez. They called him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and said he was responsible for searches and seizures at the property. Those accusations are highly significant because they point to retaliation for his perceived interference with law enforcement and with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise. The men who returned to the factory also demanded to know where Petitioners were and said the order came from Don Rafa. This made the threats look less like punishment for political dissent and more like retaliation against someone who had become a problem for a criminal boss.

## The court distinguished campaign-related threats from later targeted threats

The petitioners wanted the court to connect all of the threats together as politically motivated persecution. The court refused to flatten the chronology. It treated the early campaign-related threats as evidence that politics had caused some danger in the past, but it found that the later threats were different in character and source.

That distinction is central to the opinion. The court noted that Petitioners continued campaigning even after the early threats and the killing of a fellow campaigner. It also noted that there was no evidence that Don Rafa was behind those initial threats. By contrast, the more serious incidents followed Jimenez’s contact with Don Rafa’s property, his investigation of criminal activity there, and the resulting suspicion that he had triggered police attention. In the court’s view, the escalation was tied to this property dispute, not to the petitioners’ party activity.

## Why the court thought the real motive was protection of criminal interests

The court concluded that the “predominant motive” was Jimenez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa, especially his role in the property dispute and the resulting law-enforcement attention. That is, the threats were aimed at stopping a perceived informant and protecting a criminal operation. The fact that the attackers also mentioned politics did not change the core motive the court identified.

This is the court’s most important substantive move on nexus. It did not say political opinion was absent. It said political opinion was not the reason that mattered most. The persecutors appeared to be reacting to Jimenez as a lawyer, investigator, and perceived obstacle to their activity. Under the statute, that is not enough unless political opinion is also a central reason. The court found that it was not.

## Deference to agency factfinding sealed the result

The court’s conclusion was also shaped by the substantial-evidence standard. Under that deferential review, the court could not reverse simply because it might have weighed the evidence differently. It could reverse only if the record compelled the opposite conclusion. The panel quoted its usual reminder that it is not its job to reweigh evidence.

That mattered a great deal here. Because the record supported the agency’s view that the threats were mainly about Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s interests, the court had to uphold the BIA unless the evidence forced a contrary finding. It did not. So even though the petitioners had some evidence of political hostility, the agency’s causal finding survived review.

In the end, the court held that the political-opinion theory was too weak on nexus. The threats referenced politics, but the record supported the conclusion that the real and central reason for the persecution was Jimenez’s perceived role as a snitch and an obstacle to Don Rafa’s criminal activity.

# Chapter 5: Government Protection, Internal Relocation, and Final Disposition

## Why Government Inability or Unwillingness Matters When the Alleged Persecutors Are Private Actors

In the final part of the opinion, the Tenth Circuit addresses two doctrines that often decide asylum cases involving non-governmental persecutors: whether the home government is unable or unwilling to protect the applicants, and whether the applicants can safely and reasonably relocate within their country. These issues matter because asylum does not protect a person from every danger in the home country. Rather, the applicant must show persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution that the government cannot or will not stop. When the alleged harm comes from private actors, as it did here, the applicant must connect the danger to a failure of state protection. Without that showing, the harm is treated as private criminal conduct rather than persecution within the meaning of the INA.

The court had already concluded that the petitioners failed to prove past persecution and failed to show that their political opinion was a central reason for the threats. That made the government-protection question especially important, because their future-persecution theory depended in part on the idea that Colombian authorities could not protect them from Don Rafa and his associates. The panel rejected that theory on the record before it.

## The Role of Notice to the Government and the Importance of Reporting Persecution

The court explains that the unable-or-unwilling inquiry focuses heavily on whether the applicant gave the government notice and an opportunity to respond. This is a practical point: if the authorities were never told what happened, a reviewing court usually cannot infer from silence that the government would have failed to act. Reporting is therefore evidence that helps test the applicant’s claim. If the police or other officials were notified and did nothing, that can support a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to protect. If no report was made, the omission can weaken the claim because there is no concrete record of how the state would have responded.

Here, the court emphasized that Petitioners’ efforts to seek protection were “minimal, if any.” Jimenez and Duarte spoke with two individual police officers, but they did not make a fuller report, pursue help through higher-level channels, or use Jimenez’s own law-enforcement or prosecutorial connections. The court treated that limited outreach as insufficient to establish that the Colombian government as a whole would fail to protect them.

## When Failure to Report Is Not Fatal: Futility or Danger as Possible Excuses

The opinion also recognizes that an applicant’s failure to report is not automatically fatal. Sometimes reporting would be futile or would expose the applicant to more danger. In such cases, the applicant may still prove that the government is unable or unwilling to help by showing why contacting authorities would have been pointless or risky.

Petitioners tried to invoke that idea here. They suggested that going to the police would have been futile or dangerous because Don Rafa was allegedly aligned with the government. The court, however, found that argument unsupported by the record. It was not enough to speculate that authorities would have been compromised. The petitioners had to point to evidence that reporting would have been ineffective or dangerous in their particular circumstances.

## The Petitioners’ Limited Efforts to Seek Protection

The court characterized the petitioners’ efforts as thin. They did not meaningfully pursue state protection after the threats escalated. Jimenez and his father both had government or criminal-justice connections in Colombia, but the record did not show that either of them used those connections to make a fuller complaint or to seek assistance beyond informal conversations. That mattered to the court because the claim was not that the authorities were wholly inaccessible; rather, the petitioners had access to channels through which they might have sought help, but they did not use them.

The panel also noted that Petitioners spoke only with two individual police officers who told them not to seek trouble. The court treated those comments as too limited to prove that the Colombian government as a whole was unwilling or unable to help. In other words, a couple of discouraging remarks from street-level officers do not necessarily establish a national pattern of nonprotection.

## Why Two Police Officers Were Not Enough

A central feature of the court’s reasoning is its distinction between individual officers and the government as an institution. The petitioners relied on what two officers said, but the panel viewed that evidence as weak proof of systemic governmental failure. A single unhelpful encounter may show that one officer was apathetic or fearful, but it does not necessarily prove that the state itself would not protect the applicant if properly notified.

This distinction is important for students reading asylum cases. The question is not whether one officer was discouraging; it is whether the applicant has shown that the government, taken as a whole, is unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. The court concluded that Petitioners had not made that showing.

## The Significance of Jimenez’s Government and Law-Enforcement Connections

The court also found it notable that Jimenez and his father had ties to government institutions in Colombia. That fact undercut the petitioners’ claim that they had no realistic way to seek help. The opinion does not say those connections guaranteed protection, but it does suggest that they weaken the claim that the petitioners were trapped in a system where reporting was impossible or obviously dangerous.

The panel’s point was evidentiary rather than categorical. If someone has access to law-enforcement or criminal-justice contacts but does not use them, that omission makes it harder to argue that the state would have been unable or unwilling to respond. The court relied on that absence of a fuller attempt in concluding that the government-protection element had not been established.

## Why the Record Cut Against Futility

The petitioners argued that contacting the government would have been futile because Don Rafa and his associates were allegedly linked to the government. But the court found that the record pointed the other way. Authorities were already aware of the property that lay at the center of Jimenez’s dispute, and they had attempted several searches and seizures in response to criminal activity there. That evidence suggested that the Colombian government was not indifferent to the problem and was at least trying to address it.

That matters because an applicant who claims futility must offer some support for the claim that the authorities would not act. Here, the government had already tried to do something about the property, which undercut the notion that reporting the threats would have been pointless. The panel therefore rejected the idea that Petitioners could satisfy the government-protection element by relying on conjecture about political connections.

## The Court’s Conclusion on Government Protection

Taking all of this together, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s and IJ’s finding that the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners. The petitioners had not meaningfully reported the threats, had not shown that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, and had not shown that the Colombian state was incapable of responding. Because they could not establish that element, they also could not establish a well-founded fear of future persecution on the record presented.

## Internal Relocation and Its Place in Rebutting Fear of Future Persecution

The opinion then turns briefly to internal relocation. In asylum law, even if an applicant shows a fear of future harm, the government can defeat asylum by showing that the applicant could avoid persecution by moving to another part of the home country, so long as that relocation would be reasonable. The doctrine reflects a basic idea: asylum is not available if protection can be found safely inside the country of nationality.

The court treated relocation as an additional reason Petitioners could not prevail. Even assuming some future threat remained, the record supported the conclusion that they could avoid it by living elsewhere in Colombia.

## Facts Supporting Safe Relocation

The court pointed to several facts supporting safe relocation. First, Petitioners’ family and relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed. Second, Jimenez’s father still worked in the criminal justice system. Third, before coming to the United States, the family lived for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside without incident. Those facts suggested that the danger was not nationwide and that the family had at least one safe alternative location within Colombia.

The court also paid attention to what the persecutors actually said. The men who threatened Petitioners told them to leave the neighborhood, not the country. That detail mattered because it suggested the threat was localized rather than a countrywide campaign to hunt them down. The panel inferred from that language that moving elsewhere in Colombia was a realistic way to reduce or eliminate the risk.

## The Court’s Rejection of the Petitioners’ Relocation Objections

Petitioners argued that relocation would be impractical because of economic, administrative, and judicial burdens, and because their persecutors had ties to the president of Colombia. The court did not find those points persuasive, in part because the arguments were only lightly developed and in part because they depended on the same unproven claim that the persecutors were effectively protected by the state. The court also rejected the notion that it could grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships or speculative dangers not supported by the record.

In the court’s view, the evidence showed that Petitioners had already safely stayed elsewhere in Colombia and could relocate again if needed. That was enough to defeat their claim of an unavoidable future threat.

## Disposition: Denial of Asylum, Automatic Failure of Withholding, and Denial of the Petition for Review

The opinion closes by tying the doctrines together. Because Petitioners failed to establish eligibility for asylum, their withholding-of-removal claim necessarily failed as well. That is because withholding requires a higher probability of harm than asylum does. If an applicant cannot satisfy the lower asylum standard, the withholding claim cannot succeed on the same record.

Accordingly, the Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review. The court’s disposition reflects a common pattern in asylum appeals: once the panel concludes that the record does not compel a finding of persecution, nexus, government inability or unwillingness, or lack of safe relocation, the agency’s denial stands under the substantial-evidence standard.
