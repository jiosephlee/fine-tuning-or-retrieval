Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## Internal Relocation as a Backstop: What *Jimenez v. Bondi* Reveals About Modern Asylum Adjudication

## Why this case matters

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is, on its face, a conventional asylum denial: the Tenth Circuit held that the Colombian petitioners had not shown past persecution, had not established the required nexus to political opinion, and had not shown that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. But the opinion’s most interesting move may be the shortest one. The court’s discussion of safe internal relocation functions as a kind of backstop rationale, reinforcing the denial even after the panel had already concluded the petitioners fell short on the merits.

That matters because internal relocation is often where asylum cases turn from narrative to geography. Once a court concludes the harm is localized, or that the record does not show countrywide risk, the asylum claim can fail even if the applicant has suffered real threats from dangerous actors. *Jimenez* shows how that logic now often operates in cases involving politically connected criminals or quasi-political violence: the presence of local danger does not necessarily translate into a legally cognizable inability to remain anywhere in the country.

## The court’s relocation analysis

The petitioners argued that they could not safely relocate within Colombia because their persecutors were tied to organized crime and politically connected to the government. The panel was not persuaded. It pointed to three facts in particular: the family’s relatives remained in Colombia unharmed, Jimenez’s father continued to work in the criminal justice system, and the family had already lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to leave the country. The court also emphasized that the threats were framed as demands to leave the neighborhood, not the country.

Those details did a lot of work. They allowed the court to treat the threats as geographically limited and to infer that the danger was not so pervasive that Colombia as a whole was unavailable as a place of refuge. The opinion is careful to say that the family’s stay in the countryside was not just symbolic; it was evidence that relocation was in fact possible. And by noting that the persecutors told Jimenez to leave the neighborhood, the court drew a line between targeted intimidation and a countrywide campaign of persecution.

## The doctrinal significance

This part of the opinion fits comfortably within existing asylum doctrine, but it also illustrates how relocation analysis has become more important in practice. The legal question is not whether an applicant has been threatened by violent people, but whether the applicant can avoid future persecution by moving elsewhere in the country and doing so reasonably. That inquiry can be especially consequential where the applicant cannot prove the government is unwilling or unable to protect him, or cannot establish a protected-ground nexus.

In *Jimenez*, relocation was not the primary holding, but it reinforced each of the court’s other conclusions. If the persecution was not severe enough to qualify as past persecution, and if the threats were driven mainly by Jimenez’s dispute with a criminal actor rather than his politics, then the availability of a safe internal alternative becomes easier to see. In that sense, relocation operates less as a standalone test than as a doctrinal safety valve. It allows courts to say: even accepting that the petitioners faced genuine danger, asylum still fails if the danger appears local and manageable within the country.

## A cautious critique

There is some force to that approach, but it also has a limitation that *Jimenez* quietly exposes. The fact that relatives remain in the home country, or that an applicant managed to stay elsewhere for a few weeks, does not always answer the real question: whether internal relocation is genuinely safe and reasonable in light of the persecutor’s reach. Criminal groups, especially those described as politically connected, do not always operate in neat territorial boxes. A threat to “leave the neighborhood” can still reflect a broader ability to track, harass, or punish an applicant elsewhere.

The panel did not ignore that possibility, but it found the record too thin to support it. That is consistent with substantial-evidence review, and the court is right to resist converting speculation about national reach into a substitute for proof. Still, the opinion underscores a recurring tension in asylum law: relocation analysis can become a way to deny protection even when the applicant’s fear is understandable, so long as the record does not clearly establish that the danger extends beyond one locality.

## Downstream effects

The likely downstream effect is to make internal relocation even more central in cases involving mixed political-criminal motives. Applicants who claim threats from armed or organized actors tied loosely to politics will need to build a stronger record on why relocation is not realistic, not merely undesirable. That means evidence about the persecutor’s reach, the feasibility of life in another region, prior attempts to relocate, and why reporting or moving would not reduce the risk.

*Jimenez* also suggests that family safety in the home country will remain a powerful factual marker for the government and the court. So will evidence that threats were localized or phrased as demands to leave a particular area. For asylum applicants, that means the relocation inquiry cannot be an afterthought. It may end up doing exactly what it did here: serving as the last, and often decisive, reason the petition fails.
