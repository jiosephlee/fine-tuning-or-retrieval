Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: Government Protection, Internal Relocation, and Final Disposition

## Why Government Inability or Unwillingness Matters When the Alleged Persecutors Are Private Actors

In the final part of the opinion, the Tenth Circuit addresses two doctrines that often decide asylum cases involving non-governmental persecutors: whether the home government is unable or unwilling to protect the applicants, and whether the applicants can safely and reasonably relocate within their country. These issues matter because asylum does not protect a person from every danger in the home country. Rather, the applicant must show persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution that the government cannot or will not stop. When the alleged harm comes from private actors, as it did here, the applicant must connect the danger to a failure of state protection. Without that showing, the harm is treated as private criminal conduct rather than persecution within the meaning of the INA.

The court had already concluded that the petitioners failed to prove past persecution and failed to show that their political opinion was a central reason for the threats. That made the government-protection question especially important, because their future-persecution theory depended in part on the idea that Colombian authorities could not protect them from Don Rafa and his associates. The panel rejected that theory on the record before it.

## The Role of Notice to the Government and the Importance of Reporting Persecution

The court explains that the unable-or-unwilling inquiry focuses heavily on whether the applicant gave the government notice and an opportunity to respond. This is a practical point: if the authorities were never told what happened, a reviewing court usually cannot infer from silence that the government would have failed to act. Reporting is therefore evidence that helps test the applicant’s claim. If the police or other officials were notified and did nothing, that can support a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to protect. If no report was made, the omission can weaken the claim because there is no concrete record of how the state would have responded.

Here, the court emphasized that Petitioners’ efforts to seek protection were “minimal, if any.” Jimenez and Duarte spoke with two individual police officers, but they did not make a fuller report, pursue help through higher-level channels, or use Jimenez’s own law-enforcement or prosecutorial connections. The court treated that limited outreach as insufficient to establish that the Colombian government as a whole would fail to protect them.

## When Failure to Report Is Not Fatal: Futility or Danger as Possible Excuses

The opinion also recognizes that an applicant’s failure to report is not automatically fatal. Sometimes reporting would be futile or would expose the applicant to more danger. In such cases, the applicant may still prove that the government is unable or unwilling to help by showing why contacting authorities would have been pointless or risky.

Petitioners tried to invoke that idea here. They suggested that going to the police would have been futile or dangerous because Don Rafa was allegedly aligned with the government. The court, however, found that argument unsupported by the record. It was not enough to speculate that authorities would have been compromised. The petitioners had to point to evidence that reporting would have been ineffective or dangerous in their particular circumstances.

## The Petitioners’ Limited Efforts to Seek Protection

The court characterized the petitioners’ efforts as thin. They did not meaningfully pursue state protection after the threats escalated. Jimenez and his father both had government or criminal-justice connections in Colombia, but the record did not show that either of them used those connections to make a fuller complaint or to seek assistance beyond informal conversations. That mattered to the court because the claim was not that the authorities were wholly inaccessible; rather, the petitioners had access to channels through which they might have sought help, but they did not use them.

The panel also noted that Petitioners spoke only with two individual police officers who told them not to seek trouble. The court treated those comments as too limited to prove that the Colombian government as a whole was unwilling or unable to help. In other words, a couple of discouraging remarks from street-level officers do not necessarily establish a national pattern of nonprotection.

## Why Two Police Officers Were Not Enough

A central feature of the court’s reasoning is its distinction between individual officers and the government as an institution. The petitioners relied on what two officers said, but the panel viewed that evidence as weak proof of systemic governmental failure. A single unhelpful encounter may show that one officer was apathetic or fearful, but it does not necessarily prove that the state itself would not protect the applicant if properly notified.

This distinction is important for students reading asylum cases. The question is not whether one officer was discouraging; it is whether the applicant has shown that the government, taken as a whole, is unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. The court concluded that Petitioners had not made that showing.

## The Significance of Jimenez’s Government and Law-Enforcement Connections

The court also found it notable that Jimenez and his father had ties to government institutions in Colombia. That fact undercut the petitioners’ claim that they had no realistic way to seek help. The opinion does not say those connections guaranteed protection, but it does suggest that they weaken the claim that the petitioners were trapped in a system where reporting was impossible or obviously dangerous.

The panel’s point was evidentiary rather than categorical. If someone has access to law-enforcement or criminal-justice contacts but does not use them, that omission makes it harder to argue that the state would have been unable or unwilling to respond. The court relied on that absence of a fuller attempt in concluding that the government-protection element had not been established.

## Why the Record Cut Against Futility

The petitioners argued that contacting the government would have been futile because Don Rafa and his associates were allegedly linked to the government. But the court found that the record pointed the other way. Authorities were already aware of the property that lay at the center of Jimenez’s dispute, and they had attempted several searches and seizures in response to criminal activity there. That evidence suggested that the Colombian government was not indifferent to the problem and was at least trying to address it.

That matters because an applicant who claims futility must offer some support for the claim that the authorities would not act. Here, the government had already tried to do something about the property, which undercut the notion that reporting the threats would have been pointless. The panel therefore rejected the idea that Petitioners could satisfy the government-protection element by relying on conjecture about political connections.

## The Court’s Conclusion on Government Protection

Taking all of this together, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s and IJ’s finding that the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners. The petitioners had not meaningfully reported the threats, had not shown that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, and had not shown that the Colombian state was incapable of responding. Because they could not establish that element, they also could not establish a well-founded fear of future persecution on the record presented.

## Internal Relocation and Its Place in Rebutting Fear of Future Persecution

The opinion then turns briefly to internal relocation. In asylum law, even if an applicant shows a fear of future harm, the government can defeat asylum by showing that the applicant could avoid persecution by moving to another part of the home country, so long as that relocation would be reasonable. The doctrine reflects a basic idea: asylum is not available if protection can be found safely inside the country of nationality.

The court treated relocation as an additional reason Petitioners could not prevail. Even assuming some future threat remained, the record supported the conclusion that they could avoid it by living elsewhere in Colombia.

## Facts Supporting Safe Relocation

The court pointed to several facts supporting safe relocation. First, Petitioners’ family and relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed. Second, Jimenez’s father still worked in the criminal justice system. Third, before coming to the United States, the family lived for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside without incident. Those facts suggested that the danger was not nationwide and that the family had at least one safe alternative location within Colombia.

The court also paid attention to what the persecutors actually said. The men who threatened Petitioners told them to leave the neighborhood, not the country. That detail mattered because it suggested the threat was localized rather than a countrywide campaign to hunt them down. The panel inferred from that language that moving elsewhere in Colombia was a realistic way to reduce or eliminate the risk.

## The Court’s Rejection of the Petitioners’ Relocation Objections

Petitioners argued that relocation would be impractical because of economic, administrative, and judicial burdens, and because their persecutors had ties to the president of Colombia. The court did not find those points persuasive, in part because the arguments were only lightly developed and in part because they depended on the same unproven claim that the persecutors were effectively protected by the state. The court also rejected the notion that it could grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships or speculative dangers not supported by the record.

In the court’s view, the evidence showed that Petitioners had already safely stayed elsewhere in Colombia and could relocate again if needed. That was enough to defeat their claim of an unavoidable future threat.

## Disposition: Denial of Asylum, Automatic Failure of Withholding, and Denial of the Petition for Review

The opinion closes by tying the doctrines together. Because Petitioners failed to establish eligibility for asylum, their withholding-of-removal claim necessarily failed as well. That is because withholding requires a higher probability of harm than asylum does. If an applicant cannot satisfy the lower asylum standard, the withholding claim cannot succeed on the same record.

Accordingly, the Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review. The court’s disposition reflects a common pattern in asylum appeals: once the panel concludes that the record does not compel a finding of persecution, nexus, government inability or unwillingness, or lack of safe relocation, the agency’s denial stands under the substantial-evidence standard.
