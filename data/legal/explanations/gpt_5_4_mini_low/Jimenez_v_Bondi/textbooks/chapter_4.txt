Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Nexus to Political Opinion and the Court’s Mixed-Motive Analysis

## The petitioners narrowed their nexus theory on appeal

On appeal, the petitioners did not press every theory they may have mentioned below. Although the opinion notes that they had also invoked a particular social group theory, they did not brief any meaningful argument about that group or their membership in it. The Tenth Circuit therefore treated that theory as abandoned for purposes of its review and focused on the only preserved causation theory: that the harm they suffered was “on account of” their political opinion. For law students, this is an important appellate move. Courts generally limit themselves to arguments actually developed in the briefs, and here that narrowing mattered because it left the petitioners with one route to asylum nexus rather than two.

## The asylum statute requires a protected ground to be at least one central reason

The court framed nexus under the asylum statute’s “one central reason” requirement. It is not enough that an applicant’s political opinion played some role in the harm. The applicant must show that the protected ground was at least one central reason for the persecution. In practical terms, this means the political motive has to do real work in explaining why the persecutor acted.

The opinion emphasizes that a protected ground cannot be merely incidental, tangential, superficial, subordinate, or minor. That language matters because it shows the court was not asking whether politics was mentioned somewhere in the story. It was asking whether politics was a primary explanatory cause of the threats and intimidation. Under this standard, a protected ground can coexist with other motives, but it cannot be pushed to the sidelines.

## Mixed motives do not defeat nexus, but they do not make it easy to prove

The court acknowledged the basic mixed-motive principle: a persecutor can have more than one reason for targeting a person. That means the existence of a personal, economic, or criminal motive does not automatically defeat asylum. But mixed motives help the applicant only if a protected ground remains central among them. The court’s analysis therefore turned on relative importance. It asked whether political opinion was a central reason, or whether it was merely a label attached to conduct that was really driven by other concerns.

That distinction is the key to the court’s reasoning. The petitioners tried to show that the threats were politically charged because they had supported a rival candidate and were active in the Liberal Party. The court did not deny that politics was part of the background. But background politics is not the same thing as causation under the statute. The question was whether the persecutors targeted them because of their political views, or because of something else.

## The evidence supporting a political-opinion theory

The petitioners had some evidence that could support a political-opinion narrative. They were members of the Colombian Liberal Party, they campaigned for Federico Gutiérrez, and they went door-to-door seeking votes. Jimenez also received early threatening calls related to his party involvement. One threatening brochure said “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” which on its face connects the threat to party affiliation. There was also evidence that another Liberal Party colleague had been threatened and later kidnapped and murdered.

Taken together, this evidence could suggest that political activity created danger. The problem for the petitioners was not that the record contained no political references at all. The problem was that the court saw those political references as part of an earlier and different phase of events, not as the main driver of the specific threats that gave rise to their asylum claim.

## The evidence cutting against nexus was stronger

The court found that the later and more serious threats began only after Jimenez became involved in the property dispute involving Don Rafa’s operation. That timing mattered. The escalation coincided with Jimenez’s work as a provisional lawyer and his investigation of the property used for criminal activity, not merely with his campaign activity.

The opinion repeatedly highlights how the persecutors described Jimenez. They called him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and said he was responsible for searches and seizures at the property. Those accusations are highly significant because they point to retaliation for his perceived interference with law enforcement and with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise. The men who returned to the factory also demanded to know where Petitioners were and said the order came from Don Rafa. This made the threats look less like punishment for political dissent and more like retaliation against someone who had become a problem for a criminal boss.

## The court distinguished campaign-related threats from later targeted threats

The petitioners wanted the court to connect all of the threats together as politically motivated persecution. The court refused to flatten the chronology. It treated the early campaign-related threats as evidence that politics had caused some danger in the past, but it found that the later threats were different in character and source.

That distinction is central to the opinion. The court noted that Petitioners continued campaigning even after the early threats and the killing of a fellow campaigner. It also noted that there was no evidence that Don Rafa was behind those initial threats. By contrast, the more serious incidents followed Jimenez’s contact with Don Rafa’s property, his investigation of criminal activity there, and the resulting suspicion that he had triggered police attention. In the court’s view, the escalation was tied to this property dispute, not to the petitioners’ party activity.

## Why the court thought the real motive was protection of criminal interests

The court concluded that the “predominant motive” was Jimenez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa, especially his role in the property dispute and the resulting law-enforcement attention. That is, the threats were aimed at stopping a perceived informant and protecting a criminal operation. The fact that the attackers also mentioned politics did not change the core motive the court identified.

This is the court’s most important substantive move on nexus. It did not say political opinion was absent. It said political opinion was not the reason that mattered most. The persecutors appeared to be reacting to Jimenez as a lawyer, investigator, and perceived obstacle to their activity. Under the statute, that is not enough unless political opinion is also a central reason. The court found that it was not.

## Deference to agency factfinding sealed the result

The court’s conclusion was also shaped by the substantial-evidence standard. Under that deferential review, the court could not reverse simply because it might have weighed the evidence differently. It could reverse only if the record compelled the opposite conclusion. The panel quoted its usual reminder that it is not its job to reweigh evidence.

That mattered a great deal here. Because the record supported the agency’s view that the threats were mainly about Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s interests, the court had to uphold the BIA unless the evidence forced a contrary finding. It did not. So even though the petitioners had some evidence of political hostility, the agency’s causal finding survived review.

In the end, the court held that the political-opinion theory was too weak on nexus. The threats referenced politics, but the record supported the conclusion that the real and central reason for the persecution was Jimenez’s perceived role as a snitch and an obstacle to Don Rafa’s criminal activity.
