Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Why the Court Found No Past Persecution

## The INA’s undefined term and the Tenth Circuit’s working standard

In the asylum context, one of the first questions is whether the applicant suffered “persecution.” The Immigration and Nationality Act does not define that term, so courts supply the content through case law. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit relied on its own precedent to explain that persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm” in a way that is more serious than ordinary difficulty, harassment, or isolated threats. The court’s formulation matters because it sets a threshold: an asylum applicant must show not just that events were frightening or unpleasant, but that they crossed the legal line into persecution.

The court also reminds readers that persecution is a fact-intensive concept. It is not enough to identify a bad event in the abstract. The question is whether, on the record as a whole, the treatment rises to the level the INA requires. That framing is important because the court’s review is deferential. It is not deciding the issue from scratch; it is asking whether the record compels a contrary conclusion to the agency’s finding.

## More than restrictions or threats

The Tenth Circuit repeated a familiar rule from its asylum cases: persecution must involve harm that is more than “mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” That language shows that not every serious-seeming threat qualifies. The court treated this case as involving threats, intimidation, and psychological distress, but no physical violence against Petitioners themselves.

That distinction drove the analysis. Petitioners described a series of menacing incidents: threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, armed confrontations at the factory, and the frightening schoolyard encounter involving one of the children. The court did not dismiss these events as trivial. Instead, it accepted that they were alarming and dangerous. But under Tenth Circuit precedent, fear alone is not automatically persecution. The court asked whether the combined effect of these incidents amounted to the type of harm the asylum statute recognizes.

## The cumulative-harm approach

The court did not analyze each incident in isolation. It expressly used the cumulative-harm approach, which asks whether the aggregate of all mistreatment amounts to persecution even if no single event would do so by itself. That matters in asylum cases because a series of lesser acts can, taken together, become legally significant.

Here, however, the cumulative picture still did not persuade the court. The threats, the brochure, the factory confrontations, and the incident involving the child were serious, but the court concluded they did not add up to the kind of sustained harm that compels a finding of persecution. This is where the appellate standard becomes crucial. The court did not ask whether it might have viewed the facts differently in the first instance. It asked whether any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to find persecution. The answer was no.

## Threats and the court’s insistence that threats alone are usually not enough

A central point in the court’s reasoning was its statement that threats alone are generally insufficient to constitute persecution. That rule is important because many asylum claims involve intimidation without physical injury. The court acknowledged that threats are relevant and must be considered, but it refused to treat them as automatically satisfying the persecution element.

The court used Petitioners’ case to illustrate why. Even though they received repeated threats and one of the threats was accompanied by a gunpoint confrontation, Petitioners conceded that they were not physically harmed. The court treated that concession as significant. It did not mean physical injury is required in every case, but it did reinforce that the record lacked one of the strongest markers of persecution. Without lasting physical harm, the court looked for other unusually severe facts that might still establish persecution. It found none.

## The significance of the no-lasting-physical-harm concession

Petitioners conceded at the merits hearing that they were not physically harmed and acknowledged in their briefing that they suffered no lasting physical harm. The court was careful to note that physical harm is not a necessary prerequisite to asylum. But the concession still mattered because it narrowed the case to threats and emotional injury.

That narrowing made Petitioners’ burden harder. Once the physical-harm component dropped out, the question became whether the remaining intimidation and distress were severe enough to qualify on their own. The court’s answer was that, on this record, they were not. The opinion therefore shows that while physical injury is not required, its absence can make it much more difficult to persuade a reviewing court that the agency was compelled to find persecution.

## Emotional and psychological harm

Petitioners tried to shift the focus to the emotional and psychological effects of what happened to them. Duarte said she could not sleep or rest, and their son developed panic attacks and stress. The court did not doubt that these reactions were real or that the family had experienced distress. But it treated those effects as consequences of the threats rather than as independent harm severe enough to amount to persecution.

That distinction is central to the court’s analysis. Psychological suffering can count in asylum law, but the Tenth Circuit made clear that distress caused by frightening threats is usually not enough by itself. The court described Petitioners’ experience as unfortunate and understandably upsetting, but still outside the legal definition of persecution. In other words, the fact that threats generate fear, sleep loss, or anxiety does not automatically convert the threats into persecution.

## Why the court thought the record fell short

The opinion also situates this case within the Tenth Circuit’s prior decisions. The court pointed to other cases involving more severe or more numerous threats, yet still found no persecution. Those comparisons were meant to show how demanding the circuit’s standard is. If applications involving telephone threats, vague in-person threats, detention, armed robbery, or threats tied to compliance demands did not compel a finding of persecution, then Petitioners’ record—though serious—did not either.

This use of precedent is doing real work. The court is not saying Petitioners’ case is unimportant. It is saying that, measured against the circuit’s existing asylum cases, the events here do not cross the line. That is why the opinion repeatedly emphasizes substantial evidence. The agency’s finding survives unless the record would force any reasonable adjudicator to conclude the opposite. The court concluded that this record did not force that result.

## The substantial-evidence standard and the court’s limited role

The substantial-evidence standard shaped the outcome of the persecution issue. Under that standard, the court did not reweigh the evidence or decide whether persecution might be a plausible conclusion. It asked a narrower question: did the evidence compel a finding of past persecution? That is a demanding standard for petitioners. A claimant can present a sympathetic story and still lose if the evidence leaves room for the agency’s conclusion.

That is exactly what happened here. The court accepted that the family suffered multiple frightening incidents and emotional stress. But because the law requires more than threats and distress, and because the record did not contain physical harm or other exceptionally severe mistreatment, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s conclusion.

## The holding on past persecution

The court’s bottom line on this element was straightforward: the family’s experiences, considered together, did not amount to past persecution under the INA. The threats, psychological distress, and other hardships were real, but they did not compel a contrary finding. As a result, the court upheld the BIA on the persecution element and moved on to the separate questions of nexus and government protection.
