Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Facts, Parties, and the Path to Federal Court

## The petitioners: a Colombian family from Bogota involved in a furniture business

The petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* are a family from Bogota, Colombia: Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. Before coming to the United States, the family owned and worked in a furniture factory in Bogota. That detail matters because the factory became one of the main places where the alleged threats and confrontations occurred. The case is not just about isolated political activity or a single private dispute; the court viewed it as a record made up of several connected events involving the family’s work, home life, and public activities.

## Their political activity: membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and support for Federico Gutierrez in the 2022 presidential election

The family had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012, and in the 2022 Colombian presidential election they supported Federico Gutierrez. They went door to door in local neighborhoods to seek favorable votes. This political activity is the factual basis for their claim that they were targeted because of political opinion. In asylum cases, the applicant must show not only harm, but harm linked to a protected ground such as political opinion. Here, the family’s campaign work was the first strand of facts the court had to consider.

## Initial politically tinged threats received during campaign activity

Around the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice a week about his involvement in the Liberal Party. Other party leaders and colleagues also received threats. In early June, a colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite reporting threats. The family continued campaigning anyway. These early threats are important because they are the strongest facts tying the case to politics. But the court later distinguishes between these threats and later events tied to the property dispute, which became central to the nexus analysis.

## Jimenez’s legal and prosecutorial background: law student, provisional lawyer, and intern in the National Prosecutor’s office

Jimenez was not only a political supporter; he also had legal experience. In 2022 he was a law student, held a provisional law license, and worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia. In that role, he helped victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. The opinion uses this background to explain why others may have viewed him as connected to law enforcement or the justice system. That connection later becomes important when the court evaluates whether the threats were motivated by politics or by Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute and perceived cooperation with authorities.

## The San Bernardo property dispute and Jimenez’s involvement on behalf of a client

In June 2022, a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez for help with a property dispute. She said she had not received rent from a tenant for over a year and wanted Jimenez to speak with him about restitution and leaving the property. Jimenez visited the property to negotiate, but the tenant told him not to “look for trouble” and refused to negotiate. The client chose not to proceed with a formal eviction and instead hoped the tenant would surrender the property after a lawyer intervened.

This episode matters because it introduced a separate source of conflict, one that the court later treated as potentially more important than the political threats. The opinion distinguishes the campaign-related threats from the property dispute because the two streams of events may have had different motives.

## Discovery that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros ('Don Rafa') and the property’s connection to trafficking, prostitution, kidnapping, and murder

After reviewing the title to the property, Jimenez learned that the tenant was actually the client’s ex-husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa.” The opinion describes him as a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Gustavo Petro. Jimenez also discovered that the property had been used as a day-rental for migrants and as a base for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The property was associated with kidnapping and murder, and the health department had sealed off the third floor. Law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures, but apparently without success.

This portion of the facts is central to the court’s later reasoning about motive and government protection. If the threats came from Don Rafa because Jimenez interfered with criminal activity or with a property operation, then the threats might not be “on account of” political opinion. And if the government had already attempted searches and seizures, that undercut the claim that the Colombian government was wholly unwilling or unable to respond.

## Escalation of events: threatening brochure, gunpoint confrontation at the factory, threats to employees, and the school incident involving the younger son

The conflict escalated after the property visit. Jimenez received a threatening brochure at the furniture factory that said, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He threw it away. Then, the next month, two men confronted him at night at the factory, held him at gunpoint, and told him to leave the neighborhood. One man accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures at the property.

About two months later, the same men returned to the factory while the family was away. They assaulted an employee, demanded to know where the petitioners were, and again called them “snitches for the police” who were in politics. They said Don Rafa had sent them and threatened to kill the petitioners if they were seen again.

Days later, Jimenez saw a man put his arm around his younger son as the boy left school, cover the boy’s mouth, and tell him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez ran over and pushed the man while the child escaped. These are the most alarming facts in the case, and they are the ones the family relied on to argue that they suffered persecution and had a well-founded fear of future harm.

## Temporary move to Jimenez’s mother’s home and the family’s decision to leave Colombia

After the school incident, the family moved to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. During that time, they decided to leave for the United States. The temporary move matters because it became part of the court’s safe-relocation analysis. If they could live safely, even for a short time, in another part of Colombia, that tended to support the conclusion that they could avoid future harm by relocating within the country.

## Contacts with police officers before departure and the officers’ alleged responses

Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez told a police patrol officer what had happened. According to Jimenez, the officer said nothing could be done, claimed police officers were contributing to the problem, and told the family they should leave. Earlier, a local police officer had also told Jimenez to stay away from the property and not seek trouble.

These conversations are important because asylum applicants claiming persecution by private actors generally must show that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them. Reporting to police, or explaining why reporting would have been futile or dangerous, is often relevant to that issue. Here, the petitioners relied on these officer conversations as evidence of government inability or unwillingness, but the court later found that the record showed only limited contact with police and did not establish a broader failure of state protection.

## Removal proceedings in the United States: Notices to Appear, conceded removability, and applications for asylum, withholding, and CAT protection

Three months after arriving in the United States without being admitted or paroled, the Department of Homeland Security served the family with individual Notices to Appear. The notices charged them as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act. In August 2023, they admitted the factual allegations. The immigration judge then charged them as inadmissible and ordered them removed to Colombia.

The family applied for asylum and withholding of removal, and the minor children proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application. They also sought protection under the Convention Against Torture. This posture is typical in immigration litigation: the government initiates removal, the noncitizens concede or contest removability, and then seek relief from removal based on fear of harm if returned.

## The IJ’s ruling, the BIA’s affirmance, and the narrowed issues presented to the Tenth Circuit

The immigration judge denied all forms of relief. Although the IJ found the petitioners credible, the IJ held that they failed to show past persecution, failed to show a nexus between the harm and a protected ground, failed to show that internal relocation was unreasonable, and failed to show that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The IJ also denied CAT protection.

The petitioners appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals, which affirmed and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected their due process arguments about the right to counsel. By the time the case reached the Tenth Circuit, however, the petitioners had narrowed their challenge. They asked only for review of the asylum and withholding rulings, not the CAT denial or the due process issue.

That narrowing is important for reading the rest of the opinion. The court’s analysis focuses on three core asylum questions: whether the petitioners suffered persecution, whether the persecution was on account of political opinion, and whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The court also briefly addresses internal relocation, because even if a fear of harm exists, asylum may still be denied if the applicant can safely relocate within the home country.
