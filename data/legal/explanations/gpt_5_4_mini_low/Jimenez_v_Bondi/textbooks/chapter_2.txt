Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Standard of Review and the Asylum/Withholding Framework

## Jurisdiction and the Scope of Review

The Tenth Circuit begins by identifying its authority to hear the case. Here, the court exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which authorizes judicial review of final orders of removal. That matters because the petitioners are not asking the court to start the immigration case over; they are asking the court to review whether the agency legally and factually erred in denying relief.

The court also makes clear that its review is limited to the administrative record—the evidence that was before the agency when it decided the case. That means the court cannot consider new evidence or retry the facts. The petition for review is therefore an appellate review of the immigration record, not a fresh proceeding.

## Reviewing the BIA, While Looking to the IJ for Context

Because a single Board of Immigration Appeals member affirmed the immigration judge’s decision, the Tenth Circuit treats the BIA as the operative decisionmaker. That is the decision under review. At the same time, the court may consult the immigration judge’s reasoning when the BIA adopted or relied on the same grounds. In practical terms, the BIA’s decision is the focus, but the IJ’s opinion can help explain the factual and legal basis for the result.

This is an important feature of immigration appellate practice. The court does not simply substitute its own judgment for that of the agency. Instead, it asks whether the agency’s decision is supported by the record under the proper standard of review.

## Substantial-Evidence Review

The court repeatedly applies the substantial-evidence standard to the agency’s factual findings. Under that standard, the BIA’s findings are conclusive unless the record would compel any reasonable adjudicator to reach the opposite conclusion. That is a demanding standard for the petitioners. It is not enough to show that the evidence could support a different result; the record must require a different result.

This standard explains the court’s posture throughout the opinion. When the petitioners argue that threats, fear, and government inaction amount to persecution, the court is not asking whether it might have viewed the evidence differently in the first instance. It is asking whether the BIA’s contrary view was still reasonable in light of the record.

## Asylum as Discretionary Relief and the Burden of Proof

The opinion next sets out the basic asylum framework. Asylum is discretionary relief: even if an applicant meets the statutory definition of a refugee, the Attorney General still has discretion whether to grant asylum. But before discretion matters, the applicant must first prove eligibility.

The applicant bears the burden of showing by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a refugee. “Preponderance” means more likely than not. So the applicant must persuade the agency that the evidence makes refugee status more probable than not.

The INA defines a refugee as a person who is outside the country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return, or to avail himself or herself of that country’s protection, because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of one of five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. The protected-ground requirement is central. Harm alone is not enough; the harm must be connected to one of these statutory reasons.

## Past Persecution and the Presumption of Future Persecution

One common way to prove refugee status is to show past persecution. If an applicant proves past persecution, that generally creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. That presumption is important because it shifts some of the burden into the government’s court: the applicant no longer has to prove future fear from scratch unless the government rebuts the presumption.

But past persecution is not established merely by showing unpleasant or even frightening treatment. The applicant must satisfy three elements. First, there must be an incident or series of incidents rising to the level of persecution. Second, the persecution must be on account of a protected ground. Third, the persecution must be by the government or by private actors the government is unable or unwilling to control.

The court’s later analysis tracks these three requirements closely. It asks first whether the threats and related events were severe enough to count as persecution at all. It then asks whether the harm was inflicted because of political opinion or some other protected reason. Finally, it asks whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners from the people threatening them.

## Withholding of Removal and Its Relationship to Asylum

The petitioners also sought withholding of removal, but the court explains that their failure on asylum largely defeats withholding as well. That is because withholding generally imposes a higher standard than asylum. If an applicant cannot meet the lower asylum standard on the same set of facts and theory, the applicant usually cannot satisfy withholding either.

The opinion therefore treats asylum as the threshold claim. Once the petitioners fail to establish a well-founded fear of persecution for asylum purposes, they necessarily fail to meet the more demanding standard for withholding of removal on the same evidence.

## Claims Not Pursued on Appeal

Although the immigration judge also denied protection under the Convention Against Torture and rejected due process arguments related to the right to counsel, the petitioners did not pursue those issues in the Tenth Circuit. That means the court does not analyze them on the merits. For appellate purposes, abandoned issues are not before the court, so the opinion confines itself to asylum and withholding of removal.

Understanding what the petitioners did not appeal is as important as understanding what they did. It narrows the case to the legal questions the court actually decided: whether the record compels a finding of past persecution, whether the harm was connected to political opinion, and whether the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them.
