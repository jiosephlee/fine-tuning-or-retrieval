Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Threats Aren’t Enough: The Tenth Circuit’s High Bar for “Persecution” in *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why the case matters

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is a useful window into a recurring problem in asylum law: courts repeatedly say that physical injury is not required to prove persecution, yet applicants who present a course of escalating threats often still lose. The Tenth Circuit’s decision reinforces that pattern. Here, the petitioners described repeated death threats, an armed confrontation at their workplace, a later attack on an employee, and what looked like an attempted abduction of one of their children. But the court still held that substantial evidence supported the agency’s conclusion that they had not suffered past persecution, had not shown the requisite nexus to political opinion, and had not shown that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

The opinion is doctrinally conventional, but that is exactly why it matters. It shows how high the practical bar has become in threat-based asylum cases, especially under deferential review. The court’s reasoning also underscores a widening gap between the legal formula—persecution can include serious nonphysical harm—and what applicants must actually show to win in cases where the harm is primarily intimidation, threats, and psychological trauma.

## The court’s approach to cumulative harm

The petitioners’ strongest argument was that the agency looked at each incident too narrowly. They pointed to a series of escalating events: threatening calls tied to their Liberal Party work; a threatening flyer at Jimenez’s factory; men at gunpoint ordering him out of the neighborhood; a later visit in which the same men assaulted an employee and threatened to kill the family; and the disturbing schoolyard incident involving their son. The Tenth Circuit did what it often does in these cases: it said it considered the events cumulatively, but still found them insufficient.

That move is important. The court did not rest its holding on the notion that threats are never persecution. Instead, it reiterated the usual doctrinal caveat that threats alone are generally not enough, and then emphasized that the petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed and had no lasting physical injury. The court also treated the emotional and psychological consequences as understandable but insufficient. The result is a familiar one in Tenth Circuit asylum law: the opinion recites the aggregate-harm framework, but the actual application of that framework remains deeply skeptical of threat-based claims unless the facts are truly extreme.

In that sense, *Jimenez* fits comfortably within the circuit’s prior decisions, but it also exposes the practical reality that “cumulative” review does not necessarily help much where the cumulative events are frightening but largely non-injurious. The opinion suggests that the line is not simply between isolated threats and a broader pattern of intimidation; it is between threats plus some kind of concrete coercive impact, and threats that remain mostly in the realm of fear.

## The doctrinal gap between principle and outcome

One of the striking features of the opinion is how it acknowledges the right doctrinal proposition while producing the opposite outcome. The panel quotes the familiar rule that persecution is more than “restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and that physical harm is not necessary. But then it cites a line of cases showing that threats, even coupled with detention or other stressful encounters, have often not been enough.

That is where the tension lies. In theory, asylum law recognizes that persecution can be psychological, coercive, and cumulative. In practice, courts often seem to require either physical injury, prolonged detention, severe economic harm, or some comparably tangible deprivation before they are willing to call the conduct persecution. *Jimenez* does not announce a new rule, but it illustrates how the doctrine functions on the ground: emotional distress and repeated threats may be acknowledged, but they rarely carry the day unless they are accompanied by more concrete harm.

This may be especially true under substantial-evidence review. The standard asks not whether a court might have reached a different conclusion, but whether the record compels the opposite one. That deferential posture makes it much harder to convert a serious threat pattern into a reversible agency error. Even if a reasonable adjudicator could see persecution here, the court need only conclude that another reasonable adjudicator could see it differently. In threat-heavy cases, that often means the agency wins.

## Nexus and the narrowing of motive

The nexus discussion is also revealing. The petitioners argued that the threats were tied to their political activities—especially their support for the Liberal Party and their campaign work for a rival of the current president. But the court accepted the agency’s view that the more immediate reason for the threats was Jimenez’s entanglement with Don Rafa’s property and his perceived role in exposing criminal activity. Once the court framed the conflict that way, the political-opinion claim became secondary, “tangential and subordinate” to a private dispute.

This part of the opinion matters because it shows how easily political context can be displaced by a more individualized motive. Even if the family’s political work helped place them in the persecutors’ sights, the court treated the property dispute and the criminal investigation as the central driver of the harm. That is a familiar asylum move: once a persecutor’s motive can be characterized as retaliation, silencing a witness, or protecting a criminal enterprise, the political-opinion theory becomes much harder to sustain.

## Government protection and internal relocation

The court’s treatment of government protection is equally important, though less novel. The panel emphasized that the petitioners’ efforts to seek protection were minimal and largely confined to conversations with individual officers. It also noted that the record showed the Colombian government had attempted searches and seizures at the relevant property, which cut against the claim that the state was aligned with the persecutors or unable to respond.

That analysis underscores a recurring evidentiary problem in asylum cases: applicants often face danger precisely because they distrust the state, but courts still want some showing that the state was asked to help, or that asking would have been futile or dangerous. Here, the petitioners’ evidence fell short because they did not meaningfully test the system, and the record did not establish systemic governmental incapacity or complicity.

The relocation holding follows naturally. Because the court found no past persecution and no adequate showing of future persecution, it had little trouble concluding that the family could safely relocate within Colombia. The fact that they stayed with relatives in the countryside for three weeks before leaving, and that the threats were localized, gave the court enough support to uphold the agency’s view that internal relocation was reasonable.

## The broader takeaway

*Jimenez v. Bondi* does not rewrite asylum doctrine, but it does sharpen the divide between the doctrine’s language and its application. The court is right that asylum law does not require broken bones or hospital records. But the opinion also demonstrates that, in practice, repeated threats, a gunpoint confrontation, and even a frightening encounter involving a child may still be insufficient unless the record shows something more concrete: sustained physical abuse, severe coercion, or a clearer link to a protected ground.

That reality has downstream effects. It narrows the space for claimants whose persecution takes the form of intimidation rather than overt violence, which is often exactly how political and criminal actors operate. It also means that the outcome in many asylum cases will turn less on whether the applicant’s fear is genuine or the abuse is serious, and more on whether the facts fit the judiciary’s increasingly demanding template for what counts as persecution. In the Tenth Circuit, *Jimenez* suggests that template remains a steep one.



## Politics or Personal Retaliation? *Jimenez v. Bondi* and the Narrowing of Nexus in Mixed-Motive Asylum Claims

## Why the case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is less about whether the petitioners were threatened — the panel accepted that they were — and more about why they were threatened. That distinction is often the hardest part of an asylum case. When violence, corruption, and politics blur together, applicants frequently argue that persecution is political even if it also has a personal or criminal dimension. The court’s answer here was that the political dimension did not do enough work. In its view, the threats to the Colombian family were driven primarily by Jimenez’s intrusion into Don Rafa’s property and criminal enterprise, not by the family’s support for the Liberal Party or a rival presidential candidate.

That holding matters because nexus is the gatekeeper in many asylum cases. A petitioner can present a harrowing record of intimidation and still lose if the court concludes the harm was not “on account of” a protected ground. *Jimenez* is a good example of how the Tenth Circuit is applying the “one central reason” standard in a mixed-motive setting: political activity may be present in the background, but if the evidence points more strongly to retaliation for a dispute with a powerful local actor, asylum fails.

## How the panel separated politics from retaliation

The opinion carefully parses the chronology. Petitioners had earlier received threatening calls related to their work for the Colombian Liberal Party and support for Federico Gutiérrez. But the escalation came after Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute that exposed him to Don Rafa, a narco-trafficking figure who allegedly had a stake in the property and in the surrounding criminal activity. Once Jimenez investigated the property, he received a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” then later was confronted at gunpoint by men who called him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures at the property. The later factory visit and the schoolyard incident fit the same pattern: the threats were tied to Jimenez’s perceived role in law enforcement and interference with Don Rafa’s operations.

That matters because the court did not ignore the political evidence; it reclassified it. The earlier campaign threats and the family’s political work were real, but the panel held that the record did not compel the conclusion that those threats were the reason for the later, more serious incidents. Instead, the court saw the later violence as triggered by Jimenez’s professional and personal entanglement with Don Rafa. In short, the political label attached to some of the threats did not transform the whole dispute into political persecution.

That approach reflects a common but consequential asylum move: courts often privilege the most immediate and concrete motive when the record contains multiple plausible explanations. Here, the immediate motive was retaliation for being perceived as a “snitch,” not punishment for voting or campaigning.

## The “one central reason” test in practice

The panel’s nexus analysis is grounded in the statutory requirement that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the persecution. That standard does not require exclusive motive, and the court said so expressly. But it does require more than a minor, tangential, or subordinate role. On this record, the Tenth Circuit concluded that political opinion did not clear that bar.

What is notable is how little room the court gave to overlap. Petitioners argued that they were targeted because they supported an opposition candidate in a country where organized crime and politics are intertwined. The panel was not persuaded. It read the evidence as showing a shift in motive: political activity first, then a dispute with a criminal figure, and then threats arising from that dispute. The fact that the threats referenced the Liberal Party did not, in the court’s view, establish that politics remained central once Jimenez became entangled with the property and with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise.

That is a fairly strict application of the nexus test. It also illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s broader reluctance to infer political persecution from generalized threats or from the mere presence of political language in otherwise criminal threats. The opinion treats the words “snitch” and “prosecutor’s office” as powerful evidence of retaliation for perceived interference with criminal activity. Once that framing takes hold, the political references look more like context than cause.

## A practical lesson for mixed-motive asylum claims

The downstream effect of *Jimenez* is likely to be felt in cases where persecutors are part political actor, part criminal enterprise, and part local power broker. Those cases are common in countries where gangs, insurgent groups, and political machines overlap. Petitioners in those settings often describe threats that are at once ideological, personal, and coercive. *Jimenez* suggests that in the Tenth Circuit, applicants will need to do more than show that the persecutor had political affiliations or used political rhetoric. They will need evidence tying the harm itself to the applicant’s political opinion in a way that remains central even after the dispute becomes personal or operational.

That may be a difficult evidentiary burden. Persecutors rarely announce a clean motive. They often weaponize whatever is effective: politics, intimidation, accusations of collaboration, or simple extortion. But *Jimenez* shows that where the record points to a discrete dispute with a criminal figure, the court may treat political references as incidental unless the applicant can connect the dots more firmly.

## My take

The Tenth Circuit’s reasoning is defensible, but it also shows the limits of asylum law when political and criminal violence are intertwined. The panel was likely right that the record contained substantial evidence of retaliation for Jimenez’s involvement with the property, especially given the repeated references to “snitching” and law-enforcement interference. That is a classic non-protected motive. Still, the opinion also reveals how quickly a political narrative can be displaced once a persecutor has another reason to target the applicant.

The tension is that in unstable democracies, “politics” and “criminal retaliation” are not always separable. A lawyer investigating a property tied to a politically connected trafficker may be targeted precisely because he is seen as both a political opponent and a threat to a patronage network. The court’s opinion acknowledges that overlap but ultimately insists on motive hierarchy. If the dominant story is criminal retaliation, asylum does not follow.

That is a doctrinally orthodox result under the “one central reason” standard. But it also means that asylum law will continue to struggle with cases where persecutors operate through hybrid forms of power. For applicants like the Jimenez family, the hardest part may not be proving danger. It may be proving that the danger is the kind the refugee statute recognizes.



# Unable or Unwilling—and Unreported: *Jimenez v. Bondi*’s Demanding View of State Protection

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is not just another asylum loss on the facts. It is a useful window into how hard it can be for applicants to prove that their home government is “unable or unwilling” to protect them from private persecutors. The panel treated the absence of a formal police report as highly significant, and it was skeptical that vague warnings from a couple of officers could stand in for proof of systemic state failure. That approach matters because many asylum cases turn on exactly this problem: victims may be too afraid to report, or may reasonably assume that reporting would be pointless, yet the record often lacks the kind of documentary proof courts prefer.

In *Jimenez*, the Tenth Circuit held that Petitioners’ showing on government protection was insufficient because their attempts to seek help were “minimal, if any,” and because the record did not establish that contacting police would have been futile or dangerous. The court also pointed to evidence that Colombian authorities had attempted searches and seizures at the very property tied to the threats, which undercut the claim that the state was aligned with the persecutors or unwilling to intervene. The result is a fairly demanding version of the unable-or-unwilling test: generalized fear of official corruption will not do, and even some evidence of local police indifference may not be enough without a more concrete record of state incapacity.

## The court’s reasoning

The panel’s analysis follows a familiar structure, but with a particularly strong emphasis on notice. Citing its recent decision in *Singh v. Bondi*, the court reiterated that the government-protection inquiry asks what the state did, or would have done, if it had notice of the persecution. That makes reporting central. Without reporting, the court said, there is often no way to know whether the police would have responded or whether state protection was available at all.

Petitioners tried to overcome that problem by pointing to two police encounters in which officers told Jimenez not to “seek trouble” and, in one instance, said that police officers were contributing to the problem. But the court discounted those statements as too localized and too thin to establish the Colombian government’s position as a whole. One-off remarks from individual officers, the panel suggested, do not necessarily show that the state was unwilling or unable to protect the family. That is a notable move, because in many asylum cases the most probative evidence of official failure is precisely the applicant’s direct encounter with local police.

The court also leaned on the opposite kind of evidence: the government had already been trying to address criminal activity at Don Rafa’s property through searches and seizures. To the panel, that mattered a lot. If the state was actively targeting the underlying criminal enterprise, then the record cut against a theory that the persecutors were effectively protected by the government. In the court’s view, Petitioners’ evidence did not show a government so compromised that resort to the police would have been pointless or dangerous.

## A demanding evidentiary standard

What stands out in *Jimenez* is the court’s insistence on concrete proof of either reporting or futility. That is doctrinally defensible, but it is also demanding in practice. Many asylum seekers do not file police reports precisely because they believe, based on experience or credible threat, that doing so would expose them to more danger. Yet courts often want more than subjective fear. They want some objective basis for futility: prior reports by the applicant or similarly situated people, evidence of official collusion, proof that complaints routinely go nowhere, or some showing that the state apparatus itself is infected at a systemic level.

That is the direction the Tenth Circuit seems to be moving in. A localized warning from a hostile officer may help, but it will rarely carry the day alone. And evidence that the government has taken action against criminal activity in the abstract may be enough to defeat an unable-or-unwilling claim unless the applicant can tie that action to the particular threat at issue. In other words, *Jimenez* reflects a preference for institutional evidence over anecdotal evidence.

That preference has consequences. It may screen out weaker claims, but it also raises the bar for applicants facing corruption that is real but uneven. A family threatened by a politically connected criminal may have solid reasons not to report, yet still struggle to prove, after the fact, that reporting would have been futile. The court’s framework leaves little room for the messy reality of local intimidation, where state actors may be intermittently helpful, selectively absent, or quietly complicit.

## Broader implications

The downstream effect of *Jimenez* is likely to be felt most in cases involving cartel, gang, or politically connected violence in countries where the state is not wholly absent. The opinion reinforces a trend toward requiring applicants to show systemic failure, not just isolated police indifference or corruption in one neighborhood. That makes sense if the legal question is whether the government is “unable or unwilling” as a matter of state protection, not whether a particular officer was helpful or hostile. But it can also make relief harder to obtain where the state’s failure is fragmented rather than total.

There is also a subtle but important evidentiary lesson here. Petitioners in *Jimenez* had testimony about threats, official-sounding warnings, and a police officer’s discouraging comment. What they did not have was a formal report, documentation of an ignored complaint, or stronger evidence that broader resort to the police would have been dangerous. The panel treated that absence as dispositive enough. For future applicants, the case is a reminder that the unable-or-unwilling prong is often won or lost not on the drama of the persecution, but on the paper trail—or lack of one—showing whether the state was ever asked to help.

In that sense, *Jimenez* is less about a dramatic doctrinal shift than about the Tenth Circuit tightening the screws on proof. It is a case about what asylum law asks applicants to show when the government may be part of the problem, but the record does not clearly prove it. And for many asylum seekers, that is exactly the hardest case to make.



## Internal Relocation as a Backstop: What *Jimenez v. Bondi* Reveals About Modern Asylum Adjudication

## Why this case matters

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is, on its face, a conventional asylum denial: the Tenth Circuit held that the Colombian petitioners had not shown past persecution, had not established the required nexus to political opinion, and had not shown that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. But the opinion’s most interesting move may be the shortest one. The court’s discussion of safe internal relocation functions as a kind of backstop rationale, reinforcing the denial even after the panel had already concluded the petitioners fell short on the merits.

That matters because internal relocation is often where asylum cases turn from narrative to geography. Once a court concludes the harm is localized, or that the record does not show countrywide risk, the asylum claim can fail even if the applicant has suffered real threats from dangerous actors. *Jimenez* shows how that logic now often operates in cases involving politically connected criminals or quasi-political violence: the presence of local danger does not necessarily translate into a legally cognizable inability to remain anywhere in the country.

## The court’s relocation analysis

The petitioners argued that they could not safely relocate within Colombia because their persecutors were tied to organized crime and politically connected to the government. The panel was not persuaded. It pointed to three facts in particular: the family’s relatives remained in Colombia unharmed, Jimenez’s father continued to work in the criminal justice system, and the family had already lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to leave the country. The court also emphasized that the threats were framed as demands to leave the neighborhood, not the country.

Those details did a lot of work. They allowed the court to treat the threats as geographically limited and to infer that the danger was not so pervasive that Colombia as a whole was unavailable as a place of refuge. The opinion is careful to say that the family’s stay in the countryside was not just symbolic; it was evidence that relocation was in fact possible. And by noting that the persecutors told Jimenez to leave the neighborhood, the court drew a line between targeted intimidation and a countrywide campaign of persecution.

## The doctrinal significance

This part of the opinion fits comfortably within existing asylum doctrine, but it also illustrates how relocation analysis has become more important in practice. The legal question is not whether an applicant has been threatened by violent people, but whether the applicant can avoid future persecution by moving elsewhere in the country and doing so reasonably. That inquiry can be especially consequential where the applicant cannot prove the government is unwilling or unable to protect him, or cannot establish a protected-ground nexus.

In *Jimenez*, relocation was not the primary holding, but it reinforced each of the court’s other conclusions. If the persecution was not severe enough to qualify as past persecution, and if the threats were driven mainly by Jimenez’s dispute with a criminal actor rather than his politics, then the availability of a safe internal alternative becomes easier to see. In that sense, relocation operates less as a standalone test than as a doctrinal safety valve. It allows courts to say: even accepting that the petitioners faced genuine danger, asylum still fails if the danger appears local and manageable within the country.

## A cautious critique

There is some force to that approach, but it also has a limitation that *Jimenez* quietly exposes. The fact that relatives remain in the home country, or that an applicant managed to stay elsewhere for a few weeks, does not always answer the real question: whether internal relocation is genuinely safe and reasonable in light of the persecutor’s reach. Criminal groups, especially those described as politically connected, do not always operate in neat territorial boxes. A threat to “leave the neighborhood” can still reflect a broader ability to track, harass, or punish an applicant elsewhere.

The panel did not ignore that possibility, but it found the record too thin to support it. That is consistent with substantial-evidence review, and the court is right to resist converting speculation about national reach into a substitute for proof. Still, the opinion underscores a recurring tension in asylum law: relocation analysis can become a way to deny protection even when the applicant’s fear is understandable, so long as the record does not clearly establish that the danger extends beyond one locality.

## Downstream effects

The likely downstream effect is to make internal relocation even more central in cases involving mixed political-criminal motives. Applicants who claim threats from armed or organized actors tied loosely to politics will need to build a stronger record on why relocation is not realistic, not merely undesirable. That means evidence about the persecutor’s reach, the feasibility of life in another region, prior attempts to relocate, and why reporting or moving would not reduce the risk.

*Jimenez* also suggests that family safety in the home country will remain a powerful factual marker for the government and the court. So will evidence that threats were localized or phrased as demands to leave a particular area. For asylum applicants, that means the relocation inquiry cannot be an afterthought. It may end up doing exactly what it did here: serving as the last, and often decisive, reason the petition fails.
