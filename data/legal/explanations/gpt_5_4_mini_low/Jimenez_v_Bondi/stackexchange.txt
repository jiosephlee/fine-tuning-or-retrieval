Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why wasn't this considered "past persecution" when there were death threats, a gunpoint confrontation, an assault on an employee, and an apparent attempted abduction of a child?
Question:
I'm confused about the Tenth Circuit's persecution analysis here. The family got repeated threatening calls, a written death threat, Jimenez was held at gunpoint, armed men later came back looking for him and assaulted an employee, and then a stranger grabbed or at least physically interfered with his son outside school while telling him to run to his dad. The court still says this does not rise to the level of past persecution, especially because there was no lasting physical injury. I thought persecution could be established cumulatively and did not require serious bodily harm. How are courts drawing the line between severe threats/intimidation and legally sufficient persecution in asylum cases?
Answer:
Because in the Tenth Circuit, **“persecution” is a high threshold**: it requires more than harassment, intimidation, or even a series of serious threats. The court can consider events **cumulatively**, but the total must still amount to “the infliction of suffering or harm” that is **more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats**. *Jimenez v. Bondi* (citing *Wiransane*, *Ritonga*, and *Karki*).

Here, the court treated the evidence as serious but still short of that line for three main reasons:

1. **Threats alone generally do not equal persecution.**  
   The opinion explicitly says: “**threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution**.” So repeated calls, the brochure, and later death threats matter, but they do not automatically become persecution just because they recur.

2. **There was no showing of lasting physical harm, and the physical incidents were limited.**  
   Jimenez was held at gunpoint, and later an employee was assaulted. The child incident was troubling, but the court did not treat it as clear proof of an actual kidnapping or severe injury. The opinion emphasizes that Petitioners conceded they were **not physically harmed** and had **no lasting physical harm**. Physical injury is not required, but the absence of serious injury makes it harder to reach the persecution threshold.

3. **The court viewed the events as intimidation tied to a dispute, not persecution rising to the required severity.**  
   The opinion notes that Petitioners’ harm was “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling,” but Tenth Circuit precedent has “rarely, if ever” found **threats and emotional harm alone** enough. The court compared the facts to prior cases where even multiple threats, or threats plus other bad conduct, still did not amount to persecution.

So the key point is this: **the cumulative-effects doctrine does not mean “a lot of serious bad things” automatically becomes persecution.** It means the court aggregates all the incidents, but still asks whether the combined harm is severe enough to cross the legal line.

Also, although your question focuses on the severity of harm, the opinion separately holds that the escalated threats were not shown to be **on account of a protected ground** in the way asylum law requires. The court thought the main motive was Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa’s property and perceived interference, not primarily political opinion. That independently defeats the claim.

So, in short: the court was not saying these events were minor; it was saying they were **not severe enough, even taken together, to meet the Tenth Circuit’s persecution standard**.

### How did the court decide the harm was not "on account of" political opinion when the threats explicitly mentioned the Liberal Party?
Question:
The opinion says the nexus requirement failed because the main reason for the threats was Jimenez's involvement with Don Rafa's property and criminal operations, not the family's political opinion. But some of the threats literally said things like "death to snitches from Liberal Party," and the attackers also described them as people "in politics." I understand mixed-motive cases exist, but I'm having trouble seeing why the political component was only tangential rather than "one central reason." Is the idea that once there is a stronger non-protected motive (like retaliation for investigating criminal activity), the political motive becomes legally insufficient? How do courts evaluate evidence when the persecutor seems to invoke both political affiliation and anti-snitch retaliation?
Answer:
The key point is that **an express political reference is not enough by itself** if the record supports the agency’s finding that the **real reason** for the threats was something else.

Under the INA, an applicant must show that the protected ground was **“at least one central reason”** for the harm. The Tenth Circuit repeats the governing rule this way: the protected ground cannot be “**incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate**” to another motive. A persecutor can certainly have **mixed motives**, but one of those motives must still be central.

That is exactly how the court read the evidence here.

### Why the “Liberal Party” language did not control
The court did **not** ignore the brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party” or the later comment that Petitioners were “in politics.” Instead, it treated those statements as part of the overall record and asked what the threats were **really about**.

The court emphasized several facts showing the dominant motive was retaliation for Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa’s property and criminal operations:

- the escalated threats began **after** Jimenez became involved in the property dispute;
- one threat accused him of being a **“snitch” for the prosecutor’s office**;
- the men said he was responsible for the **searches and seizures** at the property;
- later, they again called him a **“snitch for the police”** and said the order came from **Don Rafa**.

On that record, the court concluded the political references were **secondary**. In the court’s words, Petitioners’ political opinion was “**merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm**.”

### So does a stronger non-protected motive automatically defeat nexus?
Not automatically. But if the record supports the finding that the non-protected motive was the **predominant** or **central** one, then the political motive will not satisfy the nexus requirement even if it is mentioned.

That is the important distinction in mixed-motive cases:  
- **Protected motive present** = not enough.  
- **Protected motive central** = enough.  
- **Protected motive only incidental to retaliation, revenge, or anti-snitch conduct** = not enough.

### How courts evaluate this kind of evidence
Courts look at the **whole context**, not isolated phrases. Here, the Tenth Circuit relied on the sequence of events and the persecutors’ repeated focus on Jimenez’s role as a “snitch” connected to the property, not on his partisan activity. The court also noted that Petitioners had already been involved in Liberal Party activity before the property dispute, yet the serious threats escalated only after the dispute and investigation began.

So the short answer is:

> The court did not say political language is irrelevant. It said the political references were outweighed by substantial evidence that the persecutors were mainly retaliating against Jimenez for interfering with Don Rafa’s criminal/property interests. Under the “one central reason” standard, a merely tangential political motive is legally insufficient.

### Why did the court treat the 'unable or unwilling to protect' element as unmet when police officers told them to leave because police were part of the problem?
Question:
The family apparently spoke to at least two police officers, and one officer allegedly said nothing could be done and that police officers were contributing to the problem. That sounds to me like evidence of state inability or corruption. But the court says this was only a 'one-off conversation' and that the family made minimal efforts to seek help. What level of reporting or proof is usually required to show a government is unwilling or unable to control private persecutors? Also, if an applicant has reason to think the police are compromised, how much evidence do they need to show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous?
Answer:
The court was not saying that the officer’s statement was irrelevant. It was saying that, on this record, it was not enough to compel the conclusion that the *Colombian government* was unwilling or unable to protect Petitioners.

That distinction is important in asylum law. The “unable or unwilling to control” element asks whether the *state as a whole* failed to protect the applicant from private harm. A single unhelpful officer can be evidence, but it is usually not enough by itself. As the court put it, the inquiry looks to whether the government responded to the persecution, and “a key fact” is whether the applicant reported the harm. The reason is practical: without notice, “there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” *Jimenez v. Bondi* (citing *Singh*).

Here, the Tenth Circuit emphasized three things:

1. **Minimal reporting.** Petitioners spoke to two officers, but they did not report wrongdoing through more official channels or otherwise show a broader attempt to seek protection.
2. **A “one-off conversation” is not systemic proof.** The court relied on the idea that police apathy or corruption by one officer does not necessarily show the government is unwilling or unable to protect. It cited *Osorio-Morales* for the proposition that a single unhelpful encounter does not establish a government-wide failure.
3. **The record cut the other way.** The court noted that authorities had already attempted searches and seizures at the property. That made it harder to argue that the government was aligned with or indifferent to the persecutors.

So the court’s point was not “the officer’s statement proves nothing,” but rather: *standing alone, it does not compel a finding of state inability or unwillingness, especially when the applicants did little else to seek protection and the record contains evidence of government action against the criminal actors.*

## What level of proof is usually required?

There is no rigid reporting formula. But in practice, applicants generally need evidence showing one of the following:

- they **reported** the harm and the authorities failed to act;
- they attempted to report, but the authorities **refused, ignored, or participated** in the harm;
- reporting would have been **futile or dangerous**, supported by concrete evidence; or
- the country conditions and other record evidence show a **systemic inability or unwillingness** to protect similarly situated people.

The more an applicant can show that the failure was not isolated, but part of a broader pattern, the stronger the claim.

## If police are compromised, how much evidence of futility or danger is needed?

The applicant does **not** have to risk harm just to create a record. The Tenth Circuit expressly says a failure to report is not necessarily fatal if the applicant can show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. But the claim has to be supported by evidence, not just suspicion.

In *Jimenez*, the court found the futility/danger argument unsupported because:

- the Petitioners’ claim that officers were part of the problem rested mainly on the one conversation;
- they did not use available official channels, including Jimenez’s government contacts; and
- the record showed the state had already acted against the property-linked criminal activity.

So the lesson is: if an applicant believes police are compromised, they should try to produce **corroboration** — prior reports ignored by police, threats against complainants, country reports showing police collusion, testimony about widespread corruption, or similar evidence. A bare assertion that “reporting would have been dangerous” usually will not be enough unless the record backs it up.

In short, the court treated the element as unmet because it saw **insufficient evidence of a state-wide failure of protection**, not just an isolated discouraging remark by one officer.

### Why was internal relocation discussed as if the burden was on the applicants, even though the opinion quotes the government's rebuttal standard?
Question:
The court says the government may rebut a presumption of future persecution by showing the applicant can relocate internally, citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i). But earlier the court already held there was no past persecution, so there was no presumption to rebut. Then in the relocation section the court says the petitioners 'have not demonstrated' a fear of future persecution, but 'even if they had,' the record supports relocation. I'm confused about who bears the burden on internal relocation when there is no finding of past persecution. Is the burden on the asylum applicant to prove relocation is unreasonable, or on DHS to prove relocation is reasonable? Did the court just discuss relocation as an alternative holding?
Answer:
Yes — the court was discussing **internal relocation as an alternative ground**, and the burden point changes depending on whether the applicant has already established **past persecution**.

## Short answer

- **If the applicant proves past persecution**, a **presumption** of future persecution arises.
  - Then DHS can rebut that presumption by showing, among other things, that the applicant **could safely and reasonably relocate** internally.
- **If the applicant does not prove past persecution**, there is **no presumption to rebut**.
  - In that situation, the **asylum applicant** bears the burden of proving a **well-founded fear of future persecution**, which ordinarily includes showing that safe internal relocation is not a reasonable way to avoid the harm.

So the court was not shifting the burden improperly. It was saying:

1. Petitioners **failed to show past persecution**, so no presumption attached; and
2. **Even assuming** they had shown a fear of future persecution, the record still supported denial because they could relocate within Colombia.

## How the opinion uses that framework

The key line is in Part F:

> “**Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate** to another part of Colombia.”

That is classic **alternative reasoning**. The court is saying relocation is enough to defeat the claim **even if** the applicants cleared the earlier hurdle.

## Why the opinion quotes the government rebuttal standard

The court quotes the rebuttal standard from **8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)** because that is the regulation governing the **presumption** scenario after past persecution. But the opinion does **not** rest its relocation discussion solely on that rebuttal framework.

Instead, the court has already held:
- no past persecution,
- no nexus,
- no showing that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them,
- no well-founded fear of future persecution.

Once the court gets there, the relocation discussion becomes **extra support**, not the sole basis for the result.

## Burden when there is no past persecution

The opinion itself states the general asylum rule:

> “To be eligible for a discretionary grant of asylum, **an applicant bears the burden of proving** by a preponderance of the evidence that he is a refugee.”

That means that absent past persecution, the **applicant** must establish the elements of future fear. Internal relocation is part of that future-fear analysis, so the applicant cannot simply rely on DHS to disprove it unless the regulatory presumption has been triggered.

## Bottom line

So the answer is:

- **No past persecution = no presumption = no DHS rebuttal burden.**
- The **asylum applicant** bears the burden of showing a well-founded fear of future persecution, which includes dealing with the feasibility of internal relocation.
- The court’s relocation discussion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is best read as **an alternative holding / alternative ground** supporting denial, not as a burden-shifting error.

### What does it mean that the court reviewed the BIA's decision but could still 'consult the IJ's more complete explanation'?
Question:
On the standard of review, the court says that because a single BIA member affirmed the IJ, it reviews the BIA's decision, but it is not precluded from consulting the IJ's fuller reasoning. Procedurally, I'm not sure how this works. If the BIA affirms and adds some discussion of its own, is the court reviewing only the grounds expressly adopted by the BIA, or can it rely on any rationale from the IJ? What are the limits on a circuit court using the IJ's opinion when the BIA issues a streamlined or brief affirmance?
Answer:
What the Tenth Circuit means is that the **BIA’s order is the final agency decision the court reviews**, but when the BIA issues a **brief affirmance**—especially a single-member affirmance—the court may look to the IJ’s opinion to understand the reasoning behind the BIA’s result.

That is exactly what the court says in *Jimenez v. Bondi*:

> “Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, we review the BIA’s decision, but ‘we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.’”  
> (*citing* *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*)

### What that means procedurally

The court is **not switching the object of review** from the BIA to the IJ. The BIA remains the decision under review. But if the BIA’s decision is terse, the court may read the IJ’s more detailed opinion **as a way of understanding the grounds the BIA accepted**.

So the court can consult the IJ’s reasoning **only to the extent it explains the same grounds the BIA relied on**.

### The practical limit

The circuit court generally **cannot affirm on a rationale that the BIA never adopted**. In other words:

- **Permissible:** using the IJ’s fuller explanation to flesh out the BIA’s brief reasoning on an issue the BIA actually addressed.
- **Not permissible:** relying on a wholly new IJ ground that the BIA did not reach or adopt.

That limitation is built into the language the court uses: it may consult the IJ’s explanation of “**those same grounds**.” So the IJ’s opinion is a guide to the agency’s reasoning, not a free-standing alternative decision the court can substitute in.

### In this opinion

The Tenth Circuit does exactly that. It reviews the BIA’s denial of asylum and withholding, but when discussing the facts and the agency’s conclusions—past persecution, nexus, government protection, relocation—it refers back to the IJ’s more detailed findings because the BIA’s affirmance was brief. The court is not saying “we are reviewing the IJ instead of the BIA.” It is saying “the BIA affirmed, and the IJ’s explanation helps us understand the basis for that affirmance.”

### Bottom line

A streamlined or brief BIA affirmance means:

1. **The BIA’s decision is still the one reviewed on petition for review.**
2. The court may **consult the IJ’s opinion for fuller reasoning on the same issues the BIA adopted.**
3. The court may **not** rely on an IJ theory that the BIA did not embrace.

So “consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation” is a matter of **interpretation and context**, not a license to substitute the IJ’s reasoning for the BIA’s.

### Why did the withholding of removal claim automatically fail just because the asylum claim failed?
Question:
At the end, the court says that because the petitioners failed to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution for asylum, they necessarily failed to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. I thought withholding and asylum overlap a lot, but are not identical, especially because asylum is discretionary and withholding is mandatory if the standard is met. Is the court saying withholding always fails whenever asylum fails, or only when asylum fails on certain merits elements like persecution/nexus/government protection rather than timing, discretion, or procedural bars?
Answer:
The court is **not** saying that withholding of removal always fails whenever asylum fails. It is saying something narrower: **when asylum fails because the applicant did not prove the underlying persecution-based elements, withholding will usually fail too, because withholding uses the same core factual predicates but a higher burden of proof.**

### Why that follows here

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit affirmed that petitioners failed to show:

- **past persecution**
- **nexus** to a protected ground
- **government unwillingness/unability** to protect them
- and, relatedly, a **well-founded fear of future persecution**

Those are the substantive elements that matter to both asylum and withholding. The court then cites the familiar rule that because asylum requires a **“well-founded fear”** and withholding requires the more demanding **“clear probability”** / **“more likely than not”** showing, a petitioner who cannot satisfy the lower asylum standard ordinarily cannot satisfy the higher withholding standard. That is why the opinion says they “necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.”

### The key distinction

You are right that asylum and withholding are **not identical**:

- **Asylum** is **discretionary**
- **Withholding** is **mandatory** if the standard is met
- They also differ in some **procedural bars** and eligibility rules

So the rule is **not**:

> “Any time asylum is denied, withholding automatically fails.”

Rather, it is:

> “If asylum is denied because the applicant failed to prove the merits of persecution/nexus/government protection/fear, then withholding generally fails too, because withholding requires an even stronger showing.”

### When asylum can fail but withholding still be available

That can happen when asylum is denied for reasons **not tied to the merits of persecution**, such as:

- the **one-year filing deadline**
- some **discretionary** denial of asylum
- certain asylum-specific statutory bars

In those situations, withholding may still be available because it is evaluated under a different framework and is not subject to every asylum limitation.

### Why the court’s phrasing makes sense in this opinion

Here, the denial was **not** based on a timing bar or a discretionary refusal. It was based on the petitioners’ failure to establish the basic substantive facts needed for refugee status in the first place. So once the court concluded they did not meet the asylum standard on the merits, it was correct to say they necessarily could not meet withholding’s higher standard.

### Did the court effectively treat the particular social group theory as waived because it was not developed on appeal?
Question:
The opinion notes that the petitioners argued nexus based on both political opinion and particular social group, but then says they did not brief the existence of any particular social group or establish membership in one, so the court limited review to political opinion. I'm trying to understand the appellate practice point here. In immigration petitions for review, how much development is required to preserve a PSG argument? If the issue was raised before the IJ and BIA but only mentioned in passing in the circuit brief, is that considered forfeited or waived?
Answer:
Yes. In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the Tenth Circuit effectively **declined to consider the PSG theory because it was not developed on appeal**.

The court said:

> “Petitioners, however, did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group. Rather, in arguing the nexus requirement, Petitioners allege that they were persecuted by Don Rafa because of their political opinion. Accordingly, we limit our review to whether Petitioners establish a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion.”

That is an appellate **issue-preservation** ruling, not a merits rejection of PSG on the facts. The practical point is that, even if a PSG theory was mentioned before the IJ or BIA, a petitioner must **actually present and develop the argument in the circuit opening brief**. A passing reference is usually not enough.

### Waiver vs. forfeiture
Strictly speaking, courts often distinguish:

- **Waiver** = intentional relinquishment of a known argument
- **Forfeiture** = failure to timely raise or adequately develop it

In this setting, the court’s reasoning is closer to **forfeiture through inadequate briefing**. But in everyday appellate usage, courts often loosely say the argument was “waived” when it was not meaningfully briefed.

### What this means in immigration petitions for review
If a PSG argument was preserved before the agency but on petition for review is only mentioned in passing, the circuit court will commonly treat it as **not adequately raised** and therefore decline to review it. The court here did exactly that: because Petitioners “did not brief” the PSG theory or show membership in any group, it confined the nexus analysis to **political opinion**.

### Bottom line
So, yes: **the court treated the PSG theory as abandoned on appeal because it was not developed in the brief**. In the Tenth Circuit, and generally in federal appellate practice, a bare mention is usually not enough to preserve an argument for review.
