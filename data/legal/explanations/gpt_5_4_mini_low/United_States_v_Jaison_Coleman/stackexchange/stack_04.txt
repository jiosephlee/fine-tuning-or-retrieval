Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### What exactly was the objective scope of Lisa's consent once the officers entered the home?
Question:
The court says the officers stayed within the scope of consent because their purpose was to check on the children and determine whether Coleman posed a danger. But I'm unsure how broad that makes the consent in practical terms. If Lisa only consented to the officers coming in to see whether the kids were okay, why did that also authorize a sweep of the home to find Coleman in a separate bedroom? Is that because a reasonable person would understand child-welfare checking in a domestic violence situation to include locating the allegedly violent person, or because the consent implicitly covered officer-safety steps too?
Answer:
The key is that the scope of consent is measured **objectively**, not by Lisa’s subjective, hidden intent. Under *Florida v. Jimeno*, the question is what a **typical reasonable person** would have understood from the exchange. The Seventh Circuit applied that standard here.

In this opinion, Lisa did **not** give a broad “come in and search the house” consent. But she did agree after Officer Kizer said they “need to come inside to make sure” the children were okay, and she then volunteered two highly relevant facts:

1. the children were in her bedroom, and  
2. Coleman was probably in the back bedroom.

That matters. The court says the officers’ “stated purpose was to check the welfare of her children inside, **including by confirming that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else**.” So the scope was tied to that welfare check. A reasonable person would understand that checking whether the children were safe in a domestic-violence call necessarily involves:

- entering the home,
- going to where the children are,
- making sure the suspected aggressor is not there or otherwise posing an immediate threat,
- and asking basic questions to confirm safety.

That is why the officers were allowed to do a **brief canvas** and find Coleman in a separate bedroom. The court viewed that as part of the same limited safety check, not as an independent exploratory search.

So the answer is: **both**, but in a limited sense. The consent implicitly covered officer-safety steps **because those steps were part of the agreed-upon child-welfare check**. It was not a general consent to rummage through the home; it was a reasonable, limited authorization to do what was necessary to determine whether the children were okay and whether Coleman was a present danger.
