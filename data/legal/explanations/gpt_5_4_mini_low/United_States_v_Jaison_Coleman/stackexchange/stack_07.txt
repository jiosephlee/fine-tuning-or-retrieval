Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Why was Lisa's role as a co-occupant enough for consent without discussing Georgia v. Randolph or objecting occupants?
Question:
The opinion cites Matlock and says a third party with common authority can consent, which makes sense because Lisa lived there too. But I was expecting some discussion of cases about one occupant objecting to a search. Coleman was apparently somewhere in the house when Lisa let the officers in. Does it matter that he wasn't asked first, or that he didn't object before the officers found him? I'm confused about when a present co-occupant's potential objection defeats another occupant's consent and when it doesn't.
Answer:
Because **the Randolph problem never actually arose on these facts as framed by the opinion**.

The Seventh Circuit resolved the case on a much narrower ground: **Lisa herself validly consented to entry**. Once the court concluded that her consent was voluntary and that the officers stayed within the scope of that consent, there was no need to reach a separate “present objecting occupant” question.

### Why Matlock, not Randolph?
The opinion relies on the basic rule from **Matlock**: a co-occupant with common authority may consent to entry/search of shared premises. That is the rule the parties were litigating here—whether **Lisa’s consent alone** was enough.

By contrast, **Georgia v. Randolph** matters when:
1. one co-occupant gives consent, **and**
2. another co-occupant who is **physically present** at the time **expressly refuses consent**.

The opinion contains no such refusal. Coleman was apparently elsewhere in the house when Lisa admitted the officers, but there is no finding that:
- he was at the door,
- officers confronted him before entering,
- he objected to entry, or
- Lisa’s consent was overridden by his express refusal.

So under the opinion’s facts, there is no Randolph issue to discuss.

### Does it matter that Coleman was inside the house?
Not by itself. A co-occupant being somewhere inside the home does **not automatically defeat** another occupant’s consent. The key is an **express, contemporaneous objection by the physically present co-occupant**. The court’s opinion does not describe any such objection before entry.

### What about his failure to object once found?
That also does not change the entry analysis in this opinion. By the time officers found Coleman, they had already entered on Lisa’s consent. The court then notes that Coleman later **permitted a protective pat down**, which is the opposite of an objection.

### Bottom line
The court did not discuss Randolph because **Coleman never showed the kind of present, express objection that would trigger it**. On the opinion’s facts, the search turns on ordinary third-party consent under Matlock, and Lisa’s consent was enough.
