Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### How did the later search warrant avoid 'fruit of the poisonous tree' problems if the entry had been unlawful?
Question:
I understand that because the court found the initial entry lawful by consent, the later warrant and gun recovery were fine. But I'm trying to think through the alternative. The officers learned from the children after entering that Coleman had pointed a gun and threatened Lisa, and that information helped them get the search warrant. If the entry had been illegal, would the warrant automatically be tainted as fruit of the poisonous tree? Or could the government still have saved the evidence through inevitable discovery, an independent source theory, or by showing enough untainted facts already existed before entry?
Answer:
No — an unlawful entry would **not automatically** poison the later warrant. The key question would be **causation**: whether the warrant, and the firearms recovered under it, were obtained **by exploitation of the illegal entry** or instead by a source independent of that entry.

### How the doctrines would work

If the initial entry had been unlawful, Coleman would have had a strong **fruit of the poisonous tree** argument as to anything learned inside the home, including the children’s statements. But the government would still have had possible ways to save the evidence:

1. **Independent source**  
   If the warrant application was supported by **untainted information already known before entry**, and that information alone established probable cause, the warrant could stand even if officers also included tainted facts. The warrant is not invalid merely because some information came from an unlawful entry; the issue is whether the magistrate had a sufficient independent basis.

2. **Inevitable discovery**  
   Even if the warrant was helped by tainted information, the government could argue the guns would have been found anyway through lawful means that were already underway or would have happened regardless. That doctrine is exactly the kind of fallback the Seventh Circuit mentions when it says the government also invoked “the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery,” but the court did not need to reach them because it found the entry lawful.

3. **Attenuation / cleansing the affidavit**  
   Courts often ask whether, after excising the unlawfully obtained material, the remaining affidavit still establishes probable cause. If it does, the warrant survives under the independent-source idea.

### What this opinion tells us

This opinion does **not** decide that alternative question. In fact, it expressly says:

> “These conclusions eliminate any need to consider the government’s alternative contention that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the police’s entry into and initial sweep of the Coleman residence.”

So the court **never had to decide** whether the later warrant would have been tainted if the entry had been illegal.

### Bottom line

If the entry had been unlawful, the later warrant would **not be automatically invalid**, but the government would have had to show one of the usual exceptions — most likely that the warrant rested on an **independent, untainted basis** or that the guns would have been **inevitably discovered** anyway. If the warrant depended materially on facts learned only from the unlawful entry, suppression would be a serious possibility.
