Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### How can Lisa's second 'Okay' count as consent when she had already said she preferred the officers not enter?
Question:
The facts that are tripping me up are: Officer Kizer first asked if he could enter, and Lisa said, "um ... I'd prefer you not." Then later, after he said, "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure" the kids were okay, she said, "Okay," and opened the door. I'm not sure how courts treat this kind of sequence. Does the initial refusal matter a lot, or can a later statement fully override it? Also, how do courts decide whether the later response is a real change of mind versus submission to pressure?
Answer:
Because consent is judged by the **totality of the circumstances**, a prior refusal does not permanently bar a later consent. What matters is whether, at the time of the later statement, the person **freely and voluntarily** chose to allow entry.

That is exactly how the Seventh Circuit treated Lisa’s exchange with Officer Kizer.

### 1. A prior “no” does not lock in the answer
The court expressly noted that Lisa first said, **“um … I’d prefer you not”** when asked if the officer could enter. But it did **not** treat that as dispositive. Instead, the panel looked at the **entire interaction** and asked whether the later **“Okay”** was a voluntary change of mind or the product of coercion.

The opinion emphasizes that:
- Officer Kizer was calm and respectful,
- Lisa was not in custody,
- the conversation was brief,
- Lisa had already shown she understood she could refuse by doing exactly that the first time,
- and the second request was not accompanied by coercion.

So the initial refusal mattered, but mainly as evidence that Lisa understood she had a choice.

### 2. The later “Okay” can override the earlier refusal
Yes. A later affirmative response can supply valid consent even after an earlier refusal, so long as the later consent is itself voluntary.

Here, the court relied on what happened **after** Officer Kizer said, **“I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure”** the kids were okay. Lisa replied, **“Okay,”** then immediately:
- told the officer where the children were,
- said where Coleman was likely located,
- and opened the door and let the officers in.

Those actions were important because they showed the “Okay” was not an isolated word; it was part of a broader course of conduct consistent with consent.

### 3. How courts tell “real consent” from mere submission
The doctrine comes from cases like **Schneckloth v. Bustamonte** and **Bumper v. North Carolina**: consent must be **freely and voluntarily given**, not the result of coercion, threats, or a claim of authority.

Courts look at factors such as:
- whether the person was in custody,
- whether police used physical force or intimidation,
- whether the request was repeated,
- how long the encounter lasted,
- and whether the officer’s tone was coercive.

In this case, the Seventh Circuit rejected the argument that the officer’s use of the word **“need”** made the exchange coercive. The court said that, in context, the officer was expressing concern about the children’s safety, not asserting a right to enter regardless of Lisa’s wishes.

### 4. Bottom line
So the answer is:

- **The initial refusal matters**, but it does not control forever.
- **A later consent can be valid** if it is voluntary under the totality of the circumstances.
- In *Coleman*, the court thought Lisa’s later “Okay” was a genuine consent, not a mere surrender to pressure, because her words and conduct after that point confirmed she was allowing entry.
