Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 2: # 2. Procedural Posture and the Suppression Question on Appeal

## From the Home Entry to the Federal Prosecution

The procedural path of *United States v. Coleman* begins with what the officers learned after they entered the home and ends with a federal firearms conviction. Once Officer Kizer and the other officers were inside the Coleman residence, they gathered information from the children and from their observations in the home. That information was then used to obtain a search warrant. The warrant-based search uncovered several firearms. Those firearms formed the basis for a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

That sequence matters because Coleman did not challenge the later warrant search in the abstract; he argued that everything flowed from an unlawful initial entry. In Fourth Amendment cases, if the police enter a home without valid consent, a warrant obtained using information learned during that entry may be vulnerable, and the evidence found later may be suppressible as the fruit of the unlawful search.

## The Suppression Motion and the Lower Courts

Coleman moved to suppress the firearms, contending that Lisa never validly consented to the officers’ entry into the home. His argument was not merely that the officers should not have searched certain rooms or looked at certain things; it was that the initial entry itself violated the Fourth Amendment because the officers lacked permission to come inside.

The matter first went to a magistrate judge, who issued a report concluding that Lisa did consent to entry and recommending that the suppression motion be denied. The district court adopted that recommendation. It agreed, first, that Lisa’s words and conduct amounted to consent for the officers to enter the home, and second, that the officers did not exceed the scope of that consent once inside. Those two findings were enough to defeat Coleman’s suppression motion.

After losing that motion, Coleman pleaded guilty but preserved his right to appeal the suppression ruling. That kind of plea allows a defendant to accept conviction while still obtaining appellate review of a specified pretrial issue. Here, the reserved issue was the denial of suppression.

## The Issues on Appeal

On appeal, the Seventh Circuit confronted two related Fourth Amendment questions. The first was whether Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entry. If her consent was not voluntary, the initial entry would be unlawful. The second was whether, even if the entry was lawful, the officers stayed within the scope of the consent she gave. A valid consent to enter a home does not automatically authorize every possible police action inside; the officers’ conduct must remain within what a reasonable person would understand the consent to permit.

These were the only issues the court needed to decide if it concluded that consent was valid and not exceeded. The government had also advanced alternative justifications for the entry and later search, including exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. But those theories mattered only if consent failed. Because the court of appeals affirmed on consent grounds, it had no reason to reach the alternative arguments.

## Why the Standard of Review Matters

The standard of review was central to the appeal. The Seventh Circuit reviewed the district court’s findings on voluntary consent and scope of consent for clear error. That is a deferential standard. It does not ask whether the appellate judges might have weighed the facts differently. Instead, it asks whether the district court’s factual findings were plainly mistaken in light of the record.

That matters especially here because the record included body-camera video. When video “evaporate[s]” factual disputes, appellate review becomes even more constrained: the court is not reconstructing events from competing testimony so much as checking whether the district court’s interpretation of the recorded interaction was clearly erroneous. In practical terms, Coleman had to show not just that his reading of the exchange was plausible, but that the district court’s contrary view was not supportable on the record.

So the appeal presented a narrow but important question: did the district court clearly err in concluding that Lisa freely allowed the officers inside, and that the officers then limited themselves to the welfare-check purpose for which that consent was given? The Seventh Circuit answered yes to the first and no to the second, and on that basis affirmed.
