Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can a court dismiss a copyright claim on fair use grounds at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedure here. I thought fair use is an affirmative defense that usually depends on a fact-intensive balancing of the four statutory factors, so it would normally be raised later on summary judgment, not on a motion to dismiss. But this opinion says fair use can be 'so clearly established by a complaint' that dismissal is proper under Rule 12(b)(6). What exactly has to be apparent from the complaint and incorporated materials for a court to resolve fair use this early? Is the appellate court effectively treating the videos and TV segments as documents that can be compared directly at the pleading stage?
Answer:
A court can dismiss on fair-use grounds at Rule 12(b)(6) **when the complaint itself, together with incorporated materials and the works at issue, makes fair use apparent as a matter of law**.

That is exactly the Second Circuit’s point in **Santos v. Kimmel**. The court repeats the standard that fair use is an affirmative defense, but also that it “**may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal**” at the pleading stage. The key is not that the defendant has proved fair use with evidence, but that **nothing in the pleaded facts leaves a real factual dispute on the fair-use factors**.

### What has to be apparent at the pleading stage
Under the opinion, the court may consider:

1. **The complaint itself**
2. **Documents attached to or incorporated by reference in the complaint**
3. **Judicially noticeable materials**, including copyright registrations

The Second Circuit then uses those materials to assess the statutory factors.

### Why dismissal was proper here
The opinion says several things were apparent on the face of the complaint and incorporated materials:

- **Purpose and character / transformativeness**: Santos’s own complaint alleged that his original videos conveyed “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments used them to comment on whether Santos would “say absurd things for money.” That made the secondary use **transformative** on the pleaded facts.
- **Nature/amount**: The court says the relevant copyright registrations showed the videos were published the same day they were created, and the court could compare “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue.”
- **Market effect**: The complaint itself showed no plausible market harm in the relevant sense, because the use was criticism/commentary rather than a substitute competing in the same market.

### Is the court treating the videos and TV segments as documents?
Not exactly as “documents” in the ordinary Rule 12(b)(6) sense, but the court is doing something similar: **it may consider the copyrighted works themselves** when they are part of the pleading record or otherwise properly before the court. The opinion relies on **Cariou v. Prince** for the proposition that the fair-use question can be decided by looking at the original work and the accused work.

So the procedural idea is:

- if the complaint and incorporated materials already show the relevant fair-use factors overwhelmingly favor the defendant,
- and the court can directly compare the works themselves without fact-finding,
- then fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss.

### Bottom line
**Yes, but only when the complaint “proves too much.”** In *Santos*, the plaintiff’s own allegations, plus the attached/incorporated materials and judicially noticeable registrations, made the use look transformative and non-substitutive on the face of the case. That is why the Second Circuit said dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) was proper.

### Why did the court say Kimmel's use was transformative even though he allegedly caused the videos to be created?
Question:
The part I don't understand is Santos's argument that Kimmel 'instigated the creation' of the Cameo videos, so Kimmel was really the 'designer' of their original purpose. If Kimmel was the one who submitted the requests and got Santos to say the absurd lines, why doesn't that undermine the idea that the later TV use had a different purpose from the original work? The court seems to say the key question is what a reasonable observer would think the original videos were for, not the subjective intent of either side. Is that standard always used in transformativeness analysis, and why doesn't the alleged setup make the 'original purpose' and 'secondary purpose' the same here?
Answer:
The court is treating **“transformative use” as an objective comparison between the two works**, not as an inquiry into the user’s private motives or the copyright holder’s strategic behavior.

### 1. Why Kimmel’s alleged “setup” did not defeat transformativeness

Santos argued that because Kimmel allegedly **instigated** the creation of the Cameo videos, Kimmel was really the “designer” of their original purpose. The Second Circuit rejected that framing because the fair-use inquiry asks what the works **actually communicate to a reasonable observer**, not who secretly hoped for what.

The court expressly relied on **Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith** for the proposition that:

> “the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”

So even if Kimmel orchestrated the requests, that does **not** change what the original videos were objectively for. According to Santos’s own complaint, the Cameos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments used them “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.”

Those are different purposes.

### 2. Why the original and secondary purposes were not the same

The key point is that the court did **not** define the “original purpose” as “whatever Kimmel intended the videos to be used for later.” It defined it by reference to the videos’ **apparent communicative function** when created.

That matters because the copyrighted work is the video Santos made and delivered. The question is whether the later TV segments “merely supersede[d] the objects of the original creation” or instead added “something new, with a further purpose or different character.”

Here, the videos were ordered as personalized Cameos, which on the face of Santos’s complaint were meant to communicate conventional positive messages. The JKL segments repurposed those videos for **mocking commentary** about Santos’s willingness to say anything for money. That is a different use, even if Kimmel engineered the setup to expose that difference.

### 3. Is the objective-reasonable-observer standard always used?

Not in exactly those words for every fair-use case, but **for transformativeness, yes, the analysis is generally objective**. Courts look at the **purpose and character of the use** as revealed by the work and the context, not by one side’s hidden intentions. The Supreme Court’s Warhol decision emphasized that courts should not make transformativeness turn on subjective intent.

So the court was not saying intent is irrelevant in all fair-use analysis. It was saying that **the setup does not collapse the objective distinction between the two uses**.

### 4. Bottom line

Kimmel may have arranged for the videos to be created, but that does not make the Cameos’ **objective original purpose** the same as the JKL segments’ **critical, mocking purpose**. The fair-use analysis turns on the latter comparison, and the court found that the two uses served different functions.

### What role does bad faith still play in fair use after Harper & Row and Warhol?
Question:
The opinion says Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fake identities on Cameo showed bad faith and therefore should defeat fair use. The court responds that Harper & Row mentioned good faith, but that the Supreme Court has since 'distanced itself' from that statement. I'm having trouble understanding the modern doctrine. Does bad faith still matter under factor one, or is it basically irrelevant unless it shows an intent to usurp the copyright owner's market? If someone obtains a work through deception but then uses it for criticism or parody, how do courts now weigh that?
Answer:
**Short answer: bad faith is not an independent fair-use killer anymore.** Under modern doctrine, it can still be relevant to factor one, but only as part of the overall inquiry into the **purpose and character of the use**—especially whether the secondary user is trying to **supplant** the original or instead is making a **transformative** use such as criticism, commentary, or parody.

That is essentially how the Second Circuit treats it in **_Santos v. Kimmel_**. The court acknowledged Harper & Row’s statement that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but then said the Supreme Court has **since distanced itself from that statement**. The court therefore did **not** treat Kimmel’s use of false identities as automatically defeating fair use. Instead, it asked the real factor-one question: what was the purpose and character of the use?

The key point is this:

- **Bad faith alone does not defeat fair use.**
- It matters only if it bears on the statutory factors, mainly factor one and sometimes factor four.
- If the complaint still shows the use was **critical, satirical, or transformative**, bad faith will usually not outweigh that.

In **_Santos_**, the court said the complaint itself showed the defendants were motivated by “(sarcastic) criticism and commentary,” not by a purpose to “supplant” Santos’s market for the videos. So even though Kimmel allegedly used deception to obtain the videos, that did not defeat fair use because the end use was mocking commentary, not market substitution.

So if someone obtains a work through deception but then uses it for criticism or parody, modern courts generally ask:

1. **Is the secondary use transformative?**
2. **Is it aimed at criticism/commentary/parody rather than substitution?**
3. **Does it usurp a market for the original?**

Deception may be relevant background, but it is **not dispositive**. In the Second Circuit’s view here, the decisive point is whether the use is still the kind of expressive, critical use that fair use is meant to protect.

### Why did publication on the same day as creation matter to the second fair use factor?
Question:
The court says the copyright registrations showed the videos were 'published on the same day they were created,' and it treats that as relevant to the remaining fair use factors. I'm confused about why that matters. I know the second factor looks at the nature of the copyrighted work, including whether it is unpublished, but these were still personalized expressive videos. Why does same-day publication weaken Santos's position? Is the idea that once a work has already been published, the author's interest in controlling the first public appearance is gone, even if the work is highly personal or creative?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the point.

Under the **second fair use factor**, courts look at the **nature of the copyrighted work**, and one of the classic considerations is whether the work was **published or unpublished**. Unpublished works get somewhat stronger protection because the Copyright Act recognizes an author’s interest in deciding **whether and when to make the first public appearance** of the work.

That is why the panel notes that the registrations showed Santos’s videos were **“published on the same day they were created.”** If a work is published immediately, then the author’s special interest in keeping the work private or controlling its first disclosure is basically absent. So the work is still creative and personal, but it is **not unpublished** in the way that would weigh heavily against fair use.

So the relevance is not that publication eliminates copyright protection. It is that it reduces the weight of the second factor against fair use. In other words:

- **creative/personal nature** of the videos still favors Santos to some degree;
- but because they were **published immediately**, that factor is less helpful to him than it would be if the videos had remained unpublished.

The court’s brief treatment reflects that. It does **not** say publication alone decides fair use. It just treats same-day publication as showing that Santos could not rely on the special solicitude courts often give to **unpublished** works under factor two.

So your understanding is right: the key idea is the loss of the author’s interest in controlling the **first public appearance** of the work.

### How did the court define market harm under factor four when the alleged use could still reduce the value of Santos's Cameo business?
Question:
The opinion says there was no market harm on the face of the complaint because courts ask whether the secondary use 'usurps' the market for the original by serving as a competing substitute, not whether it just damages the original through parody or criticism. But if Kimmel's segments made Santos look ridiculous and thereby reduced demand for future Cameo videos, isn't that a real economic harm? Why is that not the kind of market harm copyright law cares about under factor four? Is the distinction that copyright protects substitution-based market injury, but not reputational or criticism-based injury?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the distinction the Second Circuit was drawing.

Under fair use factor four, the question is not whether the defendant’s use caused **some economic injury** to the copyright holder in the abstract. The question is whether the use **harms the market for the copyrighted work in the way copyright law recognizes** — i.e., whether it **“usurps the market” by serving as a competing substitute** for the original.

The court said exactly that:

> “We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.”

So even if Kimmel’s segments made Santos look foolish and arguably made viewers less interested in buying future Cameo videos, that is not the kind of market harm factor four is concerned with. That would be harm from **criticism, ridicule, or reputational damage**, not from **substitution**.

Why the difference? Because copyright does **not** protect the author from all economic consequences of negative commentary on the work. If it did, then parody, satire, and criticism would be severely chilled. The Supreme Court in *Campbell* made that point, saying that the market for “critical reviews or lampoons” is not a potential licensing market that copyright owners are entitled to control.

So the answer to your last question is basically yes:

- **Copyright factor four protects against substitution-based market injury**  
- **It does not protect against loss in value caused by parody, criticism, or reputational embarrassment**

That is why the court could accept that Santos might be harmed in a broader sense, while still finding **no market harm within the meaning of fair use** on the face of the complaint. The injury alleged was not “this use replaced my videos”; it was “this use mocked my videos and made them less appealing.” Copyright law treats those differently.

### Why did the court avoid the Copyright Act preemption issue and instead reject the state-law claims on the merits?
Question:
On the state-law claims, the district court apparently held that the contract-based claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, but the Second Circuit says it 'need not resolve this issue' because the claims fail anyway under state law. Is that a common move for appellate courts? Also, on the direct contract claim, the court says Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and was not an intended third-party beneficiary under Illinois law. On the implied-contract claim, it switches to New York law and says there were no sufficiently pleaded essential terms or meeting of the minds. Why were different states' laws governing those two contract theories, and what would Santos have needed to allege to survive dismissal on either theory?
Answer:
Yes — that is a fairly common appellate move.

An appellate court may affirm on **any ground supported by the record**, even if the district court relied on a different rationale. So if the panel can dispose of the state-law claims on ordinary Rule 12(b)(6) grounds, it often will not reach a harder or more controversial issue like Copyright Act preemption. That is exactly what the Second Circuit did here: it said it “need not resolve” preemption because, even assuming the claims were not preempted, they still failed under state contract law.

## Why different state laws?

Because the two contract theories were treated as **different claims with different governing sources of law**:

1. **Direct breach of contract claim**  
   This claim was based on the **Cameo Terms of Service**. The court therefore applied the law governing that agreement — the opinion says **Illinois law governs the Terms of Service**.

2. **Implied-contract claim**  
   This was not a claim that Santos himself was a party to the Terms of Service. Instead, it was a separate theory that the defendants had an implied-in-fact obligation. The court analyzed that theory under **New York law**, which it says governs that claim.

So the difference is not that the court “switched” arbitrarily; it was addressing **two different legal theories**, each governed by the law the court identified as applicable to that theory.

## Why the direct contract claim failed

The court’s point is straightforward: **Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service**. Under Illinois law, there is a “strong presumption against” treating a nonparty as an intended third-party beneficiary. The contract must show that intent “so strongly as to be practically an express declaration.”

So to survive dismissal, Santos would have needed to allege facts showing either:

- he was actually a party to the contract, or
- the Terms of Service **clearly and expressly** intended to give him enforceable rights as a third-party beneficiary.

The opinion notes the opposite problem: the Terms of Service had **express third-party beneficiary language in other provisions**, which undercuts any claim that the relevant provisions silently conferred rights on Santos.

## Why the implied-contract claim failed

Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract still requires the usual contract basics: **essential terms** and a **meeting of the minds**.

The court held Santos did not plead either:

- he did not allege the **essential terms** of the supposed implied contract in nonconclusory form, including the specific provisions allegedly breached; and
- he did not plausibly allege a mutual understanding that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service.

So to survive dismissal, Santos would have needed to plead facts showing, at minimum:

- what the implied agreement actually was,
- who supposedly assented to it,
- what specific obligations were included,
- and facts supporting a real mutual understanding, not just a bare conclusion that a contract existed.

## Bottom line

The Second Circuit avoided the preemption question because it could affirm on a narrower, easier ground: the state-law claims failed on the merits anyway. And the two contract theories were analyzed under different state laws because they were different claims tied to different asserted contractual relationships.

### Why wasn't Santos's fraudulent inducement claim enough if he was tricked into making the videos?
Question:
The fraud claim is where I'm most lost. The court says Santos failed to allege any 'actual out-of-pocket loss' as required by New York law. But if he only made the videos because Kimmel misrepresented who he was and how the videos would be used, isn't that a concrete injury? Santos gave his labor, time, and performance in exchange for a transaction he says was procured by fraud. Why doesn't that count as pecuniary loss? Is this because New York fraud damages are limited to direct financial loss actually caused by the misrepresentation, and not things like benefit-of-the-bargain, reputational injury, or downstream harm from publication?
Answer:
Under New York law, **fraudulent inducement is not enough by itself**. The plaintiff must also plead and prove **actual, pecuniary out-of-pocket loss** caused by the misrepresentation. That is the rule the Second Circuit cites here from **Lama Holding** and **Connaughton**.

So your instinct is partly right in a factual sense — Santos says he was tricked into making the videos — but **New York fraud law does not treat “I was induced to do the transaction” as the damage element by itself**. The injury must be a **financial loss**, not just that the plaintiff acted under false pretenses.

That is why the court says Santos’s claim failed. In the opinion, the panel does **not** identify any allegation that Santos:

- paid money out of pocket,
- transferred money or property,
- gave up a concrete economic opportunity,
- or otherwise suffered a measurable pecuniary loss from the deception.

Instead, the complaint apparently alleged that he **performed the Cameo videos** and then objected to how they were used. But under New York fraud doctrine, **the value of the performance itself is not automatically the fraud damages measure** unless the plaintiff can tie it to a real economic loss. The court’s point is that the fraud claim could not rest on the idea that “I would not have done the work if I had known the truth” without a corresponding out-of-pocket loss.

This is also why your suggested categories mostly do not work here:

- **Benefit-of-the-bargain damages** are generally **not** the New York fraud measure.
- **Reputational injury** is not the out-of-pocket pecuniary loss New York requires for fraud.
- **Downstream harm from publication** may matter in some other theory, but the court held Santos’s fraud claim still lacked the required out-of-pocket loss.

So the short answer is: **being tricked into entering the transaction is the inducement; it is not, by itself, the damages element.** New York fraud law is narrower than “I was deceived and therefore hurt.” It requires a **specific, actual financial loss**, and the Second Circuit held Santos did not plead one.
