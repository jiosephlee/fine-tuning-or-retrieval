Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: Fraudulent Inducement, Disposition, and the Opinion’s Practical Significance

## Santos’s fraudulent inducement theory based on misrepresentations used to obtain the videos

Santos’s remaining state-law claim was for fraudulent inducement. His theory was straightforward: Kimmel and the other defendants allegedly used fake names and false identities to induce Santos to create personalized Cameo videos, and then used those videos for a televised mocking segment. In other words, Santos tried to recast the defendants’ deceptive requests as actionable fraud.

The court treated that claim as distinct from the copyright issues, but it did not accept that the alleged deception, by itself, satisfied the elements of fraud under New York law. The complaint alleged that Santos was tricked into making the videos, but a fraud claim still requires more than deception in the abstract. It must also allege a legally cognizable injury caused by that deception.

## New York’s out-of-pocket loss rule for fraud damages

The key doctrinal point is New York’s “out-of-pocket” rule. Under New York law, fraud damages are not measured by disappointed expectations, reputational injury, or the defendant’s gain. Instead, the plaintiff must allege actual pecuniary loss caused by the fraud. That means a concrete economic loss—money or property that the plaintiff lost because of the misrepresentation.

The opinion cites New York Court of Appeals authority for that proposition and uses it as the basis for affirming dismissal. This matters because it limits fraud claims to compensable financial injuries rather than broader grievances about being embarrassed, mocked, or manipulated.

## Why Santos failed to plead actual pecuniary loss caused by the alleged fraud

The Second Circuit agreed with the district court that Santos did not plead the kind of out-of-pocket loss New York requires. Even accepting that the defendants misrepresented who they were when they requested the Cameo videos, the complaint did not plausibly allege that Santos lost money or suffered another measurable economic injury as a result.

That distinction is central to the court’s analysis. Santos’s real complaint was that the defendants humiliated him by turning his videos into material for ridicule on Jimmy Kimmel Live!. But the complaint did not identify a financial loss flowing from the deception itself. Without that, the fraud claim could not survive.

The court’s treatment of the claim also shows the difference between the existence of misleading conduct and the presence of a legally actionable fraud claim. A deceptive setup may be offensive or embarrassing, but New York law still requires a pleaded economic injury. The opinion therefore rejects any attempt to transform a reputational or dignitary harm into fraud damages without a showing of actual monetary loss.

## The court’s affirmance of dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim

For that reason, the panel affirmed dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim. It did so without needing to revisit the Copyright Act preemption issues the district court had discussed with respect to the contract claims. The result was simple: Santos’s fraud theory failed on the merits of pleading because the complaint did not allege out-of-pocket loss.

This aspect of the opinion is a useful reminder that federal courts can dispose of state-law claims at the pleading stage when a required element is missing from the complaint. Even if the alleged facts sound unfair, the claim still must satisfy the governing state-law elements.

## The final disposition: affirmance of the district court’s judgment in full

The court’s bottom-line disposition was complete affirmance. Santos lost on every claim. The copyright claims were barred by fair use, the contract-based state claims failed to state a claim, and the fraudulent inducement claim failed for lack of pleaded pecuniary loss.

The court ends by saying it considered Santos’s remaining arguments and found them meritless. That closing sentence signals that no issue raised on appeal changed the result.

## How the opinion ties together copyright fair use and state-law pleading rules

The opinion is especially instructive because it blends two different kinds of dismissal analysis. On the copyright side, the court applied fair use at the pleading stage, showing that a complaint can be dismissed when the alleged facts themselves establish a strong fair-use defense. On the state-law side, the court required each claim to meet its own doctrinal elements, including contractual privity or third-party beneficiary status, a meeting of the minds for implied contract, and out-of-pocket loss for fraud.

Taken together, the opinion teaches that creative pleading cannot avoid substantive limits. If the real grievance is criticism, parody, or mockery, copyright law may allow the defendant’s use as fair. And if the plaintiff tries to repackage the same conduct as contract or fraud, state-law doctrines may still block recovery unless the complaint pleads the specific elements those claims require.

## The significance of the case for expressive reuse in comedy, criticism, and commentary

The decision has practical significance for comedy and commentary. The court emphasized that the challenged use was transformative because the television segment used the videos to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. That framing placed the case within the long-recognized space for parody, criticism, and satire.

The opinion also reinforces that the copyright owner’s own subjective intention does not control fair use. What matters is how a reasonable observer understands the secondary use. For creators and broadcasters, the case suggests that expressive reuse may be protected even when the original material was produced through some deceptive setup, so long as the final use is transformative and does not substitute for the original market.

## The case’s lesson on pleading-stage fair use in the Second Circuit

The Second Circuit also made clear that fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss when the complaint and judicially noticeable materials provide enough to evaluate the defense. That is significant because plaintiffs often hope to delay fair-use adjudication until summary judgment or trial. This opinion shows that, at least in the Second Circuit, a complaint can be dismissed if it establishes fair use on its face.

For students, that is an important pleading lesson. A copyright complaint is not insulated from an affirmative defense when the plaintiff’s own allegations make the defense apparent.

## The difference between injury to dignity or reputation and compensable market or out-of-pocket loss

Finally, the case highlights a recurring theme in civil litigation: not every injury is legally compensable in the same way. Santos may have suffered embarrassment or reputational harm from being ridiculed on television. But embarrassment is not the same as market harm under copyright law, and it is not the same as out-of-pocket loss under New York fraud law.

That distinction drives the entire opinion. The copyright claims failed because the defendants’ use was transformative commentary, not market substitution. The fraud claim failed because the complaint did not allege actual pecuniary loss. In both settings, the court insisted on the legal measure of injury that the doctrine requires, rather than the broader sense of unfairness that Santos’s allegations evoke.
