Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: The Remaining Fair Use Factors and Why Copyright Claims Failed at the Pleading Stage

## Santos’s bad-faith argument based on Kimmel’s use of false identities

After addressing the transformative character of the use, the court turned to Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s use of false names to obtain the Cameo videos showed bad faith and therefore should defeat fair use. This was an important move by Santos because he tried to recast the case as one about deception, not commentary. But the court treated the deception as legally insufficient to undo the fair use defense. The key point was that the complaint itself described the defendants’ purpose as satirical criticism: the segments were designed to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Once the alleged use is understood that way, the fact that the request was made under false identities does not by itself convert the use into infringement.

## The court’s treatment of Harper & Row’s “good faith and fair dealing” language

Santos relied on language from Harper & Row stating that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” The Second Circuit acknowledged that the Supreme Court had said this, but it also noted that later cases had moved away from treating good faith as a separate, controlling requirement. In other words, the court did not read Harper & Row to create a freestanding rule that any deception automatically defeats fair use.

That mattered because Santos was asking the court to treat Kimmel’s false identities as dispositive. The court refused to do that. Instead, it emphasized the ordinary fair use inquiry: whether the challenged use is transformative and whether it competes with the original in a way copyright law cares about. The court also observed that the complaint did not plausibly suggest an attempt to exploit Santos’s videos as a substitute product. Rather, it suggested criticism and commentary. So even if the defendants used deception to obtain the videos, that fact did not change the overall fair use analysis.

## Why alleged deception did not defeat fair use where the use was for criticism rather than market substitution

The opinion makes a useful distinction between deceptive conduct and copyright harm. Not every dishonest method of obtaining a work creates an infringing use. Copyright law is concerned with unauthorized copying that invades the copyright holder’s protected interests, especially by replacing the original in the market. Here, the complaint itself described the defendants as using the videos in a mocking segment whose purpose was to criticize Santos. That made the use closer to satire or commentary than to market substitution.

The court therefore treated the alleged deception as irrelevant to the central fair use question: whether the segments were new expressive uses with a different purpose. Because the answer was yes, bad faith did not undo the defense.

## The second factor: the nature of the copyrighted works

The court also said Santos did not meaningfully challenge the District Court’s analysis of the remaining factors, including the second factor, which looks to the nature of the copyrighted work. This factor asks whether the original work is more creative or more factual, and how close it is to the core of copyright protection. The court did not dwell on this point in detail, which itself is telling: the case did not turn on some difficult factual dispute about the artistic character of the videos.

Instead, the court noted a basic fact established by the copyright registrations: the videos were published on the same day they were created. That mattered because it undercut any suggestion that the work was treated as unpublished in a way that would strengthen Santos’s position under the second factor. The court’s brief treatment shows that, at least on this record, the second factor did not move the analysis away from fair use.

## The significance of the copyright registrations showing same-day publication

The court said it could take judicial notice of the copyright registrations, and those registrations showed the videos were published the same day they were created. That detail matters because publication status can affect fair use analysis, especially when a plaintiff argues that an unpublished work deserves stronger protection. If a work has already been published, the author has already exercised the right to decide whether and how to release it.

Here, same-day publication meant the videos were not being shielded as withheld, unpublished materials. The court did not need additional factual development to understand that point. The registrations were enough. This is a good example of how a court deciding a Rule 12(b)(6) motion can rely on materials properly before it to assess fair use.

## The third factor: the amount and substantiality of the use in the context of commentary and criticism

The opinion does not linger on the third factor, but it signals why Santos’s argument was weak. The third factor asks how much of the copyrighted work was used, both quantitatively and qualitatively. In a parody or critical commentary case, taking the whole work or enough of it to make the audience recognize the target can sometimes be reasonable. The question is not simply whether a lot was taken, but whether the amount taken was appropriate to the new expressive purpose.

The court’s citation to cases allowing fair use to be decided by comparing “the original version” and the challenged segments reflects that approach. It suggests that no deeper factual inquiry was necessary because the videos and the aired segments themselves showed the relationship between the works. The complaint and the incorporated materials were enough for the court to conclude that the use fell within fair use, even without a developed factual record.

## The fourth factor: market harm and the distinction between criticism that devalues a work and substitution that usurps its market

The most important remaining factor was the fourth: the effect of the use on the potential market for the copyrighted work. Santos argued, in substance, that the broadcast segments harmed the value of his videos. But the court drew a sharp line between two different kinds of harm. One is devaluation through criticism, parody, or ridicule; the other is market substitution, where the second work acts as a competing replacement for the first.

Only the second kind is the kind copyright law is concerned with here. The court quoted its own precedent to say that the question is not whether the new work damages the market for the original by mocking it, but whether it “usurps the market” by serving as a competing substitute. That distinction is central to fair use. A critical review may make a work less appealing, but that does not mean it invades the copyright holder’s protected market.

## Why parody, lampooning, or critical commentary ordinarily does not invade a cognizable licensing market

The court also relied on the Supreme Court’s teaching that copyright owners ordinarily do not have a cognizable licensing market for critical reviews or lampoons of their own works. That is because copyright does not give the owner a right to control all commentary about the work. If it did, criticism itself could be converted into an exclusive licensing market, which would undercut fair use.

This point explains why the court rejected Santos’s attempt to frame the broadcast as market injury. The segments may have mocked his videos, but mockery is not the same as substitution. The court treated the alleged “harm” as the ordinary effect of criticism, not the kind of market displacement the fourth factor targets.

## The court’s reliance on the complaint itself to find no plausible market usurpation

A notable feature of the opinion is that the court said the complaint itself made clear that Santos had not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor. That is why the case could be resolved on a motion to dismiss. The complaint described the defendants’ purpose as sarcastic criticism and commentary. It did not plausibly allege that viewers were being offered a substitute for Santos’s videos, or that the segments were occupying the same market as the originals.

This is what the court meant when it said that “on the face of Santos’s complaint” there was no market usurpation. The pleading itself established the nature of the use, and that nature was inconsistent with the kind of competitive substitution needed to defeat fair use.

## The conclusion that fair use barred the copyright claims even on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion

Putting the factors together, the court concluded that fair use was established well enough on the pleadings to support dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). That is an important procedural point. Fair use is ordinarily a fact-sensitive defense, but the Second Circuit has repeatedly held that it can be resolved at the motion-to-dismiss stage when the complaint and materials properly before the court leave no plausible room for a different result.

Here, the court said it only needed to compare the original videos with the aired segments to answer the fair use question. Because the complaint and incorporated materials showed a transformative, critical use that did not plausibly usurp Santos’s market, the copyright claims failed as a matter of law. The result was affirmance without discovery or trial, showing how a well-pleaded complaint can sometimes defeat itself when its own allegations establish the affirmative defense.
