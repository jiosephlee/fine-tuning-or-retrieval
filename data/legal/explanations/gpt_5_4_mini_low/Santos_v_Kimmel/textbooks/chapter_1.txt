Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Factual Setting, Claims, and Appellate Posture

## The Cameo transactions

This appeal grows out of a fairly simple factual sequence, but one that raises several different legal questions. Former Congressman George Santos used the Cameo platform to create personalized video messages for paying users. Jimmy Kimmel, however, did not request those videos under his own name. According to the complaint, he used various fictitious names to submit the requests, and Santos fulfilled each one. The videos were thus created in response to individualized requests and delivered through Cameo in the ordinary way.

That factual detail matters because Santos’s theory was not merely that his videos were copied, but that they were obtained through deceptive requests and then reused in a different setting. The complaint framed the videos as “Works” protected by copyright and also treated the Cameo transactions as the basis for contract-based claims. At the pleading stage, the court assumed the factual allegations were true, but the legal effect of those facts was disputed.

## The challenged use

After Santos created the videos, Kimmel aired them on Jimmy Kimmel Live!, ABC’s late-night show, as part of a recurring mock segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The opinion describes the segment as comedic and mocking. In other words, the broadcasted use was not simply a retransmission of the Cameo videos; it was a separate television presentation that used the videos for a satirical or critical purpose.

That distinction sits at the center of the copyright dispute. Santos claimed that the broadcast on JKL infringed his copyrights in the videos. The defendants responded that their use was protected by fair use because the television segment transformed the videos into commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The Second Circuit ultimately agreed with that view, but the court’s later fair use analysis builds from this factual setting.

## The defendants

Santos sued several related defendants: Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network that airs Jimmy Kimmel Live!, and The Walt Disney Company, ABC’s parent company. The opinion treats these defendants together because the alleged conduct arose from the airing of the videos on the show.

For purposes of the appellate posture, the important point is that the case was dismissed before discovery. The court therefore evaluated whether Santos’s complaint, taken as true, stated any claim on which relief could be granted against these defendants.

## Santos’s causes of action

Santos brought both federal copyright claims and state law claims. His copyright claims alleged infringement arising from the airing of the Cameo videos on JKL. He also asserted state law theories, including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The state law claims were tied to the Cameo Terms of Service and to Santos’s contention that the defendants wrongfully procured and used the videos. The complaint alleged, in substance, that the defendants used the Cameo platform in a way that violated legal duties owed to Santos. The district court rejected those theories, concluding that the contract claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficiently pled, and that the fraud claim failed for lack of an adequately alleged out-of-pocket loss.

## The district court’s disposition

The United States District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. A Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal means the court concluded that, even taking the factual allegations as true, the complaint did not state a legally viable claim.

As to copyright, the district court held that the defendants’ use of the videos was fair use. As to the state law claims, the court held that the contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise failed to state a claim, and that the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not adequately allege actual monetary loss.

The Second Circuit reviewed that ruling and affirmed.

## The issues on appeal

On appeal, Santos challenged the district court’s dismissal of each category of claims. The central copyright issue was whether fair use barred the infringement claims at the pleading stage. The state law issues were whether the contract-based claims escaped Copyright Act preemption and, if not, whether they stated viable claims under the governing state law. Santos also challenged the dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim.

The court therefore had to address both the substance of the fair use analysis and the pleading sufficiency of the noncopyright theories. In practical terms, this meant deciding whether the complaint itself contained enough facts to allow any of Santos’s claims to proceed to discovery.

## Standard of review on appeal

The Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal de novo. De novo review means the appellate court gave no deference to the district court’s legal conclusions and independently examined whether the complaint stated a claim. This is the ordinary standard for reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal.

The court cited its usual formulation: it considers the allegations in the complaint and asks whether, accepting them as true, they plausibly support a claim for relief. Because fair use can sometimes be resolved on the pleadings, the court also treated the copyright issue as amenable to dismissal if the complaint itself established the defense.

## What the court may consider on a Rule 12(b)(6) appeal

The opinion also explains what materials may be considered when reviewing a motion-to-dismiss ruling. The court may look not only at the complaint’s factual allegations, but also at documents attached to the complaint as exhibits and documents incorporated by reference into the complaint. That matters because copyright cases often turn on the copyrighted works themselves, and those works may be attached or incorporated in the pleadings.

The court further noted that it may take judicial notice of copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office’s registry. That allowed the court to consider the registration information without converting the motion to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment. In this case, the registrations helped establish facts relevant to the fair use analysis, including publication timing.

Taken together, these procedural rules explain why the Second Circuit was able to resolve the case at the pleading stage. The court did not need discovery to decide whether the complaint, together with properly considered materials, showed that fair use barred the copyright claims and that the state law claims failed on the face of the pleadings.
