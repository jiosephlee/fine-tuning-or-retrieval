Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Fair Use at the Pleading Stage, Revisited: Why Santos v. Kimmel Makes Early Dismissal Look Increasingly Normal

## Why the case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is not just another celebrity copyright dispute. It is another reminder that, in the right case, fair use can be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion — before discovery, before expert battles, and before the litigation costs start to do the work that often shapes copyright settlements. That matters a lot. When the allegedly infringing use is a clear-cut parody, critique, or commentary, the court is increasingly willing to say that the complaint itself supplies enough context to decide fair use as a matter of law.

That approach is plaintiff-unfriendly in the obvious sense. But it also reflects a strong Second Circuit instinct: copyright should not be allowed to impose the expense of full-blown litigation where the complaint and the works themselves already reveal that the defendant’s use is the kind the fair use doctrine is designed to protect.

## The court’s reasoning

The facts made the case unusually clean. Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, requested personalized Cameo videos from George Santos. Santos delivered the videos. Kimmel then aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking segment called “Will Santos Say It?” Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state-law claims. The district court dismissed, and the Second Circuit affirmed.

On the copyright claim, the panel’s analysis stays close to the now-familiar fair use framework: purpose and character, nature of the work, amount used, and market effect. The heart of the decision is the first factor, and the court had little trouble concluding that the use was transformative. Kimmel’s broadcast was not just republishing the videos for the same audience and purpose. It used them to comment on “the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” That is a classic transformative purpose: criticism through repurposing.

Santos’s main argument was that Kimmel could not claim transformation because he, Santos, allegedly created the videos for the same purpose — to be entertaining or inspirational. The court rejected that framing by emphasizing that transformation is judged from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not from the copyright holder’s subjective intent. That point is important. Copyright plaintiffs often try to collapse the original creator’s motive and the secondary user’s meaning into one another, but *Santos* refuses to let the original creator define the transformative inquiry so easily. The court also rejected a bad-faith theory based on Kimmel’s deceptive requests, noting both the Supreme Court’s retreat from older “good faith” language and the absence of any plausible claim that defendants were trying to supplant Santos’s own market for the videos.

The other fair use factors fell quickly. The court treated the videos as published on the day they were created, based on the copyright registrations and the complaint. And on market harm, the court reiterated a familiar and plaintiff-defeating point: fair use does not turn on whether parody or criticism hurts the value of the original in some abstract sense, but whether it acts as a market substitute. A mocking television segment is not a competing product for personalized Cameo videos, and the court had little difficulty saying so at the pleading stage.

## What is doctrinally notable

What makes *Santos* significant is less the outcome than the procedural posture. The Second Circuit has said before that fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss where it is “so clearly established” on the face of the complaint. *TCA Television* said as much, and later cases like *Cariou*, *Authors Guild*, *Swatch*, and *Hachette* have given the doctrine a more pragmatic, use-focused edge. *Santos* fits comfortably within that line, but it also reinforces the emerging pattern.

The court’s willingness to rely on the works themselves, the pleadings, and judicially noticeable copyright registrations shows that fair use is increasingly being treated as a question that can be decided early when the creative mismatch is obvious. That is especially true in commentary, parody, and criticism cases, where the secondary use is self-evidently non-substitutive and the market-harm argument tends to be doctrinally thin.

At the same time, the decision is careful not to announce a broader rule that all fair use disputes belong on the pleadings. The opinion stays tethered to the particular facts: the videos, the broadcast segment, and the complaint’s own description of the original works’ tone. In other words, this is not a wholesale procedural revolution. It is an application of established doctrine in a case that practically invited early dismissal.

## The downstream effects

For plaintiffs, the lesson is clear: if the complaint itself reveals a paradigmatic parody or commentary use, a copyright case may not survive long enough to reach discovery. That can be a significant barrier, because discovery is often where plaintiffs hope to develop evidence about intent, audience overlap, licensing, and market effects. *Santos* suggests that where the core fair use story is already visible from the pleadings, the court may not let plaintiffs use discovery as a fishing expedition in hopes of changing that story.

For defendants, the opinion is another strong citation for early motions to dismiss in obvious fair use cases, especially where the defendant’s use is political satire, criticism, or media commentary. And for platforms and media companies, the case reinforces the value of creating a record that makes the secondary purpose legible on the face of the complaint.

The broader doctrinal trend is also worth watching. The Second Circuit is not saying that fair use has become easy. But it is saying, more confidently than before, that when the nature of the use is plain and the complaint supplies the necessary context, courts need not postpone the fair use question until summary judgment. That may reduce litigation costs and discourage weak infringement suits. It may also, as critics will argue, reduce plaintiffs’ ability to test fair use claims through discovery. *Santos* lands firmly on the side of efficiency — and on the side of treating obvious parody and commentary as the kind of use the Copyright Act is supposed to leave alone.



## From Warhol to Kimmel: Objective Purpose, Not Authorial Intent, Drives Transformative Use

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is a useful reminder that fair use doctrine remains heavily shaped by the Supreme Court’s recent *Warhol* decision. The panel held that Jimmy Kimmel’s broadcast of George Santos’s Cameo videos was fair use at the pleading stage, and the key move was conceptual: the court rejected Santos’s attempt to collapse the analysis into the creator’s claimed intent. What matters, the panel said, is not what Santos says he meant the videos to do, but what a reasonable observer would understand their purpose to be.

That matters well beyond this case. If courts continue to take that approach, then the first fair use factor will increasingly turn on how the secondary use functions in context — especially when the second use is satire, criticism, or commentary — rather than on the copyright holder’s preferred framing of the original work.

## The court’s central move

Santos argued that Kimmel’s use was not transformative because Kimmel allegedly “instigated” the creation of the videos and therefore was the “designer” of their original purpose. The panel was unmoved. It emphasized that Santos’s own complaint described the Cameo videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while Kimmel’s use was to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Those are different purposes, the court concluded, and the difference is judged objectively.

That tracks the Supreme Court’s instruction in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* that the “subjective intent of the user” does not control the purpose inquiry. The Second Circuit’s opinion is notable for how directly it applies that principle in an especially fact-bound setting. Santos tried to characterize the initial and secondary uses as essentially the same because both involved his speaking on camera. But the panel focused instead on audience-facing meaning: the videos as originally delivered were earnest and inspirational; the television segments were mocking and critical. That is enough, the court held, to make the first factor favor fair use.

## Why the case is doctrinally important

The opinion reinforces a distinction that is easy to blur in copyright cases: the creator’s private intent is not the same thing as the work’s purpose as perceived in the marketplace of communication. That distinction matters a great deal in the era of reposts, clips, remixes, and reaction content. Courts are increasingly being asked to decide whether a later use merely republishes a work or whether it repurposes it for criticism, commentary, or parody. *Santos* suggests that when the later use plainly changes the work’s communicative function, a court need not accept the original author’s assertion that the two uses are the same.

There is some tension here, though, and the opinion does not fully resolve it. *Warhol* cautioned against overreading transformativeness in a way that would swallow the copyright owner’s licensing market, especially where the secondary use shares the same “purpose” as the original. But satire and commentary remain classic fair use uses precisely because they are socially and commercially different from the original. *Santos* fits comfortably within that tradition. The television segments were not substitutes for Santos’s Cameo messages; they were jokes about them. That is the sort of use courts have long treated as paradigmatically transformative.

## Pleading-stage fair use, and its limits

Another practical point: the Second Circuit again showed that fair use can be decided on a motion to dismiss when the complaint itself supplies the necessary facts. That is significant because it gives defendants an early exit in cases where the alleged infringement is obvious but the fair-use defense is equally apparent from the pleadings.

At the same time, this kind of dismissal works best where the secondary use is visible in the complaint and the contrast between the two works is stark. The court relied on the videos and the JKL segments themselves, plus the complaint’s own description of their different tones and purposes. In a closer case — for example, where the secondary use is more mixed, or where market harm and context are less clear — early dismissal will be harder to justify.

## The downstream effect

The larger takeaway is that criticism and commentary continue to occupy a privileged position in fair use doctrine, and *Santos* confirms that *Warhol* has not dislodged that baseline. If anything, the case suggests that *Warhol* is being read as a discipline on overbroad transformative-use arguments, not as a retreat from the core rule that uses aimed at criticism or ridicule are often transformative.

For creators, that means the copyright line remains thin when the later user is clearly speaking about the work rather than simply substituting for it. For litigants, it means arguments about subjective purpose — whether the author intended a joke, whether the later user “set up” the work, whether the creator and critic are both monetizing attention — may matter less than the objective communicative function of the secondary use.

That is a fairly modest doctrinal move, but an important one. *Santos v. Kimmel* does not reinvent fair use. It does, however, show the Second Circuit’s post-*Warhol* method: look to how the use reads to a reasonable observer, ask whether the later work adds criticism or commentary, and resist efforts to collapse that inquiry into the copyright owner’s own preferred account of what the original was “for.”



## Bad Faith Without Consequence? *Santos v. Kimmel* and the Declining Role of Deception in Fair Use

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is striking less for its outcome than for what it says about deception in fair use analysis. Former Rep. George Santos alleged that Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious identities, tricked him into creating Cameo videos that were later aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking segment. That factual setup all but invites a bad-faith argument: Kimmel lied to obtain the videos, then monetized them in a televised skit. But the panel treated that deception as beside the point. Once the court concluded that the use was plainly critical, transformative, and non-substitutive, the “bad faith” issue largely fell away.

That matters because *Santos* fits into a broader pattern in modern fair use doctrine. Courts increasingly treat fair use as a use-based inquiry focused on purpose, transformativeness, and market substitution, not as a generalized fairness test. To the extent *Harper & Row* once suggested that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” *Santos* confirms that that language no longer does much work, at least where the accused use is obviously commentary or parody.

## The court’s path: deception acknowledged, then sidelined

The panel did not deny that Kimmel used “false representations” to induce Santos to make the videos. But it refused to treat that as disabling fair use. The opinion’s reasoning is simple: whatever Kimmel’s conduct in obtaining the videos, the relevant secondary use was commentary on Santos’s willingness to “say absurd things for money,” not a market substitute for Santos’s original expressive purpose. The court emphasized that fair use turns on how a “reasonable observer” would understand the original and secondary works, not on the subjective intent of the uploader or the copyright holder.

That move is doctrinally important. It effectively relocates deception from the center of the fair use inquiry to the periphery. Kimmel’s trickery may have been unseemly, but the court viewed the heart of the dispute as the later broadcast’s critical function. The panel also noted that the complaint itself framed the videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” which made it difficult to say the JKL segment was competing with Santos’s intended expressive market. On that view, the false identities were a means of acquisition, not a feature that altered the legal character of the use.

## What happened to *Harper & Row*?

The opinion’s treatment of *Harper & Row* is telling. The Supreme Court’s statement that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing” has long been cited by copyright owners trying to argue that misrepresentation or piracy should defeat a fair use defense. But the Second Circuit reads that proposition narrowly, and with evident caution. It says the Supreme Court has since “distanced itself” from the statement, and, in any event, that the facts here do not show an effort to supplant Santos’s commercially valuable right.

That is consistent with the broader post-*Campbell* and post-*Warhol* trajectory. The Supreme Court has steadily narrowed the role of broad equitable notions in fair use, insisting instead on the statutory factors and especially the purpose-and-character inquiry. In that framework, “bad faith” is not an independent veto. At most, it may be relevant when it helps reveal that the user’s purpose was exploitative in a way that weighs against transformativeness or market substitution. But where the later use is plainly critical, the deception in obtaining the source material is unlikely to change the result.

## A narrower, more doctrinally disciplined fair use test

From a doctrinal perspective, the panel’s approach has a certain rigor to it. Fair use is supposed to ask whether the challenged use adds something new, serves a different purpose, or leaves the original market unharmed. If courts let acquisition misconduct itself control the outcome, they risk collapsing fair use into a free-floating morality test. That would be hard to reconcile with the Supreme Court’s repeated emphasis that fair use is a flexible but structured doctrine.

Still, the opinion’s treatment of deception may leave some readers uneasy. There is a difference between saying bad faith is not dispositive and saying it is irrelevant. In media-sting and undercover-acquisition cases, the way material is obtained can matter in at least two ways. First, it may illuminate the user’s purpose: was the work acquired to criticize, or to free-ride? Second, it can affect the market analysis if the plaintiff can plausibly identify a licensing or commercial interest in controlling that kind of use. *Santos* concludes neither was true here. But the opinion offers less guidance on the hard cases, where the line between commentary and opportunistic exploitation is murkier.

## Downstream effects for stings, hidden cameras, and platform trickery

The practical significance of *Santos* may be felt beyond celebrity comedy. Reporters, documentarians, activists, and creators often rely on deception, pretext, or undercover methods to obtain source material. So do platform users who exploit terms of service or pseudonymity to collect content for criticism. *Santos* suggests that, at least in the Second Circuit, the mere fact of deceptive acquisition will not defeat fair use if the eventual use is clearly transformative and non-substitutive.

That is a meaningful signal. It reduces the leverage copyright plaintiffs may have when their complaint is really about embarrassment or manipulation rather than market displacement. It also means defendants will likely frame their conduct as commentary, criticism, or reportage more aggressively, knowing that courts may discount the procurement story unless it bears directly on the statutory factors.

At the same time, the opinion should not be read as giving a green light to deceitful collection practices. The court did not bless Kimmel’s tactics; it held only that they did not matter enough to defeat fair use on these facts. Other doctrines—contract, fraud, privacy, publicity, platform rules, or consumer protection—may still regulate deceptive conduct. Indeed, Santos’s state-law claims failed for separate reasons, but that does not mean deception is categorically consequence-free.

## The bigger doctrinal picture

*Santos v. Kimmel* is another reminder that fair use is becoming less hospitable to broad equitable objections and more anchored in objective use analysis. That trend favors defendants whose secondary use is plainly satirical, critical, or documentary. It also makes copyright doctrine more predictable, because courts can resolve more cases by asking what the later user did with the work, rather than whether the user behaved honorably in obtaining it.

But predictability comes at a cost. If bad faith no longer functions as a meaningful limiter, plaintiffs who are wronged by deception may have to look elsewhere for remedies. Copyright will not do the work. That may be the right doctrinal answer, but it narrows the space in which courts can respond to conduct that feels manipulative even when it is constitutionally or statutorily protected expressive activity.

In that sense, *Santos* is less about George Santos or Jimmy Kimmel than about the continuing retreat of fairness as an independent constraint in fair use. The Second Circuit did not say deception never matters. It said, in effect, that when the use is obviously critical and non-substitutive, deception in acquisition will not rescue a copyright claim. That is a significant statement—and one likely to echo in future disputes involving undercover methods, sting operations, and platform-mediated trickery.



# Copyright Preemption Dodged, Contract Law Enforced Strictly: The Quiet State-Law Lessons of *Santos v. Kimmel*

## Why this case matters

The headline in *Santos v. Kimmel* is fair use, but the quieter and perhaps more durable lesson is what the Second Circuit did with the state-law claims. The court declined to decide whether Santos’s contract theories were preempted by the Copyright Act and instead disposed of them on ordinary pleading and contract principles. That move matters because it shows a familiar judicial instinct in copyright-adjacent disputes: when a plaintiff tries to repackage a losing infringement claim as a platform-contract or fraud claim, courts will often answer with basic doctrine rather than reach for preemption.

The result is a useful reminder that creative pleading does not create a cause of action. If the plaintiff is not actually within the class of people the contract was designed to protect, has not alleged the terms of any real agreement, or cannot plead actual fraud damages, the case will end there.

## Why the court avoided the preemption question

The district court had held that Santos’s breach of contract and implied contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. On appeal, the Second Circuit took a narrower route. It noted the preemption argument but said it did not need to resolve it because the state-law claims failed on the merits anyway.

That is a pragmatic move, and in some ways a revealing one. Preemption questions can be messy, especially where a plaintiff invokes a platform’s terms of service rather than a standalone property right. But if the plaintiff cannot satisfy the threshold elements of contract law, a court need not decide whether copyright law also displaces the claim. The Second Circuit’s approach keeps the opinion tightly bounded: no need to expand the court’s copyright-preemption doctrine when state law already supplies a complete answer.

## The contract claims failed for straightforward reasons

Santos first tried to sue directly under Cameo’s Terms of Service. The problem was simple: he was not a party to that contract. Under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, third-party enforcement is strongly disfavored. The court emphasized that a nonparty must show a contractual intent to benefit him so clearly that it is “practically an express declaration.” Santos pointed to nothing like that. In fact, the court noted that other provisions of the same Terms of Service expressly referred to third-party beneficiaries, which cut against any suggestion that the relevant terms were meant to be enforceable by Santos.

That point is doctrinally important because it blocks a common maneuver in platform disputes. A disappointed creator may see the platform’s user agreement as a source of leverage against an end user who allegedly cheated the system. But unless the contract actually extends rights to the creator, or the agreement otherwise unmistakably intends that result, the creator is still a stranger to the bargain.

Santos’s implied-contract theory fared no better. Under New York law, the court said, he had not pleaded the essential terms of any implied-in-fact agreement, including the specific provisions allegedly breached. He also failed to allege facts showing a meeting of the minds. That is a basic but often underappreciated pleading hurdle: an implied contract is not a free-floating appeal to fairness. The plaintiff still has to describe the deal, not just the disappointment.

The Second Circuit’s treatment of the implied-contract claim is especially useful because it underscores how often these theories collapse into wishful thinking. If the complaint does not identify who promised what, when, and on what terms, a court will not infer a contract merely because the parties interacted and one side feels wronged. In the social-media and creator-economy context, that principle will likely continue to do a lot of work.

## The fraud claim failed for an equally old-fashioned reason

Santos also alleged fraudulent inducement, but the court held that he had not pleaded the requisite out-of-pocket loss under New York law. That requirement is not a technicality; it separates fraud from indignation. A plaintiff must show actual, measurable loss caused by the misrepresentation. Santos did not do that.

This is where the opinion is most instructive for litigants tempted to convert deception into damages. Even if the defendants used false names to order the videos, fraud still requires the ordinary elements of reliance and actual pecuniary injury. The court did not suggest that deceptive conduct is irrelevant. It said only that deception alone is not enough. Without pleaded economic loss, the claim fails.

That holding also helps explain why fraud theories often struggle in disputes over digital content and publicity stunts. The injury is often reputational, dignitary, or expressive rather than financial. But New York fraud law is not designed to compensate for every species of unfairness. It is aimed at money loss.

## The broader lesson for copyright plaintiffs

The practical lesson is clear: when the copyright claim fails, the fallback theories must stand on their own feet. Plaintiffs sometimes try to relabel a copyright dispute as a contract, fraud, or platform-policy case in hopes of avoiding fair use, preemption, or the limits of exclusive rights. *Santos* suggests that such efforts will be met with skepticism unless the plaintiff can plead a real contract, real assent, and real loss.

That does not mean preemption will always be irrelevant. In a different case, the Second Circuit may well have to decide how far the Copyright Act reaches when a plaintiff invokes a platform agreement that arguably adds an extra promise beyond copying itself. But *Santos* shows that courts need not rush to that question. They can often dispose of the case at the more ordinary level: who can enforce the contract, whether there was one, and whether fraud damages were actually incurred.

## Downstream effects

Expect defendants in creator-platform disputes to cite *Santos* for the proposition that contract and fraud claims cannot serve as backdoor copyright claims without proper pleading support. Expect plaintiffs, too, to read the opinion carefully, because it suggests that if they want to survive a motion to dismiss, they will need much more than allegations that someone used their content deceptively or in bad faith.

That is the real state-law lesson of *Santos v. Kimmel*: copyright doctrine may have dominated the case, but the court’s treatment of the ancillary claims reinforces a more general principle of federal civil litigation. When a claim is dressed up in the vocabulary of equity, a court will still ask for the basics—party status, assent, contractual terms, and injury. Without those, there is no case, just a grievance.
