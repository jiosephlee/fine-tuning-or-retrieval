Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Chapter 1: Factual Setting, Claims, and Appellate Posture

## The Cameo transactions

This appeal grows out of a fairly simple factual sequence, but one that raises several different legal questions. Former Congressman George Santos used the Cameo platform to create personalized video messages for paying users. Jimmy Kimmel, however, did not request those videos under his own name. According to the complaint, he used various fictitious names to submit the requests, and Santos fulfilled each one. The videos were thus created in response to individualized requests and delivered through Cameo in the ordinary way.

That factual detail matters because Santos’s theory was not merely that his videos were copied, but that they were obtained through deceptive requests and then reused in a different setting. The complaint framed the videos as “Works” protected by copyright and also treated the Cameo transactions as the basis for contract-based claims. At the pleading stage, the court assumed the factual allegations were true, but the legal effect of those facts was disputed.

## The challenged use

After Santos created the videos, Kimmel aired them on Jimmy Kimmel Live!, ABC’s late-night show, as part of a recurring mock segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The opinion describes the segment as comedic and mocking. In other words, the broadcasted use was not simply a retransmission of the Cameo videos; it was a separate television presentation that used the videos for a satirical or critical purpose.

That distinction sits at the center of the copyright dispute. Santos claimed that the broadcast on JKL infringed his copyrights in the videos. The defendants responded that their use was protected by fair use because the television segment transformed the videos into commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The Second Circuit ultimately agreed with that view, but the court’s later fair use analysis builds from this factual setting.

## The defendants

Santos sued several related defendants: Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network that airs Jimmy Kimmel Live!, and The Walt Disney Company, ABC’s parent company. The opinion treats these defendants together because the alleged conduct arose from the airing of the videos on the show.

For purposes of the appellate posture, the important point is that the case was dismissed before discovery. The court therefore evaluated whether Santos’s complaint, taken as true, stated any claim on which relief could be granted against these defendants.

## Santos’s causes of action

Santos brought both federal copyright claims and state law claims. His copyright claims alleged infringement arising from the airing of the Cameo videos on JKL. He also asserted state law theories, including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The state law claims were tied to the Cameo Terms of Service and to Santos’s contention that the defendants wrongfully procured and used the videos. The complaint alleged, in substance, that the defendants used the Cameo platform in a way that violated legal duties owed to Santos. The district court rejected those theories, concluding that the contract claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficiently pled, and that the fraud claim failed for lack of an adequately alleged out-of-pocket loss.

## The district court’s disposition

The United States District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. A Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal means the court concluded that, even taking the factual allegations as true, the complaint did not state a legally viable claim.

As to copyright, the district court held that the defendants’ use of the videos was fair use. As to the state law claims, the court held that the contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise failed to state a claim, and that the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not adequately allege actual monetary loss.

The Second Circuit reviewed that ruling and affirmed.

## The issues on appeal

On appeal, Santos challenged the district court’s dismissal of each category of claims. The central copyright issue was whether fair use barred the infringement claims at the pleading stage. The state law issues were whether the contract-based claims escaped Copyright Act preemption and, if not, whether they stated viable claims under the governing state law. Santos also challenged the dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim.

The court therefore had to address both the substance of the fair use analysis and the pleading sufficiency of the noncopyright theories. In practical terms, this meant deciding whether the complaint itself contained enough facts to allow any of Santos’s claims to proceed to discovery.

## Standard of review on appeal

The Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal de novo. De novo review means the appellate court gave no deference to the district court’s legal conclusions and independently examined whether the complaint stated a claim. This is the ordinary standard for reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal.

The court cited its usual formulation: it considers the allegations in the complaint and asks whether, accepting them as true, they plausibly support a claim for relief. Because fair use can sometimes be resolved on the pleadings, the court also treated the copyright issue as amenable to dismissal if the complaint itself established the defense.

## What the court may consider on a Rule 12(b)(6) appeal

The opinion also explains what materials may be considered when reviewing a motion-to-dismiss ruling. The court may look not only at the complaint’s factual allegations, but also at documents attached to the complaint as exhibits and documents incorporated by reference into the complaint. That matters because copyright cases often turn on the copyrighted works themselves, and those works may be attached or incorporated in the pleadings.

The court further noted that it may take judicial notice of copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office’s registry. That allowed the court to consider the registration information without converting the motion to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment. In this case, the registrations helped establish facts relevant to the fair use analysis, including publication timing.

Taken together, these procedural rules explain why the Second Circuit was able to resolve the case at the pleading stage. The court did not need discovery to decide whether the complaint, together with properly considered materials, showed that fair use barred the copyright claims and that the state law claims failed on the face of the pleadings.

# Chapter 2: Fair Use Framework and the Court’s Treatment of Transformative Purpose

## Fair Use as an Affirmative Defense

The court begins its copyright analysis by framing fair use as an affirmative defense. That matters because an affirmative defense does not deny that copying occurred; instead, it says the copying is legally excused. In other words, even if a plaintiff can show that the defendant used protected material, the defendant may still avoid liability if the use falls within the fair use doctrine. The court emphasizes the policy behind the doctrine: copyright law is meant to promote creativity, not suppress it, and fair use gives courts flexibility when rigid enforcement would interfere with commentary, criticism, and other socially valuable uses.

This framing is important to understanding why Santos’s copyright claims failed. Santos alleged that Kimmel used the Cameo videos without permission, but the court did not stop at the fact of copying. It asked whether the challenged use was the kind of use the Copyright Act permits. Because the answer was yes, the copying was not actionable.

## The Four Statutory Fair Use Factors

The court applies the familiar four-factor test from 17 U.S.C. § 107. Those factors are: the purpose and character of the use; the nature of the copyrighted work; the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and the effect of the use on the potential market for the original work. The opinion treats these factors as non-exclusive and context-sensitive, meaning no one factor automatically controls in every case.

That said, the court’s discussion shows that the first and fourth factors did most of the work here. The first factor mattered because it asks whether the defendant’s use has a different purpose or character from the original. The fourth mattered because the court saw no plausible allegation that Kimmel’s segments competed with or substituted for Santos’s Cameo videos in any relevant market. The court also noted that the videos were published the same day they were created, which supported the fair use analysis, but the heart of the decision was the first factor.

## Why Fair Use Can Be Resolved on a Motion to Dismiss

A useful procedural point in the opinion is that fair use can be decided at the pleading stage. Normally, affirmative defenses are raised later in litigation, but the Second Circuit has recognized that fair use may justify dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) when the complaint and materials properly considered on a motion to dismiss make the defense clear on their face.

That is what happened here. The court relied on the complaint, the attached materials, and judicially noticeable copyright registrations. It did not need discovery or fact-finding to see the nature of the original videos and the JKL segments. Because those materials were enough to evaluate the fair use factors, the court held that dismissal was proper even before the case reached summary judgment or trial.

## Transformative Use and the First Factor

The central question under the first factor is whether the secondary use is transformative. A use is transformative when it adds something new, with a different purpose or character, rather than merely repeating or replacing the original. The court stresses that this inquiry is especially important because fair use exists to protect new expression, criticism, commentary, and other reuses that contribute something distinct.

Here, the court concluded that Kimmel’s broadcast segments were transformative because they used Santos’s Cameo videos for a new purpose: satirical criticism and commentary. The original videos were private personalized messages sent through Cameo. Kimmel’s segments turned them into a comedy bit about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. That shift in purpose was enough to strongly support fair use.

## The Objective Nature of the Transformative Inquiry

Santos tried to defeat the transformative-use analysis by arguing that Kimmel could not claim a new purpose because he allegedly “instigated” the making of the videos and thus designed their original purpose. The court rejected that approach because the fair use inquiry is objective, not subjective. The relevant question is not what the copyright holder intended, or what the secondary user privately meant, but what a reasonable observer would understand the works to be doing.

This is where the court invokes the Supreme Court’s later decision in Warhol, which makes clear that subjective intent does not control the purpose analysis. The opinion treats this as a key correction to Santos’s theory. Even if Kimmel set the process in motion by requesting the videos under false names, the legal question remained whether the resulting broadcast use had a different character from the original videos. The court answered yes.

## The Court’s Rejection of Santos’s Subjective-Purpose Theory

Santos’s argument depended on collapsing the two uses into one. He suggested that because Kimmel caused the videos to be created, Kimmel’s later broadcast could not be transformative. But the court said that is not how fair use works. The fact that someone helped bring an original work into existence does not freeze that work’s purpose forever or prevent later commentary on it.

The court also pointed out that Santos’s own complaint undermined his theory. The complaint described the Cameo videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” That description matters because it shows what a reasonable viewer would understand the videos themselves to be. Those messages are very different from a late-night comedy segment meant to mock the speaker’s willingness to perform absurdity for money.

## Reasonable Observer Analysis of the Original Videos

The court uses a reasonable-observer lens to separate the original videos from the JKL segments. Under that lens, the original Cameo videos were straightforward personalized messages with an affirmative, encouraging tone. They were not created as self-parody or as commentary on Santos’s susceptibility to ridicule.

That matters because fair use often turns on whether the later work is doing something meaningfully different. If the original work already had the same critical or comedic function as the later work, transformation is harder to show. But here the court saw a sharp contrast: the original videos were earnest, while the broadcast segments turned them into evidence of Santos’s willingness to say almost anything for money.

## Kimmel’s New Purpose: Criticism and Commentary

The court had little trouble identifying Kimmel’s purpose. According to the complaint itself, the segments were designed to comment on Santos’s readiness to produce absurd statements for compensation. That is a paradigmatic fair use purpose. Criticism and commentary are among the core activities fair use is meant to protect, especially when the new work uses the original material as the object of the joke or critique.

The court also rejected Santos’s attempt to characterize the use as bad faith. Although older cases contain language suggesting fair use “presupposes good faith,” the court treated that idea as limited and not dispositive. More importantly, the complaint did not support a claim that the defendants were trying to replace Santos’s market for the videos. Instead, it portrayed them as engaged in sarcastic commentary. That supported, rather than defeated, fair use.

## Why the First Factor Strongly Favored Fair Use

In the end, the court held that the first factor strongly favored fair use because the use was transformative and because the surrounding allegations described a commentary-driven, satirical purpose. Once the court accepted that Kimmel’s segments repurposed the videos for criticism of Santos, the first factor became a powerful answer to the copyright claim.

The opinion’s broader lesson is that fair use analysis begins with function. A plaintiff may own the original work, but if the defendant’s use changes the work’s role in a way that adds criticism, commentary, or another distinct purpose, the law may permit it. Here, the court saw exactly that kind of change, and it was enough to support dismissal at the pleading stage.

# Chapter 3: The Remaining Fair Use Factors and Why Copyright Claims Failed at the Pleading Stage

## Santos’s bad-faith argument based on Kimmel’s use of false identities

After addressing the transformative character of the use, the court turned to Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s use of false names to obtain the Cameo videos showed bad faith and therefore should defeat fair use. This was an important move by Santos because he tried to recast the case as one about deception, not commentary. But the court treated the deception as legally insufficient to undo the fair use defense. The key point was that the complaint itself described the defendants’ purpose as satirical criticism: the segments were designed to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Once the alleged use is understood that way, the fact that the request was made under false identities does not by itself convert the use into infringement.

## The court’s treatment of Harper & Row’s “good faith and fair dealing” language

Santos relied on language from Harper & Row stating that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” The Second Circuit acknowledged that the Supreme Court had said this, but it also noted that later cases had moved away from treating good faith as a separate, controlling requirement. In other words, the court did not read Harper & Row to create a freestanding rule that any deception automatically defeats fair use.

That mattered because Santos was asking the court to treat Kimmel’s false identities as dispositive. The court refused to do that. Instead, it emphasized the ordinary fair use inquiry: whether the challenged use is transformative and whether it competes with the original in a way copyright law cares about. The court also observed that the complaint did not plausibly suggest an attempt to exploit Santos’s videos as a substitute product. Rather, it suggested criticism and commentary. So even if the defendants used deception to obtain the videos, that fact did not change the overall fair use analysis.

## Why alleged deception did not defeat fair use where the use was for criticism rather than market substitution

The opinion makes a useful distinction between deceptive conduct and copyright harm. Not every dishonest method of obtaining a work creates an infringing use. Copyright law is concerned with unauthorized copying that invades the copyright holder’s protected interests, especially by replacing the original in the market. Here, the complaint itself described the defendants as using the videos in a mocking segment whose purpose was to criticize Santos. That made the use closer to satire or commentary than to market substitution.

The court therefore treated the alleged deception as irrelevant to the central fair use question: whether the segments were new expressive uses with a different purpose. Because the answer was yes, bad faith did not undo the defense.

## The second factor: the nature of the copyrighted works

The court also said Santos did not meaningfully challenge the District Court’s analysis of the remaining factors, including the second factor, which looks to the nature of the copyrighted work. This factor asks whether the original work is more creative or more factual, and how close it is to the core of copyright protection. The court did not dwell on this point in detail, which itself is telling: the case did not turn on some difficult factual dispute about the artistic character of the videos.

Instead, the court noted a basic fact established by the copyright registrations: the videos were published on the same day they were created. That mattered because it undercut any suggestion that the work was treated as unpublished in a way that would strengthen Santos’s position under the second factor. The court’s brief treatment shows that, at least on this record, the second factor did not move the analysis away from fair use.

## The significance of the copyright registrations showing same-day publication

The court said it could take judicial notice of the copyright registrations, and those registrations showed the videos were published the same day they were created. That detail matters because publication status can affect fair use analysis, especially when a plaintiff argues that an unpublished work deserves stronger protection. If a work has already been published, the author has already exercised the right to decide whether and how to release it.

Here, same-day publication meant the videos were not being shielded as withheld, unpublished materials. The court did not need additional factual development to understand that point. The registrations were enough. This is a good example of how a court deciding a Rule 12(b)(6) motion can rely on materials properly before it to assess fair use.

## The third factor: the amount and substantiality of the use in the context of commentary and criticism

The opinion does not linger on the third factor, but it signals why Santos’s argument was weak. The third factor asks how much of the copyrighted work was used, both quantitatively and qualitatively. In a parody or critical commentary case, taking the whole work or enough of it to make the audience recognize the target can sometimes be reasonable. The question is not simply whether a lot was taken, but whether the amount taken was appropriate to the new expressive purpose.

The court’s citation to cases allowing fair use to be decided by comparing “the original version” and the challenged segments reflects that approach. It suggests that no deeper factual inquiry was necessary because the videos and the aired segments themselves showed the relationship between the works. The complaint and the incorporated materials were enough for the court to conclude that the use fell within fair use, even without a developed factual record.

## The fourth factor: market harm and the distinction between criticism that devalues a work and substitution that usurps its market

The most important remaining factor was the fourth: the effect of the use on the potential market for the copyrighted work. Santos argued, in substance, that the broadcast segments harmed the value of his videos. But the court drew a sharp line between two different kinds of harm. One is devaluation through criticism, parody, or ridicule; the other is market substitution, where the second work acts as a competing replacement for the first.

Only the second kind is the kind copyright law is concerned with here. The court quoted its own precedent to say that the question is not whether the new work damages the market for the original by mocking it, but whether it “usurps the market” by serving as a competing substitute. That distinction is central to fair use. A critical review may make a work less appealing, but that does not mean it invades the copyright holder’s protected market.

## Why parody, lampooning, or critical commentary ordinarily does not invade a cognizable licensing market

The court also relied on the Supreme Court’s teaching that copyright owners ordinarily do not have a cognizable licensing market for critical reviews or lampoons of their own works. That is because copyright does not give the owner a right to control all commentary about the work. If it did, criticism itself could be converted into an exclusive licensing market, which would undercut fair use.

This point explains why the court rejected Santos’s attempt to frame the broadcast as market injury. The segments may have mocked his videos, but mockery is not the same as substitution. The court treated the alleged “harm” as the ordinary effect of criticism, not the kind of market displacement the fourth factor targets.

## The court’s reliance on the complaint itself to find no plausible market usurpation

A notable feature of the opinion is that the court said the complaint itself made clear that Santos had not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor. That is why the case could be resolved on a motion to dismiss. The complaint described the defendants’ purpose as sarcastic criticism and commentary. It did not plausibly allege that viewers were being offered a substitute for Santos’s videos, or that the segments were occupying the same market as the originals.

This is what the court meant when it said that “on the face of Santos’s complaint” there was no market usurpation. The pleading itself established the nature of the use, and that nature was inconsistent with the kind of competitive substitution needed to defeat fair use.

## The conclusion that fair use barred the copyright claims even on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion

Putting the factors together, the court concluded that fair use was established well enough on the pleadings to support dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). That is an important procedural point. Fair use is ordinarily a fact-sensitive defense, but the Second Circuit has repeatedly held that it can be resolved at the motion-to-dismiss stage when the complaint and materials properly before the court leave no plausible room for a different result.

Here, the court said it only needed to compare the original videos with the aired segments to answer the fair use question. Because the complaint and incorporated materials showed a transformative, critical use that did not plausibly usurp Santos’s market, the copyright claims failed as a matter of law. The result was affirmance without discovery or trial, showing how a well-pleaded complaint can sometimes defeat itself when its own allegations establish the affirmative defense.

# Chapter 4: The State Law Contract Claims—Third-Party Beneficiary and Implied-in-Fact Theories

## The district court’s preemption ruling and the Second Circuit’s decision to bypass the preemption issue

After rejecting Santos’s copyright claims, the Second Circuit turned to his state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The district court had dismissed the first two contract claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Preemption matters because, if federal copyright law occupies the field for a particular type of claim, state law cannot supply a separate remedy for the same conduct.

But the Second Circuit did not decide whether preemption was correct. Instead, it took a more direct route: even assuming the contract claims were not preempted, they still failed to state a claim under the relevant state law standards. That made preemption unnecessary to resolve. For a law student, this is a useful example of judicial restraint and appellate technique. A court may affirm a dismissal on any ground supported by the record, including a ground different from the one relied on below, so long as the result is correct. Here, the court preferred to dispose of the contract claims on ordinary pleading and contract-formation grounds rather than decide a potentially broader federal preemption issue.

## Why a court may affirm on state-law failure-to-state-a-claim grounds instead of reaching preemption

This opinion shows that preemption is not always the first question an appellate court must answer. If a claim fails under state law for a more basic reason—such as lack of contractual standing, failure to plead essential terms, or failure to show an agreement at all—the court can affirm on that narrower ground. That is especially true at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage, where the question is whether the complaint plausibly states a claim for relief.

The court’s approach matters because it limits the opinion to the facts and theories actually pleaded. Santos had to show that he personally had enforceable contract rights, or that the complaint plausibly alleged an implied agreement between the parties. He did not do so. Because those defects were enough to defeat the claims, the court had no need to decide whether the Copyright Act also preempted them.

## The direct breach of contract theory based on Cameo’s Terms of Service

Santos’s first contract theory was a direct breach of contract claim based on Cameo’s Terms of Service. The theory was that the users who submitted requests for personalized videos, including Kimmel using fictitious names, agreed to the platform’s terms, and that those terms were then breached. But Santos himself was not the user who agreed to those terms; he was the creator who fulfilled the requests. That distinction was fatal.

A breach of contract claim ordinarily belongs to a party to the contract, or sometimes to an intended third-party beneficiary. Santos was neither, at least not on the facts pleaded. So the court analyzed whether he could enforce the terms as a third party under the applicable law.

## Illinois law governing the Terms of Service

The Terms of Service were governed by Illinois law. That choice mattered because Illinois imposes a strong presumption against allowing noncontracting third parties to enforce a contract. In other words, if you were not a party to the agreement, you generally cannot sue on it unless the contract very clearly shows that the parties intended to give you enforcement rights.

This is an important contract principle: a plaintiff cannot simply point to a contract and say that it benefited from the agreement in some loose sense. The law asks whether the contracting parties intended to confer enforceable rights on that specific third party. Under Illinois law, the intent must be shown with unusual clarity.

## The strong presumption against third-party beneficiary status under Illinois law

The court quoted Illinois authority explaining that there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” That means the default rule is no enforcement by outsiders. The burden is on the plaintiff to overcome that presumption with contract language or surrounding circumstances showing a clear intent to benefit and empower the third party.

The court emphasized that the supposed intent must be so clear as to be “practically an express declaration.” That is a very demanding standard. It means the contract must do more than incidentally benefit a nonparty; it must effectively say that the third party is meant to have rights under the agreement. Without that kind of clarity, the claim fails.

## The requirement that third-party benefit be practically an express declaration

This “practically an express declaration” formulation is the court’s way of underscoring how difficult it is to qualify as a third-party beneficiary under Illinois law. The opinion does not require magic words, but it does require something close to them. The terms must strongly indicate that the contracting parties wanted the nonparty to be able to enforce the contract.

Santos’s complaint did not identify any such language. He pointed to the Terms of Service generally, but he did not identify a provision showing that the user-Cameo agreement was intended to create enforceable rights in creators like him. As a result, the court concluded that he could not sue directly for breach of the Terms of Service.

## Why Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service and could not enforce them

The most straightforward reason the claim failed was that Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service. Users like Kimmel had to agree to those terms before using the platform, but Santos did not allege that he was a signatory or contracting party to the user agreement he sought to enforce.

That matters because contract rights normally belong to the parties who made the bargain. A creator who performs under the platform’s request system may have some relationship with the platform, but that alone does not make the creator a contractual party to every user-side term. The court found nothing in the complaint showing that Santos was the intended beneficiary of the specific provisions he claimed were breached.

## The significance of other Terms of Service provisions that expressly mention third-party beneficiaries

The opinion also draws an inference from the structure of the Terms of Service themselves. Other provisions in the agreement expressly used third-party beneficiary language. That detail undercut Santos’s position. It suggested that when Cameo and its users intended to create third-party enforceable rights, they knew how to say so.

This is a standard contract-interpretation point. Express inclusion of third-party language in some provisions, but not the one at issue, supports the conclusion that the omission was intentional. In this case, the court used that fact to show that Santos could not plausibly claim third-party beneficiary status by implication. The contract’s own drafting demonstrated that third-party rights were not implied broadly across all provisions.

## The implied-in-fact contract claim under New York law

Santos also brought an implied-in-fact contract theory. Unlike the direct breach claim, this theory did not depend on him being an express party to the written Terms of Service. Instead, it relied on the idea that the conduct of the parties showed a separate agreement, or at least enough of a meeting of the minds to create enforceable obligations.

New York law governed this claim. Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract is still a real contract; the difference is that the agreement is inferred from conduct rather than written words. But the plaintiff must still plead the elements of formation: mutual assent, consideration, and the essential terms of the bargain.

## Requirements for pleading formation and essential terms of an implied contract

The court explained that under New York law, whether an implied-in-fact contract was formed depends on the parties’ intent and surrounding circumstances. But a complaint must do more than say “there was an agreement.” It must allege the essential terms of that agreement in nonconclusory language.

That requirement is especially important when the plaintiff seeks to enforce an implied agreement against a defendant based on platform terms or online conduct. The complaint must identify what the parties supposedly agreed to, how they agreed, and what contractual obligations were allegedly breached. Without those details, the court cannot infer a plausible contract.

## The complaint’s failure to allege nonconclusory essential terms

Santos’s complaint did not satisfy that standard. The court noted that he failed to plead the essential terms of the alleged implied contract, including the specific provisions on which liability was based. In other words, the complaint did not lay out the actual bargain in a way that would allow the court to see a contract, rather than a mere grievance.

That deficiency is fatal at the pleading stage. A plaintiff cannot rely on broad assertions that the defendant “agreed” to comply with terms; the complaint must set out the relevant promises and duties. Here, Santos did not do that. The court therefore held that the implied contract claim failed as a matter of law, regardless of any preemption issue.

## The absence of a plausible meeting of the minds that Kimmel would be bound to the Terms of Service as between Kimmel and Santos

The court also found no plausible allegation of a meeting of the minds. An implied contract requires more than unilateral expectation; it requires mutual assent. Santos needed to allege facts showing that Kimmel and Santos, through their conduct, reached an understanding that Kimmel would be bound by the Terms of Service in a way enforceable by Santos.

The complaint did not plausibly allege that. At most, it described Kimmel’s use of Cameo to request videos and Santos’s performance of those requests. That sequence does not by itself show that Kimmel agreed, as between himself and Santos, to be contractually bound to Santos under the Terms of Service. The court therefore concluded that the alleged facts did not show an “indication of a meeting of minds” sufficient to create an implied-in-fact contract.

The practical lesson is that an implied contract claim cannot rest on the mere existence of a platform relationship or on the plaintiff’s belief that the defendant should have complied with the platform rules. The law still requires assent, identifiable terms, and a basis for enforcement. Because Santos’s complaint did not supply those elements, the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of the contract claims without deciding preemption.

# Chapter 5: Fraudulent Inducement, Disposition, and the Opinion’s Practical Significance

## Santos’s fraudulent inducement theory based on misrepresentations used to obtain the videos

Santos’s remaining state-law claim was for fraudulent inducement. His theory was straightforward: Kimmel and the other defendants allegedly used fake names and false identities to induce Santos to create personalized Cameo videos, and then used those videos for a televised mocking segment. In other words, Santos tried to recast the defendants’ deceptive requests as actionable fraud.

The court treated that claim as distinct from the copyright issues, but it did not accept that the alleged deception, by itself, satisfied the elements of fraud under New York law. The complaint alleged that Santos was tricked into making the videos, but a fraud claim still requires more than deception in the abstract. It must also allege a legally cognizable injury caused by that deception.

## New York’s out-of-pocket loss rule for fraud damages

The key doctrinal point is New York’s “out-of-pocket” rule. Under New York law, fraud damages are not measured by disappointed expectations, reputational injury, or the defendant’s gain. Instead, the plaintiff must allege actual pecuniary loss caused by the fraud. That means a concrete economic loss—money or property that the plaintiff lost because of the misrepresentation.

The opinion cites New York Court of Appeals authority for that proposition and uses it as the basis for affirming dismissal. This matters because it limits fraud claims to compensable financial injuries rather than broader grievances about being embarrassed, mocked, or manipulated.

## Why Santos failed to plead actual pecuniary loss caused by the alleged fraud

The Second Circuit agreed with the district court that Santos did not plead the kind of out-of-pocket loss New York requires. Even accepting that the defendants misrepresented who they were when they requested the Cameo videos, the complaint did not plausibly allege that Santos lost money or suffered another measurable economic injury as a result.

That distinction is central to the court’s analysis. Santos’s real complaint was that the defendants humiliated him by turning his videos into material for ridicule on Jimmy Kimmel Live!. But the complaint did not identify a financial loss flowing from the deception itself. Without that, the fraud claim could not survive.

The court’s treatment of the claim also shows the difference between the existence of misleading conduct and the presence of a legally actionable fraud claim. A deceptive setup may be offensive or embarrassing, but New York law still requires a pleaded economic injury. The opinion therefore rejects any attempt to transform a reputational or dignitary harm into fraud damages without a showing of actual monetary loss.

## The court’s affirmance of dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim

For that reason, the panel affirmed dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim. It did so without needing to revisit the Copyright Act preemption issues the district court had discussed with respect to the contract claims. The result was simple: Santos’s fraud theory failed on the merits of pleading because the complaint did not allege out-of-pocket loss.

This aspect of the opinion is a useful reminder that federal courts can dispose of state-law claims at the pleading stage when a required element is missing from the complaint. Even if the alleged facts sound unfair, the claim still must satisfy the governing state-law elements.

## The final disposition: affirmance of the district court’s judgment in full

The court’s bottom-line disposition was complete affirmance. Santos lost on every claim. The copyright claims were barred by fair use, the contract-based state claims failed to state a claim, and the fraudulent inducement claim failed for lack of pleaded pecuniary loss.

The court ends by saying it considered Santos’s remaining arguments and found them meritless. That closing sentence signals that no issue raised on appeal changed the result.

## How the opinion ties together copyright fair use and state-law pleading rules

The opinion is especially instructive because it blends two different kinds of dismissal analysis. On the copyright side, the court applied fair use at the pleading stage, showing that a complaint can be dismissed when the alleged facts themselves establish a strong fair-use defense. On the state-law side, the court required each claim to meet its own doctrinal elements, including contractual privity or third-party beneficiary status, a meeting of the minds for implied contract, and out-of-pocket loss for fraud.

Taken together, the opinion teaches that creative pleading cannot avoid substantive limits. If the real grievance is criticism, parody, or mockery, copyright law may allow the defendant’s use as fair. And if the plaintiff tries to repackage the same conduct as contract or fraud, state-law doctrines may still block recovery unless the complaint pleads the specific elements those claims require.

## The significance of the case for expressive reuse in comedy, criticism, and commentary

The decision has practical significance for comedy and commentary. The court emphasized that the challenged use was transformative because the television segment used the videos to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. That framing placed the case within the long-recognized space for parody, criticism, and satire.

The opinion also reinforces that the copyright owner’s own subjective intention does not control fair use. What matters is how a reasonable observer understands the secondary use. For creators and broadcasters, the case suggests that expressive reuse may be protected even when the original material was produced through some deceptive setup, so long as the final use is transformative and does not substitute for the original market.

## The case’s lesson on pleading-stage fair use in the Second Circuit

The Second Circuit also made clear that fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss when the complaint and judicially noticeable materials provide enough to evaluate the defense. That is significant because plaintiffs often hope to delay fair-use adjudication until summary judgment or trial. This opinion shows that, at least in the Second Circuit, a complaint can be dismissed if it establishes fair use on its face.

For students, that is an important pleading lesson. A copyright complaint is not insulated from an affirmative defense when the plaintiff’s own allegations make the defense apparent.

## The difference between injury to dignity or reputation and compensable market or out-of-pocket loss

Finally, the case highlights a recurring theme in civil litigation: not every injury is legally compensable in the same way. Santos may have suffered embarrassment or reputational harm from being ridiculed on television. But embarrassment is not the same as market harm under copyright law, and it is not the same as out-of-pocket loss under New York fraud law.

That distinction drives the entire opinion. The copyright claims failed because the defendants’ use was transformative commentary, not market substitution. The fraud claim failed because the complaint did not allege actual pecuniary loss. In both settings, the court insisted on the legal measure of injury that the doctrine requires, rather than the broader sense of unfairness that Santos’s allegations evoke.
