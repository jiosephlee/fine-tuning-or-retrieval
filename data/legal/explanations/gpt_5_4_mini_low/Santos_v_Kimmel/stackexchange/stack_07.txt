Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why wasn't Santos's fraudulent inducement claim enough if he was tricked into making the videos?
Question:
The fraud claim is where I'm most lost. The court says Santos failed to allege any 'actual out-of-pocket loss' as required by New York law. But if he only made the videos because Kimmel misrepresented who he was and how the videos would be used, isn't that a concrete injury? Santos gave his labor, time, and performance in exchange for a transaction he says was procured by fraud. Why doesn't that count as pecuniary loss? Is this because New York fraud damages are limited to direct financial loss actually caused by the misrepresentation, and not things like benefit-of-the-bargain, reputational injury, or downstream harm from publication?
Answer:
Under New York law, **fraudulent inducement is not enough by itself**. The plaintiff must also plead and prove **actual, pecuniary out-of-pocket loss** caused by the misrepresentation. That is the rule the Second Circuit cites here from **Lama Holding** and **Connaughton**.

So your instinct is partly right in a factual sense — Santos says he was tricked into making the videos — but **New York fraud law does not treat “I was induced to do the transaction” as the damage element by itself**. The injury must be a **financial loss**, not just that the plaintiff acted under false pretenses.

That is why the court says Santos’s claim failed. In the opinion, the panel does **not** identify any allegation that Santos:

- paid money out of pocket,
- transferred money or property,
- gave up a concrete economic opportunity,
- or otherwise suffered a measurable pecuniary loss from the deception.

Instead, the complaint apparently alleged that he **performed the Cameo videos** and then objected to how they were used. But under New York fraud doctrine, **the value of the performance itself is not automatically the fraud damages measure** unless the plaintiff can tie it to a real economic loss. The court’s point is that the fraud claim could not rest on the idea that “I would not have done the work if I had known the truth” without a corresponding out-of-pocket loss.

This is also why your suggested categories mostly do not work here:

- **Benefit-of-the-bargain damages** are generally **not** the New York fraud measure.
- **Reputational injury** is not the out-of-pocket pecuniary loss New York requires for fraud.
- **Downstream harm from publication** may matter in some other theory, but the court held Santos’s fraud claim still lacked the required out-of-pocket loss.

So the short answer is: **being tricked into entering the transaction is the inducement; it is not, by itself, the damages element.** New York fraud law is narrower than “I was deceived and therefore hurt.” It requires a **specific, actual financial loss**, and the Second Circuit held Santos did not plead one.
