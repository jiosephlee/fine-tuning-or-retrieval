Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How did the court define market harm under factor four when the alleged use could still reduce the value of Santos's Cameo business?
Question:
The opinion says there was no market harm on the face of the complaint because courts ask whether the secondary use 'usurps' the market for the original by serving as a competing substitute, not whether it just damages the original through parody or criticism. But if Kimmel's segments made Santos look ridiculous and thereby reduced demand for future Cameo videos, isn't that a real economic harm? Why is that not the kind of market harm copyright law cares about under factor four? Is the distinction that copyright protects substitution-based market injury, but not reputational or criticism-based injury?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the distinction the Second Circuit was drawing.

Under fair use factor four, the question is not whether the defendant’s use caused **some economic injury** to the copyright holder in the abstract. The question is whether the use **harms the market for the copyrighted work in the way copyright law recognizes** — i.e., whether it **“usurps the market” by serving as a competing substitute** for the original.

The court said exactly that:

> “We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.”

So even if Kimmel’s segments made Santos look foolish and arguably made viewers less interested in buying future Cameo videos, that is not the kind of market harm factor four is concerned with. That would be harm from **criticism, ridicule, or reputational damage**, not from **substitution**.

Why the difference? Because copyright does **not** protect the author from all economic consequences of negative commentary on the work. If it did, then parody, satire, and criticism would be severely chilled. The Supreme Court in *Campbell* made that point, saying that the market for “critical reviews or lampoons” is not a potential licensing market that copyright owners are entitled to control.

So the answer to your last question is basically yes:

- **Copyright factor four protects against substitution-based market injury**  
- **It does not protect against loss in value caused by parody, criticism, or reputational embarrassment**

That is why the court could accept that Santos might be harmed in a broader sense, while still finding **no market harm within the meaning of fair use** on the face of the complaint. The injury alleged was not “this use replaced my videos”; it was “this use mocked my videos and made them less appealing.” Copyright law treats those differently.
