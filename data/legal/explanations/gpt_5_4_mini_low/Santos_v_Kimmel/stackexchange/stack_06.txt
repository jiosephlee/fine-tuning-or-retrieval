Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the court avoid the Copyright Act preemption issue and instead reject the state-law claims on the merits?
Question:
On the state-law claims, the district court apparently held that the contract-based claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, but the Second Circuit says it 'need not resolve this issue' because the claims fail anyway under state law. Is that a common move for appellate courts? Also, on the direct contract claim, the court says Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and was not an intended third-party beneficiary under Illinois law. On the implied-contract claim, it switches to New York law and says there were no sufficiently pleaded essential terms or meeting of the minds. Why were different states' laws governing those two contract theories, and what would Santos have needed to allege to survive dismissal on either theory?
Answer:
Yes — that is a fairly common appellate move.

An appellate court may affirm on **any ground supported by the record**, even if the district court relied on a different rationale. So if the panel can dispose of the state-law claims on ordinary Rule 12(b)(6) grounds, it often will not reach a harder or more controversial issue like Copyright Act preemption. That is exactly what the Second Circuit did here: it said it “need not resolve” preemption because, even assuming the claims were not preempted, they still failed under state contract law.

## Why different state laws?

Because the two contract theories were treated as **different claims with different governing sources of law**:

1. **Direct breach of contract claim**  
   This claim was based on the **Cameo Terms of Service**. The court therefore applied the law governing that agreement — the opinion says **Illinois law governs the Terms of Service**.

2. **Implied-contract claim**  
   This was not a claim that Santos himself was a party to the Terms of Service. Instead, it was a separate theory that the defendants had an implied-in-fact obligation. The court analyzed that theory under **New York law**, which it says governs that claim.

So the difference is not that the court “switched” arbitrarily; it was addressing **two different legal theories**, each governed by the law the court identified as applicable to that theory.

## Why the direct contract claim failed

The court’s point is straightforward: **Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service**. Under Illinois law, there is a “strong presumption against” treating a nonparty as an intended third-party beneficiary. The contract must show that intent “so strongly as to be practically an express declaration.”

So to survive dismissal, Santos would have needed to allege facts showing either:

- he was actually a party to the contract, or
- the Terms of Service **clearly and expressly** intended to give him enforceable rights as a third-party beneficiary.

The opinion notes the opposite problem: the Terms of Service had **express third-party beneficiary language in other provisions**, which undercuts any claim that the relevant provisions silently conferred rights on Santos.

## Why the implied-contract claim failed

Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract still requires the usual contract basics: **essential terms** and a **meeting of the minds**.

The court held Santos did not plead either:

- he did not allege the **essential terms** of the supposed implied contract in nonconclusory form, including the specific provisions allegedly breached; and
- he did not plausibly allege a mutual understanding that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service.

So to survive dismissal, Santos would have needed to plead facts showing, at minimum:

- what the implied agreement actually was,
- who supposedly assented to it,
- what specific obligations were included,
- and facts supporting a real mutual understanding, not just a bare conclusion that a contract existed.

## Bottom line

The Second Circuit avoided the preemption question because it could affirm on a narrower, easier ground: the state-law claims failed on the merits anyway. And the two contract theories were analyzed under different state laws because they were different claims tied to different asserted contractual relationships.
