Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What role does bad faith still play in fair use after Harper & Row and Warhol?
Question:
The opinion says Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fake identities on Cameo showed bad faith and therefore should defeat fair use. The court responds that Harper & Row mentioned good faith, but that the Supreme Court has since 'distanced itself' from that statement. I'm having trouble understanding the modern doctrine. Does bad faith still matter under factor one, or is it basically irrelevant unless it shows an intent to usurp the copyright owner's market? If someone obtains a work through deception but then uses it for criticism or parody, how do courts now weigh that?
Answer:
**Short answer: bad faith is not an independent fair-use killer anymore.** Under modern doctrine, it can still be relevant to factor one, but only as part of the overall inquiry into the **purpose and character of the use**—especially whether the secondary user is trying to **supplant** the original or instead is making a **transformative** use such as criticism, commentary, or parody.

That is essentially how the Second Circuit treats it in **_Santos v. Kimmel_**. The court acknowledged Harper & Row’s statement that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but then said the Supreme Court has **since distanced itself from that statement**. The court therefore did **not** treat Kimmel’s use of false identities as automatically defeating fair use. Instead, it asked the real factor-one question: what was the purpose and character of the use?

The key point is this:

- **Bad faith alone does not defeat fair use.**
- It matters only if it bears on the statutory factors, mainly factor one and sometimes factor four.
- If the complaint still shows the use was **critical, satirical, or transformative**, bad faith will usually not outweigh that.

In **_Santos_**, the court said the complaint itself showed the defendants were motivated by “(sarcastic) criticism and commentary,” not by a purpose to “supplant” Santos’s market for the videos. So even though Kimmel allegedly used deception to obtain the videos, that did not defeat fair use because the end use was mocking commentary, not market substitution.

So if someone obtains a work through deception but then uses it for criticism or parody, modern courts generally ask:

1. **Is the secondary use transformative?**
2. **Is it aimed at criticism/commentary/parody rather than substitution?**
3. **Does it usurp a market for the original?**

Deception may be relevant background, but it is **not dispositive**. In the Second Circuit’s view here, the decisive point is whether the use is still the kind of expressive, critical use that fair use is meant to protect.
