Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the court say Kimmel's use was transformative even though he allegedly caused the videos to be created?
Question:
The part I don't understand is Santos's argument that Kimmel 'instigated the creation' of the Cameo videos, so Kimmel was really the 'designer' of their original purpose. If Kimmel was the one who submitted the requests and got Santos to say the absurd lines, why doesn't that undermine the idea that the later TV use had a different purpose from the original work? The court seems to say the key question is what a reasonable observer would think the original videos were for, not the subjective intent of either side. Is that standard always used in transformativeness analysis, and why doesn't the alleged setup make the 'original purpose' and 'secondary purpose' the same here?
Answer:
The court is treating **“transformative use” as an objective comparison between the two works**, not as an inquiry into the user’s private motives or the copyright holder’s strategic behavior.

### 1. Why Kimmel’s alleged “setup” did not defeat transformativeness

Santos argued that because Kimmel allegedly **instigated** the creation of the Cameo videos, Kimmel was really the “designer” of their original purpose. The Second Circuit rejected that framing because the fair-use inquiry asks what the works **actually communicate to a reasonable observer**, not who secretly hoped for what.

The court expressly relied on **Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith** for the proposition that:

> “the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”

So even if Kimmel orchestrated the requests, that does **not** change what the original videos were objectively for. According to Santos’s own complaint, the Cameos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments used them “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.”

Those are different purposes.

### 2. Why the original and secondary purposes were not the same

The key point is that the court did **not** define the “original purpose” as “whatever Kimmel intended the videos to be used for later.” It defined it by reference to the videos’ **apparent communicative function** when created.

That matters because the copyrighted work is the video Santos made and delivered. The question is whether the later TV segments “merely supersede[d] the objects of the original creation” or instead added “something new, with a further purpose or different character.”

Here, the videos were ordered as personalized Cameos, which on the face of Santos’s complaint were meant to communicate conventional positive messages. The JKL segments repurposed those videos for **mocking commentary** about Santos’s willingness to say anything for money. That is a different use, even if Kimmel engineered the setup to expose that difference.

### 3. Is the objective-reasonable-observer standard always used?

Not in exactly those words for every fair-use case, but **for transformativeness, yes, the analysis is generally objective**. Courts look at the **purpose and character of the use** as revealed by the work and the context, not by one side’s hidden intentions. The Supreme Court’s Warhol decision emphasized that courts should not make transformativeness turn on subjective intent.

So the court was not saying intent is irrelevant in all fair-use analysis. It was saying that **the setup does not collapse the objective distinction between the two uses**.

### 4. Bottom line

Kimmel may have arranged for the videos to be created, but that does not make the Cameos’ **objective original purpose** the same as the JKL segments’ **critical, mocking purpose**. The fair-use analysis turns on the latter comparison, and the court found that the two uses served different functions.
