Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why did the Eighth Circuit vacate and remand the MFLSA claim instead of just deciding whether Cohen could recover penalties?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture of the MFLSA part of this case. Cohen stipulated that he had already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed were owed, and the only thing left seems to be statutory penalties that would be paid to the Commissioner rather than to him personally. The court then says it has a “serious concern” about jurisdiction and remands for the district court to determine whether the claim is moot. Why couldn't the court of appeals just decide the statutory-penalty issue directly? Is the idea that Article III jurisdiction has to be resolved first before the court can reach the merits, and the record was insufficient to decide whether the challenged overtime policy was still in place?
Answer:
The Eighth Circuit did **not** reach the MFLSA penalty question because it was not sure the case was still justiciable under Article III.

The key point in the opinion is this:

- Cohen had **already been paid** all overtime wages and liquidated damages he personally claimed under the MFLSA.
- The **only issue left** was whether he could still obtain **statutory penalties**, and the parties agreed those penalties would be payable to the **Commissioner**, not to Cohen.
- That raised a **jurisdictional problem**: if Cohen no longer had a live personal stake in the claim, the court would have **no Article III case or controversy** to decide.

### Why that matters
Federal courts cannot decide the merits first and worry about jurisdiction later. As the court notes, a plaintiff must have standing for **each claim and each form of relief**, and that interest must continue throughout the case; otherwise the claim becomes **moot**. The court cites:

- **Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates** for the requirement of standing for each form of relief
- **Sisney v. Kaemingk** for the rule that standing must persist through mootness
- **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw** for the mootness / voluntary-cessation framework

### Why the record was not enough
The court said it had a “serious concern” because the parties had not resolved whether Consilio’s challenged overtime policy was **still in place**. That mattered because:

- If the policy was **still ongoing**, then a claim for civil penalties might still be live.
- If the policy had been withdrawn and it was “**absolutely clear**” the conduct would **not recur**, then the claim might be **moot**.

That is exactly the kind of factual question that must be resolved before the court can decide whether it has power to act.

### So why vacate and remand?
Because the court of appeals could not safely decide the penalty issue **without first knowing whether it had jurisdiction at all**. The proper course was to:

1. **vacate** the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim, and
2. **remand** for the district court to determine whether the claim was moot.

That is also why Judge Kelly says the court has a “serious concern” about jurisdiction, and why Judge Kobes’s concurrence agrees the district court can decide mootness first on remand.

### Bottom line
Yes — your understanding is basically right. The Eighth Circuit vacated and remanded because **Article III jurisdiction had to be resolved first**, and the record left unresolved whether Cohen still had a live claim for penalties given the possibility that Consilio’s allegedly unlawful policy had ended. Without a live controversy, the court could not reach the merits of whether penalties were available.

### How can a plaintiff have standing to seek civil penalties that are payable only to the government?
Question:
The opinion cites Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for the proposition that civil penalties can sometimes redress a plaintiff's injury by deterring future violations, even when the money doesn't go to the plaintiff. But here the court also notes that the penalties under the MFLSA would go to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. I'm having trouble understanding the redressability analysis. What exactly must a plaintiff show to have standing for this kind of penalty claim, and why might that be harder here than in Laidlaw? Is Judge Kobes's concurrence suggesting that deterrence-based standing may not carry over automatically from environmental cases to wage-and-hour statutes?
Answer:
A plaintiff **does not get standing simply because a statute authorizes a civil penalty**. Under Article III, the plaintiff still must show that the requested penalty will **redress the plaintiff’s own injury**, not just punish the defendant.

That is the key lesson from **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw**. There, the Court held that civil penalties payable to the government could satisfy redressability **because the plaintiffs were still exposed to ongoing or threatened harm** from the defendant’s pollution. The penalties mattered because they would likely **deter future violations**, which in turn would lessen the plaintiffs’ own injury. In other words, deterrence was enough **only because the plaintiffs still had a live personal stake in stopping ongoing conduct**.

That is why the standing question is harder here.

### What Cohen would need to show
For a penalty claim like this, Cohen would need to show something like:

1. **An injury in fact** — which he had when he was allegedly denied overtime;
2. **A likelihood of redress through the requested penalty** — meaning the penalty would likely deter future violations that would affect him; and
3. **That the injury is still live when the court is asked to award the penalty**.

The opinion emphasizes that standing must exist **for each form of relief**, and it must continue throughout the case. So if the only remaining relief is a penalty payable to the Commissioner, Cohen has to show that the penalty would still redress a **continuing or threatened injury of his own**.

### Why that is harder than in Laidlaw
The problem in **Cohen v. Consilio** is that the parties stipulated that Cohen had already been paid all overtime and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. That makes his **personal monetary injury look fully cured**. If the unlawful overtime policy is also no longer in place, then there may be **no ongoing injury or realistic threat of recurrence** for a penalty to redress.

So unlike Laidlaw, where the plaintiffs were still affected by the challenged conduct, Cohen may be seeking only a penalty for past conduct that no longer threatens him. In that setting, a deterrence-based theory becomes much harder to fit within Article III.

### What Judge Kobes is getting at
Yes — Judge Kobes’s concurrence is basically flagging that **Laidlaw does not automatically carry over to wage-and-hour cases**. His point is not that deterrence can never support standing outside environmental law, but that the analogy is not automatic because:

- Laidlaw involved **ongoing or threatened future harm**;
- here the claimed wages and liquidated damages were already paid; and
- the penalty under the MFLSA is **payable to the Commissioner**, not to Cohen.

That last point matters because it underscores that the penalty is not compensating Cohen directly. So Cohen must rely entirely on the idea that the penalty will deter future violations affecting him. If there is no remaining or likely future injury to Cohen, redressability is doubtful.

### Bottom line
A plaintiff can sometimes have standing to seek a government-payable civil penalty, **but only if the penalty will likely redress the plaintiff’s own ongoing or threatened injury by deterring future violations**. That is what made standing work in **Laidlaw**. Here, because Cohen says he has already been paid in full and the disputed issue may be whether the policy is still in effect, the redressability/mootness analysis is much more fragile.

So yes: **Judge Kobes is signaling that deterrence-based standing may not transfer automatically from environmental cases to wage-and-hour penalty claims**. The district court on remand has to decide whether Cohen still has a live Article III interest, not just a statutory entitlement to penalties.

### Why did the court read Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act as allowing only the Commissioner—not employees—to recover the average daily wage penalty?
Question:
On the MPWA claim, Cohen argued that the text of Minn. Stat. § 181.101 authorized him to recover average daily wage penalties, but the court said only the Commissioner may seek those penalties. I'm struggling with the statutory interpretation. Section 181.171 says an employee can sue for violations of certain wage statutes and recover “civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” If that's true, why doesn't that incorporate the penalty language from § 181.101 into a private action? Is the court basically saying that because § 181.101 specifically describes the Commissioner serving a demand and then collecting the penalty, that specific enforcement mechanism overrides the more general private right language in § 181.171?
Answer:
Yes—that is essentially the court’s reasoning, but with an important statutory-text twist.

The Eighth Circuit read **§ 181.101** as creating a **Commissioner-enforced penalty regime**, not a privately enforceable one. The key point is that **§ 181.171 does not itself create new penalties**; it lets an employee sue for violations of specified wage statutes and recover the **“civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”** So the employee can recover a penalty **only if the underlying section actually provides that the employee may recover it**.

For **§ 181.101**, the court said the penalty language runs only through the **Commissioner**:

- the Commissioner may **“serve a demand for payment”** on behalf of the employee;
- if the employer does not pay, the Commissioner may **“charge and collect”** wages and a penalty;
- the money collected by the Commissioner must then be paid to the employee.

By contrast, the employee is given only the right to **“prosecut[e] a claim for wages.”** The statute does **not** say the employee may “charge and collect” the penalty.

So the court concluded that **§ 181.171’s general private-action language does not override § 181.101’s specific enforcement scheme**. Rather, § 181.171 incorporates only those penalties or damages that the violated section itself makes available to the employee. Because § 181.101 assigns the penalty remedy to the Commissioner, there is nothing for § 181.171 to incorporate into a private suit.

The court reinforced that reading with a familiar statutory-interpretation point: the Minnesota legislature **knows how to give employees direct penalty rights when it wants to**. The opinion points to §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which expressly let an employee “charge and collect a penalty.” The absence of similar language in § 181.101 was treated as deliberate.

So your intuition is right: the court is applying the idea that **specific enforcement language controls over a more general private-right provision**, and that the general incorporation in § 181.171 does not enlarge the remedy beyond what § 181.101 itself authorizes.

### What role did the comparison to Minnesota statutes §§ 181.13 and 181.14 play in the court's interpretation of § 181.101?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes that the Minnesota legislature ‘knew how’ to expressly give employees the right to collect penalties in other wage statutes, like §§ 181.13 and 181.14, but did not do so in § 181.101. I'm not sure how strong that inference is supposed to be. Is this an application of expressio unius or some other canon? Also, how much weight do courts usually give to comparisons across related statutes when the statute at issue already has a private right of action elsewhere in the same chapter?
Answer:
The comparison to §§ 181.13 and 181.14 was doing fairly conventional interpretive work: it was a **textual, intrachapter comparison** used to support the inference that the legislature **deliberately omitted** employee-initiated penalty authority from § 181.101.

### What the court was saying
The key move is this:

- In **§§ 181.13 and 181.14**, the legislature expressly said an employee may “**charge and collect a penalty**.”
- In **§ 181.101**, by contrast, the statute speaks of the **Commissioner** serving a demand, collecting wages, and collecting a penalty; for the employee, the section preserves only the right to **prosecute a claim for wages**.

So the Eighth Circuit treated the different wording as evidence that the legislature knew how to grant employees a penalty remedy when it wanted to, and chose **not** to do so in § 181.101.

### What canon is this?
Yes, it is close to **expressio unius est exclusio alterius**—the idea that when the legislature includes a particular remedy or actor in one provision, its omission in another similar provision suggests the omission was intentional.

But the opinion frames it slightly more modestly than a rigid canon. It cites **Lagasse v. Horton** for the more specific proposition that when the Legislature acts in one statute and not another, it “knows how” to accomplish the objective if it wishes. That is really a form of:

- **negative implication from statutory comparison**, and
- a **contextual reading of related provisions**.

So the court was not relying on expressio unius alone in the abstract; it was using **comparative statutory text within the same chapter** to confirm the ordinary meaning of § 181.101.

### Why that comparison matters here
It mattered because § 181.171 already provides a private cause of action for violations of § 181.101, but only for the remedies **“provided for in the section violated.”** That language cuts against Cohen’s argument. The court’s comparison to §§ 181.13 and 181.14 helped show that if the legislature had wanted employees to recover penalties under § 181.101, it knew how to say so expressly.

In other words, the comparison was used to reinforce the statute’s structure:

- **§ 181.171** gives a private action,
- but only for remedies found in the underlying substantive section,
- and **§ 181.101** assigns penalty collection to the Commissioner, not the employee.

### How much weight do courts give this kind of comparison?
Quite a bit, **when the statutes are closely related and use different language for similar situations**. Courts often treat that as meaningful evidence of legislative intent, especially when:

1. the provisions are in the **same chapter or statutory scheme**,
2. the legislature used **express penalty language elsewhere**, and
3. the provision at issue is otherwise **silent or assigns the remedy to another actor**.

That said, this is not usually dispositive by itself. It works best as a **confirmatory canon**—supporting the interpretation the text already suggests. Here, the court had more than just a comparison: it also relied on the fact that § 181.101 itself repeatedly refers to the **Commissioner** charging and collecting the penalty, and to the employee only prosecuting a claim for wages.

### Bottom line
The comparison to §§ 181.13 and 181.14 served as **evidence of deliberate legislative drafting**, not as an independent substantive rule. It is best understood as an **expressio unius / negative-implication argument grounded in related-statute comparison**. In the court’s view, that comparison reinforced the conclusion that **employees cannot directly recover § 181.101 penalties; only the Commissioner can collect them, with the money ultimately paid to the employee**.

### Why was Cohen's Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim treated as waived or inadequately briefed on appeal?
Question:
For the MWTA claim, the court says Cohen complained that the district court gave little discussion to the issue, but then the Eighth Circuit refuses to disturb the dismissal because he pointed to no statutory authority allowing civil penalties and made no meaningful argument for reversal. I'm confused about the appellate practice point here. If a party thinks the district court overlooked a claim, what exactly does the party need to do on appeal to preserve it? Was the problem that Cohen failed to identify an actual remedy under § 181.032, or that he failed to develop the argument enough in his briefing, or both?
Answer:
The short answer is: **both**.

On appeal, a party does not preserve a claim merely by saying the district court “didn’t discuss it enough.” The appellant must **present a developed argument for why the judgment was wrong**, supported by **legal authority and record citations**. If the brief does not do that, the court of appeals may treat the issue as **waived** or **forfeited** and decline to review it.

That is exactly what the Eighth Circuit did here with the MWTA claim. The court noted two things:

1. **Cohen stipulated that he had already been paid all wages owed**, so he was left only with a penalties theory.
2. But he **“points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute”** and offered **“[n]o meaningful argument in support of reversal.”**

So the problem was not just that he failed to identify the remedy; it was also that he **failed to brief the issue meaningfully at all**. Those are really the same appellate deficiency in practical terms: without a statute or some other authority showing that the MWTA authorizes civil penalties for an employee like Cohen, there is nothing for the court to reverse on.

The opinion’s citation to **Milligan v. City of Red Oak**, which says that a brief that does not support an assertion with argument or legal authority waives the issue, makes the point explicit. In other words, appellate courts do not comb through the record or construct arguments for the appellant. If you want reversal, you must tell the court:

- **what legal error the district court made,**
- **what authority supports your position,**
- and **why that error requires reversal.**

Here, Cohen’s complaint that the district court gave little discussion did not substitute for that showing. Since he did not identify a statutory basis for MWTA penalties or otherwise develop a reversal argument, the Eighth Circuit treated the claim as waived/inadequately briefed and left the dismissal undisturbed.

So the appellate-practice lesson is: **to preserve an issue, you must do more than mention it—you must argue it.**

### Why did the court refuse to consider Cohen's request for an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind its overtime policy?
Question:
The court says Cohen originally requested certain forms of injunctive relief in the complaint but later asked for different relief in summary judgment briefing—namely, an order requiring Consilio to withdraw the 2019 overtime policy. The court then says the issue was not properly before it because new claims must be raised through an amended complaint, not in opposition briefs. I'm trying to understand the line between a new claim and a new remedy. If the underlying theory is still that the policy violates Minnesota wage law, why isn't this just a different form of equitable relief rather than a new claim? Or is the court saying that the requested injunction was materially different enough from the pleaded relief that Rule 15 required amendment?
Answer:
The court refused to consider that request because Cohen did not actually plead it in the complaint, and he tried to add it only in summary-judgment briefing.

That matters because, in federal practice, you cannot use a brief to expand the case beyond the claims and relief set out in the operative complaint. The Eighth Circuit cited **InfoDeli, LLC** for the proposition that “**[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs**,” and pointed to **Rule 15(a)**. So the problem was procedural, not semantic.

### Why this was treated as more than just a “different remedy”
In theory, a plaintiff can often seek different forms of relief on the same legal theory. For example, if the complaint alleges a statutory violation, the plaintiff may later argue for damages, an injunction, declaratory relief, etc., so long as that relief is fairly tied to the pleaded claim.

But here, the court said Cohen **“sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief”** in the complaint and then **“changed course”** by asking for an injunction ordering Consilio to **withdraw/rescind the 2019 overtime policy**. The panel treated that as a materially different request, not merely a refinement of the same remedy.

Why? Because an injunction requiring rescission of the policy is not just a different label on the same relief. It is a distinct form of prospective relief directed at a continuing policy, and the district court had not been asked to adjudicate that request as pleaded. In other words, Cohen was not merely saying, “grant the injunction I already asked for, but in a slightly different way.” He was asking the court to order a different affirmative act.

### The key point
So the court was essentially saying:

- **Same legal theory** does not automatically mean
- **same claim for relief** if the plaintiff is asking for a materially different injunction that was not in the complaint.

If Cohen wanted that specific injunction, he needed to **amend the complaint** under Rule 15, not introduce it for the first time in summary judgment papers.

### Bottom line
The court did **not** say that every different form of equitable relief is a new claim. It said that **this particular request**—an order compelling Consilio to rescind the overtime policy—was not properly before the court because it was **newly raised in briefing** rather than pleaded. Under the court’s cited authority, that made it procedurally improper.

### What effect did the parties' stipulations have on the scope of the case and the court's analysis?
Question:
The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and all MFLSA liquidated damages he alleged he was owed, and they also agreed that resolving Consilio's summary judgment motion first could fully resolve the case if Consilio won. I'm not sure how much those stipulations legally narrowed the dispute. Did they effectively eliminate any live controversy as to damages, leaving only penalties and equitable relief? And if so, why didn't that automatically moot the whole case? I'm especially confused about how stipulations interact with mootness when the plaintiff still argues there is an ongoing unlawful policy that should be deterred or enjoined.
Answer:
The stipulations narrowed the case, but they did **not automatically end it**.

### 1) What the stipulations actually did
The parties stipulated that:

- Cohen had already been paid **all overtime wages** he claimed, and
- he had also been paid **all MFLSA liquidated damages** he claimed.

That means the case was no longer about whether Consilio still owed Cohen **back pay** or **liquidated damages** under the MFLSA. Those parts of the dispute were effectively off the table.

But the stipulation did **not** resolve the entire lawsuit, because Cohen still claimed:

- **statutory penalties**, and
- **injunctive relief**.

So the live controversy was reduced to whether any additional relief remained available.

### 2) Why that did not automatically moot the case
The court explains that **mootness depends on whether the plaintiff still has a continuing interest in the relief sought**. A case is moot only when it is “absolutely clear” the allegedly wrongful conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur.

Here, the stipulation showed only that Cohen had been paid what he claimed as wages and liquidated damages. It did **not** establish that Consilio’s alleged overtime policy had been abandoned or could not recur. The opinion says that **whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute**.

That matters because, under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, civil penalties can still supply redress if they would deter ongoing or future unlawful conduct. So even though the penalties would be paid to the Commissioner, they could still support a live Article III controversy if the challenged policy remained in place.

### 3) Why the summary-judgment stipulation mattered
The parties’ later stipulation that the court should decide the summary-judgment motion first was basically a **case-management agreement**: if Consilio won on the merits, the case would be over. But that did **not** waive the jurisdictional question. Federal courts must still satisfy themselves that a live case or controversy exists.

That is why the Eighth Circuit said it had a “serious concern regarding our jurisdiction” and **vacated and remanded** the MFLSA claim for the district court to decide mootness first.

### 4) Bottom line
The stipulations **narrowed the merits dispute to penalties and injunctive relief**, but they did **not** eliminate the Article III controversy by themselves. The court still had to ask whether the challenged overtime policy remained in place or whether it was absolutely clear the conduct would not recur. Until that was resolved, the case was not automatically moot.

So, in short:

- **Yes**, the stipulations largely eliminated the damages dispute.
- **No**, they did **not** automatically moot the whole case.
- The remaining issue was whether **penalties and injunctive relief** still presented a live controversy because of an alleged ongoing unlawful policy.
