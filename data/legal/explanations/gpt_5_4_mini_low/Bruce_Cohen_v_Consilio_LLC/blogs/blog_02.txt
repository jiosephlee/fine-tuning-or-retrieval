Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Paid in Full, But Still a Live Case? Mootness, Deterrence, and Article III in the MFLSA Penalties Remand

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* is not, at bottom, a sweeping wage-and-hour ruling. It is narrower than that. But the panel’s treatment of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act claim raises a recurring and important Article III question: when an employer has paid the worker everything the worker personally claims to be owed, can a lawsuit continue on the theory that civil penalties — payable not to the employee, but to the Commissioner — still keep the case alive?

That question matters well beyond this case. Wage statutes increasingly pair employee recovery with government-facing penalties designed to deter misconduct. When the private plaintiff has been made whole on wages and liquidated damages, courts have to decide whether any remaining penalty claim supplies a live personal stake, or whether the case has become moot. The Eighth Circuit did not answer that question outright. Instead, it vacated and remanded for the district court to determine whether the case is still justiciable. But the panel’s discussion, and Judge Kobes’s concurrence, strongly suggest that the real problem may be deeper than mootness.

## The panel’s move: no wages left, maybe no case left

The setup is straightforward. Cohen alleged that Consilio violated Minnesota overtime law, but before the court ruled on summary judgment, Consilio paid him and other similarly situated employees the overtime wages and liquidated damages they sought under the MFLSA. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid everything he was owed on those fronts. What remained was statutory penalties.

That posture immediately triggered the Article III question the panel highlighted: if the only remaining relief is a penalty payable to the Commissioner, does Cohen still have a live case? The court was careful. It did not say the case is moot. It said there is a “serious concern” about jurisdiction, because the record left unresolved whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy was still in place. If it remained in effect, the panel reasoned, Cohen’s penalty claim might still present a live controversy under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, which held that civil penalties can redress ongoing or threatened injury by deterring unlawful conduct. But if the challenged policy had been abandoned and recurrence was “absolutely clear” not to happen, the claim could be moot.

That is a sensible, cautious way to resolve an underdeveloped record. The court resisted the temptation to decide an Article III issue in the abstract when the underlying facts — especially whether the challenged policy had truly ceased — were disputed or at least unclear. In that sense, the remand is less a substantive holding than an insistence on jurisdictional housekeeping.

## Laidlaw and the deterrence theory of redress

The panel’s reliance on *Laidlaw* is important. That case remains the Supreme Court’s leading statement that civil penalties can redress private injury even when the penalties are not paid to the plaintiff. The logic is deterrence: if the prospect of penalties discourages continued violations, then the plaintiff has some redress for the harm caused by unlawful conduct.

But *Laidlaw* does not eliminate Article III’s limits. It was decided in the context of a citizen suit challenging ongoing environmental violations, where the defendant’s voluntary cessation did not make the case go away. The Court emphasized that a case becomes moot only when it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur.

That standard gives plaintiffs room to keep a penalty claim alive where a defendant stops its conduct only after being sued. It does not automatically do the same where the defendant has already paid all individualized compensation and the remaining penalty is legally owed to the state rather than to the plaintiff. That is the crucial tension in *Cohen*. The panel recognized the deterrence principle, but it did not assume that deterrence alone answers the jurisdictional question. Instead, it asked whether the penalty claim still corresponds to an ongoing or likely recurring injury to Cohen himself.

## Standing, mootness, and the unresolved personal stake

The doctrinal wrinkle is that this case sits at the boundary between standing and mootness. Standing asks whether the plaintiff had a sufficient injury at the outset; mootness asks whether that live stake continues. The Eighth Circuit cited its own precedent for the proposition that the “requisite personal interest” must persist throughout the litigation.

That distinction matters because Cohen’s requested penalties do not go to him. They go to the Commissioner. So even if the penalties may deter future violations in some abstract sense, the court was not prepared to say that deterrence alone establishes a continuing personal stake for Cohen once his own wage and liquidated-damages claims were satisfied. That is especially so because the statutory penalties he seeks are not the kind of individualized monetary relief that directly compensates him for a loss.

The panel stopped short of holding that a private plaintiff can never maintain such a claim. But its remand reflects a real skepticism that a plaintiff retains Article III jurisdiction simply by keeping alive a claim for penalties that the plaintiff will never receive. The court wants to know whether there is still an ongoing violation or a credible threat of recurrence that connects the penalty request to a concrete dispute between these parties.

## Judge Kobes’s concurrence points to the harder question

Judge Kobes’s special concurrence is the most revealing part of the decision. He agreed that the case should be vacated and remanded, but he said he was not convinced Cohen had standing to seek MFLSA penalties under *Laidlaw* at all. In other words, he flagged the possibility that this is not merely a mootness problem arising from post-filing events. It may be an original standing problem: whether a plaintiff can ever sue for penalties payable to the Commissioner when the plaintiff is already fully compensated.

That is a significant hint. If Judge Kobes is right, the question is not whether Consilio’s payment mooted an otherwise valid penalty claim. It is whether Cohen ever had Article III standing to pursue that kind of claim in the first place, given that the statute routes the penalty to the government. The panel did not resolve that issue, likely because neither side briefed it adequately. But the concurrence suggests that future litigation may have to squarely address whether deterrence alone is enough where the plaintiff’s own pocketbook is no longer at stake.

## The downstream effect: wage cases with government penalties

The practical implications extend beyond Minnesota. Many wage-and-hour statutes pair employee recovery with administrative or civil penalties. If courts treat those penalties as sufficient to preserve jurisdiction after payment of all individualized relief, plaintiffs will be able to keep cases alive longer, and employers will have less ability to end litigation by making the employee whole. If courts go the other way, defendants may be able to moot claims by paying wages and liquidated damages early, leaving only government-enforced penalties to the agency.

* Cohen* does not settle that tension. But it signals that courts are increasingly going to ask hard questions about the plaintiff’s remaining stake once the personal damages are paid. The key inquiry will be whether the statute’s penalty mechanism is enough, by itself, to satisfy Article III in a private suit, or whether the plaintiff must still show an ongoing injury or credible risk of repetition.

For now, the Eighth Circuit has left the door open. But Judge Kobes’s concurrence suggests that the real fight may be over whether the door should have opened at all.
