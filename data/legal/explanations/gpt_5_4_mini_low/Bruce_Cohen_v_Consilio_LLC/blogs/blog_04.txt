Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# A Narrow Win for Employees, or a Blueprint for Employers? What *Cohen v. Consilio* Signals About Strategic Tender in Wage-and-Hour Suits

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* is not a sweeping wage-and-hour ruling. But it is a useful window into a recurring litigation strategy: employers that quickly pay the core wage-and-liquidated-damages amounts in an overtime case, then argue that what remains are either unavailable penalties, moot claims, or requests for relief that the plaintiff has not properly preserved.

That strategy worked in part here. The court affirmed dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim and his wage-theft notice claim, but it vacated and remanded the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act claim because the record did not clearly resolve mootness and therefore jurisdiction. The opinion is especially notable for how much turned on the parties’ stipulations. By agreeing that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA, the parties narrowed the live dispute to penalties and jurisdictional questions. That made the appeal less about whether wages were owed and more about whether a plaintiff can keep a case alive when the only remaining relief is a civil penalty payable to the state.

## Consilio’s payment strategy and the narrowing of the case

Consilio’s move was straightforward: after the suit was filed, it paid the overtime and liquidated-damages amounts demanded under the MFLSA, and the parties stipulated that Cohen had been made whole on those components. That left Cohen pursuing penalties under three Minnesota statutes, plus injunctive relief.

From a defense perspective, this is an attractive path. If the employer can eliminate the private make-whole component early, the case may collapse into questions that are much harder for the plaintiff to use to keep federal jurisdiction: whether penalties are available at all, whether they redress the plaintiff’s injury, and whether the challenged policy is still in effect. That is exactly what happened with the MFLSA claim. The Eighth Circuit did not hold that Cohen lacked standing outright. Instead, it flagged a more threshold problem: if the allegedly unlawful policy has ended and is not likely to recur, then the remaining penalty claim may be moot. If the policy is still in place, the claim may remain live under *Friends of the Earth* because civil penalties can have deterrent value.

That conditional framing is important. The court was careful not to decide mootness on an incomplete record. But the opinion makes clear that once an employer pays the underlying wages, a plaintiff seeking only state-paid penalties may face a serious justiciability fight unless he can show ongoing conduct or a continuing interest in prospective relief.

## The MPWA holding: penalties are for the Commissioner, not the employee

On the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim, the court’s analysis is doctrinally cleaner. Cohen argued that the statute allowed him to recover average daily wage penalties for the alleged overtime violation. The court rejected that reading, relying on the statutory structure: the penalty language in § 181.101 is tied to the Commissioner’s demand-and-collection authority, and the statute’s text distinguishes between the Commissioner’s ability to collect wages and penalties and the employee’s ability to prosecute a claim for wages.

The opinion also leaned on a familiar interpretive point: when the Minnesota legislature wants to authorize employee-driven penalties, it knows how to say so. The court contrasted § 181.101 with other wage statutes that expressly permit the employee to “charge and collect a penalty.” That comparison did a lot of work. It allowed the panel to present its holding not as a policy judgment, but as a textual one.

For wage plaintiffs, the practical lesson is obvious. If a claim under the MPWA is really about penalties, counsel will need to be very precise about who can seek them and under what trigger. The court’s reading leaves little room for employees to transform a wage-payment statute into a private penalty vehicle when the statutory text routes penalties through the agency.

## The MFLSA claim: jurisdiction becomes the battleground

The more interesting part of the opinion is the remand on the MFLSA claim. The Eighth Circuit did not say Cohen’s penalty claim succeeds. It said it may not even remain justiciable. That distinction matters because it shows how a defendant can narrow a case by tendering the principal damages, but still leave the court with a live dispute if the plaintiff can tie penalties to ongoing violations or some other redressable injury.

The panel’s use of *Friends of the Earth* is telling. Civil penalties imposed in favor of the government can sometimes redress private injury indirectly by deterring future violations. But that principle does not eliminate the need to show a live controversy throughout the case. If the employer’s challenged policy has been withdrawn and is not likely to recur, the plaintiff may be left with no personal stake in the outcome.

In that sense, the opinion signals that strategic tender can be effective, but not self-executing. Payment of back wages may reduce the case to a jurisdictional question, not necessarily end it. The plaintiff can still try to preserve the dispute by showing ongoing policy, likely recurrence, or a properly pleaded claim for forward-looking relief. Consilio’s success will therefore depend on what the district court finds on remand about the status of the overtime policy.

## The stipulations shaped everything

One of the most striking features of *Cohen* is how much the appellate posture was dictated by the parties’ own stipulations. They agreed that the most efficient path was to resolve summary judgment first. They also agreed that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed. That saved time, but it also sharply constrained the record.

That matters because remedial and jurisdictional issues are highly sensitive to how the case is framed. Once the core monetary injury is gone, the plaintiff’s remaining theories need to be carefully preserved. Here, the parties’ stipulations made it easy for the court to say the wage claims were essentially over, but harder to tell whether there was still a live controversy over penalties. The result is a remand rather than a clean merits resolution.

For plaintiffs, the lesson is not to avoid stipulations altogether. It is to be careful about what gets stipulated away. If the case may depend on prospective relief, agency-enforceable penalties, fee shifting, or statutory interest, those issues need to be preserved explicitly and supported in the district court record.

## What this means for future wage litigation

The downstream effect of *Cohen* may be modest in doctrinal terms but meaningful in litigation strategy. Employers facing overtime suits may increasingly try to pay the “core” damages early and then argue that the remaining claims are either unavailable under the statute or not enough to support federal jurisdiction. That tactic is especially potent where the statutory penalty scheme routes payment to the state rather than the employee.

Plaintiffs, in turn, will likely respond in a few ways. They may emphasize ongoing and systemic violations so that civil penalties remain tied to a live dispute. They may plead and preserve fee, costs, and interest claims more carefully, since those can sometimes maintain practical stakes even after wages are paid. And they may look for statutes that expressly authorize private penalties or broader injunctive relief, rather than relying on statutes where the penalty mechanism is agency-centered.

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion does not hand employers a universal escape hatch. But it does show how effective early tender can be when paired with a statutory scheme that limits private penalty recovery. In that sense, *Cohen v. Consilio* is less about overtime law itself than about the procedural architecture of wage litigation: who can seek what relief, and whether paying the principal amount is enough to shrink the case to nothing.
