Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# 1. Factual Background, Statutory Claims, and the Case’s Unusual Procedural Posture

## Consilio’s business and Cohen’s role

Consilio, LLC is a nationwide legal document review company, and it maintains offices in Minnesota. Bruce Cohen worked for Consilio beginning in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. That detail matters because the dispute arose from how Consilio compensated hourly employees for overtime work, not from a salaried compensation system or from a dispute over professional-status exemptions. Cohen was one of a group of Minnesota-based employees affected by the company’s overtime policy change.

## The July 2019 email and the new overtime policy

In July 2019, Consilio sent Cohen and other Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers an email announcing a new overtime policy that would begin in August 2019. Under the new policy, Consilio eliminated premium overtime pay and stated that employees would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. That change is the conduct that led to the lawsuit. Cohen contended that Consilio’s policy unlawfully failed to pay overtime wages in the manner required by Minnesota law.

## Cohen’s 2020 lawsuit and the claims he brought

In August 2020, Cohen filed suit on behalf of himself and a putative class. He alleged that Consilio failed to pay overtime wages owed to him and other similarly situated employees. His complaint invoked three Minnesota statutory schemes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act, and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act.

The court’s opinion is careful to distinguish among those statutes because each one supplies different kinds of relief, and that distinction becomes central later in the case. Cohen sought not only unpaid overtime wages, but also liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. In other words, the lawsuit was not just about recovering the wages he believed were due; it also sought additional remedies designed to punish or deter unlawful pay practices.

## The three Minnesota statutory theories

Cohen’s first theory was based on the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, often abbreviated the MPWA. He alleged violations of Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101 and 181.171, subd. 1. His second theory relied on the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act, or MFLSA, specifically Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8. His third theory invoked the Minnesota Wage Theft Act, or MWTA, including provisions of Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032 and 181.171, as well as section 177.27.

Although the opinion does not provide a full doctrinal explanation of each statute at this stage, it makes clear that Cohen believed each law furnished some form of remedy for the alleged failure to pay overtime or to provide required wage notices. The later appellate analysis turns on whether those statutes actually authorize the penalties Cohen wanted.

## The remedies Cohen requested

Cohen’s requested relief is important because the case changed character once Consilio paid the wage component of the dispute. Cohen initially sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Put simply, he wanted back pay plus additional statutory consequences for the alleged violation. As the case progressed, however, the unpaid wages themselves largely dropped out of the controversy, leaving the penalty and injunction issues to do most of the remaining work.

## Consilio’s payment of overtime wages and liquidated damages

After the lawsuit began, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties also stipulated that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he did not dispute that he had been paid any liquidated damages to which he claimed entitlement under the MFLSA.

Those payments were significant because they removed the core wage-dispute issue from the case. What remained was not whether Consilio still owed the underlying overtime compensation, but whether Cohen could still pursue statutory penalties and other non-wage relief.

## The stipulated focus on penalties and other non-wage remedies

Once the wage and liquidated-damages payments were made, the parties agreed that penalties were the unresolved issue. Cohen claimed he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio argued that the statutes did not allow Cohen to recover those penalties.

This is a key procedural shift. The case was no longer a straightforward unpaid-wages action. Instead, the remaining dispute centered on whether Cohen, after receiving the wages and liquidated damages he said he was owed, still had a live claim for statutory penalties and injunctive relief.

## The parties’ agreement to proceed by summary judgment

The parties then stipulated that the most efficient path forward was for the district court to decide Consilio’s summary judgment motion on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before any additional discovery or litigation. They further acknowledged that if the court granted Consilio’s motion in full, no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved.

That stipulation is unusual but important. It shows that the parties deliberately asked the district court to treat the case as one that could be resolved on the existing record, rather than continue with discovery. It also foreshadowed the jurisdictional problem that later emerged on appeal: if the wage claims had already been satisfied, the court had to consider whether there remained a live controversy over penalties and other relief.

## The district court’s complete summary judgment and the issues on appeal

The district court granted Consilio summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appealed. On appeal, the court had to sort through the consequences of the parties’ stipulations and payments, and determine which claims remained viable. The resulting appellate decision affirms the district court in part, but vacates and remands in part because the MFLSA penalty issue raised a possible mootness problem. In that sense, the factual and procedural history of the case matters as much as the overtime policy itself: once the wages were paid, the litigation turned into a dispute over the availability of statutory penalties, the court’s jurisdiction, and whether any live controversy remained.

# 2. Appellate Framework and the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act Claim

## De novo review of summary judgment and the no-genuine-issue standard

The opinion begins this part with the appellate framework because that framework shapes how the court approaches every claim that follows. The Eighth Circuit reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, meaning it gives no deference to the district court’s legal conclusions. Instead, it looks at the record itself, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party, here Cohen. Even under that favorable view, summary judgment is proper if there is no genuine dispute about a material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The court cites this standard to signal that the key issues on the MPWA claim are legal, not factual: the question is what the Minnesota statutes authorize.

## How the court frames the MPWA question as one of statutory authorization for penalties

Cohen’s MPWA claim is not about unpaid wages by the time of appeal. Consilio had already paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages that Cohen said were due under the MFLSA theory, and Cohen agreed he had been paid all overtime wages he claimed in the lawsuit. The remaining dispute on the MPWA side is whether Cohen himself can recover “average daily wage penalties.” The court therefore treats the issue as a question of statutory authorization: does the MPWA let an employee sue for that penalty, or is that power reserved to the Commissioner?

That framing matters. If the statute does not grant an employee the right to seek the penalty, then Cohen’s claim fails as a matter of law even if the underlying wage violation occurred. The opinion’s analysis stays tightly focused on the text of the MPWA provisions that Cohen invoked.

## Section 181.171’s private right of action for specified MPWA violations

The court starts with Minn. Stat. § 181.171, which creates a civil action for violations of certain MPWA provisions, including § 181.101. Section 181.171 says a person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations of the listed provisions and that, when an employer is found to have violated one of them, the employer is liable for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” The statute also says that an employer who violates one of the listed sections is liable to the aggrieved party for “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”

That last phrase is crucial. It means § 181.171 does not independently create every remedy. Instead, it points back to the particular section allegedly violated and asks what that section itself provides. So although § 181.171 gives a private right of action for certain wage-law violations, an employee may recover civil penalties only if the underlying section being sued upon provides for those penalties.

## The significance of § 181.171’s language limiting civil penalties or damages to those “provided for in the section violated”

The court reads this limiting language carefully. Cohen sued under § 181.101, so the question becomes whether § 181.101 itself authorizes an employee to recover average daily wage penalties. If it does, § 181.171 can make those penalties available in a private suit. If it does not, then § 181.171 cannot supply them by itself.

This is why the opinion emphasizes the exact wording of § 181.171. The statute does not say that every employee suing under the MPWA may recover whatever penalties a court thinks appropriate. It says the employee may recover only the penalties or damages “provided for in the section violated.” That textual limit narrows the court’s inquiry to § 181.101.

## Cohen’s argument that § 181.101 plainly authorizes average daily wage penalties

Cohen argues that § 181.101’s “plain language and unambiguous meaning” authorizes an employee to seek average daily wage penalties. He relies on the ordinary textualist move: if the statutory words are clear, they control. The opinion cites Minnesota authority for that interpretive principle, explaining that when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts assume it reflects legislative intent and give it effect.

But the court does not accept Cohen’s reading of § 181.101. It concludes that the statute’s text does not give an employee the right to initiate a penalty claim.

## The court’s reading of § 181.101(a) as assigning penalty enforcement to the Commissioner

The key move in the court’s analysis is that § 181.101(a) repeatedly assigns penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, not the employee. The statute says penalties are available only if the Commissioner first serves a demand for payment on behalf of an employee. If wages are not paid within ten days after that demand, the Commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned and a penalty.

This structure matters because it shows who has the statutory power to act. The court reads the statute as making the Commissioner the actor who demands payment and, if necessary, pursues the penalty. By contrast, the employee is not given that same express enforcement authority.

The court relies on Minnesota cases to reinforce that reading. In particular, it cites *Milner v. Farmers Insurance Exchange* for the proposition that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee, which confirms that the employee is the beneficiary of the Commissioner’s enforcement action rather than the person empowered to impose the penalty directly.

## The statutory sequence: commissioner demand, ten-day nonpayment period, then commissioner collection of wages and penalties

The sequence in § 181.101(a) is central to the holding. First, the Commissioner serves a demand for payment on behalf of the employee. Second, if the employer does not pay within ten days, the Commissioner may collect the wages and a penalty. Third, the money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee.

The court treats that sequence as deliberate. It is not just describing a remedy; it is assigning a procedural path and a decision-maker. That path shows that the penalty is tied to Commissioner enforcement. An employee may be the person whose wages were withheld, but the statute places the authority to seek the penalty in the hands of the Commissioner.

## The distinction between money paid over to the employee and authority to seek the penalty in the first place

A subtle but important point in the opinion is the difference between receiving the money and having authority to demand it. Cohen might be the ultimate recipient of money collected through the statutory process, but that does not mean he can himself initiate the penalty claim. The court distinguishes these concepts clearly.

This distinction is easy to miss if one focuses only on the fact that penalty proceeds end up with the employee. But the court insists that the real question is who may pursue the penalty in court. On the statute’s text, that authority belongs to the Commissioner. The employee’s receipt of the money is a consequence of enforcement, not evidence of personal enforcement power.

## The employee’s preserved right under § 181.101 to prosecute a claim for wages, but not necessarily for statutory penalties

The opinion also notes that § 181.101 preserves an employee’s right to prosecute a claim for wages. That language is important because it confirms that employees are not excluded from all enforcement under the statute. They may sue for wages. But the court draws a line between a wage claim and a penalty claim.

The statute expressly says that it does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages, and it recognizes a substantive right to be paid wages and commissions at the proper time. What it does not say is equally important: it does not say that the employee may personally prosecute a penalty claim under § 181.101. The court reads that omission as meaningful.

## Use of Minnesota interpretive principles, including plain-language analysis

The opinion’s method is straightforward statutory interpretation. It applies the plain-language rule and asks what the statute says, not what Cohen thinks it should say. Once the court concludes that § 181.101 assigns penalty collection to the Commissioner, it does not look for a broader policy justification to override that text.

This is a good example of how appellate courts often resolve statutory-remedy questions. The existence of a wrong—here, alleged unpaid overtime—is not enough. The plaintiff must point to statutory language authorizing the specific remedy sought. The court’s reasoning stays anchored to that principle throughout the MPWA discussion.

## The court’s comparison to §§ 181.13 and 181.14, where the legislature expressly gave employees authority to collect penalties

To support its interpretation, the court compares § 181.101 to other Minnesota wage statutes. Sections 181.13 and 181.14 expressly allow employees to recover penalties in certain situations, such as when a discharged employee or an employee who quits demands unpaid wages or commissions. Those statutes say the employee may “charge and collect a penalty.”

That contrast is a major part of the court’s reasoning. If the legislature wanted employees under § 181.101 to have the same power, it knew how to say so. Because it did not use similar language in § 181.101, the court infers that the omission was intentional.

## The “legislature knows how” inference and its role in the court’s reasoning

The court invokes a familiar interpretive idea: when the legislature uses express penalty language in one statute but not another, courts may infer that the difference was deliberate. The opinion cites *Lagasse v. Horton* and related Minnesota authority for the proposition that the legislature “knows how” to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so.

This inference does not replace textual analysis; it reinforces it. Here, the comparison to §§ 181.13 and 181.14 confirms the court’s reading of § 181.101. Because the legislature expressly gave employees penalty-collecting authority elsewhere, the absence of that language in § 181.101 suggests that penalty authority was reserved to the Commissioner.

## Supporting authority from Minnesota and federal cases, including Allan, Milner, Lagasse, and Hull

Several cases support the court’s interpretation. *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* supplies the plain-language principle. *Milner v. Farmers Insurance Exchange* supports the view that Commissioner-collected money is paid to the employee, consistent with Commissioner-centered enforcement. *Lagasse v. Horton* supports the “legislature knows how” inference. And *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.* is a federal district court decision reading § 181.101(a) to mean that the power to enforce the penalty is limited to the Commissioner, with collected funds going to the employee.

These authorities do not create the rule by themselves; they illustrate and confirm the court’s reading of the statutory text. Together, they make the opinion’s conclusion look like a conventional application of Minnesota statutory interpretation rather than a novel federal gloss.

## The holding that only the Commissioner may seek § 181.101 average daily wage penalties, and affirmance on the MPWA claim

The holding on the MPWA claim is direct: only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. Because Cohen, as a private employee-plaintiff, lacked authority under that statute to pursue the penalty himself, the district court correctly granted summary judgment to Consilio on the MPWA claim.

So even though § 181.171 allows private suits for certain MPWA violations, that provision does not expand Cohen’s remedy beyond what § 181.101 itself authorizes. The court therefore affirms the dismissal of the MPWA claim. This part of the opinion teaches an important appellate lesson: when a statute creates both a right and a remedy, a plaintiff must show that the remedy sought is one the legislature actually made available to him.

# 3. The MFLSA Claim: Paid Wages, Remaining Penalties, and Article III Jurisdiction

## Cohen’s Stipulation Eliminated the Core Wage Dispute

By the time the case reached summary judgment, the parties had largely resolved the ordinary wage issue. Consilio had paid Cohen, and the other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers, the overtime wages and liquidated damages they claimed under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). Cohen himself received both overtime pay and liquidated damages, and the parties stipulated that he had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and that he did not dispute receiving the liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA.

That stipulation mattered because it narrowed the case. Cohen was no longer asking the court to order payment of unpaid wages or liquidated damages under the MFLSA. What remained was a dispute over statutory penalties. Those penalties would not be paid to Cohen directly; the parties agreed they would go to the Commissioner. So the case shifted from a straightforward unpaid-wages dispute to a more difficult jurisdictional question: does Cohen still have a live federal case when the only remaining relief is a civil penalty payable to the government?

## The Remaining Issue: Statutory Penalties and Federal Jurisdiction

The Eighth Circuit did not decide the merits of the penalties question. Instead, it focused on whether the federal courts still had Article III power to decide it. That is why the court said it had a “serious concern” about jurisdiction.

The key point is that federal courts can only decide actual “cases” or “controversies.” That requirement is usually discussed through standing and mootness. Standing asks whether the plaintiff had a sufficient personal stake when the suit began. Mootness asks whether that stake still exists as the case goes forward. The court emphasized that these are connected: the interest required at the start must continue throughout the litigation.

Because Cohen had already been paid everything he personally claimed for wages and liquidated damages, the remaining penalties had to be justified in some other way. The court recognized that civil penalties sometimes can support jurisdiction even if the money is not paid to the plaintiff, because penalties may deter future misconduct and thereby help redress the plaintiff’s injury. But that theory depends on the facts of the case and on whether the challenged conduct is still ongoing or likely to recur.

## Why Standing Must Exist for Each Form of Relief

The court relied on the principle that a plaintiff must show standing not just for the lawsuit in general, but for each claim and each form of relief requested. That is an important refinement. A plaintiff may have standing to seek one remedy, but not another, if the different remedies address different injuries or do different kinds of work under Article III.

The court cited Town of Chester for this proposition. In practical terms, Cohen’s earlier standing to sue for unpaid overtime did not automatically answer whether he could keep litigating statutory penalties after he had already been paid. Once the direct wage injury disappeared, the remaining question became whether penalties would still redress a live injury through deterrence.

## Laidlaw, Deterrence, and Civil Penalties

To explain why penalties might still matter, the court turned to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services. Laidlaw held that civil penalties can sometimes redress a plaintiff’s injury because they may deter the defendant from continuing or repeating unlawful conduct. That is true even though the penalty money goes to the government rather than to the plaintiff.

The Eighth Circuit used that idea to show why Cohen’s MFLSA penalties claim was not automatically dead simply because his wages had been paid. If Consilio’s challenged overtime policy was still in effect, penalties might deter ongoing violations and thus provide enough redress to keep the case alive. In that situation, the court suggested, the claim could remain justiciable.

But Laidlaw also depends on the existence of an ongoing or credibly threatened violation. If the challenged policy had been abandoned and there was no reasonable expectation that it would return, then the deterrence rationale would weaken or disappear. At that point, the claim might be moot.

## Standing, Mootness, and Voluntary Cessation

The opinion also explains the voluntary cessation doctrine. Ordinarily, a defendant cannot evade judicial review simply by stopping the challenged behavior after suit is filed. Otherwise, a defendant could avoid an adverse ruling by temporarily changing course and then resuming the conduct later. So a case is not moot just because the defendant says it has changed its ways.

But the doctrine does not automatically preserve jurisdiction either. The question is whether it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur. If the defendant has truly ended the practice and there is no realistic chance it will restart, the case may become moot.

That is why the court focused on a factual uncertainty: whether Consilio’s overtime policy remained in place. If it did, Cohen’s penalty claim might still be alive because the penalties could deter continued violations. If it did not, and if recurrence was not reasonably possible, then the court might lack jurisdiction.

## Why the Court Remanded Instead of Deciding the Issue Itself

The Eighth Circuit did not resolve the jurisdiction question on the existing record. It said the record left unresolved whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy was still operating. Without that fact, the court could not confidently say whether the case remained live or had become moot.

Rather than decide the merits of the MFLSA penalty claim, the court vacated the summary judgment ruling and remanded. The district court was directed to determine whether it still had jurisdiction. That means the lower court must first answer the threshold Article III question before any further merits decision can be made.

The appellate court’s approach is a reminder that federal courts cannot reach the merits unless jurisdiction exists. Even when the parties focus on statutory interpretation or the availability of penalties, the court must step back and ask whether there is still a live controversy.

## The Special Concurrence

Judge Kobes agreed with vacating and remanding, but wrote separately to express doubt about whether Laidlaw really supplies standing for Cohen’s penalty claim at all. He noted that there may be important differences between the penalties in Laidlaw and the penalties available under the MFLSA, and he thought the parties had not adequately briefed that issue.

He also added that he would vacate the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. But the main point of the concurrence was caution: before assuming that civil penalties preserve standing or defeat mootness, the district court should examine the jurisdictional question carefully on remand.

## Bottom Line

On the MFLSA claim, the court did not decide whether Cohen was entitled to penalties. Instead, it held that once Cohen had been paid his overtime wages and liquidated damages, the remaining penalty claim raised a serious Article III question. Because the record did not clearly show whether Consilio’s challenged policy was still in effect, the Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment on that claim and remanded for the district court to decide jurisdiction first.

# 4. The MWTA Notice Claim and the Limits of Appellate Argument

## The nature of the MWTA allegation

Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim was not about unpaid wages in the ordinary sense. Instead, he alleged that Consilio violated the statute by failing to give the notice required when it changed its overtime policy. The opinion identifies the relevant provisions as Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f) and § 181.032(d)(4), which Cohen said required notice of the employer’s new pay practice. So this claim focused on the employer’s communication procedure, not on whether Cohen had ultimately received the money he was owed.

That distinction matters because the claim was framed as a statutory-notice violation, and Cohen sought penalties as a remedy. The court’s analysis therefore turned less on whether the notice was adequate as a factual matter and more on whether Cohen had shown a legal basis for the relief he wanted.

## Cohen’s theory about the 2019 overtime-policy email

The conduct at issue was Consilio’s July 2019 email announcing a new premium overtime policy that would take effect in August 2019. Under Cohen’s theory, that communication was insufficient under the MWTA because it allegedly did not satisfy the statute’s notice requirements when Consilio changed the terms of overtime compensation.

The opinion does not spend much time parsing the statutory elements of § 181.032. That is a clue to how the court handled the claim. Instead of deciding whether the email technically violated the MWTA, the court focused on what Cohen did—and did not—show in his briefing and on the consequences of his stipulation that all pay owed had already been satisfied.

## The effect of Cohen’s stipulation that he had been paid

A key procedural fact was Cohen’s stipulation that he had already received all compensation Consilio owed him. That stipulation undercut any claim for unpaid wages or other compensation as such. In other words, whatever dispute remained was not about recovering the underlying pay itself.

Once that was established, Cohen needed to identify some independent statutory basis for another form of recovery, such as civil penalties, tied to the notice violation. The court observed that he pointed to no statutory authority authorizing civil penalties under the MWTA notice provision he invoked. That omission was fatal to the appeal as presented.

## The court’s focus on the absence of statutory authority for penalties

The court’s response was straightforward: Cohen “points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” That is the central reason the court affirmed dismissal. In statutory claims, especially wage-and-hour claims, the availability of a remedy depends on what the legislature actually provided. A plaintiff cannot assume that any statutory violation automatically carries penalties; the statute must authorize them.

Here, because Cohen had already been paid, the only apparent remaining relief was penalties. But without citation to a provision allowing penalties for the alleged MWTA notice violation, the appellate court had no basis to disturb the district court’s ruling. The court did not need to decide whether the notice was inadequate in a broader sense because the remedy problem alone was enough to resolve the claim.

## Cohen’s complaint that the district court gave little attention to the claim

Cohen argued on appeal that the district court “included little to no discussion” of the MWTA claim. The court did not treat that as a reason to reverse. A sparse district court analysis does not itself establish error. What matters on appeal is whether the appellant shows a legal or factual basis for reversal.

Because Cohen did not do that, the court was not required to supply an argument on his behalf or independently search for a remedy the statute might permit. The court’s approach reflects a basic appellate principle: the appellant bears the burden of demonstrating why the judgment should be changed.

## Waiver and forfeiture through inadequate briefing on appeal

The court relied on the idea that unsupported arguments are waived or forfeited on appeal. Cohen had not developed a meaningful argument explaining how the MWTA authorized the penalty relief he sought. In appellate practice, merely naming an issue is not enough. A party must support the issue with reasoning, statutory text, and relevant authority.

The court cited Milligan v. City of Red Oak for the proposition that when a brief does not support an assertion with argument or legal authority, the court may treat the issue as waived and decline to address it. That citation served an important function: it gave the court a procedural reason to end the analysis without reaching the merits in depth. The message is that appellate courts do not act as advocates for the losing party.

## Why the court affirmed without a deeper merits analysis

The court affirmed dismissal of the MWTA claim because Cohen’s appellate presentation was insufficient. He had already stipulated that all compensation owed had been paid, and he failed to identify any MWTA provision authorizing the civil penalties he sought for the alleged notice violation. With no developed argument and no cited statutory remedy, there was nothing for the court to overturn.

So, unlike the MFLSA portion of the case, which raised a possible mootness issue requiring remand, the MWTA claim was resolved in a much more ordinary way: the appellant did not carry his burden on appeal. The court therefore left the district court’s dismissal of that claim undisturbed.

# 5. Injunctive Relief, Pleading Discipline, and the Final Disposition

## Cohen’s request for an injunction

By the time the case reached summary judgment, Cohen was no longer focused only on recovering unpaid wages and statutory penalties. He also argued that he was entitled to injunctive relief requiring Consilio to rescind its “illegal overtime policy.” That mattered because injunctive relief is a forward-looking remedy: it is meant to stop or undo ongoing conduct, not simply compensate for past harm.

The court treated this request skeptically because Cohen’s position on the injunction had shifted over the course of the litigation. In his complaint, he had asked for certain injunctive remedies. But in his summary judgment briefing, he reframed the issue and asked for a different order—an affirmative directive that Consilio rescind the 2019 policy. The appellate court emphasized that this revised request was not the same thing as the relief originally pleaded.

## Why the court refused to consider the revised injunction theory

The key procedural point is that parties cannot usually change the shape of a claim or request for relief for the first time in briefing on summary judgment. If a plaintiff wants to pursue a materially different theory, the proper path is to amend the complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), not to smuggle the new request into an opposition brief.

That rule helps explain the court’s citation to InfoDeli, LLC and Rule 15(a). The opinion’s point is not simply technical. Pleadings define the case, and the district court is not required to address a newly minted remedy that was not properly added to the operative complaint. Here, Cohen’s later request for an order rescinding the policy was a new or materially different form of relief, and the district court therefore did not err by failing to address it.

In other words, the appellate court did not reject injunctive relief in the abstract. It held only that the particular injunction Cohen tried to press at summary judgment was not properly before the court.

## The role of pleading discipline

This part of the opinion is a useful reminder that appellate courts care not just about the substance of a claim, but about how the claim was presented. A litigant cannot avoid the amendment process by recasting the case in a summary judgment brief. The complaint sets the bounds of the dispute, and later briefing can clarify or support what is already there, but it cannot ordinarily substitute a new demand for relief.

That principle also explains why the court said the district court “understandably” did not consider the revised request. From the district court’s perspective, there was no properly pleaded claim for the new injunction, so there was nothing to decide.

## The final disposition: what was affirmed and what was vacated

The court’s disposition was mixed. It affirmed the district court in part and vacated and remanded in part.

The court affirmed the district court’s ruling on Cohen’s MPWA claim. It also affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA claim. And it rejected Cohen’s injunction argument as it was actually presented on appeal, because the requested relief had not been properly pleaded and was not before the court.

The court vacated, however, the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim. That was not because the court decided Cohen should win on the merits. Instead, the court said there was a threshold jurisdictional concern: if Cohen had already received all overtime wages and liquidated damages, and if the challenged policy had ended and could not reasonably be expected to recur, his remaining claim for statutory penalties might be moot. Because the record did not clearly resolve that question, the appellate court remanded for the district court to determine whether it still had jurisdiction.

## What the district court must do on remand

On remand, the district court must address standing and mootness as to the MFLSA penalties claim. The opinion explains that if Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still in place, Cohen may still have a live controversy because civil penalties can help deter ongoing unlawful conduct. But if the policy has been withdrawn and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur, then the case may no longer present a live Article III dispute.

The court also left open the practical consequences of that jurisdictional inquiry. If the MFLSA claim is moot, the district court may need to consider whether any remaining relief survives and whether there are consequences for fees, costs, or prejudgment interest. A special concurrence flagged that issue, even though the majority did not resolve it.

## Why this case matters

This opinion shows how a wage-and-hour case can evolve once the employer pays the underlying wages. After payment, the dispute may no longer be about unpaid compensation at all. Instead, the litigation can turn into a question about penalties, deterrence, and federal jurisdiction. The case also shows the importance of pleading discipline: if a plaintiff wants a different injunction, the plaintiff must properly amend the complaint. And even when a party has a strong substantive grievance, Article III limits remain in force. If the underlying harm has been fully remedied and the challenged conduct is gone, a federal court may lack authority to go further.
