Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## Who Can Collect Minnesota Wage Penalties? The Eighth Circuit Draws a Sharp Line Between Private Rights and Commissioner Enforcement

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* is a useful reminder that wage-and-hour litigation often turns less on whether an employee was underpaid than on who, exactly, the legislature authorized to pursue which remedy. That matters because penalty provisions can dramatically change the value of a case. Here, the court drew a sharp line under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act: average-daily-wage penalties under § 181.101 are for the Commissioner to seek, not for private employees to collect directly.

That conclusion is significant for plaintiffs trying to turn a back-pay dispute into a larger statutory recovery. It also reflects a broader pattern in modern statutory interpretation: courts are looking closely at remedial structure, not just broad pro-worker language, to decide whether a private litigant can stack penalties on top of already-paid wages.

## The court’s reading of the MPWA

The MPWA issue was the cleanest part of the opinion. Cohen argued that Consilio’s overtime policy change violated § 181.101 and that the statute allowed him to recover “average daily wage penalties.” The panel disagreed, reading the statutory text and structure to mean that only the Commissioner may initiate and collect that penalty.

The key move was the distinction between the statute’s general private-enforcement language and its specific penalty machinery. Section 181.171 allows a person to bring a civil action for violations of certain MPWA provisions and authorizes “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” But the panel emphasized that the availability of “civil penalties or damages” depends on whether those remedies are “provided for in the section violated.” That led the court back to § 181.101 itself.

There, the statute speaks in terms of the Commissioner serving a demand for payment “on behalf of an employee.” If wages are not paid within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect” both wages and a penalty. The money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned,” but the enforcement authority is the Commissioner’s. The court treated that allocation as dispositive: the employee may prosecute a wage claim, but the penalty remedy belongs to the agency.

The panel also relied on neighboring statutes. In §§ 181.13 and 181.14, the legislature expressly allowed a discharged or resigning employee to “charge and collect a penalty.” That contrast mattered. If the legislature knows how to confer penalty-collection authority on private employees in adjacent provisions, the omission of similar language in § 181.101 looks intentional, not accidental.

## A textualist reading with structural force

This is a classic textualist opinion, but not in a wooden sense. The court did not isolate a phrase and call it a day; it read the remedy scheme as a whole. That matters because Minnesota wage statutes are not a model of elegant drafting. When statutory text assigns wage collection to the Commissioner in one sentence and gives employees only a right to prosecute a claim for wages in another, the panel was on strong ground in refusing to infer a private penalty action.

The decision also reflects a familiar principle in state statutory interpretation: where the legislature creates private enforcement in some provisions but not others, courts generally resist filling in the gap. That is especially true with penalties, which are typically construed according to the statute’s explicit terms. The opinion’s reliance on the Commissioner-centered language in § 181.101 fits comfortably within that approach.

## What the court left unresolved

The MFLSA portion of the opinion is more tentative, but still important. Cohen had already been paid the overtime and liquidated damages he claimed were due under the MFLSA. That left only statutory penalties. The court did not decide whether he could recover those penalties on the merits. Instead, it flagged a jurisdictional problem: if the employer’s challenged conduct stopped and cannot reasonably recur, the claim for penalties may be moot.

That is a meaningful issue in wage cases where the defendant pays the back wages but disputes penalties. The panel recognized that penalties can sometimes supply redress even when the underlying wages have been satisfied, but only if the challenged violation is ongoing or likely to recur. If the employer has abandoned the policy and the dispute is really about a past event with no live controversy, Article III may not permit the case to continue.

The special concurrence sharpened that point, expressing skepticism that Cohen had standing under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* to pursue penalties at all, but acknowledging that the parties had not briefed the issue. On remand, the district court will have to sort out whether the MFLSA penalty claim is live or moot before reaching the merits.

## Downstream effects for wage plaintiffs

The practical implication for plaintiffs’ lawyers is straightforward: if the wage claim has been paid, the remaining fight may not be as broad as the complaint suggests. Under this decision, plaintiffs cannot assume that penalty provisions in the MPWA are automatically available as add-ons to a private overtime action. Where the statutory text routes penalty enforcement through the Commissioner, courts may treat the employee’s role as limited to recovering wages.

That could narrow settlement leverage in Minnesota wage cases. Plaintiffs often use penalty exposure to encourage resolution after the employer pays the underlying compensation. The Eighth Circuit’s reading makes clear that not every unpaid-wage statute supplies that leverage. Remedy stacking will depend on close attention to the specific section invoked, not just the general policy against wage theft.

More broadly, the decision illustrates how courts will separate compensation from enforcement authority. Even in a worker-protective statutory scheme, a court may insist that penalties are a public-enforcement tool unless the legislature unmistakably says otherwise. For employees and class-action practitioners, that is a reminder to plead carefully, identify the exact source of penalty authority, and be prepared to show a live injury if the employer has already cut the check.



# Paid in Full, But Still a Live Case? Mootness, Deterrence, and Article III in the MFLSA Penalties Remand

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* is not, at bottom, a sweeping wage-and-hour ruling. It is narrower than that. But the panel’s treatment of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act claim raises a recurring and important Article III question: when an employer has paid the worker everything the worker personally claims to be owed, can a lawsuit continue on the theory that civil penalties — payable not to the employee, but to the Commissioner — still keep the case alive?

That question matters well beyond this case. Wage statutes increasingly pair employee recovery with government-facing penalties designed to deter misconduct. When the private plaintiff has been made whole on wages and liquidated damages, courts have to decide whether any remaining penalty claim supplies a live personal stake, or whether the case has become moot. The Eighth Circuit did not answer that question outright. Instead, it vacated and remanded for the district court to determine whether the case is still justiciable. But the panel’s discussion, and Judge Kobes’s concurrence, strongly suggest that the real problem may be deeper than mootness.

## The panel’s move: no wages left, maybe no case left

The setup is straightforward. Cohen alleged that Consilio violated Minnesota overtime law, but before the court ruled on summary judgment, Consilio paid him and other similarly situated employees the overtime wages and liquidated damages they sought under the MFLSA. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid everything he was owed on those fronts. What remained was statutory penalties.

That posture immediately triggered the Article III question the panel highlighted: if the only remaining relief is a penalty payable to the Commissioner, does Cohen still have a live case? The court was careful. It did not say the case is moot. It said there is a “serious concern” about jurisdiction, because the record left unresolved whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy was still in place. If it remained in effect, the panel reasoned, Cohen’s penalty claim might still present a live controversy under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, which held that civil penalties can redress ongoing or threatened injury by deterring unlawful conduct. But if the challenged policy had been abandoned and recurrence was “absolutely clear” not to happen, the claim could be moot.

That is a sensible, cautious way to resolve an underdeveloped record. The court resisted the temptation to decide an Article III issue in the abstract when the underlying facts — especially whether the challenged policy had truly ceased — were disputed or at least unclear. In that sense, the remand is less a substantive holding than an insistence on jurisdictional housekeeping.

## Laidlaw and the deterrence theory of redress

The panel’s reliance on *Laidlaw* is important. That case remains the Supreme Court’s leading statement that civil penalties can redress private injury even when the penalties are not paid to the plaintiff. The logic is deterrence: if the prospect of penalties discourages continued violations, then the plaintiff has some redress for the harm caused by unlawful conduct.

But *Laidlaw* does not eliminate Article III’s limits. It was decided in the context of a citizen suit challenging ongoing environmental violations, where the defendant’s voluntary cessation did not make the case go away. The Court emphasized that a case becomes moot only when it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur.

That standard gives plaintiffs room to keep a penalty claim alive where a defendant stops its conduct only after being sued. It does not automatically do the same where the defendant has already paid all individualized compensation and the remaining penalty is legally owed to the state rather than to the plaintiff. That is the crucial tension in *Cohen*. The panel recognized the deterrence principle, but it did not assume that deterrence alone answers the jurisdictional question. Instead, it asked whether the penalty claim still corresponds to an ongoing or likely recurring injury to Cohen himself.

## Standing, mootness, and the unresolved personal stake

The doctrinal wrinkle is that this case sits at the boundary between standing and mootness. Standing asks whether the plaintiff had a sufficient injury at the outset; mootness asks whether that live stake continues. The Eighth Circuit cited its own precedent for the proposition that the “requisite personal interest” must persist throughout the litigation.

That distinction matters because Cohen’s requested penalties do not go to him. They go to the Commissioner. So even if the penalties may deter future violations in some abstract sense, the court was not prepared to say that deterrence alone establishes a continuing personal stake for Cohen once his own wage and liquidated-damages claims were satisfied. That is especially so because the statutory penalties he seeks are not the kind of individualized monetary relief that directly compensates him for a loss.

The panel stopped short of holding that a private plaintiff can never maintain such a claim. But its remand reflects a real skepticism that a plaintiff retains Article III jurisdiction simply by keeping alive a claim for penalties that the plaintiff will never receive. The court wants to know whether there is still an ongoing violation or a credible threat of recurrence that connects the penalty request to a concrete dispute between these parties.

## Judge Kobes’s concurrence points to the harder question

Judge Kobes’s special concurrence is the most revealing part of the decision. He agreed that the case should be vacated and remanded, but he said he was not convinced Cohen had standing to seek MFLSA penalties under *Laidlaw* at all. In other words, he flagged the possibility that this is not merely a mootness problem arising from post-filing events. It may be an original standing problem: whether a plaintiff can ever sue for penalties payable to the Commissioner when the plaintiff is already fully compensated.

That is a significant hint. If Judge Kobes is right, the question is not whether Consilio’s payment mooted an otherwise valid penalty claim. It is whether Cohen ever had Article III standing to pursue that kind of claim in the first place, given that the statute routes the penalty to the government. The panel did not resolve that issue, likely because neither side briefed it adequately. But the concurrence suggests that future litigation may have to squarely address whether deterrence alone is enough where the plaintiff’s own pocketbook is no longer at stake.

## The downstream effect: wage cases with government penalties

The practical implications extend beyond Minnesota. Many wage-and-hour statutes pair employee recovery with administrative or civil penalties. If courts treat those penalties as sufficient to preserve jurisdiction after payment of all individualized relief, plaintiffs will be able to keep cases alive longer, and employers will have less ability to end litigation by making the employee whole. If courts go the other way, defendants may be able to moot claims by paying wages and liquidated damages early, leaving only government-enforced penalties to the agency.

* Cohen* does not settle that tension. But it signals that courts are increasingly going to ask hard questions about the plaintiff’s remaining stake once the personal damages are paid. The key inquiry will be whether the statute’s penalty mechanism is enough, by itself, to satisfy Article III in a private suit, or whether the plaintiff must still show an ongoing injury or credible risk of repetition.

For now, the Eighth Circuit has left the door open. But Judge Kobes’s concurrence suggests that the real fight may be over whether the door should have opened at all.



## The Limits of Wage Theft Act Litigation: Waiver, Remedies, and the Cost of Underdeveloped Appellate Arguments

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* is, at bottom, a reminder that wage-and-hour litigation is not won on the equities alone. Even in a case where an employee alleged a plainly troubling overtime policy, the court drew hard lines around remedy, pleading, and appellate presentation. That was enough to dispose of Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim and his belated request for injunctive relief. For practitioners, the opinion is a useful lesson in how quickly a sympathetic wage case can narrow when the plaintiff has already been paid the underlying wages and does not build a developed theory for statutory penalties or equitable relief.

The panel’s broader disposition also shows a familiar appellate pattern: claims that are legally plausible in the abstract can still fail if the record and briefing do not support them. On the MWTA claim, Cohen got no traction because he had conceded full payment and pointed to no statutory basis for penalties. On the injunction issue, he ran into a different problem—he asked for a different form of relief in summary judgment briefing than the one he had pleaded, and the court would not entertain that pivot.

## The MWTA claim: no penalties without statutory footing

The court’s treatment of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim is straightforward, but important. Cohen argued that Consilio failed to provide the notice required by the statute when it changed its overtime policy, and he sought penalties. The problem was that he had already stipulated that he had been paid all wages owed. At that point, the only way the claim could survive was if the MWTA independently authorized the kind of penalty he wanted.

The panel said Cohen did not make that showing. He “points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute,” and that omission was fatal. The court did not need to decide whether the underlying notice violation occurred, because a violation without an available remedy does not produce a winning appellate issue. That may sound formalistic, but it reflects the structure of Minnesota wage statutes: some provisions expressly authorize penalties; others do not. The opinion treats that distinction as dispositive.

The most striking feature here is not the result, but the litigation posture. Cohen’s claim was not rejected after a merits-heavy interpretive analysis of the MWTA’s penalty scheme. It was effectively waived by underdevelopment. The panel cited the familiar rule that arguments unsupported by legal authority are forfeited. In practical terms, that meant Cohen needed to do more than say the statute was violated and that penalties should follow. He needed to identify the source of that remedy, explain why it applied notwithstanding full payment, and give the court a reason to read the statute expansively. He did none of that.

## Why waiver mattered so much

This is where wage litigation often runs into a recurring appellate hazard. Plaintiffs may have substantial claims for unpaid wages, but once the employer tenders payment, the case shifts from compensation to statutory enforcement. At that point, the litigation turns on whether the plaintiff can anchor a penalty or fee request in the text of the relevant statute and preserve that theory cleanly on appeal.

Here, the panel was unsparing: no meaningful argument, no reversal. That is a useful signal to litigants, especially in statutory wage cases where remedy questions can be more important than the underlying wage calculation. If the plaintiff’s recovery depends on a penalty provision, that provision has to be litigated like a claim in its own right.

The decision also hints at a deeper point about remedial statutes. Courts are often willing to infer robust employee protections, but they are far less willing to infer penalties where the legislature did not plainly provide them. The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning suggests that if the MWTA is to be read to permit penalties in this context, that argument must be made explicitly, and made early.

## The injunction request: you cannot rewrite the case in briefing

Cohen’s request for injunctive relief failed for a different reason: he changed the shape of the remedy after the case was already framed. In his complaint, he sought one set of injunction-related remedies. In summary judgment briefing, he asked for something different—an affirmative order requiring Consilio to rescind its overtime policy. The court declined to consider that request, noting that it was not properly before it and that new claims belong in amended pleadings, not opposition briefs.

That holding is not dramatic, but it is doctrinally tidy. Federal litigation depends on notice and framing. A plaintiff cannot plead one form of equitable relief, then pivot to another later and expect the court to treat the new theory as if it were always there. The Eighth Circuit’s citation to the rule that new claims are properly raised through amended complaints reinforces that point.

The practical effect is that injunctive relief is not a catchall after monetary claims have been satisfied. If a plaintiff wants an injunction—especially one designed to alter a companywide policy—he has to say so clearly, tie it to the pleaded causes of action, and give the district court a record on which to evaluate necessity, scope, and standing. Cohen’s belated request did not do that.

## The broader lesson for wage cases

The *Cohen* opinion is not a wholesale rejection of wage-theft enforcement. Indeed, the panel did vacate and remand on the MFLSA penalties issue because of a possible mootness problem, which suggests the court is attentive to Article III limits when the underlying wages have already been paid. But the MWTA and injunction rulings show that even meritorious wage disputes can narrow sharply when the plaintiff’s remedial theory is underdeveloped.

That matters because wage cases increasingly involve layered remedies: unpaid wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, notice violations, and equitable relief. Each one may have a different source of authority and a different jurisdictional posture. When the wage claim itself has been satisfied, the plaintiff must be able to identify what survives and why. Failure to do so can turn a substantive grievance into a procedural loss.

For litigants, the takeaway is simple: in wage-and-hour cases, remedy architecture is not secondary. It is the case. Plaintiffs who do not plead and brief the remedy question with precision risk losing on waiver grounds even when the facts initially look favorable. *Cohen v. Consilio* is a good example of that dynamic—and a cautionary one.



# A Narrow Win for Employees, or a Blueprint for Employers? What *Cohen v. Consilio* Signals About Strategic Tender in Wage-and-Hour Suits

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* is not a sweeping wage-and-hour ruling. But it is a useful window into a recurring litigation strategy: employers that quickly pay the core wage-and-liquidated-damages amounts in an overtime case, then argue that what remains are either unavailable penalties, moot claims, or requests for relief that the plaintiff has not properly preserved.

That strategy worked in part here. The court affirmed dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim and his wage-theft notice claim, but it vacated and remanded the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act claim because the record did not clearly resolve mootness and therefore jurisdiction. The opinion is especially notable for how much turned on the parties’ stipulations. By agreeing that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA, the parties narrowed the live dispute to penalties and jurisdictional questions. That made the appeal less about whether wages were owed and more about whether a plaintiff can keep a case alive when the only remaining relief is a civil penalty payable to the state.

## Consilio’s payment strategy and the narrowing of the case

Consilio’s move was straightforward: after the suit was filed, it paid the overtime and liquidated-damages amounts demanded under the MFLSA, and the parties stipulated that Cohen had been made whole on those components. That left Cohen pursuing penalties under three Minnesota statutes, plus injunctive relief.

From a defense perspective, this is an attractive path. If the employer can eliminate the private make-whole component early, the case may collapse into questions that are much harder for the plaintiff to use to keep federal jurisdiction: whether penalties are available at all, whether they redress the plaintiff’s injury, and whether the challenged policy is still in effect. That is exactly what happened with the MFLSA claim. The Eighth Circuit did not hold that Cohen lacked standing outright. Instead, it flagged a more threshold problem: if the allegedly unlawful policy has ended and is not likely to recur, then the remaining penalty claim may be moot. If the policy is still in place, the claim may remain live under *Friends of the Earth* because civil penalties can have deterrent value.

That conditional framing is important. The court was careful not to decide mootness on an incomplete record. But the opinion makes clear that once an employer pays the underlying wages, a plaintiff seeking only state-paid penalties may face a serious justiciability fight unless he can show ongoing conduct or a continuing interest in prospective relief.

## The MPWA holding: penalties are for the Commissioner, not the employee

On the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim, the court’s analysis is doctrinally cleaner. Cohen argued that the statute allowed him to recover average daily wage penalties for the alleged overtime violation. The court rejected that reading, relying on the statutory structure: the penalty language in § 181.101 is tied to the Commissioner’s demand-and-collection authority, and the statute’s text distinguishes between the Commissioner’s ability to collect wages and penalties and the employee’s ability to prosecute a claim for wages.

The opinion also leaned on a familiar interpretive point: when the Minnesota legislature wants to authorize employee-driven penalties, it knows how to say so. The court contrasted § 181.101 with other wage statutes that expressly permit the employee to “charge and collect a penalty.” That comparison did a lot of work. It allowed the panel to present its holding not as a policy judgment, but as a textual one.

For wage plaintiffs, the practical lesson is obvious. If a claim under the MPWA is really about penalties, counsel will need to be very precise about who can seek them and under what trigger. The court’s reading leaves little room for employees to transform a wage-payment statute into a private penalty vehicle when the statutory text routes penalties through the agency.

## The MFLSA claim: jurisdiction becomes the battleground

The more interesting part of the opinion is the remand on the MFLSA claim. The Eighth Circuit did not say Cohen’s penalty claim succeeds. It said it may not even remain justiciable. That distinction matters because it shows how a defendant can narrow a case by tendering the principal damages, but still leave the court with a live dispute if the plaintiff can tie penalties to ongoing violations or some other redressable injury.

The panel’s use of *Friends of the Earth* is telling. Civil penalties imposed in favor of the government can sometimes redress private injury indirectly by deterring future violations. But that principle does not eliminate the need to show a live controversy throughout the case. If the employer’s challenged policy has been withdrawn and is not likely to recur, the plaintiff may be left with no personal stake in the outcome.

In that sense, the opinion signals that strategic tender can be effective, but not self-executing. Payment of back wages may reduce the case to a jurisdictional question, not necessarily end it. The plaintiff can still try to preserve the dispute by showing ongoing policy, likely recurrence, or a properly pleaded claim for forward-looking relief. Consilio’s success will therefore depend on what the district court finds on remand about the status of the overtime policy.

## The stipulations shaped everything

One of the most striking features of *Cohen* is how much the appellate posture was dictated by the parties’ own stipulations. They agreed that the most efficient path was to resolve summary judgment first. They also agreed that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed. That saved time, but it also sharply constrained the record.

That matters because remedial and jurisdictional issues are highly sensitive to how the case is framed. Once the core monetary injury is gone, the plaintiff’s remaining theories need to be carefully preserved. Here, the parties’ stipulations made it easy for the court to say the wage claims were essentially over, but harder to tell whether there was still a live controversy over penalties. The result is a remand rather than a clean merits resolution.

For plaintiffs, the lesson is not to avoid stipulations altogether. It is to be careful about what gets stipulated away. If the case may depend on prospective relief, agency-enforceable penalties, fee shifting, or statutory interest, those issues need to be preserved explicitly and supported in the district court record.

## What this means for future wage litigation

The downstream effect of *Cohen* may be modest in doctrinal terms but meaningful in litigation strategy. Employers facing overtime suits may increasingly try to pay the “core” damages early and then argue that the remaining claims are either unavailable under the statute or not enough to support federal jurisdiction. That tactic is especially potent where the statutory penalty scheme routes payment to the state rather than the employee.

Plaintiffs, in turn, will likely respond in a few ways. They may emphasize ongoing and systemic violations so that civil penalties remain tied to a live dispute. They may plead and preserve fee, costs, and interest claims more carefully, since those can sometimes maintain practical stakes even after wages are paid. And they may look for statutes that expressly authorize private penalties or broader injunctive relief, rather than relying on statutes where the penalty mechanism is agency-centered.

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion does not hand employers a universal escape hatch. But it does show how effective early tender can be when paired with a statutory scheme that limits private penalty recovery. In that sense, *Cohen v. Consilio* is less about overtime law itself than about the procedural architecture of wage litigation: who can seek what relief, and whether paying the principal amount is enough to shrink the case to nothing.
