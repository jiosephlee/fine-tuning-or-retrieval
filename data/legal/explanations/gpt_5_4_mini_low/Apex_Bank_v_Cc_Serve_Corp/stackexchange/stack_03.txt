Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why was the Board's sixth DuPont factor analysis a legal error rather than just a factual judgment about weak evidence?
Question:
The Federal Circuit said the Board committed legal error by limiting the sixth factor to third-party Aspire marks used only for credit card services, instead of also considering banking and financing services that the Board had already found highly similar. I don't fully understand why this is classified as a legal flaw instead of just a debatable factual weighing of relevance. Was the problem that the Board applied the wrong legal standard for what counts as "similar goods or services" under factor six?
Answer:
Because the Federal Circuit thought the Board used the **wrong legal frame** for factor six, not merely that it gave the evidence too little weight.

The key distinction is this:

- **Factual judgment:** how much probative value to assign to the third-party “Aspire” evidence once the correct universe of relevant goods/services is identified.
- **Legal error:** deciding the wrong universe of relevant goods/services in the first place.

Under DuPont factor six, the Board must consider the **“number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”**. The opinion makes clear that “similar” does **not** mean identical. Citing *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*, the court says factor six applies to **similar goods and services**, not only the exact same ones.

That is why the Federal Circuit treated the Board’s limitation to **credit card services only** as a legal flaw. The Board had already found, under factor two, that Apex’s banking/financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were **highly similar** and even **“legally identical, in part.”** Once that finding was made, the court said the Board should not have imposed a narrower similarity standard when evaluating third-party marks under factor six.

So the problem was not just “the Board could have weighed the evidence differently.” The problem was that the Board **excluded a category of evidence that the correct legal standard required it to consider**. In the court’s words:

> “We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”

That is classic legal error: applying an **incorrect rule** to define relevance. The factual question on remand is then how much weight the broader set of Aspire-formative marks should receive.
