Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Chapter 1: The Dispute, the Marks, and the Procedural Path to Appeal

## CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration: ownership, registration date, and priority date

This case begins with CC Serve’s ownership of a federal registration for the word mark **ASPIRE**. The registration, No. 2126948, issued in 1998 and carried an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. That priority date matters because, in a trademark dispute, the earlier user generally has the stronger claim to protect its mark against later confusingly similar uses. Here, CC Serve’s registration gave it the position of the prior user when Apex later sought to register related marks.

## How CC Serve uses ASPIRE in connection with credit card services through partner banks

CC Serve does not itself issue credit cards in the ordinary retail-bank sense. Instead, it offers credit card services by partnering with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded cards and account services to customers. CC Serve and its affiliates then service those accounts. For purposes of trademark law, though, the Board and the court focus on the services as identified in the registration and applications, not just the company’s internal business model. That becomes important later, because Apex argued that CC Serve was really not offering “banking services” in the same way Apex planned to.

## Apex Bank’s business model and its planned internet bank under the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks

Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with branch locations and a traditional set of banking products: checking accounts, savings accounts, mortgages, and consumer and business loans. It does not offer credit cards. Apex also planned to launch an internet bank under a different brand, but it sought trademark protection for the names **ASPIRE BANK** in both word and design form. Those proposed marks were intended for banking and financing services. In other words, Apex was not trying to register a mark for credit cards specifically; it was trying to use ASPIRE BANK in the broader banking and financing space.

## Apex’s 2019 intent-to-use applications for ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services”

In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the USPTO for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. An intent-to-use application signals that the applicant has a bona fide plan to use the mark in commerce but has not yet started that use. The services listed in the applications were “banking and financing services.” That description later became central to the Board’s and the Federal Circuit’s DuPont analysis, because the degree of similarity between Apex’s listed services and CC Serve’s services affected the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.

## CC Serve’s letter of protest during examination and the marks’ publication despite that protest

During prosecution, CC Serve filed a letter of protest with the USPTO, arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to ASPIRE. A letter of protest is a way for a third party to alert the examining attorney to reasons an application should not be approved. Even so, the examining attorney allowed Apex’s applications to proceed, and the marks were published for opposition on December 17, 2019. Publication is significant because it opens the door for an interested party to challenge the application before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board.

## The TTAB opposition initiated by CC Serve under Lanham Act Section 2(d)

After publication, CC Serve filed an opposition in February 2020. The opposition invoked Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, which bars registration when a proposed mark is likely to cause confusion with a prior mark. This is the core trademark-registration dispute mechanism in the case. CC Serve alleged that consumers would likely confuse Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks with its own ASPIRE mark.

## The Board’s conclusion that confusion was likely and its refusal of registration

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained the opposition and refused registration of Apex’s marks. The Board concluded that the marks were likely to confuse consumers under the DuPont likelihood-of-confusion framework. Although multiple DuPont factors were discussed, the basic premise was that Apex’s marks and CC Serve’s mark were too close in light of the services at issue and the marketplace context. That refusal is what Apex challenged on appeal.

## Apex’s appeal to the Federal Circuit and the court’s jurisdiction

Apex appealed the TTAB’s refusal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The court had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which gives the Federal Circuit authority over appeals from final decisions of the TTAB in trademark cases. On review, the court addressed whether the Board correctly applied the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, especially as to the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. In this opening chapter of the case, the key point is procedural: the dispute moved from trademark filing and administrative opposition to appellate review because Apex challenged the Board’s refusal to register its marks.

# Chapter 2: The Governing Framework—Section 2(d), DuPont, and Appellate Review

## Section 2(d) and the basic bar against likely confusion

This appeal arises under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, which bars registration of a mark when its use is likely to cause confusion with a prior user’s mark. In practical terms, the question is whether consumers encountering both marks on the relevant goods or services would think they come from the same source, are affiliated, or are otherwise connected. That is why CC Serve’s opposition mattered: CC Serve claimed priority through its earlier ASPIRE registration and argued that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks would confuse consumers.

The court’s recitation of the facts shows why the Board treated this as a traditional opposition case. CC Serve owned an earlier registration for ASPIRE used with credit card services, while Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board had to compare the marks and the services in light of the marketplace context, not simply ask whether the words were identical. The Section 2(d) inquiry is broader than literal duplication; it turns on likely confusion in the minds of ordinary purchasers.

## Likelihood of confusion as the ultimate legal question

The opinion emphasizes an important doctrinal point: “likelihood of confusion” is the ultimate legal conclusion, even though it rests on subsidiary factual findings. That distinction matters on appeal. The Board makes the underlying determinations about the marks, the services, the channels of trade, third-party use, and other relevant considerations. The court then reviews whether those factual findings are supported and whether the Board applied the correct legal framework.

Here, the Federal Circuit repeatedly frames the dispute in terms of whether the Board’s analysis of particular DuPont factors was legally sound. That is because the Board can be wrong in at least two different ways. It can make unsupported factual findings, or it can use the wrong legal standard when evaluating those facts. The court’s remedy depends on which kind of error occurred.

## The DuPont factors as the underlying factual inquiries

The court relies on the familiar DuPont framework, drawn from the C.C.P.A.’s decision in In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co. The DuPont factors are the structured set of considerations used to evaluate likelihood of confusion. The opinion makes clear that each factor is a factual inquiry, but the overall likelihood-of-confusion determination is a legal conclusion built from those findings.

The Federal Circuit also notes that the Board does not need to address every DuPont factor in every case. It must consider only those factors that are relevant and supported by the record. That point is important because trademark disputes vary widely. Some factors may be irrelevant, and others may be decisive. In this case, the Board focused on the first, second, and sixth factors because those were the ones Apex challenged on appeal and because they were central to the confusion analysis.

## Appellate review: de novo for legal conclusions, substantial evidence for facts

The standard of review explains the structure of the court’s analysis. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, meaning it independently decides whether the Board used the correct legal rule. But the Board’s factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, which is a deferential standard. Under that standard, the court asks only whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the finding.

This distinction is central to the case. If Apex were merely arguing that the evidence could have been weighed differently, the court would be limited by substantial-evidence review. But Apex also argued that the Board used the wrong legal scope when analyzing the sixth DuPont factor. That is a legal issue, and the court is free to correct it.

## Why the legal-versus-factual distinction drives the outcome here

The opinion shows the practical importance of separating evidentiary sufficiency from legal error. The court affirms the Board’s second-factor finding because it concludes that substantial evidence supports the determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. That is a factual conclusion and the Board did not err in the way it evaluated the service descriptions.

By contrast, the court vacates the Board’s sixth-factor analysis because the Board adopted too narrow a universe of relevant third-party marks. The court treats that as a legal mistake in how the Board defined the scope of similarity under the factor, not merely a disagreement about the weight of the evidence. Because the sixth factor may affect the commercial-strength analysis, the first factor must also be reconsidered. In other words, once the court finds a legal flaw in the Board’s framework, it cannot simply defer to the Board’s factual weighing as though nothing had changed.

## The challenged DuPont factors: first, second, and sixth

Apex challenged three DuPont factors on appeal. The second factor concerns the similarity of the parties’ services. The first factor concerns the similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The sixth factor concerns the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods or services, which goes to the conceptual and commercial strength of the senior user’s mark.

The Board found that the second factor favored CC Serve because credit card services and banking/financing services overlap in important ways. It also found that the first factor favored CC Serve, and that the sixth factor did not meaningfully show weakness in CC Serve’s mark. The Federal Circuit upheld the second-factor finding but vacated the first and sixth-factor findings because the Board’s sixth-factor analysis was too narrow and that error could affect the overall impression of the marks.

That is the procedural and doctrinal backdrop for the remainder of the opinion: the court is not deciding the registration question from scratch. Instead, it is reviewing how the Board applied the DuPont framework and determining which findings can stand and which must be reconsidered.

# Chapter 3: The Second DuPont Factor—Similarity of the Parties’ Services

## The Legal Standard for Relatedness

The second DuPont factor asks whether the parties’ goods or services are sufficiently related that consumers could mistakenly believe they come from the same source. The key point is that the services do not have to be identical. The Federal Circuit repeated the familiar rule that it is enough if the services are “related in some manner” or if the way they are marketed could lead consumers to think they originate from the same place. That standard matters because trademark law is concerned with likely consumer confusion, not formal categories. Even when two parties operate in different parts of the financial-services market, the question is whether the overlap in what they do makes source confusion plausible.

## Defining “Credit Card Services” After the ID Manual Entry Was Deleted

A complication in this case was that the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been deleted after CC Serve’s registration. That meant the Board could not simply rely on the current manual entry to understand the scope of CC Serve’s registered services. Instead, it had to determine what the registration actually covered. The Board therefore interpreted the term by looking to the ordinary meaning of the service description and the surrounding evidence. This was important because the scope of CC Serve’s services would shape the relatedness analysis under the second factor.

## What the Board Understood Credit Card Services To Include

The Board concluded that “credit card services” is broader than just issuing a plastic card. It includes issuing credit cards for purchases, managing credit card accounts, processing payments and transactions, authorizing charges, monitoring accounts, providing alerts, counseling on credit card debt, and even providing access to credit scores. That broad understanding helped the Board see CC Serve’s services as a suite of financial functions rather than a narrow product line. Once the service was defined this way, the overlap with Apex’s proposed banking and financing services became much more apparent.

## Why the Board Looked to Dictionary Definitions of Banking and Finance

To compare the parties’ services, the Board also examined the meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.” It found that these terms include extending credit and providing funds, which can occur through the issuance of credit cards. In other words, the Board reasoned that the ordinary meaning of Apex’s services reaches some of the same financial activities involved in CC Serve’s credit card business. That led the Board to conclude that the services are “legally identical, in part.” The phrase does not mean the companies sell exactly the same thing in every respect. Rather, it reflects that the legal descriptions of the services overlap at least to some degree.

## Why the Board Found the Services Highly Similar

The Board also relied on third-party registrations that covered both credit card services and banking or financing services. Those registrations showed that the marketplace and the PTO itself treat these kinds of services as capable of coming from a single source under one mark. That is an important use of third-party registrations in a trademark case: they are not direct proof of actual confusion, but they can help show that consumers might expect related services to be offered under the same brand. Combining the overlap in the service descriptions with the third-party registration evidence, the Board concluded that the services were highly similar and that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of likely confusion.

## Apex’s Argument on Appeal

Apex argued that this analysis overstated the overlap. Its position was that the two companies are not direct competitors and that CC Serve does not itself provide banking services. Instead, CC Serve partners with banks that issue and manage the cards. From Apex’s perspective, that distinction meant its own banking services should not be treated as the same as CC Serve’s credit card services. Apex therefore asked the court to reject the Board’s view that the services were closely related.

## Why the Federal Circuit Rejected Apex’s Challenge

The Federal Circuit was not persuaded. It emphasized that the Board had carefully examined the service descriptions and that the finding of high similarity was supported by substantial evidence. The court did not require direct competition or identical services. What mattered was the relatedness of the services and the possibility that consumers could think they came from the same source. On that record, the court held that the Board did not err in concluding that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar.

## Why This Factor Matters to the Rest of the Opinion

The court’s affirmance of the second DuPont factor was significant beyond this one issue. The Board’s conclusion that the services were highly similar became the baseline for analyzing the other DuPont factors, especially the sixth factor, which concerns third-party use of similar marks on similar goods, and the first factor, which concerns the overall similarity of the marks. The Federal Circuit later faulted the Board for using too narrow a view of similarity when analyzing those other factors. But it did not disturb the Board’s core finding here: the parties’ services overlap enough to support a likelihood-of-confusion analysis. That factual foundation shaped the rest of the case.

# Chapter 4: The Sixth DuPont Factor—Third-Party Use, Mark Strength, and the Error in Defining the Relevant Field

## The Sixth DuPont Factor and What It Is Supposed to Measure

The sixth DuPont factor asks about “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” In a likelihood-of-confusion case, this factor is important because it goes to the strength of the senior user’s mark in the marketplace. A mark may be conceptually strong on its face—meaning it is inherently distinctive—but still be commercially weak if many other businesses use similar marks on similar goods or services. If consumers are repeatedly exposed to a crowded field of similar marks, they may become better at distinguishing among them, and the scope of protection for any one mark may be narrower.

That is the basic idea the Federal Circuit had in mind here. The Board was not deciding whether ASPIRE was distinctive enough to be registered in the abstract. It was deciding how much protection ASPIRE should receive against Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks. The sixth factor mattered because Apex tried to show that “Aspire” was used by others in the financial-services marketplace, which would suggest that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to a more limited scope of protection.

## Why Third-Party Use Evidence Matters

The Federal Circuit reiterated a settled principle: evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods or services can show that a mark is relatively weak and therefore entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The logic is practical. If a term is commonly used by several market participants, consumers may not automatically assume that every use of that term points to the same source. Instead, they may learn to pay attention to other differences in the marks.

This does not mean that any third-party use is enough to weaken a mark. The evidence has to be meaningful in light of the relevant market. The court’s earlier cases, such as Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin, recognized that extensive third-party use can matter a great deal, especially when the field is crowded enough that consumers are conditioned to distinguish among similar marks. But the inquiry remains contextual. The key question is not simply how many similar marks exist somewhere in commerce, but how many similar marks exist on similar goods or services in the relevant marketplace.

## Apex’s Third-Party Evidence

Apex submitted several exhibits showing third-party use of marks containing the word “Aspire.” Some of those uses were in connection with credit card-related services. Others were more generally tied to financial services, banking, or financing. Apex’s point was straightforward: if “Aspire” and Aspire-formative marks are already being used by multiple businesses in the financial-services sector, then CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark should not be treated as especially strong.

The Board accepted that some of the evidence showed third-party uses of Aspire in connection with credit card services. But it treated the rest of the evidence—uses involving other financial services—as outside the relevant universe. That narrowing of the evidence became the core problem on appeal.

## The Board’s Narrow Definition of the Relevant Public

The Board first defined the relevant public as consumers of “credit card services,” because that was the area in which it had chosen to evaluate the third factor evidence. Having defined the relevant public so narrowly, the Board then limited its sixth-factor analysis to Aspire-formative marks used for credit card services. It treated Aspire marks used for other banking or financing services as “essentially irrelevant.”

The Board ultimately identified nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, but it concluded that nine was not enough to show that the field was “considerable” or “ubiquitous” in the way prior Federal Circuit cases had found persuasive. On that basis, the Board held that Apex had not shown that ASPIRE was commercially or conceptually weak. It therefore gave ASPIRE the normal scope of protection accorded to an inherently distinctive mark.

## Apex’s Argument on Appeal

Apex argued that the Board had drawn the relevant field too narrowly. In Apex’s view, the Board should not have confined itself to credit-card-specific uses when the parties’ services were not limited to identical offerings. Apex’s marks were for “banking and financing services,” and the Board had already recognized that those services were highly similar to CC Serve’s credit card services. Apex therefore argued that third-party uses of Aspire in the broader financial-services market should have counted too.

That argument mattered because if the broader group of third-party uses were considered, the number of similar marks would be much larger. A larger number could support a finding that ASPIRE was weaker than the Board concluded, which in turn could affect the overall likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

## The Federal Circuit’s Holding on the Sixth Factor

The Federal Circuit agreed with Apex that the Board’s sixth-factor analysis was legally flawed. The court emphasized that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods or services. It requires similar goods or services. That distinction was fatal to the Board’s approach.

The court pointed to its own precedent, including Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation, to reinforce that point. Because the Board had already found, under the second DuPont factor, that the parties’ services were highly similar and even partially legally identical, the Federal Circuit held that the Board should not have applied a narrower notion of similarity when it turned to the sixth factor. In other words, the court rejected the Board’s attempt to isolate only credit-card-specific uses when it had already recognized a broader overlap between the parties’ financial services.

The Federal Circuit’s criticism was not that the Board was forbidden from making distinctions, but that its distinction was inconsistent with its own earlier findings. Once the Board determined that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar, it needed to consider third-party use evidence within that same broader domain of similarity.

## The Internal Inconsistency in the Board’s Analysis

The key error was inconsistency. Under the second DuPont factor, the Board had treated the parties’ services as highly similar. Under the sixth factor, however, it effectively narrowed the relevant field to credit card services alone and disregarded similar marks used for other financial services. The Federal Circuit found that approach too rigid.

The court explained that there was no reason to impose a more stringent standard for similarity under the sixth factor than under the second. If the Board had already found that the services were highly similar, then the universe of third-party marks relevant to the strength inquiry should be measured against that same level of similarity. The sixth factor is about how crowded the field is for similar marks on similar goods or services. It is not about artificially slicing the field so narrowly that relevant evidence disappears.

## Why the Court Vacated and Remanded

The Federal Circuit did not itself decide whether the sixth factor favored Apex. Instead, it vacated the Board’s finding and remanded for reconsideration. That choice reflects the court’s limited appellate role in trademark cases. The Board is the factfinder, and the Federal Circuit reviews factual findings for substantial evidence. Once the court concluded that the Board used the wrong legal framework, the proper course was to send the issue back so the Board could reevaluate the third-party evidence under the correct standard.

The remand was important for another reason as well. The first DuPont factor—the similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—can be affected by how strong or weak the prior mark is. Because the Board’s sixth-factor analysis may change on remand, the court also remanded the first factor. In practical terms, the court was saying that the Board’s mistaken narrowing of the market may have affected more than just one piece of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

The result was not a final victory for Apex on the sixth factor, but it was a meaningful appellate correction. The Federal Circuit held that the Board had used too narrow a lens, and it required the Board to reconsider third-party Aspire evidence in light of the broader similarity it had already found between the parties’ services.

# Chapter 5: The First DuPont Factor, the Disposition, and the Opinion’s Practical Significance

## The first DuPont factor: similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression

The Federal Circuit’s discussion of the first DuPont factor is brief, but it is important because it shows how the court connects different parts of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The first factor asks how similar the marks are when viewed as a whole, considering appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression. Here, the marks were Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK word and design marks and CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE word mark. On their face, the shared term “Aspire” obviously matters. But the court did not decide the first factor on a stand-alone basis. Instead, it held that the Board had to reconsider this factor because its analysis of the sixth factor could change how strong or weak the shared term “Aspire” is in the relevant marketplace.

That is the key move in the opinion. The court is not saying that the first factor was necessarily wrong in every respect. Rather, it is saying that the Board’s analysis of commercial impression may depend on whether “Aspire” is shown to be weak or crowded by third-party use. If a term is widely used in the relevant field, consumers may pay less attention to it and may focus more on the additional words, design features, or other distinguishing elements. That can change the overall commercial impression of the marks. So even though the first factor formally concerns the marks themselves, the court recognizes that the assessment cannot be fully isolated from evidence about marketplace strength.

## The principle that the marks must be evaluated in their entireties

Trademark law requires the Board to compare marks in their entireties, not by dissecting them into isolated pieces. That principle matters here because Apex’s marks included not only “Aspire” but also “Bank” and a design element, while CC Serve’s mark was simply ASPIRE. The first DuPont factor therefore asks whether the marks, taken as complete commercial symbols, create similar impressions.

The court’s reasoning assumes that this whole-mark comparison is sensitive to context. A shared term may dominate one mark, but if that term is weak in the relevant field, the rest of the mark may carry more weight in the consumer’s perception. Conversely, if the term is distinctive and strong, the shared portion may drive confusion more readily. That is why the first factor cannot be decided mechanically just by spotting a common word. The analysis must ask how the mark will actually be encountered and remembered by consumers.

## Why commercial impression can be affected by evidence that a shared term is commercially weak in a crowded field

The opinion ties the first factor to the sixth factor because both can be influenced by evidence that a term is common in the market. The sixth factor concerns the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods or services. When a marketplace contains many third-party uses of the same or similar term, that term may be commercially weak. If so, consumers may be accustomed to distinguishing among many similar marks, which reduces the likelihood that one shared element will create confusion.

That matters for commercial impression because a mark does not have a fixed meaning divorced from the marketplace. A consumer seeing ASPIRE in connection with financial services may react differently if the record shows that many businesses in the field use “Aspire” for related offerings. In that setting, the word may evoke a broader suggestive quality rather than a single source-identifying badge. The Board’s failure to consider all relevant third-party uses therefore could distort not only the sixth-factor strength analysis, but also the first-factor evaluation of how the marks are perceived overall.

This is why the court treated the first and sixth factors as linked. The first factor is about similarity, but similarity in trademark law is not merely visual or phonetic identity. It includes the impression created by the mark in context. A crowded field can make two marks look and sound less source-specific than they otherwise would. The Federal Circuit therefore concluded that if the sixth-factor analysis changes, the first-factor analysis may need to change too.

## The Federal Circuit’s decision to vacate the Board’s analysis of the first factor because reconsideration of factor six may alter the strength assessment and thus the marks’ commercial impression

The court vacated the Board’s ruling on the first factor because it could not be confident that the Board’s commercial-impression analysis was complete if the sixth factor had been analyzed under an unduly narrow view of the relevant services. The Board had limited its third-party-use review to Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, even though it had already found under the second factor that the parties’ services were highly similar and, in part, legally identical. The Federal Circuit held that the Board should have used the same service-similarity understanding when evaluating third-party marks under the sixth factor.

That mattered for the first factor because a revised sixth-factor analysis might show that “Aspire” is more commercially weak than the Board concluded. If so, the shared term could have less source-identifying power, which in turn could alter the overall commercial impression of the marks. The court therefore vacated the first-factor finding not because it made its own final judgment on similarity, but because the Board’s analysis depended on a strength determination that might change on remand.

This is a good example of how the DuPont factors interact. They are not rigid boxes. Even though each factor addresses a different aspect of the confusion inquiry, a factual error in one factor can ripple into another. The Federal Circuit’s remedy reflects that reality: the Board must redo the analysis in a way that keeps the relevant factual premises consistent across the factors.

## The court’s rejection of the parties’ remaining arguments as unpersuasive

After addressing the factors Apex specifically challenged, the court said it had considered the remaining arguments and found them unpersuasive. That signals that the panel did not see any other issue that would change the outcome or require separate discussion. In appellate opinions, this kind of statement often means the court believes the unaddressed arguments either lack merit, are unsupported by the record, or are unnecessary to decide once the main analytical error is identified.

For the student reader, the important point is that the Federal Circuit was not reopening the entire opposition proceeding. It focused on the errors it identified in the Board’s legal approach to factors six and one, while leaving the rest of the Board’s work intact. That limited approach is typical in trademark appeals, where courts often correct the analytical framework without deciding every factual dispute themselves.

## The final judgment: affirming the Board on factor two, vacating on factors six and one, and remanding

The disposition is mixed. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor because substantial evidence supported the finding that the parties’ services were highly similar. It vacated the Board’s findings on the sixth factor and the first factor. Then it remanded the case so the Board can reconsider those factors using the correct scope of similarity evidence.

This is an important procedural point. A remand means the Federal Circuit did not finally resolve whether Apex’s marks are registrable. The Board must revisit the analysis, and its new findings could still lead to the same ultimate result or a different one. The appellate court’s role here was to ensure that the Board used the right legal framework, not to substitute its own final confusion determination.

The court also awarded costs to Apex as the appellant. That does not mean Apex “won” the case in full. It means that, as the prevailing party to the extent of obtaining a partial reversal and remand, Apex is entitled to recover appellate costs under the court’s judgment.

## The opinion’s doctrinal takeaway: consistency in defining service similarity across DuPont factors

The main doctrinal lesson of the case is consistency. Once the Board determines that services are highly similar for purposes of the second DuPont factor, it should not silently narrow the same service universe when evaluating the sixth factor. The Federal Circuit’s view is that similarity should mean the same thing across the analysis unless a factor specifically calls for a different inquiry. Here, the court saw no reason to adopt a stricter similarity standard for the sixth factor than the one used for the second factor.

That matters because otherwise a tribunal could create an artificial mismatch in the analysis: the services could be “highly similar” when judging the relationship between the parties’ offerings, yet “not similar enough” when judging third-party-use evidence relevant to mark strength. The Federal Circuit rejected that approach. For the court, the Board must keep its factual framing aligned unless the structure of the particular factor requires otherwise.

## The practical lesson for TTAB litigants: third-party use evidence should be evaluated in light of the full range of similar services recognized elsewhere in the Board’s analysis

For practitioners, the lesson is straightforward. If a TTAB case involves a broad category of related financial or other services, and the Board has already found those services highly similar, then third-party use evidence should not be artificially confined to only one narrow subset. Litigants offering evidence of a crowded field should press for a service definition that matches the Board’s own similarity findings elsewhere in the opinion. Conversely, parties opposing such evidence should be prepared to explain why the relevant universe is genuinely narrower.

The opinion also suggests that third-party-use evidence is most persuasive when it shows actual market exposure within the relevant field as properly defined by the Board’s own analysis. Apex’s evidence was not rejected because third-party uses are irrelevant in principle. It was rejected, in part, because the Board looked at too small a slice of the field. That makes the case especially useful for litigants thinking about how to frame strength evidence in a TTAB opposition.

## The case’s limited but important holding: the Federal Circuit did not decide whether confusion ultimately exists, but required the Board to redo key parts of its analysis under the correct legal approach

The most important thing to understand about the opinion is what it did not decide. The Federal Circuit did not hold that Apex’s marks are registrable, and it did not hold that confusion is or is not ultimately likely. Instead, it held that the Board’s analysis of the first and sixth factors was legally flawed because those factors were evaluated using an improperly narrow view of the relevant services.

That limited holding is still important. In trademark law, the outcome often turns on how the DuPont factors are framed and weighted. By requiring the Board to redo key parts of the analysis under a consistent conception of service similarity, the Federal Circuit ensured that the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry will be performed on a sound factual basis. The case therefore stands less for a final answer on confusion than for the method the Board must use when third-party-use evidence and service similarity interact.
