Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# When “Credit Card Services” Become “Banking and Financing Services”: Legacy Identifications and the Breadth of Trademark Rights

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is a useful reminder that old trademark registrations can still pack a surprising punch. The immediate dispute was over the mark ASPIRE, used by CC Serve for credit card services, and Apex Bank’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks for banking and financing services. The court mostly left the Board’s likelihood-of-confusion finding intact, but its treatment of the second DuPont factor is the part practitioners will want to study closely.

Why? Because the Board interpreted CC Serve’s older registration for “credit card services” broadly enough to overlap with Apex’s banking-and-financing identification, even though “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual by the time of the opposition. That expansive reading helped the Board conclude that the parties’ services were “highly similar,” even “legally identical, in part,” which in turn drove the confusion analysis. For owners of legacy registrations, the case underscores that aging wording can still be read in a capacious, source-indicating way—sometimes more broadly than modern drafting habits might suggest.

## The dispute and the Board’s approach

CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for ASPIRE for credit card services. Apex later sought to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services” for an internet bank. The overlap was obvious enough to draw an opposition, and the Board sustained it under Section 2(d).

The most important part of the Board’s analysis was factor two, which asks how similar the parties’ goods or services are. The Board did not treat “credit card services” as a narrow silo limited to issuing cards. Instead, it looked to dictionary definitions and concluded that the phrase encompasses a set of activities that can include extending credit, processing transactions, managing accounts, and related financial functions. On that basis, and bolstered by third-party registrations covering both credit card and banking/financing services, the Board found the services highly similar.

The Federal Circuit affirmed that part of the decision. It rejected Apex’s argument that CC Serve was merely a credit card-program partner rather than a bank providing banking services. The court emphasized that the Board had carefully construed the recited services and that substantial evidence supported the finding of high similarity.

## The legacy wording problem

The more interesting issue is what the case says about legacy identifications. Apex argued, in essence, that because “credit card services” had been deleted from the ID Manual, the Board should not read CC Serve’s registration too broadly. But the court’s answer was not to freeze the registration in a historical snapshot. Instead, it accepted a flexible understanding of what that old phrase covers.

That matters because the Trademark ID Manual is a drafting aid, not a substantive limiter on the scope of a registration. The Board was free to interpret the registered wording according to its ordinary meaning, and the Federal Circuit was comfortable with a definition that captured more than just the mechanical issuance of cards. In other words, older registration language can carry a real-world business meaning that extends beyond how an applicant might draft the same concept today.

This is a sensible result in one sense. Trademark rights are anchored in the identification as registered, not in the vocabulary conventions of a later era. If “credit card services” in common usage naturally includes account management, payment processing, and related consumer finance functions, it would be artificial to narrow the registration just because modern drafting practices might separate those activities more granularly.

But the flip side is that older identifications may be doing more work than later readers expect. A legacy term can function as a kind of umbrella, covering a broader slice of the market than a more modern application would specify. That can make opposition proceedings especially difficult for later filers trying to enter adjacent financial-services spaces.

## Why factor two did so much work

The court’s affirmance on factor two is not just a technical point. In a likelihood-of-confusion analysis, service similarity often shapes the entire case. When the goods or services are highly similar, the mark comparison gets less room to save the junior user. That is exactly what happened here.

By treating credit card services and banking/financing services as highly similar, the Board set up a framework in which even modest mark similarity could support confusion. And because the Federal Circuit agreed, the central lesson is that financial-services applicants cannot assume that “banking” and “credit card” occupy meaningfully separate lanes for Section 2(d) purposes. In practice, they often do not.

The court’s reasoning also reinforces a broader doctrinal point: similarity under DuPont factor two is not a formal classification exercise. It is a functional inquiry into market overlap and consumer perception. If consumers could plausibly think the services come from the same source, the fact that the services are not identical—or are delivered through different business arrangements—may not matter much.

## The downstream effect of the court’s remand on factors one and six

Although the court affirmed factor two, it vacated and remanded factors one and six. That matters because the Board had drawn a narrower circle around third-party Aspire-formative uses when analyzing the strength of the mark and the overall commercial impression. The Federal Circuit thought that was inconsistent with the Board’s own finding that the services were highly similar. If the services are similar enough for factor two, the court reasoned, they are similar enough for factor six as well.

That portion of the opinion is important because it links service similarity to mark strength. The more similar the goods or services, the more relevant third-party uses in adjacent fields become to the question whether the shared term is crowded or weak. The court did not hold that Apex necessarily wins on remand; it simply said the Board’s scope was too cramped. But the practical effect is that a broader pool of third-party uses may now be considered, which could weaken CC Serve’s mark and affect the overall confusion analysis.

That is where legacy registrations face their own tension. An older registration may be interpreted broadly for factor two, but the same surrounding market evidence may also reveal that the shared term is diluted across the field. So the very breadth that helps establish overlap can also make a mark easier to attack as conceptually or commercially weak.

## Takeaways

This is not a case that rewrites trademark law, but it does clarify how old service identifications can operate in modern disputes. The Federal Circuit accepted an expansive reading of “credit card services” and treated that wording as overlapping with banking and financing services for confusion purposes. For owners of older registrations, that is a reminder that legacy language can remain potent, especially in financial services where products and services blur into one another.

For applicants, the lesson is the opposite: don’t assume that a label like “credit card services” will be read narrowly just because today’s ID Manual might invite more precision. And for practitioners litigating factor six, the opinion is a warning that the scope of “similar goods or services” should not shrink just because the analysis moves from service overlap to third-party mark strength. The Federal Circuit wants consistency across the DuPont factors, and this case pushes the Board to use the same market lens throughout.
