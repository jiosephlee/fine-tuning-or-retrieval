Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Same Similarity, Different Factor? The Federal Circuit Rejects a Narrower Lens for DuPont Factor Six

## Why the case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is not just another trademark likelihood-of-confusion opinion. It sharpens an issue that comes up often in TTAB practice but has not always been handled consistently: how broadly should the Board define the relevant market when it weighs third-party use evidence under DuPont factor six?

The panel’s answer is straightforward and important. If the Board has already found that two parties’ services are highly similar under factor two, it cannot then turn around and use a narrower, more confined version of the market when deciding whether third-party marks make the asserted mark weak. In the court’s view, that mismatch was legal error, not just an ordinary disagreement about weight. The result is a reminder that the DuPont factors may be distinct, but they are not supposed to be siloed in a way that produces internally inconsistent premises.

## The setup: ASPIRE, credit cards, and banking services

The underlying dispute is a familiar one in trademark law: one party owns an older registration, and another wants to use a similar mark in a neighboring financial-services space. CC Serve owns the word mark ASPIRE for credit card services. Apex Bank sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained CC Serve’s opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion.

On appeal, Apex challenged three DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit had little trouble affirming the Board’s factor-two analysis. The Board reasonably found the services to be highly similar, even “legally identical, in part,” because the definitions of banking and financing can overlap with credit-card issuance and related account services. The court also accepted the Board’s basic conclusion that this overlap supported confusion.

The more interesting part of the opinion is what happened next. Apex had submitted third-party uses and registrations incorporating “Aspire” in connection with financial services. The Board, however, limited its factor-six review to the narrow set of marks tied specifically to credit card services, treating other finance-related uses as essentially irrelevant. That move drew the court’s correction.

## The doctrinal move: factor six cannot be narrower than factor two on the same services

DuPont factor six asks about “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” That language matters. The Board had already found that Apex’s banking/financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar, and in part legally identical. But when it reached factor six, the Board effectively shrank the universe of relevant third-party marks to those used only for credit card services.

The Federal Circuit said that was too narrow. If the goods or services are highly similar for factor two, then the same similarity should define the scope of the market for factor six. The court relied on its prior cases recognizing that factor six does not require identical goods, only similar ones. In that sense, the opinion reads as an enforcement of basic analytic consistency: once the Board has chosen a factual frame for similarity, it should not adopt a different frame just because it is analyzing a different factor.

That is the opinion’s core doctrinal point. The court did not say the Board must give the third-party evidence any particular amount of weight on remand. It said the Board must use the correct universe of evidence. The difference is subtle but important. The Board retains discretion to assess whether nine Aspire-formative uses are enough to show weakness. What it cannot do is artificially limit the marketplace in which that question is asked.

## Why the court treated this as legal error

The panel framed the problem as a legal flaw because the Board’s approach implicated the standard governing factor six, not merely the persuasiveness of the evidence. In other words, the issue was not “How much third-party evidence is enough?” but “What counts as the relevant set of similar marks?”

That distinction matters for appellate review. The Federal Circuit has generally been deferential to TTAB factfinding, but deference depends on the Board applying the right legal lens. Here, the court concluded that the Board imposed “a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity” in factor six than it had already used in factor two. That is exactly the kind of internal inconsistency appellate courts tend to police.

The practical implication is that litigants should pay close attention to whether the Board is defining the marketplace in a way that changes across factors. If the Board says the services are broad enough to be highly similar in one part of the opinion, it may have to live with that determination when evaluating third-party-use evidence elsewhere.

## What this means for third-party-use evidence

The decision should be useful to parties trying to prove or rebut strength under factor six. For challengers, the case supports a broader evidentiary showing: third-party marks in adjacent segments of the same industry may be relevant if the services are already found similar. For registrants, the case makes it harder to dismiss such evidence by saying it falls just outside the narrow description of the applicant’s identified services.

That does not mean every financial-services mark using “Aspire” becomes relevant in every dispute involving ASPIRE. The court did not hold that the Board must count all third-party uses across the entire financial industry with equal force. It held that the Board cannot draw an artificially tight circle around only credit-card services once it has already found a broader overlap between credit-card and banking/financing services. The scope of the relevant market still depends on the facts. But the scope must be logically consistent.

## The remand and the first factor

The court also vacated the Board’s first-factor analysis, but not because it independently found the marks to be similar or dissimilar in a new way. Rather, the first factor — similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression — can be influenced by the mark’s conceptual and commercial strength. Because the Board’s factor-six analysis may change on remand, the panel thought the first-factor analysis should be reconsidered as well.

That is a sensible move. In practice, weakness and commercial impression often bleed into each other. A mark that sits in a crowded field may not be as source-indicating as one standing alone. The court avoided prematurely deciding that issue and instead sent it back for a fresh look once the Board applies the proper evidentiary scope.

## Broader implications

The opinion pushes TTAB practice toward greater internal consistency across DuPont factors. That has two downstream effects. First, it limits the Board’s ability to define “similarity” in a factor-specific way that shrinks the relevance of third-party evidence. Second, it reinforces the idea that factor six is not some free-floating inquiry into abstract mark strength; it is tethered to the same goods-and-services comparison that drives factor two.

That said, the court’s reasoning may also make factor-six disputes more fact-intensive. Parties will now have stronger incentives to argue over how broadly the relevant market should be drawn once any overlap is found. In financial-services cases, where products and services often blur across lending, banking, payment processing, and account management, that could enlarge the evidentiary field significantly.

In that sense, *Apex Bank* is less about ASPIRE than about methodology. The Federal Circuit is telling the Board that likelihood-of-confusion analysis cannot rely on a narrow, factor-by-factor segmentation of the same marketplace. When the Board finds services highly similar, it should expect that finding to carry through to the strength inquiry. That is a modest doctrinal correction, but an important one for future trademark oppositions.



# Crowded Fields and Weak Marks: What *Apex Bank* Means for Third-Party Use Evidence at the TTAB

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* is not a sweeping pronouncement that ASPIRE is weak. It does something narrower, but important: it tells the TTAB that when it has already found two services to be highly similar—or even “legally identical, in part”—it cannot then cabin its third-party-use analysis to only the most cramped slice of the market. That matters because the sixth DuPont factor is often where applicants try to show that a term is crowded, diluted, or at least entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. *Apex Bank* pushes that evidence closer to the center of the analysis in financial-services cases, especially where “banking,” “credit card,” and “financing” overlap.

## The court’s basic move

The Board had taken a fairly narrow approach. It treated the relevant public for the weakness inquiry as consumers of “credit card services” and discounted third-party uses of ASPIRE or ASPIRE-formative marks in broader financial services. That led it to conclude that nine similar uses were not enough to show a crowded field.

The Federal Circuit thought that was the wrong lens. Its reasoning is straightforward: the sixth factor asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” And earlier in the same decision, the Board had already found Apex’s banking and financing services highly similar to CC Serve’s credit card services, and partly legally identical. In the court’s view, once that factual finding is made, the Board should not apply a different, tighter similarity standard when deciding what third-party uses count as “similar goods and services” for weakness purposes.

That is the key doctrinal point. The court did not say every financial-service use of ASPIRE must count. It said the Board’s cutoff was too restrictive in light of its own similarity finding.

## How this fits with *Juice Generation* and *Jack Wolfskin*

The opinion sits comfortably in the line of cases that made third-party evidence more consequential in modern TTAB practice. *Juice Generation* and *Jack Wolfskin* both recognized that a mark may be commercially weak when the relevant field is crowded with similar terms or designs. Those cases did not require a magical number of examples; they emphasized context, marketplace saturation, and the practical effect of repeated third-party use on consumer perception.

*Apex Bank* extends that logic in an important evidentiary way. The fight was not over whether third-party use evidence is relevant in the abstract. It was over how broadly the Board should define the “similar goods” universe. The Federal Circuit’s answer is that similarity does not evaporate once the analysis shifts from the second DuPont factor to the sixth. If the services are highly similar, the Board should not shrink the relevant field so much that the weakness inquiry becomes artificially anemic.

That is a meaningful signal to practitioners. The court is not lowering the bar in the sense of announcing that a handful of third-party uses automatically proves weakness. But it is lowering the practical barriers to getting broader marketplace evidence into the record and into the analysis.

## The downstream effect: broader records, less siloing

The biggest practical implication is for applicants and opposers in financial-services disputes. If the Board has already accepted that services are closely related, parties now have a stronger argument that evidence of third-party use across adjacent categories should be considered together rather than in silos. That includes marks used for credit cards, banking, financing, lending, account services, and perhaps related consumer-finance offerings, depending on the record.

That may be especially helpful for applicants trying to show a term’s weakness. A term like ASPIRE may be used in multiple corners of the financial marketplace, but those uses may not all sit under the same narrow product label. *Apex Bank* suggests that narrowing the universe too aggressively can understate the true crowdedness of the field.

At the same time, the decision gives brand owners a warning. If your mark depends on a common suggestive term in a clustered industry, it may be harder to defend a broad scope of exclusivity if the record contains a meaningful number of uses across related financial services. The Board can no longer assume that uses outside one subcategory are irrelevant when the services overlap substantially.

## The court’s remand on the first factor matters too

The Federal Circuit also vacated the Board’s first-factor analysis, but only because the commercial-strength inquiry on remand could affect the marks’ commercial impression. That is a limited but important move. It reflects the reality that weakness evidence does not live in a vacuum: a crowded field can blunt the significance of similarities in appearance, sound, or connotation. In other words, the sixth factor can reshape the first.

That interdependence is often underappreciated in TTAB practice. A mark that looks and sounds similar on paper may be less likely to confuse if consumers have been exposed to many comparable marks in the same or adjacent market. *Apex Bank* reinforces that the Board should account for that interaction rather than treating each factor as sealed off from the others.

## Bottom line

*Apex Bank* is a targeted, evidence-driven decision, but it carries real weight for weakness arguments at the TTAB. The Federal Circuit did not hold that ASPIRE is weak. It held that the Board used too narrow a lens when deciding what third-party use evidence counted. For practitioners, the lesson is clear: in crowded financial-services markets, build the weakness record broadly, not just within one sublabel. And if the Board has already found the services highly similar, expect the Federal Circuit to insist that the marketplace evidence be evaluated with that same breadth in mind.



# When “Credit Card Services” Become “Banking and Financing Services”: Legacy Identifications and the Breadth of Trademark Rights

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is a useful reminder that old trademark registrations can still pack a surprising punch. The immediate dispute was over the mark ASPIRE, used by CC Serve for credit card services, and Apex Bank’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks for banking and financing services. The court mostly left the Board’s likelihood-of-confusion finding intact, but its treatment of the second DuPont factor is the part practitioners will want to study closely.

Why? Because the Board interpreted CC Serve’s older registration for “credit card services” broadly enough to overlap with Apex’s banking-and-financing identification, even though “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual by the time of the opposition. That expansive reading helped the Board conclude that the parties’ services were “highly similar,” even “legally identical, in part,” which in turn drove the confusion analysis. For owners of legacy registrations, the case underscores that aging wording can still be read in a capacious, source-indicating way—sometimes more broadly than modern drafting habits might suggest.

## The dispute and the Board’s approach

CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for ASPIRE for credit card services. Apex later sought to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services” for an internet bank. The overlap was obvious enough to draw an opposition, and the Board sustained it under Section 2(d).

The most important part of the Board’s analysis was factor two, which asks how similar the parties’ goods or services are. The Board did not treat “credit card services” as a narrow silo limited to issuing cards. Instead, it looked to dictionary definitions and concluded that the phrase encompasses a set of activities that can include extending credit, processing transactions, managing accounts, and related financial functions. On that basis, and bolstered by third-party registrations covering both credit card and banking/financing services, the Board found the services highly similar.

The Federal Circuit affirmed that part of the decision. It rejected Apex’s argument that CC Serve was merely a credit card-program partner rather than a bank providing banking services. The court emphasized that the Board had carefully construed the recited services and that substantial evidence supported the finding of high similarity.

## The legacy wording problem

The more interesting issue is what the case says about legacy identifications. Apex argued, in essence, that because “credit card services” had been deleted from the ID Manual, the Board should not read CC Serve’s registration too broadly. But the court’s answer was not to freeze the registration in a historical snapshot. Instead, it accepted a flexible understanding of what that old phrase covers.

That matters because the Trademark ID Manual is a drafting aid, not a substantive limiter on the scope of a registration. The Board was free to interpret the registered wording according to its ordinary meaning, and the Federal Circuit was comfortable with a definition that captured more than just the mechanical issuance of cards. In other words, older registration language can carry a real-world business meaning that extends beyond how an applicant might draft the same concept today.

This is a sensible result in one sense. Trademark rights are anchored in the identification as registered, not in the vocabulary conventions of a later era. If “credit card services” in common usage naturally includes account management, payment processing, and related consumer finance functions, it would be artificial to narrow the registration just because modern drafting practices might separate those activities more granularly.

But the flip side is that older identifications may be doing more work than later readers expect. A legacy term can function as a kind of umbrella, covering a broader slice of the market than a more modern application would specify. That can make opposition proceedings especially difficult for later filers trying to enter adjacent financial-services spaces.

## Why factor two did so much work

The court’s affirmance on factor two is not just a technical point. In a likelihood-of-confusion analysis, service similarity often shapes the entire case. When the goods or services are highly similar, the mark comparison gets less room to save the junior user. That is exactly what happened here.

By treating credit card services and banking/financing services as highly similar, the Board set up a framework in which even modest mark similarity could support confusion. And because the Federal Circuit agreed, the central lesson is that financial-services applicants cannot assume that “banking” and “credit card” occupy meaningfully separate lanes for Section 2(d) purposes. In practice, they often do not.

The court’s reasoning also reinforces a broader doctrinal point: similarity under DuPont factor two is not a formal classification exercise. It is a functional inquiry into market overlap and consumer perception. If consumers could plausibly think the services come from the same source, the fact that the services are not identical—or are delivered through different business arrangements—may not matter much.

## The downstream effect of the court’s remand on factors one and six

Although the court affirmed factor two, it vacated and remanded factors one and six. That matters because the Board had drawn a narrower circle around third-party Aspire-formative uses when analyzing the strength of the mark and the overall commercial impression. The Federal Circuit thought that was inconsistent with the Board’s own finding that the services were highly similar. If the services are similar enough for factor two, the court reasoned, they are similar enough for factor six as well.

That portion of the opinion is important because it links service similarity to mark strength. The more similar the goods or services, the more relevant third-party uses in adjacent fields become to the question whether the shared term is crowded or weak. The court did not hold that Apex necessarily wins on remand; it simply said the Board’s scope was too cramped. But the practical effect is that a broader pool of third-party uses may now be considered, which could weaken CC Serve’s mark and affect the overall confusion analysis.

That is where legacy registrations face their own tension. An older registration may be interpreted broadly for factor two, but the same surrounding market evidence may also reveal that the shared term is diluted across the field. So the very breadth that helps establish overlap can also make a mark easier to attack as conceptually or commercially weak.

## Takeaways

This is not a case that rewrites trademark law, but it does clarify how old service identifications can operate in modern disputes. The Federal Circuit accepted an expansive reading of “credit card services” and treated that wording as overlapping with banking and financing services for confusion purposes. For owners of older registrations, that is a reminder that legacy language can remain potent, especially in financial services where products and services blur into one another.

For applicants, the lesson is the opposite: don’t assume that a label like “credit card services” will be read narrowly just because today’s ID Manual might invite more precision. And for practitioners litigating factor six, the opinion is a warning that the scope of “similar goods or services” should not shrink just because the analysis moves from service overlap to third-party mark strength. The Federal Circuit wants consistency across the DuPont factors, and this case pushes the Board to use the same market lens throughout.



# A Partial Vacatur with Teeth: Why the Federal Circuit Sent the Mark-Similarity Analysis Back Too

## Why this decision matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* is a good reminder that likelihood-of-confusion analysis under *DuPont* is not a set of sealed compartments. The court affirmed the TTAB’s conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar, but it vacated and remanded both the crowded-field factor and the mark-similarity factor because the Board’s narrow treatment of third-party “Aspire” evidence may have skewed the broader confusion analysis. That linkage matters well beyond this dispute over banking and credit card services. It reinforces a recurring theme in trademark law: evidence of strength or weakness does not live only in one box on the *DuPont* checklist. In the right case, it can shape how the marks themselves are perceived.

The result is especially significant because the panel did not merely say that factor six needed a do-over. It expressly tied factor six to factor one, reasoning that the commercial strength or weakness of a mark can affect overall commercial impression. That is an important doctrinal move for TTAB practice, where the factors are often recited one by one, but the real question is whether the Board has actually grappled with how the evidence operates across the analysis.

## The court’s core move: similarity of goods controls the scope of the crowded-field inquiry

The court’s treatment of factor six is the key to understanding the rest of the opinion. The Board had limited the relevant third-party evidence to Aspire-formative marks used for credit card services, even though it had already found under factor two that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar, and in part legally identical. The Federal Circuit said that was too narrow. If the services are highly similar for purposes of factor two, the court reasoned, the Board should not suddenly narrow the field for factor six as though the services were less related than it had already found them to be.

That holding is not revolutionary, but it is useful. The *DuPont* factors are distinct, but they are not hermetically sealed. If the dispute is about marks used in overlapping segments of financial services, then third-party uses of “Aspire” in the broader financial-services market may well be relevant to whether the cited mark is weak. The panel’s point is that the Board cannot define similarity one way when assessing the parties’ services, and then apply a more cramped standard when assessing the marketplace evidence bearing on strength.

The opinion also implicitly rejects a common TTAB instinct to over-particularize the “relevant public” in a way that can drain strength evidence of meaning. Here, the Board treated only nine Aspire-formative uses tied to credit card services as relevant, and brushed aside other Aspire uses in banking and finance. The Federal Circuit thought that was legally flawed because the goods and services overlap more broadly than the Board credited in factor six.

## Why factor one had to go back too

The more interesting part of the decision is the remand on factor one. The court did not hold that the Board’s comparison of the marks—ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK and design—was independently wrong on the existing record. Instead, it said the first factor had to be reconsidered because the factor-six analysis may change the strength calculus, which in turn affects commercial impression.

That is a meaningful doctrinal statement. Trademark lawyers often talk about “strength” as if it were confined to the sixth factor, while factor one is about the marks’ visual, aural, and connotative similarity. But the Federal Circuit treated commercial strength and commercial impression as interconnected. In practical terms, a mark that sits in a crowded field may be perceived as less source-indicating, and that reality can affect how consumers process the differences between the marks. A weak mark may command a narrower zone of protection not only because competitors have used similar wording, but also because consumers are accustomed to seeing and distinguishing those formulations in the marketplace.

That does not collapse the factors into each other. The court did not say factor six dictates factor one. But it did say the Board cannot assess the overall impression of the marks without knowing whether “Aspire” is a relatively distinctive signal in this space or one among many commonly used aspirational terms. That is a subtle but important point: the same market evidence can inform both how strong a mark is and how much weight consumers will give to shared elements when they see them in use.

## The practical lesson for TTAB litigants

For TTAB litigants, the case is a warning against treating strength evidence as a one-factor-only project. Parties will still want to marshal crowded-field evidence primarily under factor six, because that is where it naturally belongs. But *Apex Bank* suggests that the record built there can reverberate elsewhere, especially when the mark is a common word and the goods or services are close.

That may affect how parties develop the record. Applicants and opposers alike should think carefully about the full market context, not just uses that are clipped to an artificially narrow service description. If a mark is used across adjacent financial services, the question is not always whether the third-party use matches the exact subcategory in the registration. The more important issue may be whether the relevant consumers encounter the term in a broader field that conditions their expectations about source.

The decision also reinforces the importance of making the Board’s own factfinding do some work. The Federal Circuit relied heavily on the Board’s factor-two finding that the services were highly similar. Once that finding was in place, the Board had less room to cabin factor six to a narrower slice of the market. Litigants should expect panels to use their own factual findings as guardrails against inconsistent treatment of the same commercial setting.

## A modest but real doctrinal nudge

This is not a sweeping doctrinal overhaul, but it is more than a routine remand. The panel is signaling that crowded-field evidence can have a broader jurisprudential role than TTAB decisions sometimes give it. In other words, strength is not merely a discrete tally of third-party uses. It is part of the background commercial environment in which similarity is perceived.

That approach is sensible. Consumers do not parse trademarks by factor number. They encounter words, brands, and related services in a marketplace, and their perceptions are shaped by repetition and context. A mark’s meaning, distinctiveness, and source-indicating force are all affected by how the term is used around it. The Federal Circuit’s linkage of factors one and six reflects that commercial reality.

At the same time, the opinion leaves the TTAB with some discretion on remand. The Board still must decide how much weight the third-party Aspire evidence deserves, and whether it shows only some weakness or meaningful weakness. The court did not hold that nine third-party uses are enough, or that the mark is weak as a matter of law. It only held that the Board used too cramped a lens and therefore may have reached the wrong overall impression.

That makes the decision a useful one for trademark practice: not dramatic, but clarifying. It tells the Board that when the record suggests a crowded field, it should not quarantine that evidence to a single factor and move on. In a close case, that kind of analytical siloing can matter. Here, it mattered enough to send the case back.
