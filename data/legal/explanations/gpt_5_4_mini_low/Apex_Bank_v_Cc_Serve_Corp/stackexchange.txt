Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why could the TTAB treat "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" as legally identical in part?
Question:
I'm confused about the court's affirmance on the second DuPont factor. CC Serve does not seem to operate as a traditional bank, and the opinion says it partners with a bank that actually issues the ASPIRE-branded cards. Apex, by contrast, applied for "banking and financing services" and apparently does not even offer credit cards right now. So how did the Board and the Federal Circuit get to the conclusion that the services are "legally identical, in part"? Is this because trademark law looks only at the wording in the application/registration rather than the parties' real-world business models, or was there something else going on?
Answer:
Because for the **second DuPont factor**, the TTAB compares the **services as identified in the application/registration**, not the parties’ actual business models in the marketplace.

That is exactly what happened here.

### What the Board compared
- **CC Serve’s registration** covered **“credit card services.”**
- **Apex’s application** covered **“banking and financing services.”**

The Board then construed those terms using ordinary dictionary meanings and the Trademark ID Manual record. On that basis, it found that **“banking”** and **“financing”** can include **extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards**. So, while Apex is not a credit-card issuer in practice, its application for broad “banking and financing services” was legally broad enough to cover services that overlap with credit card services.

### Why “legally identical, in part”?
That phrase does **not** mean the parties are actually doing the same business. It means the **scope of the identifications overlaps** as a matter of trademark law. In the court’s words, the Board found the services were “**legally identical, in part**” because Apex’s identified services were broad enough to include credit-card-related financing, and CC Serve’s identified services were credit card services. See slip op. at III.A.

### Why Apex’s real-world model didn’t save it
Apex argued that:
- it is a retail bank,
- it does not currently offer credit cards, and
- CC Serve partners with a bank that issues the cards.

But the Federal Circuit rejected that argument because likelihood of confusion is assessed based on the **services described in the application and registration**, not on what each party happens to be doing at the moment. The Board was entitled to look at the full scope of Apex’s identified “banking and financing services,” and the court held that substantial evidence supported the finding of high similarity.

### Bottom line
So yes, this was driven by **trademark law’s focus on the wording of the identifications**, not the parties’ actual operating structures. The key point is that **Apex’s application was broad enough to encompass credit-card-related services**, which allowed the Board to treat the services as **partly legally identical** for likelihood-of-confusion purposes.

### In a Section 2(d) opposition, how much do the applicant's actual planned services matter compared with the identification of services in the application?
Question:
Apex argued that the parties' services were not directly competitive and that CC Serve was really managing card programs rather than providing banking services itself. But the court seemed to focus heavily on the listed services and dictionary definitions instead. In TTAB opposition proceedings, is the tribunal generally required to compare the application and registration as written, even if the evidence shows the parties' actual businesses are narrower? If so, are there any limits on that rule?
Answer:
Yes—**as a general rule, the TTAB compares the services as they are identified in the application and registration, not as the parties may actually plan to use them in the marketplace**.

That is exactly what happened in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* The Board did not ask whether CC Serve, in practice, acted like a bank or whether Apex’s intended internet bank would operate in a narrower way. Instead, it compared:

- CC Serve’s registered **“credit card services”**, and
- Apex’s application for **“banking and financing services.”**

The Federal Circuit approved that approach on the second *DuPont* factor, explaining that the Board “carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services,” and that substantial evidence supported the finding that the services were highly similar. The court also accepted the Board’s use of dictionary definitions and third-party registrations to understand the scope of those identified services.

### Why this is the rule
In a Section 2(d) opposition, the likelihood-of-confusion analysis is based on the **identifications** in the application and registration because those identify the legal scope of the parties’ rights. The tribunal is not trying to decide the dispute based on a party’s current business model or business plans, but on the reach of the claimed services.

So if an application says “banking and financing services,” the Board treats that as covering the ordinary meaning of those words, even if the applicant says it will only offer a subset of those services. Likewise, if a registration says “credit card services,” the registrant is entitled to the ordinary scope of that identification, not merely the precise way it currently operates.

### The limits
There are limits, but they are limits on the **identification itself**, not on the applicant’s unlisted business intentions.

1. **The wording controls.**  
   The Board will not narrow a broad identification based on extrinsic evidence of how a party says it actually does business.

2. **Ambiguous terms may be interpreted.**  
   As in *Apex*, the Board may look to dictionary definitions and the Trademark ID Manual to determine what a term in the identification means.

3. **The evidence still matters for relatedness and use in the marketplace.**  
   The Board can use evidence like third-party registrations to show that services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark. In *Apex*, that was part of the second-factor analysis.

4. **A narrower identification changes the analysis.**  
   If a party limits its identification during prosecution or frames it narrowly in the application/registration, the Board is bound by that narrower wording.

### Bottom line
So, **yes**: in TTAB opposition proceedings, the Board generally compares the application and registration **as written**. The parties’ actual or planned business operations usually do not control unless they are reflected in the identification itself or are relevant to interpreting its scope.

In *Apex*, Apex’s argument that CC Serve was “really” just managing card programs did not carry the day because the registration and the evidence supported treating the services as credit card services, and the applicant’s broader “banking and financing services” identification made the services highly similar.

### Why was the Board's sixth DuPont factor analysis a legal error rather than just a factual judgment about weak evidence?
Question:
The Federal Circuit said the Board committed legal error by limiting the sixth factor to third-party Aspire marks used only for credit card services, instead of also considering banking and financing services that the Board had already found highly similar. I don't fully understand why this is classified as a legal flaw instead of just a debatable factual weighing of relevance. Was the problem that the Board applied the wrong legal standard for what counts as "similar goods or services" under factor six?
Answer:
Because the Federal Circuit thought the Board used the **wrong legal frame** for factor six, not merely that it gave the evidence too little weight.

The key distinction is this:

- **Factual judgment:** how much probative value to assign to the third-party “Aspire” evidence once the correct universe of relevant goods/services is identified.
- **Legal error:** deciding the wrong universe of relevant goods/services in the first place.

Under DuPont factor six, the Board must consider the **“number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”**. The opinion makes clear that “similar” does **not** mean identical. Citing *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*, the court says factor six applies to **similar goods and services**, not only the exact same ones.

That is why the Federal Circuit treated the Board’s limitation to **credit card services only** as a legal flaw. The Board had already found, under factor two, that Apex’s banking/financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were **highly similar** and even **“legally identical, in part.”** Once that finding was made, the court said the Board should not have imposed a narrower similarity standard when evaluating third-party marks under factor six.

So the problem was not just “the Board could have weighed the evidence differently.” The problem was that the Board **excluded a category of evidence that the correct legal standard required it to consider**. In the court’s words:

> “We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”

That is classic legal error: applying an **incorrect rule** to define relevance. The factual question on remand is then how much weight the broader set of Aspire-formative marks should receive.

### Does the sixth DuPont factor require evidence of use in the exact same market, or just use on sufficiently similar goods/services?
Question:
The opinion says the sixth factor concerns the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods," and it cites cases saying the goods do not need to be identical. I'm trying to pin down the rule. If a party is trying to show that a cited mark is commercially weak because the field is crowded, how close do the third-party uses have to be? Would use on general financial services, fintech, lending, or banking all potentially count against strength of ASPIRE here, even if they are not literally credit card services?
Answer:
No. Under this opinion, the sixth DuPont factor does **not** require third-party uses in the exact same market or on identical services.

The court says the sixth factor concerns the “**number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods**,” and it expressly holds that the Board used an **overly narrow** similarity standard when it limited the evidence to marks used only for credit card services. The Federal Circuit states:

- “**the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones**,” citing *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*.
- Because the Board had already found the parties’ services were “**highly similar**” and even “**partially legally identical**” for the second factor, it should have used that same, broader similarity analysis when deciding which third-party marks to consider under the sixth factor.

So the rule is not “same market only.” It is more like: **third-party marks are relevant if they are used on goods/services that are similar enough to the cited goods/services to show that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks in that field.**

Applied here, that means the Board erred by treating Aspire marks used in “other banking and financing services” as “essentially irrelevant” just because they were not labeled “credit card services.” The court says those uses should have been considered because banking and financing services were found to be highly similar to credit card services.

That said, the opinion does **not** say every mark in the broad financial-services universe counts automatically. The evidence still has to involve **similar** goods/services, and the weight of the evidence will depend on how close those services are to the cited services and whether the relevant public would encounter the marks as part of a crowded field.

So your examples are best understood this way:

- **General financial services, fintech, lending, banking**: potentially relevant **if** they are sufficiently similar to the cited services.
- **Not necessarily the exact same service**: the court rejects that requirement.
- **But not merely any remotely related business**: there still has to be real similarity, not just a loose industry connection.

In short: **the sixth factor looks to similar goods/services, not identical ones, and the scope should track the similarity finding already made in the confusion analysis.**

### Why did the Federal Circuit also vacate the first DuPont factor without deciding for itself whether the marks ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK are similar?
Question:
On the marks themselves, I would have thought the court could separately analyze whether ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK are similar in sight, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Instead, it said the first-factor analysis had to be vacated because reconsideration of factor six could change the mark's commercial strength or weakness and therefore the commercial impression analysis. How exactly does evidence of third-party Aspire marks feed into the first-factor comparison of the two marks themselves?
Answer:
Because the Federal Circuit treats the **first DuPont factor** as more than a bare side-by-side comparison of the words “ASPIRE” and “ASPIRE BANK.”

The first factor asks about similarity in **appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression**. In trademark law, “commercial impression” is not created in a vacuum; it depends in part on **how strong or weak the shared element is in the marketplace**. That is where the sixth factor comes in.

### How the two factors interact

- **Factor 6** looks at the **number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods/services**.
- Evidence of many third-party “Aspire” marks can show that **ASPIRE is weak** in the relevant field.
- A weak mark gets a **narrower scope of protection**.
- If the shared term is weak, consumers may perceive “ASPIRE BANK” as less source-indicating of CC Serve’s mark and more as just one of many “Aspire”-formative marks in the market.

So when the court says reconsidering factor 6 “may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression,” it means this:

> The Board’s assessment of whether the marks create a similar **commercial impression** depends partly on whether consumers have been conditioned by third-party use to view “ASPIRE” as a crowded or weak term.

### Why the court vacated factor 1 instead of deciding it itself

The court did **not** say it was unable to compare the marks’ sight or sound. Rather, it said the **commercial impression** portion of the first factor could change once the Board properly reassesses the sixth factor. Since the Board’s first-factor analysis was intertwined with its mistaken narrowing of the sixth factor, the court remanded for the Board to redo both together.

### In short

Third-party “Aspire” marks matter to factor 1 because they affect the **context in which consumers encounter the marks**. If “ASPIRE” is weak and commonly used, then “ASPIRE BANK” may make a different overall impression than it would if ASPIRE were a strong, distinctive term. That is why the court vacated the first factor as well.

### What is the practical difference between a mark's 'commercial strength,' 'conceptual strength,' and the 'normal scope of protection' the Board mentioned?
Question:
The Board said CC Serve had not shown any particular commercial strength, but Apex had also not shown that ASPIRE was commercially or conceptually weak, so the mark got the normal scope of protection for an inherently distinctive mark. I'm having trouble separating these concepts. What does it mean for a mark to be conceptually weak versus commercially weak, and how does that affect the breadth of protection in a likelihood-of-confusion analysis?
Answer:
The Board is using three related but distinct ideas:

1. **Conceptual strength** = how inherently distinctive the mark is on its face.
2. **Commercial strength** = how much marketplace recognition the mark has earned through use.
3. **Normal scope of protection** = the default breadth of protection a mark gets when it is inherently distinctive and not shown to be commercially weak.

### 1. Conceptual strength: strength in the abstract
A mark is **conceptually strong** if it is inherently distinctive—i.e., it is fanciful, arbitrary, or suggestive rather than descriptive or generic.

In this opinion, the Board treated **ASPIRE** as an **inherently distinctive** mark. That means, conceptually, it starts out entitled to protection because it is capable of identifying source without needing proof of acquired distinctiveness.

So “conceptual weakness” would mean the opposite: the term is weak because of its nature—close to descriptive, suggestive at the edge, or otherwise less distinctive in idea/content.

### 2. Commercial strength: strength in the market
A mark is **commercially strong** if consumers have been exposed to it so much that it has a strong source-identifying presence in the marketplace. That often comes from long use, heavy advertising, sales volume, or widespread recognition.

Here, the Board said CC Serve **“has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength.”** That means there was no evidence that ASPIRE had especially strong market recognition beyond what inheres in the mark itself.

The key point: a mark can be **conceptually strong but commercially ordinary**. It may be distinctive as a word, but not famous or especially well-known in the market.

### 3. How third-party use affects strength
This is where the sixth DuPont factor comes in. Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods can show that a mark is **commercially weak**—because consumers have been exposed to many similar marks and are conditioned to distinguish among them.

That does **not necessarily** mean the mark is conceptually weak. The mark can remain inherently distinctive in the abstract, but still have a narrower practical scope because the marketplace is crowded.

The opinion cites **Juice Generation** and **Jack Wolfskin** for that point: extensive third-party use can show consumers are used to seeing similar marks and therefore the mark deserves a narrower scope of protection.

### 4. What “normal scope of protection” means
When the Board said ASPIRE was entitled to the **“normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks,”** it meant:

- ASPIRE is not so weak that it should be given only a very narrow range of protection;
- but it is also not shown to be especially commercially strong so as to deserve unusually broad protection.

So the practical result is a middle position: the mark is protected, but within the ordinary range expected for an inherently distinctive mark.

### Practical difference in a likelihood-of-confusion analysis
These strength concepts matter because they affect how sensitive the confusion analysis is:

- **Strong mark** → broader protection; smaller differences between marks may still cause confusion.
- **Weak mark** → narrower protection; consumers are more used to distinguishing similar marks, so closer similarity may be required to find confusion.

In this case, the Board’s problem was that it said ASPIRE was neither commercially nor conceptually weak, but then limited the sixth-factor analysis too narrowly. The Federal Circuit held that the Board had to consider third-party marks in the full set of **similar services**, not just a narrow subset, because that evidence bears on whether ASPIRE is commercially weak and therefore entitled to a narrower scope of protection.

### Bottom line
- **Conceptual strength** asks: *How distinctive is the mark by nature?*
- **Commercial strength** asks: *How strong is the mark in the marketplace?*
- **Normal scope of protection** means: *If the mark is inherently distinctive but not shown to be commercially weak or unusually famous, it gets ordinary—not expanded or narrowed—protection.*

### Why didn't the Federal Circuit simply reverse the likelihood-of-confusion finding if the Board mishandled two important DuPont factors?
Question:
The court affirmed factor two, vacated factors six and one, and remanded, rather than holding outright that confusion either was or was not likely. Since likelihood of confusion is ultimately a legal question, why couldn't the Federal Circuit resolve it on appeal once it found errors in the Board's analysis? Is this because the underlying DuPont findings are fact-intensive and the appellate court wanted the Board to evaluate the third-party evidence first?
Answer:
Because in this opinion the Federal Circuit did **not** have a clean basis to decide likelihood of confusion as a matter of law after correcting the Board’s errors.

The key point is the court’s own framing of the standard:

- **Likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion**, but
- it is “**based on findings of relevant underlying facts**,” and
- each DuPont factor is itself a **question of fact** reviewed for substantial evidence.

That means the Federal Circuit can correct legal errors and determine whether particular factual findings are supported, but it generally does **not** reweigh the DuPont factors from scratch in the first instance when the Board has applied the wrong legal framework to important factors.

### Why remand here?
The opinion says the Board made a legal error in factor six by using too narrow a universe of third-party marks, because the Board had already found the services highly similar under factor two. That error mattered because factor six is about the **number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods**. The court therefore vacated that factor and remanded so the Board could reconsider the proper scope of evidence.

The court then vacated factor one as well because the first factor’s “overall commercial impression” analysis may change once factor six is reassessed. In other words, the court viewed factors six and one as **interconnected**: the strength/weakness of the mark can affect how similar the marks appear in commercial impression.

### Why not just decide confusion itself?
Because doing so would require the court to **reweigh the DuPont factors after correcting the Board’s methodology**. The opinion does not say the record compelled only one outcome. Instead, it says the Board must reconsider the third-party Aspire evidence under the correct standard, and then revisit the commercial impression analysis in light of that reconsideration.

So your intuition is basically right: this is a fact-intensive inquiry, and the Federal Circuit wanted the Board—the trier of fact in the TTAB proceeding—to evaluate the third-party evidence first under the proper legal standard. The court corrected the legal error, but left the ultimate balancing of the factors to the Board on remand.

In short: **the Federal Circuit could not simply reverse or affirm the overall confusion finding because the Board’s error affected the factual premises of the balancing analysis, and the court chose not to perform that balancing itself in the first instance.**
