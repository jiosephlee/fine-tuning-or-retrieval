Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why does the Federal Circuit apply Fifth Circuit law to JMOL and new-trial issues in a patent appeal?
Question:
I'm confused about the standard of review discussion at the beginning of the opinion. This is a patent case in the Federal Circuit, but the court says it reviews the denial of JMOL and the denial of a new trial under Fifth Circuit law, while it reviews claim construction under Federal Circuit law. How does that split work? Is the idea that some issues are considered 'procedural' and therefore governed by the regional circuit, while patent-specific substantive issues stay under Federal Circuit precedent? If so, where exactly is the line drawn?
Answer:
Yes — that’s basically the right way to think about it, but the cleaner way to say it is:

**The Federal Circuit uses regional-circuit law for ordinary civil-procedure issues, and Federal Circuit law for patent-specific issues.**

That is exactly what the panel is doing in *Finesse Wireless*.

### Why JMOL and new trial are under Fifth Circuit law
A motion for JMOL under Rule 50 and a motion for a new trial under Rule 59 are **general post-trial procedural motions**, not patent doctrines. So the Federal Circuit applies the **law of the regional circuit where the district court sits**. Here, the case was tried in the Eastern District of Texas, so the relevant regional circuit is the Fifth Circuit.

That’s why the opinion says:

> “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit.”  
> “We review a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial under regional circuit law.”

The Federal Circuit cites *Wi-LAN* for that rule.

### Why claim construction is under Federal Circuit law
Claim construction is different because it is **patent-specific**. It defines the scope of the patent right itself, so the Federal Circuit applies its own precedent to keep patent law uniform nationwide.

That is why the same opinion says:

> “We review claim construction de novo…”

and then applies Federal Circuit claim-construction doctrine.

### Where the line is drawn
The line is not perfectly “procedural vs. substantive,” though that’s a useful shorthand. More accurately:

- **Regional circuit law** governs **general civil-procedure issues** and other questions **not unique to patent law**  
  - e.g., JMOL, new trial, many evidentiary and trial-management questions
- **Federal Circuit law** governs **issues unique to patent law**  
  - e.g., claim construction, infringement standards, validity doctrines, prosecution-history disclaimer

### In this opinion
The court is doing both at once:

1. **Regional circuit law** tells the court how to review the district court’s denial of JMOL/new trial.
2. **Federal Circuit patent law** tells the court how to interpret the patent claims and assess the infringement verdict.

So the split is not accidental — it is the Federal Circuit’s standard choice-of-law approach in patent appeals.

### When can an appellate court treat expert testimony as too contradictory to support a jury verdict?
Question:
The court says Finesse's expert gave 'confusing and unclear' testimony and relies on Johns Hopkins to hold that no reasonable jury could find infringement based on self-contradictory expert testimony. I'm struggling with that. Usually I thought credibility and weighing conflicting testimony are for the jury. So what makes this different? Is the rule that a jury can choose between two competing experts, but cannot rely on one expert whose own testimony defeats his infringement theory? How inconsistent does the testimony have to be before it stops being substantial evidence?
Answer:
The short answer is: **an appellate court is not reweighing credibility when it does this. It is deciding whether the testimony, taken as a whole, is still “substantial evidence” at all.**

Normally, yes — **conflicts between witnesses are for the jury**. If one expert says X and the other says not-X, the jury can choose. But that rule assumes each expert’s testimony is still a **coherent factual basis** for the verdict. It does **not** require an appellate court to accept a verdict that depends on testimony that is internally self-defeating on an essential element.

That is what the Federal Circuit is doing in *Finesse Wireless*.

## What the court thought was different here

For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that the claimed “interference generating signals” were the **modeled PIM path**. But the accused radio’s RF ADC samples **before** that modeled PIM path is generated. So if his mapping is right, then the claim limitation cannot be met because the “interference generating signals” would not exist at the time of sampling.

When Finesse tried to save the verdict, it argued Dr. Wells really meant **x1 and x2**, not the modeled PIM path. But the court found that Dr. Wells never said that clearly; instead, he kept saying the modeled PIM path was the relevant claim element. The court called this “confusing and unclear” and concluded:

> “This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.”

That is the key point: **the jury was not being asked to choose between two competing, intelligible expert opinions. It was being asked to rely on an expert theory that collapsed under its own contradictions.**

## The doctrine

The Federal Circuit’s citation to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* is doing the work. There, the court held that when the burdened party’s expert gives **self-contradictory testimony** on a claim element, a reasonable jury cannot treat that testimony as substantial evidence supporting infringement.

So the rule is not:

- “Any inconsistency destroys the testimony.”

It is closer to:

- **If the expert’s testimony on a required element is materially inconsistent, unexplained, and the inconsistency goes to the heart of the theory of infringement, the testimony may fail as substantial evidence.**

## How inconsistent is enough?

The opinion suggests the inconsistency has to be **material and unresolved**:

1. **It must concern an essential claim element.**  
   Here, whether the accused radios sample the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.”

2. **The testimony must not be reasonably reconcilable.**  
   The court said Dr. Wells never clearly tied x1 and x2 to the claim terms, even though Finesse tried to infer that after the fact.

3. **The contradiction must undermine the only evidence supporting the verdict.**  
   If the expert is the key proof and his testimony defeats the theory, the verdict cannot stand.

## So can a jury choose between two experts?

Yes. A jury can choose between **competing expert interpretations**.

But a jury cannot base a verdict on an expert opinion that, as presented at trial, is **internally self-contradictory on the dispositive point** and never repaired by a clear explanation. In that situation, the issue is no longer “who is more credible?” It is “is there actually evidence from which a reasonable jury could find the element satisfied?”

That is why the Federal Circuit reversed JMOL here.

## Bottom line

Your instinct is right that credibility is usually for the jury. But this case falls on the other side of the line because the court viewed Dr. Wells’ testimony not as a mere credibility contest, but as **confused testimony that never supplied a coherent factual basis for infringement**.

So, in this opinion, the answer is:

- **jury question:** conflicting but coherent expert opinions;
- **JMOL question:** a verdict resting on an expert’s self-contradictory, element-defeating testimony.

### Why wasn't it enough for the jury to infer that x1 and x2 were the claimed 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals'?
Question:
On the '134 patent, the Federal Circuit seems to say the expert expressly mapped the claimed 'interference generating signals' to the 'modeled PIM path,' which was a problem because that path is generated only after sampling. But the district court thought he was 'clearly referring to x1 and x2' when talking about the two signals entering the RF ADC. Why couldn't the jury make that inference from the overall testimony and the diagram? Is the problem that the inference required too much reconstruction by counsel/court after the fact, rather than an actual claim-by-claim mapping presented to the jury?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the Federal Circuit’s point.

In patent cases, the plaintiff has to present **substantial evidence on each claim limitation**, usually through an **element-by-element mapping**. The jury is allowed to draw reasonable inferences, but it cannot be asked to **reconstruct the plaintiff’s infringement theory from scattered, inconsistent testimony** after the fact.

That is why the court rejected Finesse’s “x1 and x2” argument. Dr. Wells did **not** clearly testify, “x1 is the signal of interest and x2 is the interference generating signal” (or vice versa). Instead, as the panel emphasized, he **repeatedly and expressly** mapped the claim terms to the **DL(TX) reference** and the **modeled PIM path**. The problem is that the modeled PIM path is generated **after sampling**, so if that is the “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] fails. The court therefore saw the later “x1 and x2” references as an attempted **pivot**, not a clear alternative mapping.

That is also why the panel said:

> “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony.”

and noted that Finesse was trying to support the verdict by **“piecing together parts of Dr. Wells’ testimony over ninety pages apart in the record.”**

So the issue was not that a jury can never infer from a diagram. It’s that here the inference would have required the jury — or the court on JMOL — to **supply a missing claim-by-claim mapping** that the expert never actually gave, while also disregarding his **contradictory testimony**. Under the opinion’s approach, that is not enough to sustain a verdict. The panel even cites **Johns Hopkins v. Datascope** for the proposition that a verdict cannot rest on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony when the patentee bears the burden of proof.

So, yes: the Federal Circuit’s objection was that this was too much **post hoc reconstruction**, not a sufficiently clear infringement presentation to the jury.

### Why didn't changing the claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' create prosecution disclaimer?
Question:
For the '775 patent, I expected the amendment history to be fatal. The original claims apparently said 'two or three signals,' the examiner rejected them over Filipovic, and the applicant amended the claims to require 'three signals S1, S2 and S3.' My instinct is that this should surrender systems using only two unique input signals. But the Federal Circuit says there was no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal because the applicant also distinguished Filipovic on the ground that it addressed active rather than passive IMPs. How can an applicant make a narrowing amendment like that and still avoid disclaimer? Is the court distinguishing between amendment-based estoppel/disclaimer and mere evidence of what the claims require?
Answer:
Because **prosecution disclaimer is narrow**. A claim amendment does **not** automatically surrender every embodiment that was covered by the earlier, broader language. The Federal Circuit requires a **“clear and unambiguous” disavowal** of scope.

That is what the court says happened **not** to occur here.

### What the prosecution history showed
Finesse did amend the claim from **“two or three signals”** to **“three signals S1, S2 and S3.”** But the prosecution record did **not** stop there. Finesse also argued that the Filipovic reference was different because it addressed **active** IMPs, while the claimed invention concerns **passive** IMPs. So the amendment was not an unmistakable statement like:

> “We are giving up all two-signal systems.”

Instead, the record left open **why** the claim was changed and **what exactly** was being surrendered.

### Why that matters
Under Federal Circuit law, an amendment can narrow a claim, but **disclaimer only happens if the patentee clearly surrenders the disputed scope**. If the history is mixed or ambiguous, the court will not infer a waiver.

That is why the court said the amendment **“does not rise to the level of clear and unambiguous disavowal”** and read it, at most, as requiring **three separately identifiable signals**, **not** three **unique** input signals. On that reading, a device using **two unique signals plus a copy** is not automatically excluded.

### So yes — the court is distinguishing two things
- **Claim language after amendment**: the claim now says “three signals S1, S2 and S3.”
- **Prosecution disclaimer**: whether the applicant clearly gave up two-signal implementations.

The first can narrow the claim, but the second requires a much clearer record. The court found that clarity missing here.

In short: **the amendment narrowed the claim, but the prosecution history did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim all two-unique-signal embodiments.**

### What is the legal difference between 'three separately identifiable signals' and 'three unique signals'?
Question:
The district court construed 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to mean 'separately identifiable,' but not necessarily 'unique.' I'm not sure I understand what that means in practice. If a system has only two underlying input signals, how can it still have three separately identifiable signals? Does this mean one of the signals can just be a copy or repetition of another, as long as it plays a distinct role in the multiplication terms? And is that a normal way courts read signal-processing claim language, or was it specific to this patent's intrinsic and extrinsic evidence?
Answer:
The short answer is that **“three separately identifiable signals” is weaker than “three unique signals.”**

### What the difference means
- **Unique signals** = three different underlying signals or inputs.
- **Separately identifiable signals** = three signals that can be **distinguished as separate claim elements in the claimed computation**, even if they are not all different in origin.

So, in principle, a system with only **two physical input signals** could still satisfy a “three separately identifiable signals” requirement if one of those signals is **duplicated, branched, or otherwise represented separately** for purposes of the claim’s multiplication terms. The key idea is that the claim needs **three identifiable inputs to the formula**, not necessarily three distinct sources.

### What the Federal Circuit actually said in *Finesse Wireless*
The court relied on the claim language and prosecution history to reject the idea that the claim required **three unique input signals**. It specifically said:

> “At most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals.”

That conclusion was based on the intrinsic record, including:
- the amendment to “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” and
- the lack of a **clear and unambiguous disavowal** of claim scope covering products with two unique input signals.

The court also noted inventor testimony that “S1, S2 and S3” could be “the same signal or different signals,” which supported the non-uniqueness reading.

### But that does not mean “copying” always suffices
Your instinct is right that the practical question is whether one signal can be counted twice or copied into a third role. Under this opinion, the answer is: **possibly yes, if the claim only requires separate identifiability**. But the accused product still has to **perform the claimed multiplications**. In this case, the Federal Circuit reversed because the evidence did **not** show the accused radios performed the **seven** required multiplications, not because the court adopted a general rule that duplicated signals always satisfy the claim.

### Is this a general rule?
No. This was **specific to this patent’s claim language and record**. The court’s reading depended on:
- the wording of the claim,
- the prosecution history, and
- the extrinsic inventor testimony.

That is standard claim-construction methodology, but the result is **fact- and patent-specific**. Courts do **not** generally read signal-processing claims to allow copies unless the intrinsic evidence supports that reading.

So, in this case:
- **“three unique signals”** would have been a stricter requirement,
- **“three separately identifiable signals”** allows more flexibility,
- and the Federal Circuit held that the patent did **not** clearly demand uniqueness.

### Why were three distinct multiplication expressions not enough to satisfy a claim listing seven multiplications?
Question:
The court ultimately reverses on the '775 patent because the accused radios only showed three distinct multiplications, while the claim required generating an n-th order ICS by multiplying and filtering seven listed products (S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, etc.). The district court seemed to think three distinct products could still correspond to the seven claimed products if some terms overlap when signals are not unique. Why did the Federal Circuit reject that? If S1, S2, and S3 are not required to be unique, wouldn't some of the seven listed products collapse into the same expression? Or is the court saying that, even if some terms could theoretically overlap, the patentee still had to prove at trial exactly how the accused system performed all seven claimed multiplications?
Answer:
Because the Federal Circuit treated the two issues as different:

1. **How many input signals the claim requires**, and  
2. **How many multiplication operations the claim requires**.

The district court’s construction of **“three signals S1, S2 and S3”** as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique” goes only to the **first** issue. So the panel agreed that the claim does **not** require three distinct physical signals in the sense of three different waveforms. The same underlying signal could potentially play more than one role.

But that does **not** eliminate the second requirement. Claim 1 of the ’775 patent expressly says the method “includes generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering” the **seven listed products**. The Federal Circuit read that as requiring proof that the accused radios actually performed the claimed multiplication set—not merely that they performed some mathematically related operations.

That is why the court rejected the district court’s “three distinct multiplications are enough because they overlap” reasoning. The opinion says:

- Dr. Wells identified only **three distinct multiplications** in Nokia’s document;
- he **did not testify how those three mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications**; and
- there was **no evidence** that any multiplications were repeated so that all seven claimed products were in fact performed.

So even if some of the seven claim recitations could theoretically collapse when the same signal is used more than once, **Finesse still had to prove that the accused system performed the full set of seven claimed multiplications, or at least provide evidence showing how the three observed expressions corresponded to all seven recited products**. It did neither.

In short: **non-unique signals might reduce the number of distinct inputs, but they do not automatically reduce the number of claimed multiplication operations.** The Federal Circuit reversed because the trial evidence showed only three distinct expressions, with no substantial evidence tying those expressions to the seven operations required by limitation 1[d].

### Why did the court vacate the damages award instead of deciding whether the damages trial was flawed?
Question:
The appellants also challenged the damages award and asked for JMOL or at least a new trial on damages, but after reversing on infringement the Federal Circuit just vacates the $166 million award. Is that because once all asserted claims are found not infringed, the damages issues become moot? If so, is 'vacated' the normal disposition rather than 'reversed' for damages, and does that leave any damages rulings with precedential or preclusive effect if the case were somehow revived later?
Answer:
Yes. In this opinion, the damages issue was effectively wiped out by the liability ruling.

The Federal Circuit first held that the jury’s infringement verdict could not stand and therefore **reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims**. Once that happened, there was no remaining infringement liability to support any damages award. So the court had no reason to decide whether the damages trial itself was flawed; even if it had been flawless, the $166 million award could not survive without infringement.

That is why the court says:

> “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, **we vacate the damages award**.”

### Why “vacated” rather than “reversed”?
These are doing different jobs:

- **Reverse** = the appellate court concludes the lower court got a ruling wrong on the merits.
- **Vacate** = the appellate court sets aside a judgment or award because it no longer has a valid basis.

Here, the court **reversed** the noninfringement ruling because that was the merits issue it decided. But the damages award was not independently analyzed and found erroneous; it was simply **derivative of the infringement verdict**, which had just been undone. So vacatur is the normal disposition.

### Does that make the damages questions moot?
Functionally, yes. Once all asserted claims are held not infringed, the damages arguments no longer matter to the outcome of the appeal. The court can dispose of the damages award without reaching the appellants’ JMOL/new-trial arguments on damages.

### Any precedential or preclusive effect left behind?
Not from the **vacated award** itself. A vacated judgment is treated as having been set aside, so it does not carry ordinary preclusive effect. And because the Federal Circuit did **not** decide the merits of the damages-trial arguments, it created no appellate precedent on those damages issues.

So if the case were somehow revived later, damages would not be bound by this vacated award; they would have to be litigated again unless some separate, surviving ruling controlled.
