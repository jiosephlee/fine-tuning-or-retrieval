Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Technical Setting, Patents, and Procedural Posture

## The radio-frequency interference problem

This case starts with a familiar communications-engineering problem: radio signals can interfere with one another when they interact with real-world hardware and surroundings. The patents describe “intermodulation products,” or IMPs, which are new unwanted frequencies created when transmit signals combine in a non-linear way. The opinion gives simple examples of the kinds of conditions that can cause this—metal fences, loose cable connections, and rusted connectors. These are not the intended parts of the communication system; they are obstacles or defects that distort the signals. When that happens, the system can generate interference in the same band as the desired signal, making reception worse.

The opinion also uses the term passive intermodulation, or PIM. PIM is a type of IMP caused by passive obstacles or components, meaning things that are not actively generating signals on their own. The court further distinguishes internal PIM from external PIM: internal PIM comes from mechanical issues inside the radio itself, while external PIM comes from sources outside the radio. That distinction matters because the patents are aimed at dealing with interference in radio systems, and the accused Nokia feature was designed as a PIM-cancellation tool.

## The patents and what they are trying to do

Finesse owns two patents at issue here, U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775. Both patents generally concern methods for mitigating IMP interference in radios. In broad terms, the inventions try to identify the signals that create interference, compute an estimate of the unwanted product, and cancel the interference before it harms performance.

The asserted claims from the ’134 patent, especially claim 1, focus on a method that oversamples received signals, isolates the signals of interest and the interference-generating signals, computes an estimate of the intermodulation products, and then cancels the in-band interference. The asserted claims from the ’775 patent take a different technical route, but the goal is the same: interference cancellation in a receiver. Claim 1 of the ’775 patent describes generating IMP cancellation signals continuously and near real time using copies of transmitter signals, with the process involving a power-series description and a specified set of signal multiplications. Although the technical details differ, both patents are aimed at reducing PIM-related interference in radio systems.

## The parties and the accused Nokia feature

Finesse is the patent owner and plaintiff. It sued AT&T Mobility LLC for infringement, and Nokia of America Corporation later intervened. Nokia was important because it supplied the accused radios, and the allegedly infringing functionality was Nokia’s PIM cancellation feature, often abbreviated PIM-C. In other words, AT&T was accused of using Nokia radios that supposedly practiced the patent claims.

The complaint focused on specific claims from each patent: claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. The infringement theory was that the accused Nokia radios implemented the claimed interference-cancellation methods. As the opinion later makes clear, the case turned on very technical signal-processing evidence about how Nokia’s radios worked and whether that operation matched the exact claim language.

## The trial and jury verdict

The case went to a jury in the Eastern District of Texas. The jury found all asserted claims valid and infringed, and it awarded Finesse a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the two patents. A lump-sum award is a single damages figure rather than an ongoing royalty or a per-unit calculation, so the award reflected the jury’s conclusion about the value of the alleged infringement over the rest of the patents’ term.

After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia challenged the result. They asked the district court for judgment as a matter of law, or JMOL, on noninfringement. JMOL is the procedural device a party uses to argue that no reasonable jury could have found for the other side on the evidence presented. They also sought JMOL on damages and, in the alternative, a new trial on damages. The district court denied those requests.

## The appeal to the Federal Circuit

AT&T and Nokia then appealed to the Federal Circuit. The appeal presented two main subjects. First, they argued that the evidence was legally insufficient to support the jury’s infringement findings for the asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents. Second, they challenged the damages ruling and sought a new trial on damages.

The Federal Circuit ultimately reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award. For a reader coming into the case, the important point is that the dispute was not about a broad policy question but about whether highly specific technical testimony and documentary evidence really matched the exact signal-processing limitations recited in the patent claims. That is why the technical setting, the patent language, and the procedural posture all matter from the very beginning.

# The Appellate Framework: JMOL, Substantial Evidence, Claim Construction, and Proof of Infringement

## Regional-circuit law controlled the post-trial motions

This appeal is a good reminder that not every issue in a patent case is governed by Federal Circuit law. When AT&T and Nokia challenged the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law and their request for a new trial, the Federal Circuit applied the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit, because those are ordinary procedural questions arising from a case tried in the Eastern District of Texas. That meant the court used Fifth Circuit standards for the Rule 50 motion and for the new-trial motion, even though the underlying dispute was a patent case. By contrast, patent-specific questions such as claim construction remained subject to Federal Circuit law.

The practical consequence is that the court was not asking a free-floating “did the jury get it right?” question. It was applying structured review doctrines that heavily shape how a verdict can be attacked on appeal. The opinion repeatedly shows that even when a party loses at trial, the verdict will stand if the record contains enough legally sufficient evidence under the governing standard.

## JMOL review asks whether the verdict could reasonably stand

A motion for judgment as a matter of law asks the court to take the case away from the jury because no reasonable jury could find for the nonmovant on the evidence presented. Under Fifth Circuit law, the denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, but with the evidence viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict. The question is whether the proof points so strongly and overwhelmingly in one direction that reasonable jurors could not have reached the contrary result.

That standard matters because the Federal Circuit was not simply deciding whether it agreed with the jury. It was deciding whether, given the record actually made at trial, the verdict had a legally adequate evidentiary basis. In patent cases, that often turns on whether the patentee proved each claim limitation with enough clarity to support a finding of infringement. If the record leaves a key limitation unsupported, or supported only by speculation or an internally inconsistent expert explanation, JMOL can be appropriate even after a jury verdict.

## Substantial evidence is the lens for reviewing the infringement finding

The Federal Circuit also described its review of the jury’s infringement finding as substantial-evidence review. That standard asks whether a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party on the evidence presented. It is a deferential standard, but not a rubber stamp. The court will uphold a verdict only if the trial record contains evidence that actually supports the specific claim limitations the patentee had to prove.

That point is central to this opinion. Finesse, as the patentee, bore the burden of proving that the accused Nokia radios met every asserted limitation of the claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents. The appellate problem was not simply whether the accused radios generally dealt with passive intermodulation. It was whether Finesse’s evidence, through its expert and the Nokia technical documents, actually established each recited limitation in a coherent way. The court concluded that it did not.

## Claim construction sets the legal frame for infringement

Claim construction is a legal issue reviewed de novo, although any subsidiary factual findings that support the construction are reviewed for clear error. That framework matters because the meaning of the claim terms determines what the patentee must prove at trial. In the ’775 patent dispute, the district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” That construction mattered because AT&T and Nokia argued that the accused system used only two unique signals. The district court rejected a uniqueness requirement, and the Federal Circuit did not reverse on that point.

Even so, the construction did not save the verdict. A favorable claim construction does not by itself prove infringement. The patentee still has to connect the accused technology to the claim language through evidence. Here, the court held that Finesse still failed to show the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications required by the ’775 patent, even under the district court’s construction.

## Expert testimony was the bridge between the claim language and the accused products

Because the accused radios were described through technical diagrams and internal Nokia documents, Dr. Wells’s testimony was the key evidentiary bridge between the patents and the accused products. That is common in patent cases: a technical expert translates diagrams, signal paths, and engineering terminology into the language of the asserted claims. But the expert’s testimony has to be coherent. It must actually map the claim limitations to the accused system in a way a reasonable jury can follow.

For the ’134 patent, the fatal problem was that Dr. Wells repeatedly tied the claim terms “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, even though the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the RF ADC that allegedly “samples” the signals. When confronted with that timing problem, he shifted and said there were “two signals” on the red path and suggested they were x1 and x2. But he never clearly and consistently said that x1 and x2 were the claim-mapped “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court treated that as a failure of proof, not merely a minor ambiguity.

For the ’775 patent, the same problem appeared in a different form. Dr. Wells pointed to three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document, but the claim required seven multiplications of the recited signals. The record did not show how the three document-based multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications, and the expert did not supply a clear explanation. That left a gap in the limitation-by-limitation showing that the jury was not free to fill with guesswork.

## The court drew the line between reasonable inference and after-the-fact reconstruction

A jury may draw reasonable inferences from evidence, but a party cannot win by reconstructing its proof after trial from scattered fragments of testimony that were never clearly tied together for the jury. That distinction is important here. On appeal, Finesse tried to piece together parts of Dr. Wells’s testimony from different parts of the record and to explain that he really meant x1 and x2 when he spoke about the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path. The Federal Circuit rejected that approach because the trial record itself did not clearly supply the needed mapping.

The opinion emphasizes that “there is nothing clear” about testimony that changes course without a coherent explanation. In other words, a verdict cannot be sustained by a lawyer’s later reconstruction of what the expert “must have meant.” The evidence presented at trial has to do the work.

## Johns Hopkins v. Datascope illustrates why contradictory expert testimony can fail as a matter of law

The Federal Circuit relied on Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. for the proposition that self-contradictory expert testimony may be insufficient to support a verdict of infringement. Johns Hopkins is useful because it shows that an expert’s mere presence on the stand does not automatically create substantial evidence. If the expert says incompatible things and does not reconcile them, the trier of fact may be left without a legally sufficient basis to find the claim limitation satisfied.

That is exactly how the court treated Dr. Wells’s testimony here. For the ’134 patent, his testimony was inconsistent about what the accused system sampled. For the ’775 patent, his testimony did not clearly establish the seven claimed multiplications. The court did not say that contradiction always destroys expert proof. Rather, it held that when the patentee’s case depends on an expert who repeatedly contradicts himself on the key limitation, and the record does not provide a clear, consistent mapping, the evidence can fail as a matter of law.

In the end, the standards of review and the evidentiary rules worked together to decide the appeal. Because Finesse did not present substantial evidence of infringement for the asserted claims of either patent, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and, once infringement fell away, vacated the damages award as well.

# The ’134 Patent: Why Finesse Failed to Prove Sampling of Both Claimed Signal Types

## What the ’134 Patent Is About

The ’134 patent concerns a way to reduce interference in radio systems caused by intermodulation products, or IMPs. In the opinion, the court explains that radios can generate unwanted new frequencies when transmit signals interact with obstacles or imperfect components, such as loose connections or rusted parts. When the source of the interference is a passive obstacle, the resulting interference is called passive intermodulation, or PIM. The patent’s basic idea is to identify the signals that matter, identify the signals that generate interference, estimate the resulting intermodulation products, and then cancel them.

That general concept matters because Finesse did not have to prove only that the accused radios performed some kind of interference cancellation. It had to prove the specific steps recited in the asserted claims. The Federal Circuit’s noninfringement ruling on the ’134 patent turned on one of those steps, limitation 1[a], and on whether Finesse’s expert testimony actually matched the accused system to the claim language in a coherent way.

## Representative Claim 1 and Limitation 1[a]

Claim 1 is representative of the asserted claims. It recites a method that begins by “over-sampling” a passband of received signals to create a bit stream. The claim then requires that the received signals include two categories: “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Later steps isolate those signals, compute an estimate of the intermodulation products, cancel them, and perform phase and amplitude adjustment.

The key point for the court was limitation 1[a]. Finesse had the burden to show that the accused radios sample a passband of received signals that includes both categories of signals. In other words, it was not enough to show that the radios do some interference cancellation or that they contain signals that can be associated with the claim at some later stage. The claimed receiver has to over-sample a received passband that already includes both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.”

That temporal and functional requirement became the central problem for Finesse’s proof.

## Dr. Wells’s Trial Theory and the Nokia Document

At trial, Finesse relied on its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, and on a Nokia technical document that depicted how the accused radios operate. In that document, the court describes a blue box for the RF ADC and a dashed red path showing relevant signals. Dr. Wells testified that the RF ADC samples the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to create a bit stream.

Dr. Wells gave a very specific mapping. He said the downlink transmit reference, or DL(TX) reference, was the “signal of interest.” He also said the modeled PIM path was the “interference generating signals.” He repeated that mapping in both testimony and his expert report. So, at least on its face, his opinion seemed to satisfy limitation 1[a]: the RF ADC samples a passband containing the downlink reference and the modeled PIM path.

The problem was that the accused system’s own structure undercut that theory.

## The Technical Problem on Cross-Examination

On cross-examination, Appellants showed that the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling has already occurred. That matters because the claim requires the receiver to sample signals that include the interference-generating signals. But a signal cannot be sampled before it exists.

This is the heart of the Federal Circuit’s reversal. If the modeled PIM path is the “interference generating signals,” then it cannot also be one of the signals sampled by the RF ADC, because the modeled PIM path is created later in the process. The district court recognized that “the modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals.’” Once that mapping is accepted, Finesse’s infringement theory collapses on limitation 1[a].

## Dr. Wells’s Attempted Pivot to x1 and x2

Confronted with this problem, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony. He suggested that there were “two signals on that red path” and referred to x1 and x2. But the Federal Circuit emphasized that he never clearly said x1 and x2 were the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” He continued to describe the red path itself as the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, and when asked directly, he still identified the modeled PIM path as the interference-generating signal.

This is where the testimony became internally inconsistent. Finesse’s appellate position was that Dr. Wells really meant x1 and x2 when he talked about the red path, and that the court should read his testimony that way in light of the Nokia diagram. But the Federal Circuit was not willing to reconstruct a clearer infringement theory out of scattered pieces of testimony. The court noted that Dr. Wells never expressly mapped x1 or x2 onto either required claim category.

In other words, the pivot did not fix the original problem. It introduced ambiguity where the claim required a clear showing.

## Why the Federal Circuit Rejected the District Court’s Reading

The district court thought Dr. Wells was “clearly referring to x1 and x2” when he testified that there are two signals entering the RF ADC. The Federal Circuit disagreed. It said there was “nothing clear” about the testimony because Dr. Wells never actually testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the two claim categories.

The appellate court also rejected Finesse’s effort to assemble a better theory by combining testimony from different parts of the record, separated by many pages. The court explained that Finesse was trying to piece together a mapping from testimony that was not itself clear or consistent. That was not enough to support a jury verdict.

This is an important appellate lesson: even under the deferential substantial-evidence standard, a verdict cannot stand if the patentee’s key proof depends on a witness who contradicts himself on the very element at issue and never gives a clean explanation that the jury could reasonably adopt.

## The Role of Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony

The court relied on Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. for the principle that a party with the burden of proof cannot rely on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony to sustain a verdict when the contradictions go to an essential claim limitation. The Federal Circuit saw Dr. Wells’s testimony in exactly that light.

He first said the DL(TX) reference was the signal of interest and the modeled PIM path was the interference-generating signal. When that theory was challenged because the modeled PIM path is created after sampling, he shifted to x1 and x2. But he never clearly connected x1 and x2 to the claim terms. The result was not a coherent alternate theory; it was a contradiction that never got resolved.

Because Finesse bore the burden of proof, the court did not have to treat this contradictory expert testimony as enough for a reasonable jury to find infringement.

## The Holding on the ’134 Patent

The Federal Circuit therefore held that substantial evidence did not support the jury’s infringement verdict on the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. The key failure was limitation 1[a]: Finesse did not prove that the accused radios over-sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” in the manner the claim requires.

Accordingly, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the ’134 patent. Because the noninfringement ruling eliminated the basis for the verdict, it also contributed to the court’s later decision to vacate the damages award.

# The ’775 Patent: Claim Scope, Prosecution History, and the Failure to Prove Seven Claimed Multiplications

## What the ’775 Patent Covers

The ’775 patent concerns passive intermodulation, or PIM, in radio systems. The basic idea is that signals traveling through a transmitter and receiver can create unwanted interference when they interact with passive components, such as connectors or other physical obstacles. The patent claims a method for canceling those passive intermodulation products by generating cancellation signals in a receiver that is co-located with a transmitter.

That general purpose matters, but the infringement dispute turned on the details of claim 1. The Federal Circuit was not deciding whether Nokia’s radios could cancel PIM in some general sense. It was deciding whether Finesse proved that the accused radios practiced the specific claim limitations the patent actually recites.

## Representative Claim 1 and the Seven-Multiplication Limitation

Claim 1 is the key claim. It requires, among other things, generating intermodulation product cancellation signals continuously and near real time, and it requires doing so “using copies of transmitter signals.” The claim’s most important technical limitation for purposes of this appeal is the one in subsection [d], which says that generating an n-th order cancellation signal includes, “given three signals S1, S2 and S3,” digitally multiplying and filtering seven listed triple products.

Those seven listed products are not incidental. They are the specific mathematical operations the patentee chose to claim. So to prove infringement, Finesse needed evidence that the accused radios performed those claimed multiplications, not just something loosely similar.

## The District Court’s Construction of “Three Signals S1, S2 and S3”

At summary judgment, the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, but not necessarily “unique” signals. In other words, the court accepted that the claim did not require three entirely different input signals in the strictest sense. The court relied on the intrinsic record and inventor testimony suggesting that the signals could be the same or different, so long as they were separately identifiable.

That construction left Finesse some room. Under the district court’s view, a system might still satisfy the claim even if it used only two unique inputs plus a copy of one of them. But even with that broader reading, Finesse still had to prove the accused system actually performed the claim’s seven multiplications.

## The Waiver Problem and the Prosecution-History Argument

The defendants argued in the district court that Finesse could not now read the claim to cover systems with only two unique signals, because Finesse had amended the claims during prosecution to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” instead of “two or three signals.” The district court rejected that line of attack in part as waived, because Appellants had not preserved the precise “unique signals” argument in their Rule 50(a) motion.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit did not treat the amendment as a clear surrender of two-signal systems. The court looked at the prosecution history and concluded there was no “clear and unambiguous” disavowal. The examiner had rejected broader claims that allowed “two or three signals” over Filipovic, but Finesse responded by distinguishing Filipovic on the ground that Filipovic addressed active intermodulation, whereas the patent at issue dealt with passive intermodulation. The amendment to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” therefore did not amount to an unmistakable disclaimer of any system involving only two unique inputs. At most, the court read the amendment as requiring three separately identifiable signals, not a fixed number of unique signals.

## Dr. Wells’s Trial Theory and the Nokia Document

Even though the Federal Circuit rejected the defendants’ narrowing claim-scope argument, Finesse still had to connect the accused radios to the seven claimed multiplications. Its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to do that. His testimony focused on two signals, x1 and x2, and he tried to equate the document’s operations with the claim’s required multiplication steps.

The problem was that Dr. Wells’s testimony was confused and inconsistent. At times, he mapped the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. At other times, after being pressed on cross-examination, he suggested that x1 and x2 were the actual inputs on the red path. But he never gave a clear trial-level explanation tying each of the seven claim products to the accused operations.

## Why the Proof Failed on the Seven Multiplications

The Federal Circuit’s key point was that the Nokia document did not show the seven claimed multiplications. Instead, the document showed only three distinct multiplications: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', and x1·|x2|·|x2|. Those expressions involved modulus and complex-conjugate operations, not the exact set of seven triple products recited in the claim.

That mattered because Finesse’s burden was not just to show “some” third-order multiplication. It had to show the seven specific multiplications listed in claim 1[d]. The record did not supply a mapping from the three disclosed operations to all seven claimed products, and Dr. Wells did not testify how the three accused operations corresponded to each claimed multiplication. Nor did the evidence show that any of the three multiplications were repeated in a way that would add up to the seven claimed operations.

Finesse tried to patch the gap on appeal by offering a more elaborate explanation of how the Nokia document should be read, but appellate argument is not evidence. The jury never heard a clear, supported mapping, and the record could not support one after the fact. The court also noted the logical difficulty in Finesse’s theory: it would require treating S3 as if it could simultaneously correspond to x2, x2', and |x2|, which the trial evidence did not explain.

## The Federal Circuit’s Holding on the ’775 Patent

The Federal Circuit therefore split the defendants’ claim-scope argument from the infringement proof. It rejected the idea that Finesse had clearly disclaimed systems using only two unique inputs, but it still held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement because the evidence did not establish the seven claimed multiplications. On that basis, the court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted ’775 claims.

That reversal mattered procedurally as well as substantively. Because the court reversed noninfringement on the merits, the patent damages award could not stand. The ’775 patent issue thus illustrates an important appellate lesson: winning a claim-construction argument does not help the patentee if the proof at trial still fails to match the exact claim language.

# Disposition, Damages, and the Case’s Litigation Lessons

## Why the damages award did not survive the liability reversal

The court’s treatment of damages follows directly from its holding on infringement. Finesse won a jury verdict of infringement and a large lump-sum damages award, but the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to every asserted claim. Once the liability verdict fell, there was no basis left for a damages award tied to that verdict. The court therefore vacated the damages award rather than separately affirming, modifying, or recalculating it.

That is an important remedial point in patent cases. Damages are not an independent entitlement floating free of infringement liability. If the patentee does not prove infringement, the damages award based on that finding cannot stand. So even though the district court had also denied Appellants’ separate post-trial challenges to damages and their request for a new trial, the appellate court did not need to decide whether the damages evidence was independently defective.

## The standard of review for a new trial on damages

The opinion briefly states the governing review framework. On a motion for a new trial, the Federal Circuit applies regional circuit law—in this case, Fifth Circuit law. Under that law, denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. That is a deferential standard: the appellate court does not reweigh the evidence as a first-instance matter, but asks whether the trial judge made a legally mistaken or unreasonable decision in refusing a new trial.

Here, however, the court never had to apply that standard to the damages merits because the liability verdict itself was reversed. The damages challenge became unnecessary to resolve in light of the noninfringement holding. The court’s reasoning shows a common appellate sequence: if the predicate liability judgment is undone, the court may dispose of the damages award without reaching whether the damages trial was independently flawed.

## Why the court vacated damages instead of reaching the damages arguments

The court’s decision to vacate the damages award is doctrinally straightforward. The jury’s damages figure rested on the premise that AT&T and Nokia infringed valid claims. Once the Federal Circuit concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement on the record presented, the damages award lost its legal foundation. Vacatur is the cleanest disposition because it removes an award that cannot survive without a liability finding.

This also explains the court’s terse treatment of damages. The opinion does not analyze methodology, apportionment, or the amount of the award. Those issues became irrelevant once the court determined that Finesse had not carried its burden on infringement for either patent. In that posture, the court’s job was not to recalibrate the damages case, but to eliminate an award that depended on an overturned verdict.

## The formal disposition

The case ends with a simple but consequential judgment: the Federal Circuit “REVERSED AND VACATED.” The reversal was directed at the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement. The vacatur applied to the damages award. Costs were taxed to the defendants-appellants, which is a formal appellate allocation of litigation costs and does not change the merits outcome.

For the parties, the practical result is that Finesse lost its infringement judgment and the associated damages recovery. For the defendants, the ruling erased the jury’s liability and monetary award. The disposition thus reflects the court’s view that the record did not support the verdicts on which the judgment rested.

## Claim scope versus proof at trial

One of the opinion’s central lessons is that claim construction and proof of infringement are separate steps. Finesse had a potentially favorable construction fight on the ’775 patent: the district court rejected the argument that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” had to be unique signals, and the Federal Circuit agreed that Finesse had not clearly disavowed two-signal embodiments. But that construction win did not end the case. Finesse still had to prove, with trial evidence, that the accused radios satisfied each express limitation.

The Federal Circuit’s analysis repeatedly shows this distinction. On the ’134 patent, the problem was not only how the claim should be read, but whether the evidence really showed the accused system sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” On the ’775 patent, the court accepted that the claim did not require unique signals, yet still found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven multiplications recited by the claim. A patentee can win on claim scope and still lose on infringement if the proof does not match the claim language.

## The need for precise limitation-by-limitation mapping

The opinion is especially instructive on how technical documents must be mapped to claim terms. The court was not persuaded by broad conclusions that the accused Nokia radios “basically” did what the claims require. It wanted a clear mapping from each claim limitation to an identified feature in the accused system. When the mapping was unclear, internally inconsistent, or incomplete, the verdict could not stand.

That problem appeared in both patents. For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells’ testimony shifted between the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path on one hand and x1 and x2 on the other, without a clean explanation of how those elements corresponded to the claim terms. For the ’775 patent, the evidence showed only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document, while the claim required seven specified multiplications. The court found no record evidence explaining how those three operations covered all seven claim requirements. In a complex case, the jury still needs a coherent, limitation-by-limitation roadmap.

## Why post hoc explanations could not save the verdict

A major theme in the opinion is that appellate counsel’s later explanation cannot cure what the expert actually told the jury. Finesse tried on appeal to reconstruct Dr. Wells’ testimony into a more favorable and internally consistent theory. But the court emphasized that the jury did not hear that clean theory from the witness in the first place. The record, as presented at trial, remained confusing and contradictory.

That matters because JMOL review asks whether the evidence presented to the jury was sufficient to support the verdict. It is not enough that a party can later assemble a plausible interpretation from scattered statements in the record, especially when those statements were not clearly connected at trial. The court’s response to Finesse’s post hoc explanation is a reminder that appellate reconstruction is not a substitute for trial proof.

## The warning on contradictory expert testimony

The opinion uses one of the strongest possible signals that expert testimony was not enough: it cites prior Federal Circuit precedent holding that a verdict cannot rest on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony. That is the real danger the case illustrates. In a technically dense patent trial, an expert may sound persuasive on direct examination but then undermine the infringement theory on cross-examination or in later questioning. If the testimony never settles into a stable, claim-specific explanation, the patentee risks losing even after a favorable jury verdict.

The Federal Circuit’s treatment of both patents shows that contradiction is not a harmless flaw when the patentee bears the burden of proof. If the expert’s testimony leaves the court unable to identify a supported finding that each claim limitation is met, JMOL of noninfringement may be warranted. The case therefore serves as a warning to patent plaintiffs: technical shorthand and diagram-based explanations must still map cleanly onto the claim language.

## Significance for future JMOL motions in technical patent cases

The broader litigation lesson is that JMOL remains a meaningful tool in patent cases built on schematics, diagrams, and expert interpretation. Even when a jury returns a plaintiff’s verdict, the court will test whether the record truly contains substantial evidence for each limitation. The presence of a technical document or a highly qualified expert is not enough if the testimony is unclear, inconsistent, or fails to explain the mapping in a way the jury could reasonably rely on.

For future cases, this opinion underscores the importance of preparing expert testimony that is both technically accurate and legally precise. The expert must identify which accused components correspond to which claim terms and must do so consistently. If the theory depends on a later appellate explanation, or if the trial testimony slides between multiple possible mappings, the verdict may be vulnerable on JMOL. That is the practical significance of this case: in patent litigation, winning the claim-construction fight does not end the burden of proving infringement with clear, trial-ready evidence.
