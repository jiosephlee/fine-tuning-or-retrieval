Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Seven Multiplications Means Seven: Algorithmic Exactness in Proving Infringement of Signal-Processing Patents

## Why this case matters

In **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility**, the Federal Circuit did something that matters well beyond this dispute: it treated a mathematically specific patent claim as exactly that — specific. Finesse had won a sprawling jury verdict, including more than $166 million in damages, by arguing that Nokia’s PIM-cancellation feature in AT&T’s radios practiced two signal-processing patents. But the court reversed on noninfringement and vacated the damages award, finding that Finesse’s proof never actually lined up with the claim language.

The deeper lesson is especially important for software, DSP, and telecom patents. In these cases, infringement often turns on whether the accused product performs a precisely defined computational sequence. Broad functional similarity is not enough if the claim recites a particular set of inputs, operations, or outputs. Here, the panel’s message was blunt: if the claim says seven multiplications, the patentee has to prove seven multiplications — not three, not ten, and not a hand-waving approximation involving modulus or complex-conjugate operations.

## Seven multiplications, not a vibe

The cleanest part of the opinion concerns the ’775 patent. That patent claims a method for interference cancellation in a receiver, including a limitation that an n-th order cancellation signal be generated by “digitally multiplying” three signals in seven specified combinations. The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique,” and the Federal Circuit did not disturb that construction. So this was not a loss on claim construction.

It was a loss on proof.

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to argue that the accused radios practiced the claim. But the document did not show seven claimed multiplications. It showed three distinct multiplications, and those operations involved things like modulus and complex conjugate terms. That is not the same thing as the seven multiplications recited in the claim. The panel emphasized that Dr. Wells never explained how the three operations in the document mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications, and Finesse’s later attempt to build a more elaborate mapping on appeal could not save the verdict because that theory was never presented to the jury.

That is the heart of the decision. The court was not saying that all mathematical transformations must be identical in literal form to infringe, nor was it announcing a categorical rule that modulus or conjugation can never fit within a claim. It was saying something more basic and more demanding: the plaintiff’s trial proof did not establish the specific sequence of operations the claims required. In a highly technical case, that distinction is fatal.

## The court’s insistence on evidentiary precision

The opinion is also notable for how little patience it shows for expert testimony that shifts under pressure. Finesse’s case depended heavily on Dr. Wells, but his testimony was, in the court’s words, confused and unclear. On appeal, Finesse tried to recast his statements as referring to different signals or to different parts of the Nokia diagram. The panel was not persuaded. It read the testimony as self-contradictory: the expert repeatedly mapped the claim to the wrong path, then tried to pivot when cross-examination exposed the mismatch.

That matters doctrinally because it shows the Federal Circuit is willing to treat internal inconsistency in expert proof as a noninfringement problem, not merely a credibility issue for the jury. The court relied on precedent holding that a verdict cannot stand where the patentee’s expert gives contradictory testimony on a limitation the patentee must prove. For litigants, that is a warning: if the infringement theory depends on reconstructing the claim from scattered snippets of testimony, the verdict may not survive JMOL.

## Claim scope was not the problem; proof was

One potentially important nuance is that the court did **not** accept AT&T and Nokia’s broader claim-scope argument that Finesse had clearly surrendered coverage over two-signal implementations during prosecution. The panel concluded there was no clear and unambiguous disclaimer. So the claim construction fight did not resolve the case against Finesse.

But that actually makes the decision more interesting. The Federal Circuit was willing to preserve some breadth in the claim language while still holding the patentee to a strict evidentiary burden at trial. In other words, a patent can remain broad enough in theory and still fail in practice because the accused product does not do what the claim, as construed, requires.

That separation between scope and proof is likely to matter in future DSP and telecom cases. Plaintiffs often rely on diagrams, models, and vendor documents to show that a receiver or baseband processor performs the claimed steps. This case suggests that such proof needs to be mapped with real rigor. It is not enough to show that the accused system performs a broadly similar cancellation routine or that it operates in the same conceptual neighborhood. The theory has to account for each claimed operation, including exactly how many times a multiplication is performed and what the operands are.

## The downstream effect

The broader trend here is familiar: as patent claims become more algorithmic, infringement analysis becomes less forgiving. That is especially true in signal-processing litigation, where the line between “mathematically related” and “claim-satisfying” can be very thin. Finesse Wireless reinforces that courts will not let experts collapse those categories by using technical shorthand or by retrofitting a theory after the fact.

The practical takeaway for patentees is straightforward. If your claim recites a particular computational recipe, your infringement case has to show that recipe, step by step, with the same kind of precision. For accused infringers, the opinion is a reminder that a mismatch between the claim language and the accused math can be decisive even after a jury verdict.

And because the Federal Circuit reversed noninfringement on both patents, the damages award fell with them. But the ’775 ruling is the one that will likely echo in future cases: in the world of DSP patents, **seven really does mean seven**.
