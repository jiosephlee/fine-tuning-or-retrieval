Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## Three Signals, Two Inputs, No Disclaimer: The Federal Circuit’s Narrow View of Prosecution-Based Surrender

## Why this case matters

The most important part of *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is not the radio-frequency math. It is the Federal Circuit’s reminder that a patent amendment does not become a prosecution-history surrender just because it looks narrower on paper.

That matters because patent applicants often trim claim language to get around prior art, and accused infringers just as often argue that the trim amounts to a confession of lost scope. In this case, the applicant had originally pursued language reciting “two or three signals” and later amended to “three signals.” The defendants argued that amendment gave up two-input implementations. The panel said no: not without a clear and unambiguous record of surrender.

## The Federal Circuit’s reading of the prosecution history

The ’775 patent claims a method for generating passive intermodulation cancellation signals using “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” and the claim at issue requires seven specified multiplications. During prosecution, the applicant faced an obviousness rejection over Filipovic. It did respond by changing the claim language from “two or three signals” to “three signals,” but that change did not come in a vacuum. The applicant also argued that Filipovic was about active IMPs, while the claimed invention addressed passive IMPs.

That context mattered. The Federal Circuit refused to treat the amendment as a clear and unmistakable disavowal of two-input embodiments. In the court’s view, the prosecution record did not say, in substance, “we are giving up two-signal implementations.” At most, the amendment suggested that the claim requires three separately identifiable signals, not three unique input signals. That distinction is important, and the panel was careful not to collapse it.

This is classic Federal Circuit disclaimer doctrine, but applied in a notably restrained way. A narrowing amendment can support surrender, but only when the public record makes the renunciation plain. Here, the record was mixed: the applicant amended, but it also won allowance by distinguishing the prior art on a different basis. The court was unwilling to infer that the numerical edit did all the work.

## Why the court’s approach is doctrinally significant

There is a real tension here between the intuitive force of numeric claim language and the Federal Circuit’s insistence on clarity before finding disclaimer. On one reading, “three signals” sounds like a hard boundary. On another, it is simply a label for three identifiable signal slots in the calculation, which might or might not be populated by three distinct physical inputs. The court chose the second, more flexible reading, and it did so without treating the amendment as a hidden surrender of two-signal variants.

That is, in my view, the right instinct. Prosecution-history disclaimer is meant to protect the public from gamesmanship and from patentees trying to recapture what they gave up. But it is not supposed to turn every amendment into an estoppel trap. When an applicant amends and also distinguishes the prior art on a separate ground, courts should be cautious about treating the amendment as a wholesale concession on an issue the applicant never squarely addressed.

The downstream lesson for prosecutors is straightforward: if you mean to preserve certain embodiments, say so; if you mean to give up others, say that too. Vague numerical edits are a poor substitute for a precise record. For litigators, the message is equally clear. A change from “two or three” to “three” may be useful evidence, but it is not enough by itself to establish surrender. You need something in the file history that ties the amendment to the disputed scope.

## The practical takeaway

It is also worth noting that Finesse’s win on disclaimer did not save the day. The Federal Circuit still reversed because it found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. So the case ends up illustrating two separate points at once: prosecution history did not narrow the claim as far as the defendants wanted, but the infringement proof still failed on the merits.

That combination makes the decision especially useful for future cases. It reinforces a high bar for prosecution-based surrender while also underscoring that patentees cannot rely on broad claim scope alone. Even if the claim survives construction, the trial record has to map the accused technology onto every limitation with something more than suggestive diagrams and contradictory testimony.

For patent prosecutors and litigators, *Finesse* is a reminder that numbers in claims are not self-defining, and amendments are not self-executing disclaimers. Clear surrender requires clear words.
