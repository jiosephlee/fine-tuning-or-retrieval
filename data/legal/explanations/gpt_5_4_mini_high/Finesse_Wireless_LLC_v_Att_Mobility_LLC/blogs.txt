Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When an Expert’s Mapping Collapses: *Finesse Wireless* and the Federal Circuit’s Limits on Salvaging Jury Verdicts

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility* is important for a reason that goes well beyond radio-frequency jargon: it is a reminder that patent infringement remains an element-by-element proof exercise, not a post-verdict reconstruction project. When a patentee’s expert gives inconsistent testimony at trial, a district court cannot rescue the verdict by stitching together the cleanest pieces of that testimony after the fact. That point drove the reversal here on both patents, but it is especially sharp in the court’s treatment of the ’134 patent, where Chief Judge Moore’s opinion insists that the record must contain a coherent mapping, not merely an arguable one assembled in appellate briefing.

The decision also matters because it shows the Federal Circuit using familiar JMOL principles to police the boundary between jury factfinding and evidentiary sufficiency. Patent plaintiffs often survive by showing a technical expert with a diagram, a claim chart, and enough testimony to leave infringement “for the jury.” But *Finesse* underscores that the jury still needs a stable theory to decide. If the expert keeps shifting what the accused system supposedly maps to the claim language, the verdict may not have the substantial-evidence footing required to stand.

## The ’134 patent: no amount of post hoc smoothing can fix contradictory testimony

The court’s reversal on the ’134 patent is the most striking part of the opinion because the problem was not simply that the proof was thin. The problem was that the plaintiff’s own expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, gave internally inconsistent testimony about what in the accused Nokia radios corresponded to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.”

That mattered because the claim required the accused radios to sample a passband of received signals that includes both categories. Dr. Wells initially mapped those claim terms to the downlink transmit reference and the “modeled PIM path” shown in Nokia’s technical document. But that theory ran into a basic temporal problem: the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling, so it could not have been part of what the ADC sampled. When pressed on cross-examination, Dr. Wells tried to pivot, saying there were “two signals on that red path” and referring to x1 and x2. The difficulty, as the panel saw it, was that he never clearly testified that x1 and x2 were the claim elements the way Finesse later claimed on appeal. Instead, he kept reverting to the downlink reference and modeled PIM path.

Chief Judge Moore’s response is notable for its plainness: “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony.” That sentence captures the whole case. Finesse asked the court to read the record as if the expert had really meant x1 and x2 all along, pointing to scattered testimony and the Nokia document’s legend. The panel refused. It was not enough for the patentee to assemble a plausible mapping by piecing together answers from different parts of the transcript, especially when the expert had repeatedly said something else.

The court’s reliance on *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* is doing real work here. *Datascope* stands for the proposition that a jury verdict cannot rest on expert testimony that contradicts itself on the very limitation at issue. *Finesse* applies that principle in a straightforward but important way: if the burdened party’s proof depends on an expert’s self-contradictory explanation, the court need not pretend the inconsistency can be harmonized. That is a useful reminder in patent cases, where technical witnesses sometimes use visual aids and shorthand in ways that can be hard to police in real time. When the inconsistency goes to the core element of infringement, JMOL is not only available but required.

## The ’775 patent: another reminder that “close enough” is not enough

The court’s treatment of the ’775 patent is different in detail but consistent in method. There, the panel rejected the argument that Finesse had clearly surrendered claim scope over products using only two unique input signals. The prosecution history did not amount to the kind of “clear and unambiguous” disavowal needed to narrow the claim that far. But that win for Finesse at the claim-scope level did not save the verdict, because the record still failed on proof of the claimed seven multiplications.

Again, the point is evidentiary. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that listed only three distinct multiplications, and the opinion emphasizes that Finesse never explained how those three corresponded to the seven specific multiplications recited in the claim. The accused radios’ document also involved modulus and complex conjugate operations, which made the mapping even more problematic. The Federal Circuit was not willing to let Finesse convert “three distinct multiplications” into “seven claimed multiplications” by implication, especially when the expert did not supply a trial-ready explanation.

This part of the opinion reinforces a theme that runs through modern patent litigation: claim construction and prosecution history can set the outer boundaries, but infringement still turns on concrete proof. A patentee can win the claim-scope fight and still lose badly if the trial evidence never actually walks through each required step. In that sense, *Finesse* is less about technical radio design than about the discipline of proof.

## What the decision means going forward

The practical effect of *Finesse* will be felt most in how patent cases are tried. Experts will need to be much more careful about keeping their claim charts, demonstratives, and oral testimony aligned. A witness who gives a nuanced answer on direct but a different one on cross may create exactly the kind of record that invites JMOL. And because the Federal Circuit here was willing to say that the district court could not reconstruct a coherent theory from scattered testimony, trial lawyers should expect less patience for “the jury could figure it out” arguments when the record is messy.

The opinion also says something important about appellate review. The court did not reweigh the evidence or substitute its technical judgment for the jury’s. Instead, it focused on whether a reasonable jury had a stable evidentiary basis to find infringement. Once the panel concluded the answer was no on the ’134 patent, the rest followed: reversal of JMOL denial, and because infringement fell away on all asserted claims, vacatur of the damages award.

That last point matters too. The court did not engage the damages theory on the merits; it simply vacated the award once the liability verdict collapsed. That is standard, but it shows how much rides on the integrity of the infringement record. In a case with a nine-figure lump-sum verdict, an expert’s inability to keep the mapping straight can be outcome-determinative.

## Bottom line

*Finesse Wireless* is a useful corrective to any temptation to treat patent infringement as a puzzle that can be solved after trial by cleaning up the record. The Federal Circuit’s message is that the plaintiff must prove each claim element through a coherent and consistent trial theory. If the expert’s mapping collapses under cross-examination, the verdict may not survive just because the patentee can, on appeal, suggest a better version of what the witness must have meant.



## Three Signals, Two Inputs, No Disclaimer: The Federal Circuit’s Narrow View of Prosecution-Based Surrender

## Why this case matters

The most important part of *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is not the radio-frequency math. It is the Federal Circuit’s reminder that a patent amendment does not become a prosecution-history surrender just because it looks narrower on paper.

That matters because patent applicants often trim claim language to get around prior art, and accused infringers just as often argue that the trim amounts to a confession of lost scope. In this case, the applicant had originally pursued language reciting “two or three signals” and later amended to “three signals.” The defendants argued that amendment gave up two-input implementations. The panel said no: not without a clear and unambiguous record of surrender.

## The Federal Circuit’s reading of the prosecution history

The ’775 patent claims a method for generating passive intermodulation cancellation signals using “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” and the claim at issue requires seven specified multiplications. During prosecution, the applicant faced an obviousness rejection over Filipovic. It did respond by changing the claim language from “two or three signals” to “three signals,” but that change did not come in a vacuum. The applicant also argued that Filipovic was about active IMPs, while the claimed invention addressed passive IMPs.

That context mattered. The Federal Circuit refused to treat the amendment as a clear and unmistakable disavowal of two-input embodiments. In the court’s view, the prosecution record did not say, in substance, “we are giving up two-signal implementations.” At most, the amendment suggested that the claim requires three separately identifiable signals, not three unique input signals. That distinction is important, and the panel was careful not to collapse it.

This is classic Federal Circuit disclaimer doctrine, but applied in a notably restrained way. A narrowing amendment can support surrender, but only when the public record makes the renunciation plain. Here, the record was mixed: the applicant amended, but it also won allowance by distinguishing the prior art on a different basis. The court was unwilling to infer that the numerical edit did all the work.

## Why the court’s approach is doctrinally significant

There is a real tension here between the intuitive force of numeric claim language and the Federal Circuit’s insistence on clarity before finding disclaimer. On one reading, “three signals” sounds like a hard boundary. On another, it is simply a label for three identifiable signal slots in the calculation, which might or might not be populated by three distinct physical inputs. The court chose the second, more flexible reading, and it did so without treating the amendment as a hidden surrender of two-signal variants.

That is, in my view, the right instinct. Prosecution-history disclaimer is meant to protect the public from gamesmanship and from patentees trying to recapture what they gave up. But it is not supposed to turn every amendment into an estoppel trap. When an applicant amends and also distinguishes the prior art on a separate ground, courts should be cautious about treating the amendment as a wholesale concession on an issue the applicant never squarely addressed.

The downstream lesson for prosecutors is straightforward: if you mean to preserve certain embodiments, say so; if you mean to give up others, say that too. Vague numerical edits are a poor substitute for a precise record. For litigators, the message is equally clear. A change from “two or three” to “three” may be useful evidence, but it is not enough by itself to establish surrender. You need something in the file history that ties the amendment to the disputed scope.

## The practical takeaway

It is also worth noting that Finesse’s win on disclaimer did not save the day. The Federal Circuit still reversed because it found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. So the case ends up illustrating two separate points at once: prosecution history did not narrow the claim as far as the defendants wanted, but the infringement proof still failed on the merits.

That combination makes the decision especially useful for future cases. It reinforces a high bar for prosecution-based surrender while also underscoring that patentees cannot rely on broad claim scope alone. Even if the claim survives construction, the trial record has to map the accused technology onto every limitation with something more than suggestive diagrams and contradictory testimony.

For patent prosecutors and litigators, *Finesse* is a reminder that numbers in claims are not self-defining, and amendments are not self-executing disclaimers. Clear surrender requires clear words.



# Seven Multiplications Means Seven: Algorithmic Exactness in Proving Infringement of Signal-Processing Patents

## Why this case matters

In **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility**, the Federal Circuit did something that matters well beyond this dispute: it treated a mathematically specific patent claim as exactly that — specific. Finesse had won a sprawling jury verdict, including more than $166 million in damages, by arguing that Nokia’s PIM-cancellation feature in AT&T’s radios practiced two signal-processing patents. But the court reversed on noninfringement and vacated the damages award, finding that Finesse’s proof never actually lined up with the claim language.

The deeper lesson is especially important for software, DSP, and telecom patents. In these cases, infringement often turns on whether the accused product performs a precisely defined computational sequence. Broad functional similarity is not enough if the claim recites a particular set of inputs, operations, or outputs. Here, the panel’s message was blunt: if the claim says seven multiplications, the patentee has to prove seven multiplications — not three, not ten, and not a hand-waving approximation involving modulus or complex-conjugate operations.

## Seven multiplications, not a vibe

The cleanest part of the opinion concerns the ’775 patent. That patent claims a method for interference cancellation in a receiver, including a limitation that an n-th order cancellation signal be generated by “digitally multiplying” three signals in seven specified combinations. The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique,” and the Federal Circuit did not disturb that construction. So this was not a loss on claim construction.

It was a loss on proof.

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to argue that the accused radios practiced the claim. But the document did not show seven claimed multiplications. It showed three distinct multiplications, and those operations involved things like modulus and complex conjugate terms. That is not the same thing as the seven multiplications recited in the claim. The panel emphasized that Dr. Wells never explained how the three operations in the document mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications, and Finesse’s later attempt to build a more elaborate mapping on appeal could not save the verdict because that theory was never presented to the jury.

That is the heart of the decision. The court was not saying that all mathematical transformations must be identical in literal form to infringe, nor was it announcing a categorical rule that modulus or conjugation can never fit within a claim. It was saying something more basic and more demanding: the plaintiff’s trial proof did not establish the specific sequence of operations the claims required. In a highly technical case, that distinction is fatal.

## The court’s insistence on evidentiary precision

The opinion is also notable for how little patience it shows for expert testimony that shifts under pressure. Finesse’s case depended heavily on Dr. Wells, but his testimony was, in the court’s words, confused and unclear. On appeal, Finesse tried to recast his statements as referring to different signals or to different parts of the Nokia diagram. The panel was not persuaded. It read the testimony as self-contradictory: the expert repeatedly mapped the claim to the wrong path, then tried to pivot when cross-examination exposed the mismatch.

That matters doctrinally because it shows the Federal Circuit is willing to treat internal inconsistency in expert proof as a noninfringement problem, not merely a credibility issue for the jury. The court relied on precedent holding that a verdict cannot stand where the patentee’s expert gives contradictory testimony on a limitation the patentee must prove. For litigants, that is a warning: if the infringement theory depends on reconstructing the claim from scattered snippets of testimony, the verdict may not survive JMOL.

## Claim scope was not the problem; proof was

One potentially important nuance is that the court did **not** accept AT&T and Nokia’s broader claim-scope argument that Finesse had clearly surrendered coverage over two-signal implementations during prosecution. The panel concluded there was no clear and unambiguous disclaimer. So the claim construction fight did not resolve the case against Finesse.

But that actually makes the decision more interesting. The Federal Circuit was willing to preserve some breadth in the claim language while still holding the patentee to a strict evidentiary burden at trial. In other words, a patent can remain broad enough in theory and still fail in practice because the accused product does not do what the claim, as construed, requires.

That separation between scope and proof is likely to matter in future DSP and telecom cases. Plaintiffs often rely on diagrams, models, and vendor documents to show that a receiver or baseband processor performs the claimed steps. This case suggests that such proof needs to be mapped with real rigor. It is not enough to show that the accused system performs a broadly similar cancellation routine or that it operates in the same conceptual neighborhood. The theory has to account for each claimed operation, including exactly how many times a multiplication is performed and what the operands are.

## The downstream effect

The broader trend here is familiar: as patent claims become more algorithmic, infringement analysis becomes less forgiving. That is especially true in signal-processing litigation, where the line between “mathematically related” and “claim-satisfying” can be very thin. Finesse Wireless reinforces that courts will not let experts collapse those categories by using technical shorthand or by retrofitting a theory after the fact.

The practical takeaway for patentees is straightforward. If your claim recites a particular computational recipe, your infringement case has to show that recipe, step by step, with the same kind of precision. For accused infringers, the opinion is a reminder that a mismatch between the claim language and the accused math can be decisive even after a jury verdict.

And because the Federal Circuit reversed noninfringement on both patents, the damages award fell with them. But the ’775 ruling is the one that will likely echo in future cases: in the world of DSP patents, **seven really does mean seven**.



## How a $166 Million Patent Verdict Vanished: Rule 50, Waiver, and the Federal Circuit’s Appetite for Technical JMOL

## Why this case matters

A $166 million patent verdict did not fall because of a damages glitch or a late-breaking claim-construction twist. It fell because the Federal Circuit concluded that the plaintiff’s infringement proof never got past the basics. In **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility**, the court reversed JMOL denials on both asserted patents and, with liability gone, vacated the entire damages award. For patent litigants, that is a sharp reminder that even a major jury win in a technically dense case can be fragile on appeal if the expert testimony does not cleanly map onto the claims.

The case also highlights a recurring procedural theme in patent appeals: Rule 50 preservation matters, but so does the quality of the evidentiary record. When the infringement story depends on diagrams, signal paths, and expert gloss, the Federal Circuit is willing to look past the jury’s verdict and ask whether a reasonable juror actually had enough to work with.

## The Federal Circuit’s core move

The opinion is strongest, and most revealing, on the court’s treatment of the plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. On the **’134 patent**, the disputed limitation required sampling received signals that include both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Wells tried to tie those claim terms to a Nokia technical document, saying the radio’s RF ADC sampled the relevant signals on the red path. But under cross-examination, that theory became unstable: the “modeled PIM path” he had repeatedly identified as the “interference generating signals” was generated downstream, after sampling, which made it a poor fit for the claim language. Wells then shifted to saying that x1 and x2 were the real signals, but he never clearly mapped those quantities onto the claim terms.

That mattered. The Federal Circuit did not treat this as a mere credibility dispute for the jury. Citing **Johns Hopkins v. Datascope**, it held that a party with the burden of proof cannot rely on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony and expect the verdict to survive JMOL. The court’s point is not that every inconsistency is fatal; it is that the plaintiff has to offer a coherent theory of how the accused device meets each claim limitation. Here, the testimony was too muddled to support a finding of infringement.

The **’775 patent** failed on a different but equally concrete ground. That claim required generating cancellation signals using three signals, S1, S2, and S3, and performing seven third-order multiplications. The district court had already construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique,” and the Federal Circuit accepted that there was no clear prosecution-history surrender of two-signal products. That should have helped Finesse. But it did not solve the larger problem: the Nokia document Wells relied on showed only three distinct multiplications, not the seven the claim requires, and the record did not show that any of them were repeated in the way Finesse needed. The court was blunt that there was no evidence the accused radios performed the full set of seven multiplications.

So the opinion’s real lesson is not about claim construction. It is about proof. Even when the claims are construed in a plaintiff-friendly way, infringement still has to be established limitation by limitation with a record that actually matches the theory being advanced.

## Rule 50, waiver, and why the district court’s posture mattered

The procedural backdrop is just as important. The district court had treated AT&T and Nokia’s “unique signals” argument on the ’775 patent as waived because it had not been preserved in the Rule 50(a) motion. The Federal Circuit did not need to resolve that waiver fight to reverse. Instead, it took the narrower route: even under the district court’s own construction, the evidence did not support a finding that the accused radios performed all seven claimed multiplications.

That is a telling appellate strategy. Waiver can be a serious obstacle in patent cases, especially where parties are trying to sharpen sufficiency arguments after the verdict. But the Federal Circuit often avoids getting trapped in preservation disputes if it can dispose of the case on a cleaner insufficiency ground. In practical terms, that means Rule 50 still matters enormously at trial, but a waiver ruling does not necessarily insulate a verdict if the substantive proof is weak enough.

There is a broader institutional point here too. Patent cases involving radio-frequency engineering, signal processing, or software often generate dense expert testimony that sounds precise but can unravel when pressed. The Federal Circuit is not formally hostile to juries, but it is plainly willing to intervene when the plaintiff’s case rests on a technical narrative that does not hold together. That is especially true where the verdict is large and the mapping from document to claim feels more inferential than demonstrative.

## What the decision may mean going forward

For patentees, **Finesse Wireless** is a warning about expert choreography. A clean infringement case in a technically complex patent should not depend on an expert oscillating between one mapping and another, or on appellate counsel trying to reconstruct the theory from scattered pages of testimony. If the accused functionality is not plainly the claimed functionality, a jury verdict may not survive.

For defendants, the case is a reminder to make the record as explicit as possible at trial and in Rule 50 motions. The court’s willingness to reverse on sufficiency grounds suggests that a focused evidentiary attack can be more effective than a sprawling claim-construction war. Where the expert testimony is internally inconsistent, that can be enough.

And for the Eastern District of Texas more generally, the opinion fits a familiar pattern: big patent verdicts grounded in technical testimony remain vulnerable when the Federal Circuit looks closely at whether the record actually proves the claim limitations. That does not mean the venue is uniquely suspect. It does mean that in high-stakes wireless and software cases, appellate review is likely to be exacting, especially when the infringement story depends on diagrams, labels, and expert interpretation rather than straightforward device behavior.

In the end, **Finesse Wireless** is less a statement about patents than about proof. A $166 million verdict can vanish quickly when the appellate court concludes that the jury was asked to bridge gaps the evidence never really closed.
