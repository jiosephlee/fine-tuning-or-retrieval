Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### When can an appellate court treat expert testimony as too contradictory to support a jury verdict?
Question:
The court says Finesse's expert gave 'confusing and unclear' testimony and relies on Johns Hopkins to hold that no reasonable jury could find infringement based on self-contradictory expert testimony. I'm struggling with that. Usually I thought credibility and weighing conflicting testimony are for the jury. So what makes this different? Is the rule that a jury can choose between two competing experts, but cannot rely on one expert whose own testimony defeats his infringement theory? How inconsistent does the testimony have to be before it stops being substantial evidence?
Answer:
The short answer is: **an appellate court is not reweighing credibility when it does this. It is deciding whether the testimony, taken as a whole, is still “substantial evidence” at all.**

Normally, yes — **conflicts between witnesses are for the jury**. If one expert says X and the other says not-X, the jury can choose. But that rule assumes each expert’s testimony is still a **coherent factual basis** for the verdict. It does **not** require an appellate court to accept a verdict that depends on testimony that is internally self-defeating on an essential element.

That is what the Federal Circuit is doing in *Finesse Wireless*.

## What the court thought was different here

For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that the claimed “interference generating signals” were the **modeled PIM path**. But the accused radio’s RF ADC samples **before** that modeled PIM path is generated. So if his mapping is right, then the claim limitation cannot be met because the “interference generating signals” would not exist at the time of sampling.

When Finesse tried to save the verdict, it argued Dr. Wells really meant **x1 and x2**, not the modeled PIM path. But the court found that Dr. Wells never said that clearly; instead, he kept saying the modeled PIM path was the relevant claim element. The court called this “confusing and unclear” and concluded:

> “This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.”

That is the key point: **the jury was not being asked to choose between two competing, intelligible expert opinions. It was being asked to rely on an expert theory that collapsed under its own contradictions.**

## The doctrine

The Federal Circuit’s citation to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* is doing the work. There, the court held that when the burdened party’s expert gives **self-contradictory testimony** on a claim element, a reasonable jury cannot treat that testimony as substantial evidence supporting infringement.

So the rule is not:

- “Any inconsistency destroys the testimony.”

It is closer to:

- **If the expert’s testimony on a required element is materially inconsistent, unexplained, and the inconsistency goes to the heart of the theory of infringement, the testimony may fail as substantial evidence.**

## How inconsistent is enough?

The opinion suggests the inconsistency has to be **material and unresolved**:

1. **It must concern an essential claim element.**  
   Here, whether the accused radios sample the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.”

2. **The testimony must not be reasonably reconcilable.**  
   The court said Dr. Wells never clearly tied x1 and x2 to the claim terms, even though Finesse tried to infer that after the fact.

3. **The contradiction must undermine the only evidence supporting the verdict.**  
   If the expert is the key proof and his testimony defeats the theory, the verdict cannot stand.

## So can a jury choose between two experts?

Yes. A jury can choose between **competing expert interpretations**.

But a jury cannot base a verdict on an expert opinion that, as presented at trial, is **internally self-contradictory on the dispositive point** and never repaired by a clear explanation. In that situation, the issue is no longer “who is more credible?” It is “is there actually evidence from which a reasonable jury could find the element satisfied?”

That is why the Federal Circuit reversed JMOL here.

## Bottom line

Your instinct is right that credibility is usually for the jury. But this case falls on the other side of the line because the court viewed Dr. Wells’ testimony not as a mere credibility contest, but as **confused testimony that never supplied a coherent factual basis for infringement**.

So, in this opinion, the answer is:

- **jury question:** conflicting but coherent expert opinions;
- **JMOL question:** a verdict resting on an expert’s self-contradictory, element-defeating testimony.
