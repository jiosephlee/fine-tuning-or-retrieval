Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why didn't changing the claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' create prosecution disclaimer?
Question:
For the '775 patent, I expected the amendment history to be fatal. The original claims apparently said 'two or three signals,' the examiner rejected them over Filipovic, and the applicant amended the claims to require 'three signals S1, S2 and S3.' My instinct is that this should surrender systems using only two unique input signals. But the Federal Circuit says there was no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal because the applicant also distinguished Filipovic on the ground that it addressed active rather than passive IMPs. How can an applicant make a narrowing amendment like that and still avoid disclaimer? Is the court distinguishing between amendment-based estoppel/disclaimer and mere evidence of what the claims require?
Answer:
Because **prosecution disclaimer is narrow**. A claim amendment does **not** automatically surrender every embodiment that was covered by the earlier, broader language. The Federal Circuit requires a **“clear and unambiguous” disavowal** of scope.

That is what the court says happened **not** to occur here.

### What the prosecution history showed
Finesse did amend the claim from **“two or three signals”** to **“three signals S1, S2 and S3.”** But the prosecution record did **not** stop there. Finesse also argued that the Filipovic reference was different because it addressed **active** IMPs, while the claimed invention concerns **passive** IMPs. So the amendment was not an unmistakable statement like:

> “We are giving up all two-signal systems.”

Instead, the record left open **why** the claim was changed and **what exactly** was being surrendered.

### Why that matters
Under Federal Circuit law, an amendment can narrow a claim, but **disclaimer only happens if the patentee clearly surrenders the disputed scope**. If the history is mixed or ambiguous, the court will not infer a waiver.

That is why the court said the amendment **“does not rise to the level of clear and unambiguous disavowal”** and read it, at most, as requiring **three separately identifiable signals**, **not** three **unique** input signals. On that reading, a device using **two unique signals plus a copy** is not automatically excluded.

### So yes — the court is distinguishing two things
- **Claim language after amendment**: the claim now says “three signals S1, S2 and S3.”
- **Prosecution disclaimer**: whether the applicant clearly gave up two-signal implementations.

The first can narrow the claim, but the second requires a much clearer record. The court found that clarity missing here.

In short: **the amendment narrowed the claim, but the prosecution history did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim all two-unique-signal embodiments.**
