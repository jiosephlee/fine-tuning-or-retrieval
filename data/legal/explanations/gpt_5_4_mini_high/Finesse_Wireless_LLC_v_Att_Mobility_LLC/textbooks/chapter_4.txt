Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # The ’775 Patent: Claim Scope, Prosecution History, and the Failure to Prove Seven Claimed Multiplications

## What the ’775 Patent Covers

The ’775 patent concerns passive intermodulation, or PIM, in radio systems. The basic idea is that signals traveling through a transmitter and receiver can create unwanted interference when they interact with passive components, such as connectors or other physical obstacles. The patent claims a method for canceling those passive intermodulation products by generating cancellation signals in a receiver that is co-located with a transmitter.

That general purpose matters, but the infringement dispute turned on the details of claim 1. The Federal Circuit was not deciding whether Nokia’s radios could cancel PIM in some general sense. It was deciding whether Finesse proved that the accused radios practiced the specific claim limitations the patent actually recites.

## Representative Claim 1 and the Seven-Multiplication Limitation

Claim 1 is the key claim. It requires, among other things, generating intermodulation product cancellation signals continuously and near real time, and it requires doing so “using copies of transmitter signals.” The claim’s most important technical limitation for purposes of this appeal is the one in subsection [d], which says that generating an n-th order cancellation signal includes, “given three signals S1, S2 and S3,” digitally multiplying and filtering seven listed triple products.

Those seven listed products are not incidental. They are the specific mathematical operations the patentee chose to claim. So to prove infringement, Finesse needed evidence that the accused radios performed those claimed multiplications, not just something loosely similar.

## The District Court’s Construction of “Three Signals S1, S2 and S3”

At summary judgment, the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, but not necessarily “unique” signals. In other words, the court accepted that the claim did not require three entirely different input signals in the strictest sense. The court relied on the intrinsic record and inventor testimony suggesting that the signals could be the same or different, so long as they were separately identifiable.

That construction left Finesse some room. Under the district court’s view, a system might still satisfy the claim even if it used only two unique inputs plus a copy of one of them. But even with that broader reading, Finesse still had to prove the accused system actually performed the claim’s seven multiplications.

## The Waiver Problem and the Prosecution-History Argument

The defendants argued in the district court that Finesse could not now read the claim to cover systems with only two unique signals, because Finesse had amended the claims during prosecution to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” instead of “two or three signals.” The district court rejected that line of attack in part as waived, because Appellants had not preserved the precise “unique signals” argument in their Rule 50(a) motion.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit did not treat the amendment as a clear surrender of two-signal systems. The court looked at the prosecution history and concluded there was no “clear and unambiguous” disavowal. The examiner had rejected broader claims that allowed “two or three signals” over Filipovic, but Finesse responded by distinguishing Filipovic on the ground that Filipovic addressed active intermodulation, whereas the patent at issue dealt with passive intermodulation. The amendment to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” therefore did not amount to an unmistakable disclaimer of any system involving only two unique inputs. At most, the court read the amendment as requiring three separately identifiable signals, not a fixed number of unique signals.

## Dr. Wells’s Trial Theory and the Nokia Document

Even though the Federal Circuit rejected the defendants’ narrowing claim-scope argument, Finesse still had to connect the accused radios to the seven claimed multiplications. Its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to do that. His testimony focused on two signals, x1 and x2, and he tried to equate the document’s operations with the claim’s required multiplication steps.

The problem was that Dr. Wells’s testimony was confused and inconsistent. At times, he mapped the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. At other times, after being pressed on cross-examination, he suggested that x1 and x2 were the actual inputs on the red path. But he never gave a clear trial-level explanation tying each of the seven claim products to the accused operations.

## Why the Proof Failed on the Seven Multiplications

The Federal Circuit’s key point was that the Nokia document did not show the seven claimed multiplications. Instead, the document showed only three distinct multiplications: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', and x1·|x2|·|x2|. Those expressions involved modulus and complex-conjugate operations, not the exact set of seven triple products recited in the claim.

That mattered because Finesse’s burden was not just to show “some” third-order multiplication. It had to show the seven specific multiplications listed in claim 1[d]. The record did not supply a mapping from the three disclosed operations to all seven claimed products, and Dr. Wells did not testify how the three accused operations corresponded to each claimed multiplication. Nor did the evidence show that any of the three multiplications were repeated in a way that would add up to the seven claimed operations.

Finesse tried to patch the gap on appeal by offering a more elaborate explanation of how the Nokia document should be read, but appellate argument is not evidence. The jury never heard a clear, supported mapping, and the record could not support one after the fact. The court also noted the logical difficulty in Finesse’s theory: it would require treating S3 as if it could simultaneously correspond to x2, x2', and |x2|, which the trial evidence did not explain.

## The Federal Circuit’s Holding on the ’775 Patent

The Federal Circuit therefore split the defendants’ claim-scope argument from the infringement proof. It rejected the idea that Finesse had clearly disclaimed systems using only two unique inputs, but it still held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement because the evidence did not establish the seven claimed multiplications. On that basis, the court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted ’775 claims.

That reversal mattered procedurally as well as substantively. Because the court reversed noninfringement on the merits, the patent damages award could not stand. The ’775 patent issue thus illustrates an important appellate lesson: winning a claim-construction argument does not help the patentee if the proof at trial still fails to match the exact claim language.
