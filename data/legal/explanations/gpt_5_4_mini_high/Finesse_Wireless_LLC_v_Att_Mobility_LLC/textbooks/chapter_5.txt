Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 5: # Disposition, Damages, and the Case’s Litigation Lessons

## Why the damages award did not survive the liability reversal

The court’s treatment of damages follows directly from its holding on infringement. Finesse won a jury verdict of infringement and a large lump-sum damages award, but the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to every asserted claim. Once the liability verdict fell, there was no basis left for a damages award tied to that verdict. The court therefore vacated the damages award rather than separately affirming, modifying, or recalculating it.

That is an important remedial point in patent cases. Damages are not an independent entitlement floating free of infringement liability. If the patentee does not prove infringement, the damages award based on that finding cannot stand. So even though the district court had also denied Appellants’ separate post-trial challenges to damages and their request for a new trial, the appellate court did not need to decide whether the damages evidence was independently defective.

## The standard of review for a new trial on damages

The opinion briefly states the governing review framework. On a motion for a new trial, the Federal Circuit applies regional circuit law—in this case, Fifth Circuit law. Under that law, denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. That is a deferential standard: the appellate court does not reweigh the evidence as a first-instance matter, but asks whether the trial judge made a legally mistaken or unreasonable decision in refusing a new trial.

Here, however, the court never had to apply that standard to the damages merits because the liability verdict itself was reversed. The damages challenge became unnecessary to resolve in light of the noninfringement holding. The court’s reasoning shows a common appellate sequence: if the predicate liability judgment is undone, the court may dispose of the damages award without reaching whether the damages trial was independently flawed.

## Why the court vacated damages instead of reaching the damages arguments

The court’s decision to vacate the damages award is doctrinally straightforward. The jury’s damages figure rested on the premise that AT&T and Nokia infringed valid claims. Once the Federal Circuit concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement on the record presented, the damages award lost its legal foundation. Vacatur is the cleanest disposition because it removes an award that cannot survive without a liability finding.

This also explains the court’s terse treatment of damages. The opinion does not analyze methodology, apportionment, or the amount of the award. Those issues became irrelevant once the court determined that Finesse had not carried its burden on infringement for either patent. In that posture, the court’s job was not to recalibrate the damages case, but to eliminate an award that depended on an overturned verdict.

## The formal disposition

The case ends with a simple but consequential judgment: the Federal Circuit “REVERSED AND VACATED.” The reversal was directed at the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement. The vacatur applied to the damages award. Costs were taxed to the defendants-appellants, which is a formal appellate allocation of litigation costs and does not change the merits outcome.

For the parties, the practical result is that Finesse lost its infringement judgment and the associated damages recovery. For the defendants, the ruling erased the jury’s liability and monetary award. The disposition thus reflects the court’s view that the record did not support the verdicts on which the judgment rested.

## Claim scope versus proof at trial

One of the opinion’s central lessons is that claim construction and proof of infringement are separate steps. Finesse had a potentially favorable construction fight on the ’775 patent: the district court rejected the argument that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” had to be unique signals, and the Federal Circuit agreed that Finesse had not clearly disavowed two-signal embodiments. But that construction win did not end the case. Finesse still had to prove, with trial evidence, that the accused radios satisfied each express limitation.

The Federal Circuit’s analysis repeatedly shows this distinction. On the ’134 patent, the problem was not only how the claim should be read, but whether the evidence really showed the accused system sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” On the ’775 patent, the court accepted that the claim did not require unique signals, yet still found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven multiplications recited by the claim. A patentee can win on claim scope and still lose on infringement if the proof does not match the claim language.

## The need for precise limitation-by-limitation mapping

The opinion is especially instructive on how technical documents must be mapped to claim terms. The court was not persuaded by broad conclusions that the accused Nokia radios “basically” did what the claims require. It wanted a clear mapping from each claim limitation to an identified feature in the accused system. When the mapping was unclear, internally inconsistent, or incomplete, the verdict could not stand.

That problem appeared in both patents. For the ’134 patent, Dr. Wells’ testimony shifted between the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path on one hand and x1 and x2 on the other, without a clean explanation of how those elements corresponded to the claim terms. For the ’775 patent, the evidence showed only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document, while the claim required seven specified multiplications. The court found no record evidence explaining how those three operations covered all seven claim requirements. In a complex case, the jury still needs a coherent, limitation-by-limitation roadmap.

## Why post hoc explanations could not save the verdict

A major theme in the opinion is that appellate counsel’s later explanation cannot cure what the expert actually told the jury. Finesse tried on appeal to reconstruct Dr. Wells’ testimony into a more favorable and internally consistent theory. But the court emphasized that the jury did not hear that clean theory from the witness in the first place. The record, as presented at trial, remained confusing and contradictory.

That matters because JMOL review asks whether the evidence presented to the jury was sufficient to support the verdict. It is not enough that a party can later assemble a plausible interpretation from scattered statements in the record, especially when those statements were not clearly connected at trial. The court’s response to Finesse’s post hoc explanation is a reminder that appellate reconstruction is not a substitute for trial proof.

## The warning on contradictory expert testimony

The opinion uses one of the strongest possible signals that expert testimony was not enough: it cites prior Federal Circuit precedent holding that a verdict cannot rest on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony. That is the real danger the case illustrates. In a technically dense patent trial, an expert may sound persuasive on direct examination but then undermine the infringement theory on cross-examination or in later questioning. If the testimony never settles into a stable, claim-specific explanation, the patentee risks losing even after a favorable jury verdict.

The Federal Circuit’s treatment of both patents shows that contradiction is not a harmless flaw when the patentee bears the burden of proof. If the expert’s testimony leaves the court unable to identify a supported finding that each claim limitation is met, JMOL of noninfringement may be warranted. The case therefore serves as a warning to patent plaintiffs: technical shorthand and diagram-based explanations must still map cleanly onto the claim language.

## Significance for future JMOL motions in technical patent cases

The broader litigation lesson is that JMOL remains a meaningful tool in patent cases built on schematics, diagrams, and expert interpretation. Even when a jury returns a plaintiff’s verdict, the court will test whether the record truly contains substantial evidence for each limitation. The presence of a technical document or a highly qualified expert is not enough if the testimony is unclear, inconsistent, or fails to explain the mapping in a way the jury could reasonably rely on.

For future cases, this opinion underscores the importance of preparing expert testimony that is both technically accurate and legally precise. The expert must identify which accused components correspond to which claim terms and must do so consistently. If the theory depends on a later appellate explanation, or if the trial testimony slides between multiple possible mappings, the verdict may be vulnerable on JMOL. That is the practical significance of this case: in patent litigation, winning the claim-construction fight does not end the burden of proving infringement with clear, trial-ready evidence.
