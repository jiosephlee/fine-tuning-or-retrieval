Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Chapter 1: The Dispute in Context — Financed Auto Policies, Cancellation, and the Road to Appeal

## Who the parties are: Williams, Wright, Jenkins, GoAuto, and APAC

This case began with a group of insureds who did not pay GoAuto’s full auto insurance premium up front. Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright were former GoAuto insureds. Nicholas Jenkins was also involved in the lawsuit because he had been in an accident with a different GoAuto insured whose policy was financed through the same arrangement. GoAuto Insurance Company was the insurer, and American Premium Assistance Company, LLC, or APAC, was the premium finance company that handled the financing and cancellation process.

The basic relationship matters because the dispute was not about an ordinary cancellation by the insurer itself. Instead, the insureds used premium financing, which meant APAC fronted the premium payment to GoAuto and then collected installment payments from the insureds. That financing structure placed APAC at the center of the cancellation procedure when payments were missed.

## How premium financing worked: APAC paid the full premium and insureds repaid in installments

Under the financing arrangement described in the opinion, APAC paid GoAuto the insureds’ full premium at the outset. In return, the insureds agreed to repay APAC in monthly installments. This kind of arrangement is common when a policyholder cannot or does not want to pay the entire premium at once.

The arrangement also gave APAC a contractual role if the insured defaulted. If the insured failed to make the required installment payments, APAC could move to cancel the policy. In other words, the finance company—not just the insurer—had the practical ability to end coverage when the financing agreement was not honored.

## The power-of-attorney clauses allowing APAC to cancel for uncured nonpayment

Each financing agreement gave APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the policy if the insured failed to pay. That clause is important because it gave APAC authority to act on behalf of the insured in the cancellation process. The court’s later statutory analysis focused on whether APAC and GoAuto followed the Louisiana cancellation procedures that apply when a premium finance company exercises that authority after a default.

The opinion makes clear that the cancellations here were triggered by nonpayment. APAC sent a ten-day notice warning that the policy would be cancelled if the payment was not made, and when the default was not cured, the cancellation process went forward.

## Why cancellation mattered in this case, including Jenkins’s accident-related stake

Cancellation mattered because it determined whether insurance coverage remained in force. For Williams and Wright, the issue was whether their policies had been effectively cancelled at all. For Jenkins, the issue was tied to an accident involving a different GoAuto insured. If that insured’s policy had not been properly cancelled, coverage could still have existed when the accident occurred.

That is why the plaintiffs framed the case as more than a mere paperwork dispute. They claimed GoAuto’s cancellation procedures were legally ineffective, and they tied that alleged defect to additional claims sounding in misrepresentation and breach of good faith and fair dealing.

## The plaintiffs' theories: ineffective cancellation, misrepresentation, and breach of good faith and fair dealing

The plaintiffs’ core theory was that GoAuto never properly cancelled the policies under Louisiana law. If the cancellation was ineffective, then the policies would have remained in force despite the nonpayment. Based on that premise, they also asserted claims for misrepresentation and for breach of good faith and fair dealing.

Those additional claims depended on the cancellation issue. The opinion does not treat them as separate factual disputes requiring independent development at the summary-judgment stage. Instead, the parties focused first on whether the cancellation process complied with the Louisiana statute governing premium-financed policies. If it did, the rest of the plaintiffs’ theories failed with it.

## Removal from Louisiana state court to the Middle District of Louisiana

Williams and Jenkins filed the lawsuit as a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC. Wright later joined as an additional plaintiff when the complaint was amended. GoAuto then removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana.

Removal changed the forum but not the central issue. The federal court inherited the same dispute: whether the financed policies had been cancelled in compliance with Louisiana law.

## The parties' cross-motions for summary judgment limited to statutory compliance

The litigation narrowed to a legal question on summary judgment. The parties filed motions addressing only whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedure satisfied the Louisiana statute governing cancellation of financed insurance policies. That means the district court was not asked to decide every factual issue in the case, but instead to resolve whether the cancellation method used here was legally sufficient.

This posture matters because it explains why the Fifth Circuit’s review was so focused. The appellate court was not reviewing a full trial record. It was reviewing the district court’s legal conclusion that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with the statute.

## The district court's ruling for GoAuto and dismissal of all claims with prejudice

The district court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment. It held that GoAuto’s procedures complied with Louisiana law. Because the cancellation was valid, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. A dismissal with prejudice means the claims are finally resolved and cannot be refiled in the same court.

That ruling left the plaintiffs with an appeal rather than a remand for further proceedings.

## The three appellate arguments: delivery of notice, GoAuto's receipt of cancellation requests, and denial of plaintiffs' partial summary judgment motion

On appeal, the plaintiffs challenged three points. First, they argued the district court wrongly concluded that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation had been satisfied. Second, they argued GoAuto never properly “received” the cancellation requests, so the cancellations were ineffective. Third, they argued the district court erred by denying their own motion for partial summary judgment.

Those arguments show the shape of the appeal. The plaintiffs were not merely asking the Fifth Circuit to second-guess the district court’s view of the facts; they were asking the court to interpret the Louisiana cancellation statute and decide whether APAC’s and GoAuto’s procedures fit within it.

# Chapter 2: The Governing Louisiana Statute — How a Premium Finance Company Cancels an Insurance Policy

## The role of Louisiana Revised Statutes section 9:3550 in financed-policy cancellations

This appeal turns on a Louisiana premium-financing statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, which creates a special method for canceling an insurance policy when the insured financed the premium and then failed to make the required installment payments. The basic arrangement is common: a premium finance company advances the full premium to the insurer, and the insured repays the finance company in monthly installments. If the insured defaults, the finance company—not the insured—may initiate cancellation under the statute.

That statutory mechanism matters because cancellation is not automatic the moment a payment is missed. Instead, Louisiana law prescribes a set of steps that must be followed precisely. In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the Fifth Circuit had to decide whether GoAuto and APAC followed those steps closely enough to make the cancellations effective.

## The finance company’s authority to act through a valid power of attorney

The statute allows the finance company to cancel the policy only if the premium finance agreement gives it a valid power of attorney to do so. That authorization is critical. Without it, the finance company has no statutory basis to act on the insured’s behalf in requesting cancellation.

In practical terms, the power of attorney is what lets the finance company stand in the shoes of the insured for the limited purpose of canceling the policy after a default. The Fifth Circuit treated this as one of the threshold statutory requirements: the cancellation request must certify that the agreement contains a valid power of attorney, because the insurer is entitled to rely on that representation before it acts.

## The insured’s default and the prerequisite notice-and-cure period

The statute does not permit immediate cancellation after a missed payment. The finance company must first give the insured notice of the default and an opportunity to cure it within the time the statute and the financing agreement provide. In the opinion, APAC used a ten-day notice process: when the insured failed to pay, APAC sent a notice warning that the policy would be canceled if payment was not received within ten days.

This notice-and-cure feature is central to the Louisiana scheme. It ensures that cancellation is not a surprise and that the insured has a minimum period in which to fix the default. The Fifth Circuit later emphasized that the statute is designed not merely to allow cancellation, but to do so in a way that preserves fairness and certainty.

## Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a): the notice of cancellation and the accompanying statement “certifying” four statutory facts

The key statutory provision in the case is § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). It requires the premium finance company, after an uncured default, to send the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement “certifying” four listed facts. The finance company may send that package by mail, private carrier, or electronic mail. The statute is specific about what must be certified, even though it is less specific about the form the certification must take.

The statute requires certification of four points: first, that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; second, that the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; third, that a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured after default and specifying when it was sent; and fourth, that copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss, including relevant governmental agencies, lienholders, or other interested third parties. The insurer’s cancellation authority depends on receiving this complete statutory package.

## What the certification must cover: valid power of attorney, uncured default, prior notice to the insured, and notice to other interested persons

The certification requirement is substantive, not ceremonial. It gives the insurer a basis to conclude that the finance company has complied with the statutory prerequisites before the insurer cancels the policy. The first item—valid power of attorney—confirms authority. The second—uncured default—confirms that cancellation is not premature. The third—notice to the insured—confirms that the insured was given the chance to avoid cancellation by curing the default. The fourth—notice to other interested persons—protects others who may have a stake in coverage.

In the opinion, the court treated these certifications as the statutory safeguards that make the cancellation process reliable. The insurer is not expected to investigate each default from scratch; it may rely on the finance company’s certified statement if the statutory conditions are met. That is why the contents of the request matter so much in this case.

## Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i): the insurer’s ability to cancel upon “receipt” of the notice and statement

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) tells the insurer when it may act. Once the insurer has “receipt” of the notice of cancellation and the finance company’s statement, it may treat cancellation as having been requested and may proceed to cancel the policy. The word “receipt” becomes important because it identifies the point at which the insurer is authorized to move from passive recipient to active canceler.

The opinion emphasizes that the statute uses “receipt,” not merely “sending.” That choice suggests that the finance company’s request must actually reach the insurer in some meaningful way before cancellation occurs. The Fifth Circuit later analyzed that term in a modern context, where receipt may occur through computer systems rather than only through human hands.

## Louisiana cases requiring strict adherence to statutory cancellation procedures

Louisiana courts have repeatedly held that the cancellation process under § 9:3550 must be followed strictly. The Fifth Circuit quoted this principle from Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions such as Delatte, Eaglin, and Britten. Strict adherence means more than substantial compliance; the finance company and insurer must follow the statute’s required steps exactly.

The reason for this insistence is simple: cancellation cuts off insurance coverage, often with significant consequences for the insured and for third parties. Because the statute creates a special cancellation method that overrides the ordinary expectation of continuous coverage, Louisiana courts require the statutory conditions to be met without defect. If a required step is missing or flawed, the attempted cancellation is ineffective.

## The consequences of defect: ineffective cancellation

A key lesson from the Louisiana cases is that the cancellation process is all-or-nothing. If the finance company does not comply with the statute, the policy is not validly canceled. The defect does not merely create a technical irregularity; it defeats cancellation altogether.

That consequence explains why the litigation in Williams focused so heavily on the mechanics of APAC’s cancellation process. The plaintiffs argued that if APAC’s notice or GoAuto’s receipt procedure failed to satisfy the statute, then the cancellation never legally took effect. If that were true, claims based on cancellation would fail, because the policies would still be in force.

## The two policy functions identified by Louisiana courts: fixing the exact time of cancellation and preserving a minimum cure period

Louisiana courts have also explained why the statute is written so carefully. In Britten, the court identified two major functions served by strict compliance. First, the statute gives a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation. Second, it guarantees a minimum period during which the insured may cure the default before coverage is lost.

Those two functions help explain the structure of § 9:3550. The notice provisions tell everyone when the process begins, the certification tells the insurer that the prerequisites were satisfied, and the receipt requirement tells the insurer when it may act. Together, these provisions create an orderly cancellation process that is supposed to be both predictable and fair. In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the Fifth Circuit’s task was to decide whether the modern electronic process used by APAC and GoAuto fit within that statutory design.

# Chapter 3: GoAuto and APAC's Cancellation Process — The 'Certifying' Question and Whether a Signature Is Required

## The APAC-GoAuto Cancellation Workflow

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, it helps to follow the cancellation process from beginning to end. The policies at issue were not ordinary directly billed policies. Instead, each insured financed the premium through APAC, which paid GoAuto the full premium up front and then collected monthly installments from the insured. The financing agreement also gave APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the policy if the insured defaulted on payment.

When an insured missed a payment, APAC first sent the insured a ten-day notice of cancellation. That notice told the insured that the policy would be cancelled if the overdue payment was not made within ten days. If the insured still did not pay by the end of that period, APAC’s computer system automatically generated and sent GoAuto an email with the cancellation materials attached. GoAuto’s system then cancelled the policy automatically upon receiving that email. Only later, after the cancellation had already occurred in GoAuto’s system, did an APAC employee place a signature stamp on the printed documents. The plaintiffs focused heavily on that sequence, arguing that the cancellation could not be effective unless APAC’s certification had been signed before GoAuto acted.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Cancellation

The opinion turns on Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3), which governs cancellation of financed insurance policies. The statute allows a premium finance company, after an uncured default, to request cancellation by sending the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying four things: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in default and the default has not been cured, that the insured was sent notice of cancellation, and that copies of the cancellation notice were sent to others with a protected interest.

The court emphasizes that Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to these procedures. That phrase matters because it means the finance company must comply with the statute exactly. But strict adherence does not authorize a court to invent new requirements that the legislature did not write into the statute. The purpose of the statutory scheme is to create a clear cancellation date and give the insured a minimum period to cure the default. Those purposes guide the court’s reading of the text.

## The Plaintiffs' Signature Argument

The plaintiffs’ first major argument was simple: APAC’s notice was ineffective because no signature existed on the certification until after GoAuto had already cancelled the policy. In their view, the statute’s requirement that the finance company “certif[y]” the listed facts necessarily meant that a signed certification had to exist before cancellation could occur. If the certification was unsigned at the moment GoAuto acted, then, according to the plaintiffs, GoAuto had not received a valid cancellation request under Louisiana law.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that argument because the statute itself never says anything about a signature. The relevant statutory language requires a certification, but not a signature by a particular person. That silence is important: if the legislature intended a signature requirement, it could have said so expressly.

## Why the Court Rejected a Signature Requirement

Because Louisiana appellate courts had not directly addressed whether a signature is required, the Fifth Circuit looked to the available Louisiana cases for guidance. The cases did not provide a clear answer, but they suggested that the key statutory requirement is a certification of the listed facts, not a signed form in some formalistic sense. In KMJ Services, the Louisiana court described the statutory requirement as a “statement certifying compliance,” which supported the view that the substance of the certification matters more than the presence of a signature. Hodges likewise spoke in terms of receipt of “such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company,” again focusing on the notice and statement, not on a signature as an independent statutory element.

The plaintiffs relied most heavily on Benitez v. Elsayed, where the Louisiana Fifth Circuit noted that the notice at issue was not signed and did not identify any particular employee. But the federal Fifth Circuit treated that discussion as non-dispositive. Benitez did not hold that the absence of a signature alone invalidates cancellation. Rather, it mentioned the missing signature as one of several shortcomings in that particular notice. The Fifth Circuit therefore read Benitez as showing that the lack of a signature can matter in context, but not as establishing a standalone signature rule.

That distinction allowed the court to make its central interpretive move. The statute says that the insurance premium finance company cancels the policy by “certifying” certain facts. It does not say that cancellation depends on the signature of a named employee. In the court’s view, a company can certify information without identifying a particular human signatory, especially where the notice itself contains the required certifications. The court was unwilling to add a signature requirement to a statute that did not include one.

## The Meaning of Strict Adherence

The court’s treatment of “strict adherence” is a useful lesson in statutory interpretation. Strict adherence means that a party must follow the statutory steps exactly, but it does not allow a court to rewrite the statute by adding steps the legislature omitted. Here, the plaintiffs tried to turn the word “certifying” into a requirement that the certification be signed before cancellation. The court declined to do that. In its view, the statutory command was satisfied so long as APAC, as the finance company, sent a notice containing the required certifications.

That approach fits the court’s broader view of the statute as a practical, modern cancellation scheme. The court noted that the statute operates in an era where companies use computer systems to transmit and process notices. In that setting, the court saw no reason to treat the absence of a pre-cancellation handwritten or stamped signature as fatal when the notice already contained the required company certification.

## The Holding on the Certification Issue

The Fifth Circuit’s holding on this point was clear: Louisiana law does not require the premium finance company’s cancellation notice to be signed before the insurer cancels the policy. Because APAC’s notice contained the required certifications, the absence of a pre-cancellation signature did not make the cancellation ineffective. This ruling disposed of the plaintiffs’ first major statutory challenge and set the stage for the court’s separate discussion of whether GoAuto properly received the cancellation request.

# Chapter 4: ‘Receipt’ in the Digital Age — Whether GoAuto Properly Received the Cancellation Request

## Why the Word “Receipt” Mattered

The plaintiffs’ second major attack on GoAuto’s cancellation procedure focused on one word in the Louisiana premium-finance statute: “receipt.” Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), once the premium finance company has sent the insurer the notice of cancellation and the required statement, the insurer may treat cancellation as requested only “[u]pon receipt” of that notice and statement. The plaintiffs’ theory was simple: if GoAuto never truly “received” APAC’s cancellation request before the policy was cancelled, then the cancellation was ineffective under Louisiana law. That mattered because an ineffective cancellation would mean the policy remained in force, which in turn would support the plaintiffs’ misrepresentation and good-faith claims.

The Fifth Circuit therefore treated “receipt” as a statutory prerequisite to cancellation, not a mere formality. The question was not whether APAC intended to cancel the policies, but whether GoAuto had the kind of receipt the statute requires before it could act on APAC’s request.

## The Plaintiffs’ Challenges to GoAuto’s Pre- and Post-2017 Practices

The plaintiffs attacked GoAuto’s practices both before and after 2017. Before 2017, the printed cancellation documents were taken to a filing cabinet that was shared by GoAuto and APAC, but the cabinet was located in a shared room belonging to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. The plaintiffs argued that this arrangement did not amount to GoAuto’s receipt of the cancellation request because the documents were not shown to have come into GoAuto’s possession in the way the statute demands.

After 2017, the process changed. An APAC employee would physically carry the signed cancellation documents to a GoAuto employee, who would stamp them “received” and place them in a shared filing cabinet. The plaintiffs still said this was not enough, because the person delivering the documents was an APAC employee and because the cancellation had already been triggered by GoAuto’s computer system earlier in the day. In other words, they argued that there was no meaningful pre-cancellation receipt by GoAuto.

## The Court’s Ordinary-Meaning Approach

The Fifth Circuit began with the statute’s text, which does not define “receipt.” Nor, the court noted, has the Louisiana Supreme Court supplied a controlling definition in this setting. To fill that gap, the panel turned to ordinary legal dictionaries. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.”

Using those ordinary meanings, the court adopted a practical standard: GoAuto had “receipt” if it took possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. That mattered because the statute requires adherence to the procedure, but strict adherence does not authorize courts to add new requirements that the statute itself does not contain. The panel refused to read “receipt” as requiring a particular human ritual or a signed paper form in the hands of a GoAuto employee before cancellation could occur.

## Why Electronic Intake Counted as Receipt

The most important part of the court’s reasoning was its treatment of the electronic communication between APAC’s system and GoAuto’s system. The record showed that APAC’s computer system automatically sent GoAuto an e-mail with the cancellation notice and request. A GoAuto deponent explained that the APAC process made a call to a GoAuto web service, which functioned like a “listener”: it waited to be called, and when it was called, it performed the cancellation activity if it understood the request. No person was involved at that point. The GoAuto computer system cancelled the policy immediately, and only later in the morning did an APAC employee stamp the printed documents.

That sequence led the court to its core holding on this issue. Because the statute asks whether the insurer had “receipt” of the notice and statement before cancellation, and because modern businesses often receive and process notices through automated systems, the court held that GoAuto’s computer system’s intake of the e-mail was enough. In the panel’s view, a modern statute should be read in light of modern technology: a computer system can receive, possess, or hold a notice just as a person can. So long as GoAuto’s system took custody of the cancellation request and acted on it, the statutory requirement was satisfied.

This was the key move in the opinion. The court did not require that a human at GoAuto open the e-mail, read it, and manually approve cancellation before the policy could be terminated. The statute required receipt by the insurer, and the court concluded that receipt by GoAuto’s automated system was receipt by GoAuto. On that basis, the panel held that GoAuto’s electronic intake and automatic response satisfied § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

## The District Court’s Alternative “Private Carrier” Theory

The district court had offered a different theory for why the statute was satisfied. It reasoned that the APAC employee who physically delivered the cancellation documents might qualify as a “private carrier,” which would fit one of the statute’s methods for sending the notice to the insurer. The Fifth Circuit described that theory as “imaginative and unconventional,” and noted the lack of Louisiana case law defining “private carrier” in this context.

But the appellate court did not need to decide whether that theory was right. Once it concluded that the e-mail transmission to GoAuto’s computer system constituted sufficient receipt, the private-carrier question became unnecessary. The panel therefore affirmed on the narrower ground that electronic receipt alone satisfied the statute.

## The Holding on Receipt

The court’s bottom-line holding on this issue was that GoAuto did receive APAC’s cancellation request within the meaning of Louisiana law. The statute did not require a signature before cancellation, and it did not require human physical possession before the insurer could act. GoAuto’s automated system received the e-mail notice, triggered cancellation, and therefore complied with § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Because the plaintiffs’ “no receipt” argument failed, their challenge to the effectiveness of the cancellation failed as well.

# Chapter 5: Certification, Disposition, and the Opinion's Practical Significance

## The plaintiffs’ request to certify the state-law questions

After resolving the statutory text itself, the court turned to the plaintiffs’ alternative request: that it send the interpretive questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Certification is a procedure federal courts sometimes use when a case turns on an unsettled issue of state law. Rather than guess how the state’s highest court would interpret the statute, the federal court may ask that court for an authoritative answer. Here, the plaintiffs argued that the meaning of Louisiana’s financed-policy cancellation statute mattered to the state’s insurance industry and therefore warranted certification.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that request. The court had already decided the dispute by reading the statute, looking to Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions, and applying ordinary dictionary meanings to the undefined terms. In the court’s view, that was enough to decide the case without asking for a state-court advisory ruling.

## The Fifth Circuit’s cautious approach to certification

The court explained that it is generally reluctant to certify questions unless there is a compelling reason. Certification is treated as a useful but limited resource, not something to be used whenever a state-law issue is difficult or important. The court quoted its own prior warning that it is “chary” about certification and should not overuse the procedure.

That caution matters because federal courts do not certify simply because a state supreme court has not yet spoken. The absence of controlling state supreme court precedent is not, by itself, enough. If federal courts certified every unsettled state-law question, the procedure would become routine rather than exceptional. The Fifth Circuit therefore asked whether the statute’s meaning was truly beyond judicial resolution on the existing record and authorities. It concluded that it was not.

## Why the lack of Louisiana Supreme Court precedent did not justify certification

The plaintiffs emphasized that the Louisiana Supreme Court had not interpreted the precise statutory terms at issue. But the Fifth Circuit treated that fact as only the starting point, not the end of the analysis. The court relied on the statute’s language, Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions, and basic dictionary definitions of the disputed words. In particular, the court had already determined that the statute did not require a handwritten signature on the cancellation notice and that “receipt” could be satisfied when GoAuto’s computerized system obtained and acted on the emailed request.

Because the panel believed the statutory questions were answerable through ordinary judicial interpretation, it saw no need to wait for the Louisiana Supreme Court. That is why certification was denied: the case did not present the kind of unresolved, dispositive state-law mystery that justifies sending the issue upward.

## The plaintiffs’ own summary-judgment motion failed for the same reason

The plaintiffs also challenged the denial of their motion for partial summary judgment. But once the court concluded that GoAuto’s cancellation procedure complied with Louisiana law, the plaintiffs’ motion necessarily failed. Summary judgment turns on whether there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Here, the plaintiffs’ legal theory depended on a narrower reading of the statute than the court accepted.

So the same statutory interpretation that supported judgment for GoAuto also defeated the plaintiffs’ request for judgment in their favor. The appellate court therefore had no reason to disturb the district court’s denial of the plaintiffs’ motion. Once the court held that the cancellation process was lawful, the plaintiffs could not obtain relief on a theory that the cancellations were ineffective.

## The final disposition: affirmance in full

The opinion ends with a straightforward disposition: affirmed. That means the Fifth Circuit upheld the district court’s summary judgment for GoAuto and the dismissal of all claims with prejudice. The court did not remand for further proceedings, and it did not revive any of the plaintiffs’ claims for misrepresentation or breach of good faith and fair dealing. Those claims fell with the statutory challenge, because the plaintiffs’ theories depended on showing that the policies had not been validly canceled.

The district court had also offered an alternative rationale—that the APAC employee who physically handled the documents might qualify as a “private carrier”—but the Fifth Circuit expressly declined to rely on that theory. Because the court found the emailed request itself satisfied the statute’s receipt requirement, it had no need to address the district court’s separate explanation.

## What the opinion means for insurers and premium finance companies

The practical significance of the case is that Louisiana’s financed-policy cancellation statute can be satisfied through modern electronic procedures. The court accepted a process in which APAC sent an automated email request, GoAuto’s computer system received and acted on it, and a signature stamp was applied later to the printed documents. In other words, the statute did not require a paper-first, physically signed, manually delivered cancellation workflow.

That matters for insurers and premium finance companies because it gives legal effect to automated cancellation systems, so long as the statutory prerequisites are met. The decision recognizes that current business practices often involve software, email, and digital recordkeeping rather than handwritten signatures and in-person delivery. At the same time, the opinion does not give companies a free pass. It approves electronic compliance only because the court believed the statute’s actual requirements were satisfied, not because courts may relax the statute whenever technology changes.

## The narrower doctrinal lesson: strict compliance means the statute, not extra formalities

The opinion’s most important doctrinal point is also its narrowest one. Louisiana requires strict adherence to the cancellation statute, and strict adherence is serious. A defect in the statutory process can make a cancellation ineffective. But strict adherence does not allow courts to invent extra requirements that the legislature did not write. Here, that principle worked in GoAuto’s favor: the court refused to read into the statute a requirement that the premium finance company’s certification be signed before cancellation, or that the insurer receive a physical paper copy in a particular form.

That is the case’s lasting lesson for reading statutory-cancellation opinions. “Strict compliance” does not mean “maximum formality.” It means compliance with the text as written. Where the legislature required a certification and receipt of notice, the court enforced those requirements. Where the plaintiffs asked the court to add a pre-cancellation signature rule or a physical-possession rule not found in the statute, the court declined.
