Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: GoAuto and APAC's Cancellation Process — The 'Certifying' Question and Whether a Signature Is Required

## The APAC-GoAuto Cancellation Workflow

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, it helps to follow the cancellation process from beginning to end. The policies at issue were not ordinary directly billed policies. Instead, each insured financed the premium through APAC, which paid GoAuto the full premium up front and then collected monthly installments from the insured. The financing agreement also gave APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the policy if the insured defaulted on payment.

When an insured missed a payment, APAC first sent the insured a ten-day notice of cancellation. That notice told the insured that the policy would be cancelled if the overdue payment was not made within ten days. If the insured still did not pay by the end of that period, APAC’s computer system automatically generated and sent GoAuto an email with the cancellation materials attached. GoAuto’s system then cancelled the policy automatically upon receiving that email. Only later, after the cancellation had already occurred in GoAuto’s system, did an APAC employee place a signature stamp on the printed documents. The plaintiffs focused heavily on that sequence, arguing that the cancellation could not be effective unless APAC’s certification had been signed before GoAuto acted.

## The Statutory Framework Governing Cancellation

The opinion turns on Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3), which governs cancellation of financed insurance policies. The statute allows a premium finance company, after an uncured default, to request cancellation by sending the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying four things: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in default and the default has not been cured, that the insured was sent notice of cancellation, and that copies of the cancellation notice were sent to others with a protected interest.

The court emphasizes that Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to these procedures. That phrase matters because it means the finance company must comply with the statute exactly. But strict adherence does not authorize a court to invent new requirements that the legislature did not write into the statute. The purpose of the statutory scheme is to create a clear cancellation date and give the insured a minimum period to cure the default. Those purposes guide the court’s reading of the text.

## The Plaintiffs' Signature Argument

The plaintiffs’ first major argument was simple: APAC’s notice was ineffective because no signature existed on the certification until after GoAuto had already cancelled the policy. In their view, the statute’s requirement that the finance company “certif[y]” the listed facts necessarily meant that a signed certification had to exist before cancellation could occur. If the certification was unsigned at the moment GoAuto acted, then, according to the plaintiffs, GoAuto had not received a valid cancellation request under Louisiana law.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that argument because the statute itself never says anything about a signature. The relevant statutory language requires a certification, but not a signature by a particular person. That silence is important: if the legislature intended a signature requirement, it could have said so expressly.

## Why the Court Rejected a Signature Requirement

Because Louisiana appellate courts had not directly addressed whether a signature is required, the Fifth Circuit looked to the available Louisiana cases for guidance. The cases did not provide a clear answer, but they suggested that the key statutory requirement is a certification of the listed facts, not a signed form in some formalistic sense. In KMJ Services, the Louisiana court described the statutory requirement as a “statement certifying compliance,” which supported the view that the substance of the certification matters more than the presence of a signature. Hodges likewise spoke in terms of receipt of “such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company,” again focusing on the notice and statement, not on a signature as an independent statutory element.

The plaintiffs relied most heavily on Benitez v. Elsayed, where the Louisiana Fifth Circuit noted that the notice at issue was not signed and did not identify any particular employee. But the federal Fifth Circuit treated that discussion as non-dispositive. Benitez did not hold that the absence of a signature alone invalidates cancellation. Rather, it mentioned the missing signature as one of several shortcomings in that particular notice. The Fifth Circuit therefore read Benitez as showing that the lack of a signature can matter in context, but not as establishing a standalone signature rule.

That distinction allowed the court to make its central interpretive move. The statute says that the insurance premium finance company cancels the policy by “certifying” certain facts. It does not say that cancellation depends on the signature of a named employee. In the court’s view, a company can certify information without identifying a particular human signatory, especially where the notice itself contains the required certifications. The court was unwilling to add a signature requirement to a statute that did not include one.

## The Meaning of Strict Adherence

The court’s treatment of “strict adherence” is a useful lesson in statutory interpretation. Strict adherence means that a party must follow the statutory steps exactly, but it does not allow a court to rewrite the statute by adding steps the legislature omitted. Here, the plaintiffs tried to turn the word “certifying” into a requirement that the certification be signed before cancellation. The court declined to do that. In its view, the statutory command was satisfied so long as APAC, as the finance company, sent a notice containing the required certifications.

That approach fits the court’s broader view of the statute as a practical, modern cancellation scheme. The court noted that the statute operates in an era where companies use computer systems to transmit and process notices. In that setting, the court saw no reason to treat the absence of a pre-cancellation handwritten or stamped signature as fatal when the notice already contained the required company certification.

## The Holding on the Certification Issue

The Fifth Circuit’s holding on this point was clear: Louisiana law does not require the premium finance company’s cancellation notice to be signed before the insurer cancels the policy. Because APAC’s notice contained the required certifications, the absence of a pre-cancellation signature did not make the cancellation ineffective. This ruling disposed of the plaintiffs’ first major statutory challenge and set the stage for the court’s separate discussion of whether GoAuto properly received the cancellation request.
