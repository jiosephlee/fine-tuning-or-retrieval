Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Why did the court say "strict adherence" to the cancellation statute does not imply a signature requirement?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly says Louisiana requires strict adherence to the premium-finance cancellation statute and that any defect makes cancellation ineffective. But when the plaintiffs argued that "certifying" the statutory facts should require a signature before cancellation, the Fifth Circuit said strict adherence is not an invitation to add procedures the statute doesn't expressly contain. I'm struggling with that move. If Louisiana courts strictly construe this statute, how do courts decide whether something is a missing statutory requirement versus an impermissible extra-textual requirement? In other words, why wasn't a signature treated as part of what it means to "certify"?
Answer:
Because “strict adherence” means strict adherence to **what the statute actually says**, not to extra formalities a court thinks would be nice to have.

That is the core move in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*. The Fifth Circuit treated the statute as having two separate questions:

1. **What does the statute require?**  
2. **Did GoAuto comply with those requirements?**

On the signature point, the court answered the first question by looking at the text. Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send the insurer a copy of the cancellation notice **“together with a statement certifying”** four listed facts. The statute does **not** say “signed statement,” “signed certification,” “sworn affidavit,” or “identified employee.” The court therefore refused to read a signature requirement into the word “certifying” unless the word itself necessarily carried that meaning.

The court’s reasoning is basically this:

- **Strict adherence does not let courts add procedures.**  
  The opinion expressly says: “*Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.*”

- **A missing requirement must be one the statute actually imposes, expressly or by necessary implication.**  
  Here, the statute imposes a certification requirement, but the court found no textual basis for concluding that certification must be accomplished by a signature.

- **“Certifying” is not the same as “signing.”**  
  In ordinary usage, to certify means to attest or declare that something is true. A signature may be one way to do that, but it is not the only way unless the statute says so. The court also noted that the statute speaks in terms of **“the insurance premium finance company”** certifying the facts, not a particular employee.

That is why the court did **not** treat a signature as part of the statute’s command. A signature would have been an **extra-textual requirement** unless the text, context, or Louisiana authority made it necessary. The court found none of those.

So the distinction is:

- **Missing statutory requirement** = something the statute requires, either expressly or because the statutory term cannot function without it.
- **Impermissible extra-textual requirement** = a formality the statute does not require, but a litigant asks the court to add in the name of “strict construction.”

The opinion also points out that the one Louisiana case plaintiffs relied on, *Benitez*, did not hold that the absence of a signature alone defeats cancellation. It merely mentioned the lack of a signature as one factor among several shortcomings.

So the short answer is: **Louisiana’s “strict adherence” rule is strict about compliance with the statute’s text, but it does not authorize courts to rewrite the statute by adding a signature requirement that the legislature did not include.**
