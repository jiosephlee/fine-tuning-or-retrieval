Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### When should a federal court certify an unresolved state-law question instead of making an Erie guess?
Question:
The plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify the interpretation of terms like "certifying" and "receipt" to the Louisiana Supreme Court, and the panel refused. The opinion says the absence of a state supreme court decision is not enough by itself and that the Fifth Circuit is "chary" about certification absent a compelling reason. Under Erie, how do federal courts decide when to certify versus when to predict state law themselves? Since this issue apparently affects Louisiana's insurance industry and there wasn't controlling Louisiana Supreme Court authority, what more would have been needed to justify certification?
Answer:
Under Erie, a federal court sitting in diversity is supposed to apply **state substantive law**, and if the state’s highest court has not spoken, the federal court usually makes its **best prediction** of how that court would rule. Certification is the exception, not the default.

### The basic rule
A federal court will usually **predict state law itself** when it has enough material to do so:
- statutory text,
- relevant state intermediate-appellate decisions,
- analogous state cases,
- and ordinary interpretive tools like dictionaries and canons.

It will **certify** only when the state-law question is **truly unsettled** and there is a **compelling reason** to ask the state supreme court to answer it instead of having the federal court guess.

That is exactly the Fifth Circuit’s point in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*. The panel quoted *Jefferson v. Lead Industries* for the proposition that the Fifth Circuit is “**chary**” about certification and that “[t]hat a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court is not sufficient to warrant certification.” So the mere absence of Louisiana Supreme Court precedent was not enough.

### Why the Fifth Circuit refused here
The court could still do a normal Erie prediction because it had enough to work with:
- the statute’s text,
- Louisiana intermediate appellate cases like *Delatte*, *Eaglin*, *Britten*, *Hodges*, *KMJ Servs.*, *Lewis*, and *Benitez*,
- and ordinary dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive.”

On that basis, the panel concluded that Louisiana law did **not** require a signature on the certification and that GoAuto’s computer-based receipt process satisfied the statute.

### What would have strengthened the case for certification?
Not just “this matters to Louisiana’s insurance industry.” The Fifth Circuit generally wants something more, such as:

1. **A genuine lack of guidance**
   - not just no Louisiana Supreme Court case, but also little or no usable lower-court guidance;
   - or lower-court decisions that point in different directions.

2. **A hard, ambiguous state-law issue**
   - where the federal court cannot make a confident Erie prediction from text and precedent;
   - in other words, where any answer would be close to pure speculation.

3. **A particularly important state-policy question**
   - especially one with broad statewide consequences **plus** real uncertainty in the law.

4. **A risk of creating bad state law**
   - for example, if the question is novel and likely to recur, and a federal guess could disrupt a carefully balanced state regulatory scheme.

### Bottom line
Certification is appropriate when the federal court thinks: **“This is an important and genuinely unsettled state-law issue, and we cannot predict the state supreme court’s answer with confidence.”**  
If the court can reach a reasoned answer from the statute and existing Louisiana cases, it will usually do that itself.

So in *Williams*, what was missing was not importance, but **compelling uncertainty**. The panel had enough state-law material to make an Erie guess, so it declined to certify.
