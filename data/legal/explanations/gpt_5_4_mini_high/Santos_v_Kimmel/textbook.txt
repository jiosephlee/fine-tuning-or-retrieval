Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# From Cameo Videos to the Second Circuit

## The parties and the posture

This appeal began as an unusual clash between political notoriety and late-night comedy. Former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel, the ABC television network, and Disney, ABC’s parent company, after Kimmel used Santos’s personalized Cameo videos in a recurring segment on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (“JKL”). Santos claimed that the defendants had infringed his copyrights and committed several state-law wrongs. The case was dismissed in the Southern District of New York, and Santos appealed to the Second Circuit.

For a law student, the most important thing to notice at the outset is the procedural posture. The district court dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6), meaning it concluded that Santos had not stated a claim that could proceed even if the factual allegations in the complaint were accepted as true. That posture matters because the appellate court is not reviewing a trial record; it is deciding whether the complaint itself, together with materials properly considered at the pleading stage, supports the claims Santos pleaded.

## How the Cameo videos were created

The dispute centered on the Cameo platform, which the opinion describes as a service through which users can request personalized videos from a speaker. In this case, Kimmel did not submit the requests in his own name. Instead, he used various fictitious names to ask Santos for the videos. Santos fulfilled each request and delivered the recordings.

Those facts were legally important because Santos’s claims depended on the proposition that he had created original expressive works that the defendants later used without permission. The opinion shows that the parties disagreed not just about copying, but about what the videos were for and how they should be understood. Santos’s own allegations described the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the defendants later used them as material for ridicule.

## Kimmel’s broadcast use on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*

Kimmel did not keep the videos private. According to the complaint, ABC aired them on JKL as part of a mocking segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segment repurposed the videos for comedy and commentary, with the apparent aim of highlighting Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money.

That use is what turned a simple recording request into a copyright case. Santos objected that the defendants had taken the videos and republished them on television without authorization. The defendants responded that the use was protected by the fair use doctrine, which can permit copying when the later use is sufficiently transformative or otherwise consistent with copyright’s purposes. The appeal therefore raised the question whether the complaint itself showed that the television segments were lawful fair use.

## Santos’s claims and the district court’s dismissal

Santos’s complaint asserted copyright infringement and several state-law theories, including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. He also argued that the Cameo terms of service imposed obligations on the defendants. The district court rejected the case at the pleading stage. It held that the copyright claims were barred by fair use and that the state-law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim under the relevant state law.

The Second Circuit thus had to decide two sets of issues. First, it had to determine whether the complaint and the incorporated materials showed fair use as a matter of law, which would defeat the copyright claims even before discovery. Second, it had to decide whether any of Santos’s state-law theories could survive, either because they were not preempted or because they adequately pleaded the elements required by state contract and fraud law. The court ultimately affirmed the dismissal on both fronts.

# Appellate Posture and the Pleading-Stage Fair Use Inquiry

## De novo review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal

The Second Circuit began by identifying the procedural posture: the district court had dismissed Santos’s complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. That matters because appellate review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is de novo, meaning the court of appeals gives no deference to the district court’s legal conclusions. The panel therefore asked the same question the district court asked: taking the relevant materials as true and reading them in the light most favorable to Santos, did the complaint still fail as a matter of law?

That posture is important in a case like this because the court was not deciding whether Santos could ultimately prove his claims after discovery. It was deciding whether the complaint, together with the documents the court could properly consider on a motion to dismiss, already showed that no viable claim existed.

## What counts as part of the record on a motion to dismiss

In applying Rule 12(b)(6), the court did not confine itself to the bare allegations of the complaint. It could also consider documents attached to the complaint as exhibits and documents incorporated by reference. That is a standard feature of motion-to-dismiss practice: a plaintiff cannot avoid dismissal by omitting or selectively describing the very documents that shape the claim.

Here, that meant the court could look not only at Santos’s allegations about the Cameo videos and the Jimmy Kimmel Live! segments, but also at the materials he attached or incorporated. Because the court was evaluating the legal sufficiency of the claims on the pleadings, those materials were part of the operative record for the appellate court as well.

## Judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations

The panel also noted that it could take judicial notice of copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office’s registry. Judicial notice allows a court to recognize the existence and content of certain public records without requiring proof in the ordinary evidentiary sense. The Second Circuit has long permitted notice of copyright registrations at the pleading stage because they are official public records and their authenticity is not seriously in dispute.

This mattered because the registrations supplied additional information relevant to the fair use analysis. The court did not need discovery to know what had been registered, when the works were created, and when they were published. Those dates were part of the public copyright record.

## Why the publication information in the registrations mattered

One detail the panel specifically mentioned was that the registrations showed the videos were published on the same day they were created. That fact matters because publication status is relevant to the second fair use factor, the nature of the copyrighted work. Unpublished works generally receive stronger protection than published works, in part because copyright law is concerned with the author’s right to control first publication.

By pointing to the registration data, the court made clear that Santos could not plausibly rely on any argument that the works were unpublished in a way that would weigh heavily against fair use. The publication information therefore supported the court’s conclusion that the fair use inquiry could be resolved from the pleadings and public records, without factual development.

## Fair use as an affirmative defense under the Copyright Act

Fair use is an affirmative defense, which ordinarily means the defendant bears the burden of proving it. But an affirmative defense does not always require a trial. If the complaint itself and the materials properly before the court establish the defense on their face, dismissal at the pleading stage is appropriate.

The panel reiterated the familiar statutory factors: purpose and character of the use, nature of the copyrighted work, amount used, and effect on the market. The opinion focused especially on the first and fourth factors. The court concluded that the challenged use was transformative because the JKL segments used the videos to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, not to serve the same expressive purpose as Santos’s original videos, which the complaint itself described as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” On that record, the court held that the first factor strongly favored fair use.

The court also rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s deception in obtaining the videos defeated fair use. Even assuming the false names and requests were misleading, the complaint did not plausibly show an intent to usurp Santos’s commercial market for the videos. Instead, the complaint described a satirical and critical use. The panel therefore treated the alleged bad faith as insufficient to overcome the broader fair use analysis.

## When fair use may be decided at the pleading stage

The key procedural lesson from the opinion is that fair use can sometimes be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The Second Circuit said expressly that fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal” at the pleading stage. That is what happened here.

The reason is that fair use often turns on comparing the challenged use with the original work and asking whether the new use adds something different, comments on the original, or substitutes for it in the market. If the complaint itself includes enough about both works, the court may be able to answer those questions without discovery. In Santos, the complaint described the original Cameo videos, described the JKL segments, and attached or incorporated materials that let the court see the context. Those facts were enough for the panel to conclude that the defense was apparent on the face of the pleadings.

## The significance of comparing the original works and the challenged use directly

The opinion underscores that fair use analysis depends on direct comparison. The court cited precedent stating that, for fair use, it may consider the original version of the work and the challenged secondary use at issue. That comparison is central because the question is not whether the defendant used the plaintiff’s work at all, but whether the use had a new purpose or character and whether it competed with the original as a substitute.

Here, the panel compared Santos’s personalized videos with the broadcast segments that repurposed them as material for ridicule. That comparison drove the court’s conclusions on transformativness and market effect. The JKL segments were not presented as replacements for the Cameo videos; they were presented as commentary on them. Because the court could make that comparison from the face of the pleadings and the public copyright records, it affirmed dismissal without needing discovery or trial.

# The Fair Use Framework and the Court’s Focus on Transformative Purpose

## The statutory four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The panel began with the basic structure of fair use. Fair use is an affirmative defense that can excuse conduct that would otherwise look infringing. Congress codified the defense in 17 U.S.C. § 107, which instructs courts to consider four non-exclusive factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and the effect of the use on the market for the original. The opinion emphasizes that fair use is flexible, not mechanical, and that a court may resolve it at the pleading stage when the complaint itself makes the defense clear. That mattered here because the Second Circuit was reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and could rely on the complaint, its exhibits, incorporated documents, and judicially noticeable copyright registrations.

## The policy behind fair use: preventing rigid enforcement that would hinder creativity and commentary

The court framed fair use as a doctrine designed to prevent copyright from being applied so rigidly that it stifles the very creativity copyright is supposed to encourage. That policy explanation is important because it helps explain why the court looked closely at whether Kimmel’s use added something new rather than merely copying Santos’s videos for the same expressive function. The court’s focus was not on whether the videos were copied in a literal sense, but on whether the later use served a different purpose, especially one involving commentary or criticism. In that way, fair use is not simply a loophole; it is part of copyright’s own design.

## The first factor: “purpose and character of the use”

The first factor received the most attention. The court explained that this factor asks about the purpose and character of the secondary use, and that the key question is whether the use is transformative. A transformative use is one that does not simply supersede the original, but instead adds something new, with a different purpose or character. In practice, this means the court looks at how the new work functions in context. If the later use is aimed at criticism, parody, commentary, or some other distinct expressive purpose, that often weighs strongly toward fair use.

The panel also noted that fair use can be evident from the face of the complaint itself. That is what made the first factor especially significant here: the complaint and its attached materials were enough for the court to assess how the videos were being used on Kimmel’s show.

## Transformative use and the distinction between superseding the original and adding a new purpose

Applying that framework, the court held that Kimmel’s use was transformative because the JKL segments used Santos’s Cameo videos to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. That, the court said, was a different purpose from the original videos. The opinion repeatedly returns to the idea that the question is not whether the second work uses the first work, but whether it uses it for a different expressive end.

Santos tried to resist that conclusion by arguing that Kimmel had “instigated” the creation of the original videos and therefore became the designer of their original purpose. The court rejected that argument. Even though Kimmel created the circumstances that led Santos to produce the videos, the relevant comparison is between the message and function of the original videos and the later broadcast segments. On that comparison, the original videos were alleged to convey hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity, while the JKL segments used them as material for mockery and commentary. That difference in message and function was central to the court’s finding of transformation.

## Santos’s argument that Kimmel “instigated” the videos and therefore shared their original purpose

Santos’s “instigation” theory tried to collapse the distinction between creation and later use. In his view, because Kimmel set up the requests, Kimmel should be treated as having shared the original purpose of the videos from the beginning. The court was not persuaded because fair use does not turn on who prompted the creation of the original work or on the user’s subjective motive in obtaining it. What mattered was how a reasonable observer would understand the original videos and the later segments. The court treated the fact that Kimmel solicited the videos as irrelevant to the fair use analysis once the videos themselves were then repurposed for criticism on television.

## The court’s reliance on an objective “reasonable observer” perspective rather than subjective intent

A key move in the opinion is its reliance on an objective perspective. Santos argued that his own intent in making the videos was also to convey absurdity, which he said undermined the claim that Kimmel’s use had a different purpose. The court rejected that line of reasoning and turned to the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision, which cautions that subjective intent does not control the purpose inquiry. In other words, the court does not ask what the copyright holder secretly hoped the work would mean, or what the secondary user says he intended; it asks how the work would be understood by a reasonable observer.

That approach allowed the panel to treat Santos’s own allegations against him. His complaint described the videos as expressing hope and positivity. If that was the work’s apparent original character, then Kimmel’s later use of the videos for ridicule and commentary was meaningfully different. The opinion thus uses the complaint’s own characterization of the videos to show why the first factor favored fair use.

## The role of Warhol in rejecting subjective-purpose arguments

The court’s treatment of Warhol is especially important for understanding the opinion’s logic. Santos tried to argue that the purpose of the secondary use should be assessed in light of the alleged original purpose or the user’s subjective framing. The panel responded by citing Warhol for the proposition that subjective intent does not determine the purpose of the use. That allowed the court to keep the analysis anchored in what the work objectively does in context. In this case, Kimmel’s broadcast segments were not treated as a substitute version of the Cameo videos; they were treated as commentary that used the videos as raw material for criticism.

## Santos’s bad-faith theory based on Kimmel’s false representations

Santos also argued that Kimmel’s false representations in ordering the videos showed bad faith and therefore defeated fair use. The court acknowledged Harper & Row’s statement that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but it said the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that broad proposition. More importantly, the complaint itself showed that the defendants’ purpose was not to supplant Santos’s commercial interest in the videos, but to engage in sarcastic criticism and commentary. That mattered because the fair use inquiry is concerned with whether the secondary use displaces the market for the original, not simply whether the defendant behaved misleadingly in obtaining the original material.

## Why the court treated bad faith as insufficient here, especially where the complaint itself showed criticism rather than market substitution

The court’s response to the bad-faith argument also ties back to the market-substitution idea underlying fair use. Even if Kimmel used deception to obtain the videos, that fact did not, by itself, defeat fair use where the later use was critical commentary rather than a competing substitute. The complaint described a use aimed at ridicule, not one that was trying to replace Santos’s market for personalized videos. In the court’s view, a use that criticizes or lampoons an original work is not the kind of use that fair use seeks to forbid, even if the user’s route to the material involved false pretenses.

## The conclusion that the first factor strongly favored fair use

By the end of the analysis, the court held that the first factor strongly favored fair use. That conclusion drove the rest of the opinion’s copyright discussion, and it was enough—together with the court’s rejection of Santos’s other challenges—to affirm dismissal at the pleading stage. For a law student reading the case, the central lesson is that fair use turns on function and context. Here, the court saw Santos’s videos as original expressive works that were later repurposed not to replace them, but to criticize and mock the very willingness they allegedly displayed. That difference in purpose was enough to carry the first factor decisively in defendants’ favor.

# The Remaining Fair Use Factors and Why Copyright Claims Failed as a Matter of Law

After holding that Kimmel’s use was transformative, the court turned briefly to the other fair use factors. The important lesson for reading the opinion is that fair use is a holistic inquiry, not a mechanical count of how many factors favor one side. Here, the panel concluded that nothing in the second, third, or fourth factors changed the basic picture already created by the first factor: the JKL segments were commentary on Santos, not a substitute for his videos. Because that conclusion was apparent from the materials properly before the court, fair use could be resolved at the pleading stage.

## The Second Factor: The Nature of the Copyrighted Work

The second factor asks about the character of the work copied. In broad terms, courts give more protection to highly creative works than to factual or informational ones, and they also pay attention to whether the work was published or unpublished. The panel pointed out that the copyright registrations showed Santos’s videos were published on the same day they were created. That detail mattered because it undercut any suggestion that Kimmel had taken unreleased material and interfered with Santos’s control over its first public appearance.

The court did not dwell on this factor, which signals that publication status did not help Santos much. Once the videos were already public, the second factor had less force on his side. The opinion treats that fact as one more reason the fair use defense was not defeated by the nature of the works.

## The Third Factor: Amount and Substantiality of the Portion Used

The panel was similarly unpersuaded by Santos’s objections to the third factor, which concerns how much of the original was taken and whether what was taken was qualitatively important. The court did not write at length on this point, and that brevity is itself telling. Santos’s arguments about the amount used were not enough to alter the overall fair use balance.

For students, the key point is that the third factor does not operate in isolation. Even if a defendant uses a noticeable portion of a work, that does not end the analysis if the use is strongly transformative and does not usurp the original’s market. Here, the court saw no reason to disturb the district court’s weighing of that factor.

## The Fourth Factor: Market Harm and the Difference Between Devaluation and Substitution

The court spent a bit more attention on the fourth factor because Santos’s strongest remaining point was that Kimmel’s broadcast harmed the value of his videos. But the opinion draws an important distinction: copyright law is concerned with market usurpation, not with every kind of injury to reputation or value. A work may be mocked, criticized, or ridiculed in a way that makes it less popular, but that is not the same as a competing substitute that takes the original’s place in the market.

That is why the panel relied on Hachette and Campbell. Hachette explains that the relevant question is not whether the secondary work devalues the first through parody or criticism, but whether it supplants the original by serving as a substitute. Campbell adds that creators ordinarily do not license critical reviews or lampoons of their own works, so those kinds of uses do not create a cognizable lost licensing market. On that logic, Santos’s complaint did not allege market harm in the copyright sense; it alleged embarrassment and criticism, which may be real injuries but are not the kind the fourth factor targets.

## Why Only the Original Videos and the JKL Segments Mattered

The panel also made clear that it needed only the original videos and the JKL segments to decide fair use. Citing Cariou, the court explained that the fair use question can be answered by comparing the copyrighted work and the challenged secondary use themselves. That means the court did not need a wider factual record or discovery into collateral matters to decide whether the use was fair.

This matters procedurally because it shows why dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) was proper. When the complaint, its attachments, and judicially noticeable materials already show that the secondary use is transformative and does not usurp the market for the original, a court may resolve fair use as a matter of law. The Second Circuit concluded that this was such a case.

## The Bottom-Line Holding: Fair Use Barred the Copyright Claims at the Pleading Stage

Putting the factors together, the court held that the remaining fair use considerations did not rescue Santos’s copyright claims. The second factor was weakened by the fact that the videos were already published; the third factor did not undermine the district court’s ruling; and the fourth factor failed because criticism and lampooning are not the same as market substitution. Since the works themselves showed a parody-like commentary use, fair use was sufficiently clear to support dismissal before discovery.

That is why the panel affirmed the judgment on the copyright claims as a matter of law. The opinion’s core message is that a copyright plaintiff cannot turn reputational injury from ridicule into a viable infringement case when the challenged use is transformative commentary and does not function as a market substitute for the original.

# State Law Claims, Unresolved Preemption, and the Final Affirmance

## The preemption issue the court left open

After rejecting the copyright claims on fair use grounds, the Second Circuit turned to Santos’s state-law theories: breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The district court had dismissed the contract claims in part on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. On appeal, Santos argued that those claims were “qualitatively different” from copyright infringement and therefore not preempted.

The panel did not resolve that question. Instead, it used a narrower route: even assuming the claims were not preempted, Santos still had not stated a viable state-law claim. That matters because an appellate court may affirm a dismissal on any ground supported by the record. Here, the court could leave the preemption issue undecided and affirm because the complaint failed on ordinary pleading grounds.

## Why the court could affirm on alternative grounds

This part of the opinion is a good reminder that appellate review is not limited to the district court’s precise reasoning. If the complaint itself shows that a claim fails as a matter of law, the court of appeals can affirm without deciding a more difficult or broader doctrinal question. That is exactly what happened here. The panel treated preemption as unnecessary to the result because Santos’s state-law theories did not survive even on their own terms.

The practical effect is important. A plaintiff cannot avoid dismissal simply by labeling a copyright grievance as contract or fraud. The court looked at whether Santos had actually pleaded the elements of those state-law claims, and concluded that he had not.

## The direct breach-of-contract claim under Cameo’s Terms of Service

Santos first tried to sue directly for breach of Cameo’s Terms of Service. But he was not himself a party to the user agreement he claimed had been breached. That immediately raised a threshold problem: absent privity, Santos needed some basis to enforce the contract as a third-party beneficiary.

The governing law for the Terms of Service was Illinois law, and Illinois strongly presumes against treating noncontracting parties as intended beneficiaries. The court quoted the Illinois rule that the contract’s intent to benefit a third party must be so clear as to be “practically an express declaration.” Santos’s complaint did not come close to that standard. He pointed to no language showing that the relevant provisions were meant to create enforceable rights in him or in other noncontracting parties.

The opinion also emphasizes a detail that cut against Santos’s theory: other provisions of the Terms of Service did include explicit third-party-beneficiary language. That mattered because it showed the drafters knew how to extend contractual rights to third parties when they wanted to do so. The absence of similar language in the provisions Santos relied on made it difficult to infer any intent to give him enforcement rights. In short, the court treated the contract as one between Cameo and its users, not one that Santos could directly enforce.

## The implied-in-fact contract claim under New York law

Santos also pleaded an implied contract theory. Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract is still a real contract: it depends on mutual assent, inferred from the parties’ conduct and the surrounding circumstances. It is not enough for one side to believe that an agreement existed; the complaint must plausibly allege a meeting of the minds.

The Second Circuit found that Santos’s pleading fell short. It did not allege the essential terms of the supposed implied agreement in nonconclusory fashion, including the specific provisions that defendants supposedly violated. That omission was fatal because a court cannot infer a contract where the complaint does not identify what the parties allegedly agreed to. The opinion also says Santos did not plausibly allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service as between himself and Santos. In other words, Santos described conduct that may have disappointed him, but not a mutual agreement between the parties to the lawsuit.

This part of the decision shows the difference between alleging that someone acted unfairly and alleging that they formed a contract. An implied contract claim still requires facts showing assent, terms, and breach. Without those allegations, the claim is only a repackaged objection to the underlying conduct.

## The fraudulent inducement claim and New York’s out-of-pocket loss requirement

Santos’s final state-law theory was fraudulent inducement. The court rejected that claim because New York requires a plaintiff to plead actual out-of-pocket loss caused by the fraud. That means a concrete pecuniary loss, not merely deception, embarrassment, or the loss of a hoped-for benefit.

The complaint did not plausibly allege that Santos suffered any such loss. The court therefore agreed with the district court that the fraud claim failed. This is a useful doctrinal point: even where a plaintiff alleges misrepresentation, fraud is not established unless the plaintiff pleads that the deception caused measurable economic harm. The opinion cites New York cases for the proposition that fraud damages are measured by actual loss, not by a theoretical bargain or subjective dissatisfaction.

## The final affirmance and why the case matters

Because the copyright claims were barred by fair use and the state-law claims failed to state a claim, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment in full. The case therefore ended not only with a copyright defeat for Santos, but also with the failure of the contract and fraud theories he tried to use as alternative routes to relief.

The larger lesson is straightforward. First, fair use can protect a televised criticism or parody built around commissioned content when the use is transformative and does not usurp the market for the original. Second, a plaintiff cannot easily turn a copyright dispute into a contract case without privity, enforceable beneficiary status, or well-pleaded contract terms. Third, fraud claims in New York require concrete allegations of actual monetary loss. Together, those principles made Santos’s complaint vulnerable at the pleading stage and left the district court’s judgment intact.
