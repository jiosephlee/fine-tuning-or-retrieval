Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### When can a court decide fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion instead of waiting for summary judgment?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. Fair use is an affirmative defense, so I thought defendants usually have to prove it later in the case after discovery. But the Second Circuit says fair use can be so clearly established by the complaint that dismissal at the pleading stage is proper. What exactly has to be in the complaint or incorporated materials for that to happen? Is the idea that the court can compare the original videos and the TV segment itself without resolving factual disputes?
Answer:
Yes. **Under Santos v. Kimmel, fair use can be decided on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and the materials properly before the court already make the defense clear as a matter of law.**

The Second Circuit reiterated two key points:

1. **At the pleading stage, the court may consider**
   - the complaint,
   - documents attached to it,
   - documents incorporated by reference, and
   - judicially noticeable materials like copyright registrations.

   That is straight from the opinion’s Rule 12(b)(6) discussion.

2. **Fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss when the works themselves show it.**
   The court relied on the rule that fair use may be “**so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal**,” and then said that it “**need only consider the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue**” to decide fair use.

So, in practical terms, the court can dismiss when:

- the copyrighted work and the allegedly infringing use are both before the court;
- the complaint’s own allegations describe the purpose and character of the use; and
- those materials leave no plausible factual basis to defeat fair use.

That is exactly what happened in Santos. The complaint itself showed that the TV segment used the videos to **comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money**, which made the use transformative. The opinion also notes that the complaint and the copyright registrations made the other factors clear enough, including the lack of cognizable market harm.

So your instinct is right: **the court is not supposed to resolve disputed facts on a 12(b)(6) motion.** But when the dispute can be answered by comparing the original work and the secondary work, and the complaint’s own allegations already point strongly to fair use, the court may decide the defense at the pleading stage rather than wait for summary judgment.

### How can a use be 'transformative' if the alleged infringer caused the original work to be created?
Question:
Santos argued that Kimmel was basically the 'designer' of the original purpose of the Cameo videos because he submitted the requests under fake names and prompted Santos to make the videos in the first place. The court still says the later broadcast use was transformative because a reasonable observer would see the original videos as positive messages, while the TV segment used them to comment on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. If the defendant sets up the facts so that the original work exists, why doesn't that collapse the distinction between the original purpose and the secondary use?
Answer:
Because fair use asks about the **later use**, not about who first set the chain of events in motion.

In **Santos v. Kimmel**, Santos’s argument was essentially: *Kimmel engineered the videos, so Kimmel was the “designer” of their original purpose too.* The Second Circuit rejected that because the **transformative-use inquiry is objective and comparison-based**. The question is whether the secondary work:

- **adds something new**, and
- has a **different purpose or character** from the original.

That is straight from the court’s discussion of the first fair-use factor.

### Why Kimmel’s solicitation did not defeat transformativeness
The court said the relevant question is **how a reasonable observer would understand the two uses**:

- the **original Cameo videos**: the complaint itself described them as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity”;
- the **JKL broadcast segments**: used to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money.

So even if Kimmel’s fake requests caused the videos to be created, the **original expressive content** was still one thing, and the **later broadcast use** was another. The later use had a different communicative purpose: **satire/commentary**, not the delivery of a personalized positive message.

### The key doctrinal point
The court expressly said that **subjective intent does not control** the fair-use analysis:

> “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.”

So Kimmel’s role in setting up the facts might matter morally or tactically, but it does **not** collapse the fair-use distinction. Fair use is concerned with the **character of the secondary use itself**, and here the court viewed that use as **critical commentary**, not a substitute for the original Cameo videos.

In short: **causing a work to be created is not the same as using that work in the same way.** The opinion treats those as separate questions, and it is the second one that matters for transformation.

### After Harper & Row, does bad faith or deception still matter in a fair use analysis?
Question:
The opinion says Santos claimed Kimmel's false representations and use of fictitious names showed bad faith, which should defeat fair use. The court responds that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from Harper & Row's statement that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing. I'm having trouble understanding the current law here. Does bad faith still count as part of factor one, or is it mostly irrelevant unless it shows market substitution or some other concrete harm?
Answer:
Short answer: **no—bad faith is not a separate bar to fair use**.

What *Santos v. Kimmel* says is the modern rule: the Supreme Court’s old Harper & Row statement that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” has been **limited**. Fair use is now analyzed under the statutory factors, with the main focus on:

1. **the purpose and character of the use** — especially whether it is **transformative**, and  
4. **market effect** — whether the defendant’s use **usurps** the market for the original.

So where does deception fit?

- **If the deception helps show the defendant’s purpose was nontransformative or market-substituting, it can matter.**
- **If it’s just evidence of bad manners, trickery, or even hidden identity, it does not automatically defeat fair use.**

That is exactly how the Second Circuit handled Santos’s argument. Santos said Kimmel’s use of fictitious names and false representations showed bad faith and should defeat fair use. The court rejected that, explaining that the complaint itself showed the use was aimed at **sarcastic criticism and commentary**, not at **supplanting Santos’s commercially valuable right** in the videos. On that record, the court said the first factor strongly favored fair use.

So the current doctrine is:

> **Fair use does not require “good faith” as an independent prerequisite; bad faith matters only insofar as it bears on the statutory fair-use inquiry, especially transformative purpose or market substitution.**

In other words, **concrete harm or nontransformative use matters; bad faith alone usually does not**.

### Why didn't reputational or commercial damage from mockery count as market harm under factor four?
Question:
The court says the fourth fair use factor asks whether the secondary work usurps the market for the original by acting as a competing substitute, not whether it damages the original through parody or criticism. But if Kimmel's segment makes people less likely to buy Santos Cameos in the future, isn't that still economic harm flowing from the use? I'm confused why that kind of lost demand is not considered market harm under copyright law.
Answer:
Because **factor four is not about any and all economic fallout from a use**. It is about whether the secondary work **takes the market for the copyrighted work**, i.e. whether it acts as a **competing substitute** or invades a cognizable licensing market.

That is exactly how the Second Circuit framed it here:

> “We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.”  
> *Santos v. Kimmel* (quoting *Hachette Book Grp.*)

So if Kimmel’s mocking segment makes people think less of Santos and therefore less likely to buy future Cameos, that is **reputational devaluation caused by criticism**. Copyright law generally does **not** treat that as the kind of “market harm” factor four is asking about.

Why not?

1. **Copyright protects against substitution, not hurt feelings or lost popularity.**  
   The question is whether viewers could use Kimmel’s segment **instead of** Santos’s Cameos. They cannot. A satirical late-night segment is not a substitute for a personalized Cameo.

2. **Negative commentary is outside the ordinary licensing market.**  
   The court relied on *Campbell* for the point that creators do not normally license “critical reviews or lampoons” of their works, so the law does not treat the absence of such a licensing fee as market harm.

3. **Otherwise criticism would always count as market harm.**  
   If every drop in demand caused by ridicule counted, then parody and criticism would almost always fail fair use, which is the opposite of what *Campbell* and the Second Circuit say.

So the short answer is: **yes, the segment may have had real economic consequences, but those consequences flowed from criticism and mockery, not from market substitution.** Factor four is concerned with the latter, not the former.

### Why did the court rely on the copyright registrations to say the videos were published the same day they were created?
Question:
In discussing the other fair use factors, the court says the copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created, and it takes judicial notice of the Copyright Office records. How does that matter for the fair use analysis? Is the unpublished/published distinction still important under factor two, and can a court really treat those registration facts as settled on a motion to dismiss?
Answer:
The court used the copyright registrations for a fairly narrow reason: to show that these were **published works**, not unpublished ones.

That matters because under **fair use factor two**—the “nature of the copyrighted work”—courts pay attention to both:

1. whether the work is **creative** or more factual, and  
2. whether it was **published** or **unpublished**.

The unpublished/published distinction is still relevant because an **unpublished** work usually gets stronger protection, largely because the author has a stronger interest in the **first publication** of the work. If a work is already published, that particular concern carries much less weight.

So when the Second Circuit says the registrations show the videos were “published on the same day they were created,” it is basically saying: **these were not unpublished, private works whose first publication Kimmel was commandeering**. That weakens any argument that factor two should cut heavily against fair use. The court did not say publication alone decides fair use; it just noted that this aspect of factor two did not help Santos much.

As for the procedural point: **yes, at the motion-to-dismiss stage the court can rely on the registration records**. The opinion expressly says the Second Circuit may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry,” citing *Island Software* and *TCA Television*. That means the court can accept the existence and contents of those public records without converting the motion into summary judgment.

So the short answer is:

- **Why it mattered:** publication status is part of factor two, and published works get less protection than unpublished ones.  
- **Why the registrations were enough:** the Copyright Office records are public records the court may judicially notice on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.  
- **What the court did not do:** it did not treat the registrations as conclusive of every factual issue, only as reliable evidence that the videos were published when created.

### Why could the Second Circuit avoid the Copyright Act preemption issue and just affirm on state-law pleading grounds?
Question:
The district court dismissed the contract claims as preempted, but the Second Circuit says it 'need not resolve this issue' because the claims fail under state law anyway. Is that just a general appellate rule that a court can affirm on any ground supported by the record? Or is there some reason preemption should have been decided first? I'm trying to understand the sequencing here.
Answer:
Yes — this is the ordinary appellate rule, and nothing in the opinion suggests preemption had to be decided first.

The Second Circuit was reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo, so it could affirm on **any ground supported by the record**. That is exactly what it did here. After noting that the district court had dismissed the breach-of-contract and implied-contract claims as preempted, the panel said:

> “We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.”

That sequencing makes sense because **preemption is not jurisdictional**. It is a merits defense. If the plaintiff’s state-law claims are already defective under the governing state law, the court can stop there and avoid deciding whether the Copyright Act would also preempt them.

And the panel had concrete state-law reasons to affirm:

- **Direct breach of contract**: Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service, and under Illinois law the opinion says there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.”
- **Implied contract**: under New York law, the complaint did not plead the essential terms of any implied-in-fact agreement or a plausible meeting of the minds.
- **Fraudulent inducement**: the complaint did not allege the required out-of-pocket loss.

So the court did not need to decide whether the claims were preempted, because even if they were not, they still failed on their own terms. In other words, preemption would have been an additional reason to dismiss, but not a necessary one.

This is a common appellate approach: decide the case on the **narrowest sufficient ground** and avoid unnecessary issues.

### Can a Cameo creator sue a customer for breaching the platform's Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary?
Question:
For the express contract claim, Santos relied on the Cameo Terms of Service that users like Kimmel agreed to, but the court says Santos was not a party to that contract and was not an intended third-party beneficiary under Illinois law. What would Santos have needed to point to in the Terms of Service to get around the strong presumption against third-party enforcement? The court mentions that other provisions expressly gave rights to third parties. Is that what doomed this claim?
Answer:
**Yes — that was a big part of why the claim failed.**

Under **Illinois law**, a nonparty can enforce a contract only if the contract shows a **clear intent** to benefit that third party. The court quoted the Illinois rule that there is a **“strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties,”** and that the contract must contain an implication **“so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”** *Santos v. Kimmel* (quoting *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*).

So if Santos wanted to sue as a third-party beneficiary, he needed to point to **language in the Cameo Terms of Service showing that the relevant promises were made for the benefit of creators and were intended to be enforceable by them**. In practical terms, that would mean something like:

- an **express third-party beneficiary clause** naming creators or performers;
- language saying the user’s obligations are **for the benefit of the creator**;
- or language giving the creator **the right to enforce** those terms.

What the opinion says is that Santos pointed to **nothing close to that**. And the court noted that **other provisions in the Terms of Service did expressly use third-party beneficiary language**. That mattered because it shows the drafters knew how to create third-party rights when they wanted to do so. The absence of similar language in the provisions Santos relied on made it hard to infer that creators were intended beneficiaries of those terms.

So the short answer is: **yes, the existence of other express third-party beneficiary provisions helped doom the claim**, because it undercut any argument that the specific provisions Santos relied on were meant to be enforced by him.

### What was missing from Santos's implied contract and fraudulent inducement claims under New York law?
Question:
I'm also confused about the state-law merits rulings. For implied-in-fact contract, the court says Santos did not plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would follow the Terms of Service. What sort of allegations would normally be enough to show an implied agreement in this kind of online transaction? And for fraudulent inducement, why wasn't being tricked into making the videos enough to show damages if New York requires an 'out-of-pocket loss'?
Answer:
Under New York law, Santos’s **implied-in-fact contract** and **fraudulent inducement** claims failed because he left out the specific elements those causes of action require.

### 1) Implied-in-fact contract: what was missing?
The Second Circuit said Santos did **not** plausibly allege:

- the **essential terms** of any implied agreement, and
- a **meeting of the minds** that Kimmel would abide by the Cameo Terms of Service.

That is the key point. Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract is still a contract: you need facts showing mutual assent inferred from the parties’ conduct and circumstances. The court cited *Rocky Point Properties* for the rule that intent and surrounding circumstances matter, but then held Santos’s complaint was too thin because it did not plead, “in nonconclusory language,” the contract’s essential terms or any agreement by Kimmel to be bound. It also relied on *Caniglia* and *DG & A Mgmt. Servs.* for those requirements.

So, in a case like this, allegations that are often enough would look like facts showing:

- the user was shown the relevant terms before ordering,
- the platform required assent to those terms,
- Santos knew the user was assenting on behalf of the transaction,
- the parties’ communications or course of dealing showed mutual understanding that the user would comply with those terms, and
- the particular terms allegedly breached were identified with some specificity.

What Santos had, according to the opinion, was basically just the conclusion that the Terms of Service should apply. The court said that was not enough.

### 2) Fraudulent inducement: why was “being tricked” not enough?
Because New York fraud law requires **actual out-of-pocket loss**.

The court cited *Lama Holding* and *Connaughton* for the rule that a fraud plaintiff must allege a **pecuniary loss actually suffered** as a result of the fraud. Being deceived into doing something is not, by itself, enough unless it produced a measurable financial loss.

Here, the court said Santos did **not** allege that he suffered any such out-of-pocket loss. In other words, he did not plead that the alleged deceit caused him to part with money or incur a concrete financial loss. The opinion treats the claim as missing the damages element, not just the deception element.

So the basic distinction is:

- **Deception alone** → not enough for New York fraud.
- **Deception + actual pecuniary loss** → potentially enough.

That is why “I was tricked into making the videos” was insufficient, absent allegations that the trick caused a concrete monetary loss.
