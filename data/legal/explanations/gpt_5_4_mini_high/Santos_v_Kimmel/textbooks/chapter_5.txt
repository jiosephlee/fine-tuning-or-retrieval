Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 5: # State Law Claims, Unresolved Preemption, and the Final Affirmance

## The preemption issue the court left open

After rejecting the copyright claims on fair use grounds, the Second Circuit turned to Santos’s state-law theories: breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The district court had dismissed the contract claims in part on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. On appeal, Santos argued that those claims were “qualitatively different” from copyright infringement and therefore not preempted.

The panel did not resolve that question. Instead, it used a narrower route: even assuming the claims were not preempted, Santos still had not stated a viable state-law claim. That matters because an appellate court may affirm a dismissal on any ground supported by the record. Here, the court could leave the preemption issue undecided and affirm because the complaint failed on ordinary pleading grounds.

## Why the court could affirm on alternative grounds

This part of the opinion is a good reminder that appellate review is not limited to the district court’s precise reasoning. If the complaint itself shows that a claim fails as a matter of law, the court of appeals can affirm without deciding a more difficult or broader doctrinal question. That is exactly what happened here. The panel treated preemption as unnecessary to the result because Santos’s state-law theories did not survive even on their own terms.

The practical effect is important. A plaintiff cannot avoid dismissal simply by labeling a copyright grievance as contract or fraud. The court looked at whether Santos had actually pleaded the elements of those state-law claims, and concluded that he had not.

## The direct breach-of-contract claim under Cameo’s Terms of Service

Santos first tried to sue directly for breach of Cameo’s Terms of Service. But he was not himself a party to the user agreement he claimed had been breached. That immediately raised a threshold problem: absent privity, Santos needed some basis to enforce the contract as a third-party beneficiary.

The governing law for the Terms of Service was Illinois law, and Illinois strongly presumes against treating noncontracting parties as intended beneficiaries. The court quoted the Illinois rule that the contract’s intent to benefit a third party must be so clear as to be “practically an express declaration.” Santos’s complaint did not come close to that standard. He pointed to no language showing that the relevant provisions were meant to create enforceable rights in him or in other noncontracting parties.

The opinion also emphasizes a detail that cut against Santos’s theory: other provisions of the Terms of Service did include explicit third-party-beneficiary language. That mattered because it showed the drafters knew how to extend contractual rights to third parties when they wanted to do so. The absence of similar language in the provisions Santos relied on made it difficult to infer any intent to give him enforcement rights. In short, the court treated the contract as one between Cameo and its users, not one that Santos could directly enforce.

## The implied-in-fact contract claim under New York law

Santos also pleaded an implied contract theory. Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract is still a real contract: it depends on mutual assent, inferred from the parties’ conduct and the surrounding circumstances. It is not enough for one side to believe that an agreement existed; the complaint must plausibly allege a meeting of the minds.

The Second Circuit found that Santos’s pleading fell short. It did not allege the essential terms of the supposed implied agreement in nonconclusory fashion, including the specific provisions that defendants supposedly violated. That omission was fatal because a court cannot infer a contract where the complaint does not identify what the parties allegedly agreed to. The opinion also says Santos did not plausibly allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service as between himself and Santos. In other words, Santos described conduct that may have disappointed him, but not a mutual agreement between the parties to the lawsuit.

This part of the decision shows the difference between alleging that someone acted unfairly and alleging that they formed a contract. An implied contract claim still requires facts showing assent, terms, and breach. Without those allegations, the claim is only a repackaged objection to the underlying conduct.

## The fraudulent inducement claim and New York’s out-of-pocket loss requirement

Santos’s final state-law theory was fraudulent inducement. The court rejected that claim because New York requires a plaintiff to plead actual out-of-pocket loss caused by the fraud. That means a concrete pecuniary loss, not merely deception, embarrassment, or the loss of a hoped-for benefit.

The complaint did not plausibly allege that Santos suffered any such loss. The court therefore agreed with the district court that the fraud claim failed. This is a useful doctrinal point: even where a plaintiff alleges misrepresentation, fraud is not established unless the plaintiff pleads that the deception caused measurable economic harm. The opinion cites New York cases for the proposition that fraud damages are measured by actual loss, not by a theoretical bargain or subjective dissatisfaction.

## The final affirmance and why the case matters

Because the copyright claims were barred by fair use and the state-law claims failed to state a claim, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment in full. The case therefore ended not only with a copyright defeat for Santos, but also with the failure of the contract and fraud theories he tried to use as alternative routes to relief.

The larger lesson is straightforward. First, fair use can protect a televised criticism or parody built around commissioned content when the use is transformative and does not usurp the market for the original. Second, a plaintiff cannot easily turn a copyright dispute into a contract case without privity, enforceable beneficiary status, or well-pleaded contract terms. Third, fraud claims in New York require concrete allegations of actual monetary loss. Together, those principles made Santos’s complaint vulnerable at the pleading stage and left the district court’s judgment intact.
