Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # The Remaining Fair Use Factors and Why Copyright Claims Failed as a Matter of Law

After holding that Kimmel’s use was transformative, the court turned briefly to the other fair use factors. The important lesson for reading the opinion is that fair use is a holistic inquiry, not a mechanical count of how many factors favor one side. Here, the panel concluded that nothing in the second, third, or fourth factors changed the basic picture already created by the first factor: the JKL segments were commentary on Santos, not a substitute for his videos. Because that conclusion was apparent from the materials properly before the court, fair use could be resolved at the pleading stage.

## The Second Factor: The Nature of the Copyrighted Work

The second factor asks about the character of the work copied. In broad terms, courts give more protection to highly creative works than to factual or informational ones, and they also pay attention to whether the work was published or unpublished. The panel pointed out that the copyright registrations showed Santos’s videos were published on the same day they were created. That detail mattered because it undercut any suggestion that Kimmel had taken unreleased material and interfered with Santos’s control over its first public appearance.

The court did not dwell on this factor, which signals that publication status did not help Santos much. Once the videos were already public, the second factor had less force on his side. The opinion treats that fact as one more reason the fair use defense was not defeated by the nature of the works.

## The Third Factor: Amount and Substantiality of the Portion Used

The panel was similarly unpersuaded by Santos’s objections to the third factor, which concerns how much of the original was taken and whether what was taken was qualitatively important. The court did not write at length on this point, and that brevity is itself telling. Santos’s arguments about the amount used were not enough to alter the overall fair use balance.

For students, the key point is that the third factor does not operate in isolation. Even if a defendant uses a noticeable portion of a work, that does not end the analysis if the use is strongly transformative and does not usurp the original’s market. Here, the court saw no reason to disturb the district court’s weighing of that factor.

## The Fourth Factor: Market Harm and the Difference Between Devaluation and Substitution

The court spent a bit more attention on the fourth factor because Santos’s strongest remaining point was that Kimmel’s broadcast harmed the value of his videos. But the opinion draws an important distinction: copyright law is concerned with market usurpation, not with every kind of injury to reputation or value. A work may be mocked, criticized, or ridiculed in a way that makes it less popular, but that is not the same as a competing substitute that takes the original’s place in the market.

That is why the panel relied on Hachette and Campbell. Hachette explains that the relevant question is not whether the secondary work devalues the first through parody or criticism, but whether it supplants the original by serving as a substitute. Campbell adds that creators ordinarily do not license critical reviews or lampoons of their own works, so those kinds of uses do not create a cognizable lost licensing market. On that logic, Santos’s complaint did not allege market harm in the copyright sense; it alleged embarrassment and criticism, which may be real injuries but are not the kind the fourth factor targets.

## Why Only the Original Videos and the JKL Segments Mattered

The panel also made clear that it needed only the original videos and the JKL segments to decide fair use. Citing Cariou, the court explained that the fair use question can be answered by comparing the copyrighted work and the challenged secondary use themselves. That means the court did not need a wider factual record or discovery into collateral matters to decide whether the use was fair.

This matters procedurally because it shows why dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) was proper. When the complaint, its attachments, and judicially noticeable materials already show that the secondary use is transformative and does not usurp the market for the original, a court may resolve fair use as a matter of law. The Second Circuit concluded that this was such a case.

## The Bottom-Line Holding: Fair Use Barred the Copyright Claims at the Pleading Stage

Putting the factors together, the court held that the remaining fair use considerations did not rescue Santos’s copyright claims. The second factor was weakened by the fact that the videos were already published; the third factor did not undermine the district court’s ruling; and the fourth factor failed because criticism and lampooning are not the same as market substitution. Since the works themselves showed a parody-like commentary use, fair use was sufficiently clear to support dismissal before discovery.

That is why the panel affirmed the judgment on the copyright claims as a matter of law. The opinion’s core message is that a copyright plaintiff cannot turn reputational injury from ridicule into a viable infringement case when the challenged use is transformative commentary and does not function as a market substitute for the original.
