Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # From Cameo Videos to the Second Circuit

## The parties and the posture

This appeal began as an unusual clash between political notoriety and late-night comedy. Former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel, the ABC television network, and Disney, ABC’s parent company, after Kimmel used Santos’s personalized Cameo videos in a recurring segment on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (“JKL”). Santos claimed that the defendants had infringed his copyrights and committed several state-law wrongs. The case was dismissed in the Southern District of New York, and Santos appealed to the Second Circuit.

For a law student, the most important thing to notice at the outset is the procedural posture. The district court dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6), meaning it concluded that Santos had not stated a claim that could proceed even if the factual allegations in the complaint were accepted as true. That posture matters because the appellate court is not reviewing a trial record; it is deciding whether the complaint itself, together with materials properly considered at the pleading stage, supports the claims Santos pleaded.

## How the Cameo videos were created

The dispute centered on the Cameo platform, which the opinion describes as a service through which users can request personalized videos from a speaker. In this case, Kimmel did not submit the requests in his own name. Instead, he used various fictitious names to ask Santos for the videos. Santos fulfilled each request and delivered the recordings.

Those facts were legally important because Santos’s claims depended on the proposition that he had created original expressive works that the defendants later used without permission. The opinion shows that the parties disagreed not just about copying, but about what the videos were for and how they should be understood. Santos’s own allegations described the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the defendants later used them as material for ridicule.

## Kimmel’s broadcast use on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*

Kimmel did not keep the videos private. According to the complaint, ABC aired them on JKL as part of a mocking segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segment repurposed the videos for comedy and commentary, with the apparent aim of highlighting Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money.

That use is what turned a simple recording request into a copyright case. Santos objected that the defendants had taken the videos and republished them on television without authorization. The defendants responded that the use was protected by the fair use doctrine, which can permit copying when the later use is sufficiently transformative or otherwise consistent with copyright’s purposes. The appeal therefore raised the question whether the complaint itself showed that the television segments were lawful fair use.

## Santos’s claims and the district court’s dismissal

Santos’s complaint asserted copyright infringement and several state-law theories, including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. He also argued that the Cameo terms of service imposed obligations on the defendants. The district court rejected the case at the pleading stage. It held that the copyright claims were barred by fair use and that the state-law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim under the relevant state law.

The Second Circuit thus had to decide two sets of issues. First, it had to determine whether the complaint and the incorporated materials showed fair use as a matter of law, which would defeat the copyright claims even before discovery. Second, it had to decide whether any of Santos’s state-law theories could survive, either because they were not preempted or because they adequately pleaded the elements required by state contract and fraud law. The court ultimately affirmed the dismissal on both fronts.
