Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # The Fair Use Framework and the Court’s Focus on Transformative Purpose

## The statutory four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The panel began with the basic structure of fair use. Fair use is an affirmative defense that can excuse conduct that would otherwise look infringing. Congress codified the defense in 17 U.S.C. § 107, which instructs courts to consider four non-exclusive factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and the effect of the use on the market for the original. The opinion emphasizes that fair use is flexible, not mechanical, and that a court may resolve it at the pleading stage when the complaint itself makes the defense clear. That mattered here because the Second Circuit was reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and could rely on the complaint, its exhibits, incorporated documents, and judicially noticeable copyright registrations.

## The policy behind fair use: preventing rigid enforcement that would hinder creativity and commentary

The court framed fair use as a doctrine designed to prevent copyright from being applied so rigidly that it stifles the very creativity copyright is supposed to encourage. That policy explanation is important because it helps explain why the court looked closely at whether Kimmel’s use added something new rather than merely copying Santos’s videos for the same expressive function. The court’s focus was not on whether the videos were copied in a literal sense, but on whether the later use served a different purpose, especially one involving commentary or criticism. In that way, fair use is not simply a loophole; it is part of copyright’s own design.

## The first factor: “purpose and character of the use”

The first factor received the most attention. The court explained that this factor asks about the purpose and character of the secondary use, and that the key question is whether the use is transformative. A transformative use is one that does not simply supersede the original, but instead adds something new, with a different purpose or character. In practice, this means the court looks at how the new work functions in context. If the later use is aimed at criticism, parody, commentary, or some other distinct expressive purpose, that often weighs strongly toward fair use.

The panel also noted that fair use can be evident from the face of the complaint itself. That is what made the first factor especially significant here: the complaint and its attached materials were enough for the court to assess how the videos were being used on Kimmel’s show.

## Transformative use and the distinction between superseding the original and adding a new purpose

Applying that framework, the court held that Kimmel’s use was transformative because the JKL segments used Santos’s Cameo videos to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. That, the court said, was a different purpose from the original videos. The opinion repeatedly returns to the idea that the question is not whether the second work uses the first work, but whether it uses it for a different expressive end.

Santos tried to resist that conclusion by arguing that Kimmel had “instigated” the creation of the original videos and therefore became the designer of their original purpose. The court rejected that argument. Even though Kimmel created the circumstances that led Santos to produce the videos, the relevant comparison is between the message and function of the original videos and the later broadcast segments. On that comparison, the original videos were alleged to convey hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity, while the JKL segments used them as material for mockery and commentary. That difference in message and function was central to the court’s finding of transformation.

## Santos’s argument that Kimmel “instigated” the videos and therefore shared their original purpose

Santos’s “instigation” theory tried to collapse the distinction between creation and later use. In his view, because Kimmel set up the requests, Kimmel should be treated as having shared the original purpose of the videos from the beginning. The court was not persuaded because fair use does not turn on who prompted the creation of the original work or on the user’s subjective motive in obtaining it. What mattered was how a reasonable observer would understand the original videos and the later segments. The court treated the fact that Kimmel solicited the videos as irrelevant to the fair use analysis once the videos themselves were then repurposed for criticism on television.

## The court’s reliance on an objective “reasonable observer” perspective rather than subjective intent

A key move in the opinion is its reliance on an objective perspective. Santos argued that his own intent in making the videos was also to convey absurdity, which he said undermined the claim that Kimmel’s use had a different purpose. The court rejected that line of reasoning and turned to the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision, which cautions that subjective intent does not control the purpose inquiry. In other words, the court does not ask what the copyright holder secretly hoped the work would mean, or what the secondary user says he intended; it asks how the work would be understood by a reasonable observer.

That approach allowed the panel to treat Santos’s own allegations against him. His complaint described the videos as expressing hope and positivity. If that was the work’s apparent original character, then Kimmel’s later use of the videos for ridicule and commentary was meaningfully different. The opinion thus uses the complaint’s own characterization of the videos to show why the first factor favored fair use.

## The role of Warhol in rejecting subjective-purpose arguments

The court’s treatment of Warhol is especially important for understanding the opinion’s logic. Santos tried to argue that the purpose of the secondary use should be assessed in light of the alleged original purpose or the user’s subjective framing. The panel responded by citing Warhol for the proposition that subjective intent does not determine the purpose of the use. That allowed the court to keep the analysis anchored in what the work objectively does in context. In this case, Kimmel’s broadcast segments were not treated as a substitute version of the Cameo videos; they were treated as commentary that used the videos as raw material for criticism.

## Santos’s bad-faith theory based on Kimmel’s false representations

Santos also argued that Kimmel’s false representations in ordering the videos showed bad faith and therefore defeated fair use. The court acknowledged Harper & Row’s statement that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but it said the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that broad proposition. More importantly, the complaint itself showed that the defendants’ purpose was not to supplant Santos’s commercial interest in the videos, but to engage in sarcastic criticism and commentary. That mattered because the fair use inquiry is concerned with whether the secondary use displaces the market for the original, not simply whether the defendant behaved misleadingly in obtaining the original material.

## Why the court treated bad faith as insufficient here, especially where the complaint itself showed criticism rather than market substitution

The court’s response to the bad-faith argument also ties back to the market-substitution idea underlying fair use. Even if Kimmel used deception to obtain the videos, that fact did not, by itself, defeat fair use where the later use was critical commentary rather than a competing substitute. The complaint described a use aimed at ridicule, not one that was trying to replace Santos’s market for personalized videos. In the court’s view, a use that criticizes or lampoons an original work is not the kind of use that fair use seeks to forbid, even if the user’s route to the material involved false pretenses.

## The conclusion that the first factor strongly favored fair use

By the end of the analysis, the court held that the first factor strongly favored fair use. That conclusion drove the rest of the opinion’s copyright discussion, and it was enough—together with the court’s rejection of Santos’s other challenges—to affirm dismissal at the pleading stage. For a law student reading the case, the central lesson is that fair use turns on function and context. Here, the court saw Santos’s videos as original expressive works that were later repurposed not to replace them, but to criticize and mock the very willingness they allegedly displayed. That difference in purpose was enough to carry the first factor decisively in defendants’ favor.
