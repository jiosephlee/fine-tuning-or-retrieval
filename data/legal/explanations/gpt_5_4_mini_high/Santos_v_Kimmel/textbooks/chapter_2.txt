Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Appellate Posture and the Pleading-Stage Fair Use Inquiry

## De novo review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal

The Second Circuit began by identifying the procedural posture: the district court had dismissed Santos’s complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. That matters because appellate review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is de novo, meaning the court of appeals gives no deference to the district court’s legal conclusions. The panel therefore asked the same question the district court asked: taking the relevant materials as true and reading them in the light most favorable to Santos, did the complaint still fail as a matter of law?

That posture is important in a case like this because the court was not deciding whether Santos could ultimately prove his claims after discovery. It was deciding whether the complaint, together with the documents the court could properly consider on a motion to dismiss, already showed that no viable claim existed.

## What counts as part of the record on a motion to dismiss

In applying Rule 12(b)(6), the court did not confine itself to the bare allegations of the complaint. It could also consider documents attached to the complaint as exhibits and documents incorporated by reference. That is a standard feature of motion-to-dismiss practice: a plaintiff cannot avoid dismissal by omitting or selectively describing the very documents that shape the claim.

Here, that meant the court could look not only at Santos’s allegations about the Cameo videos and the Jimmy Kimmel Live! segments, but also at the materials he attached or incorporated. Because the court was evaluating the legal sufficiency of the claims on the pleadings, those materials were part of the operative record for the appellate court as well.

## Judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations

The panel also noted that it could take judicial notice of copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office’s registry. Judicial notice allows a court to recognize the existence and content of certain public records without requiring proof in the ordinary evidentiary sense. The Second Circuit has long permitted notice of copyright registrations at the pleading stage because they are official public records and their authenticity is not seriously in dispute.

This mattered because the registrations supplied additional information relevant to the fair use analysis. The court did not need discovery to know what had been registered, when the works were created, and when they were published. Those dates were part of the public copyright record.

## Why the publication information in the registrations mattered

One detail the panel specifically mentioned was that the registrations showed the videos were published on the same day they were created. That fact matters because publication status is relevant to the second fair use factor, the nature of the copyrighted work. Unpublished works generally receive stronger protection than published works, in part because copyright law is concerned with the author’s right to control first publication.

By pointing to the registration data, the court made clear that Santos could not plausibly rely on any argument that the works were unpublished in a way that would weigh heavily against fair use. The publication information therefore supported the court’s conclusion that the fair use inquiry could be resolved from the pleadings and public records, without factual development.

## Fair use as an affirmative defense under the Copyright Act

Fair use is an affirmative defense, which ordinarily means the defendant bears the burden of proving it. But an affirmative defense does not always require a trial. If the complaint itself and the materials properly before the court establish the defense on their face, dismissal at the pleading stage is appropriate.

The panel reiterated the familiar statutory factors: purpose and character of the use, nature of the copyrighted work, amount used, and effect on the market. The opinion focused especially on the first and fourth factors. The court concluded that the challenged use was transformative because the JKL segments used the videos to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, not to serve the same expressive purpose as Santos’s original videos, which the complaint itself described as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” On that record, the court held that the first factor strongly favored fair use.

The court also rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s deception in obtaining the videos defeated fair use. Even assuming the false names and requests were misleading, the complaint did not plausibly show an intent to usurp Santos’s commercial market for the videos. Instead, the complaint described a satirical and critical use. The panel therefore treated the alleged bad faith as insufficient to overcome the broader fair use analysis.

## When fair use may be decided at the pleading stage

The key procedural lesson from the opinion is that fair use can sometimes be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The Second Circuit said expressly that fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal” at the pleading stage. That is what happened here.

The reason is that fair use often turns on comparing the challenged use with the original work and asking whether the new use adds something different, comments on the original, or substitutes for it in the market. If the complaint itself includes enough about both works, the court may be able to answer those questions without discovery. In Santos, the complaint described the original Cameo videos, described the JKL segments, and attached or incorporated materials that let the court see the context. Those facts were enough for the panel to conclude that the defense was apparent on the face of the pleadings.

## The significance of comparing the original works and the challenged use directly

The opinion underscores that fair use analysis depends on direct comparison. The court cited precedent stating that, for fair use, it may consider the original version of the work and the challenged secondary use at issue. That comparison is central because the question is not whether the defendant used the plaintiff’s work at all, but whether the use had a new purpose or character and whether it competed with the original as a substitute.

Here, the panel compared Santos’s personalized videos with the broadcast segments that repurposed them as material for ridicule. That comparison drove the court’s conclusions on transformativness and market effect. The JKL segments were not presented as replacements for the Cameo videos; they were presented as commentary on them. Because the court could make that comparison from the face of the pleadings and the public copyright records, it affirmed dismissal without needing discovery or trial.
