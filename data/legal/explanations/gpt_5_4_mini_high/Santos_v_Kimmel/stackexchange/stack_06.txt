Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why could the Second Circuit avoid the Copyright Act preemption issue and just affirm on state-law pleading grounds?
Question:
The district court dismissed the contract claims as preempted, but the Second Circuit says it 'need not resolve this issue' because the claims fail under state law anyway. Is that just a general appellate rule that a court can affirm on any ground supported by the record? Or is there some reason preemption should have been decided first? I'm trying to understand the sequencing here.
Answer:
Yes — this is the ordinary appellate rule, and nothing in the opinion suggests preemption had to be decided first.

The Second Circuit was reviewing a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo, so it could affirm on **any ground supported by the record**. That is exactly what it did here. After noting that the district court had dismissed the breach-of-contract and implied-contract claims as preempted, the panel said:

> “We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.”

That sequencing makes sense because **preemption is not jurisdictional**. It is a merits defense. If the plaintiff’s state-law claims are already defective under the governing state law, the court can stop there and avoid deciding whether the Copyright Act would also preempt them.

And the panel had concrete state-law reasons to affirm:

- **Direct breach of contract**: Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service, and under Illinois law the opinion says there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.”
- **Implied contract**: under New York law, the complaint did not plead the essential terms of any implied-in-fact agreement or a plausible meeting of the minds.
- **Fraudulent inducement**: the complaint did not allege the required out-of-pocket loss.

So the court did not need to decide whether the claims were preempted, because even if they were not, they still failed on their own terms. In other words, preemption would have been an additional reason to dismiss, but not a necessary one.

This is a common appellate approach: decide the case on the **narrowest sufficient ground** and avoid unnecessary issues.
