Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What was missing from Santos's implied contract and fraudulent inducement claims under New York law?
Question:
I'm also confused about the state-law merits rulings. For implied-in-fact contract, the court says Santos did not plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would follow the Terms of Service. What sort of allegations would normally be enough to show an implied agreement in this kind of online transaction? And for fraudulent inducement, why wasn't being tricked into making the videos enough to show damages if New York requires an 'out-of-pocket loss'?
Answer:
Under New York law, Santos’s **implied-in-fact contract** and **fraudulent inducement** claims failed because he left out the specific elements those causes of action require.

### 1) Implied-in-fact contract: what was missing?
The Second Circuit said Santos did **not** plausibly allege:

- the **essential terms** of any implied agreement, and
- a **meeting of the minds** that Kimmel would abide by the Cameo Terms of Service.

That is the key point. Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract is still a contract: you need facts showing mutual assent inferred from the parties’ conduct and circumstances. The court cited *Rocky Point Properties* for the rule that intent and surrounding circumstances matter, but then held Santos’s complaint was too thin because it did not plead, “in nonconclusory language,” the contract’s essential terms or any agreement by Kimmel to be bound. It also relied on *Caniglia* and *DG & A Mgmt. Servs.* for those requirements.

So, in a case like this, allegations that are often enough would look like facts showing:

- the user was shown the relevant terms before ordering,
- the platform required assent to those terms,
- Santos knew the user was assenting on behalf of the transaction,
- the parties’ communications or course of dealing showed mutual understanding that the user would comply with those terms, and
- the particular terms allegedly breached were identified with some specificity.

What Santos had, according to the opinion, was basically just the conclusion that the Terms of Service should apply. The court said that was not enough.

### 2) Fraudulent inducement: why was “being tricked” not enough?
Because New York fraud law requires **actual out-of-pocket loss**.

The court cited *Lama Holding* and *Connaughton* for the rule that a fraud plaintiff must allege a **pecuniary loss actually suffered** as a result of the fraud. Being deceived into doing something is not, by itself, enough unless it produced a measurable financial loss.

Here, the court said Santos did **not** allege that he suffered any such out-of-pocket loss. In other words, he did not plead that the alleged deceit caused him to part with money or incur a concrete financial loss. The opinion treats the claim as missing the damages element, not just the deception element.

So the basic distinction is:

- **Deception alone** → not enough for New York fraud.
- **Deception + actual pecuniary loss** → potentially enough.

That is why “I was tricked into making the videos” was insufficient, absent allegations that the trick caused a concrete monetary loss.
