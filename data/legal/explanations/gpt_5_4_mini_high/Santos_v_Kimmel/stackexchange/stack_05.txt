Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the court rely on the copyright registrations to say the videos were published the same day they were created?
Question:
In discussing the other fair use factors, the court says the copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created, and it takes judicial notice of the Copyright Office records. How does that matter for the fair use analysis? Is the unpublished/published distinction still important under factor two, and can a court really treat those registration facts as settled on a motion to dismiss?
Answer:
The court used the copyright registrations for a fairly narrow reason: to show that these were **published works**, not unpublished ones.

That matters because under **fair use factor two**—the “nature of the copyrighted work”—courts pay attention to both:

1. whether the work is **creative** or more factual, and  
2. whether it was **published** or **unpublished**.

The unpublished/published distinction is still relevant because an **unpublished** work usually gets stronger protection, largely because the author has a stronger interest in the **first publication** of the work. If a work is already published, that particular concern carries much less weight.

So when the Second Circuit says the registrations show the videos were “published on the same day they were created,” it is basically saying: **these were not unpublished, private works whose first publication Kimmel was commandeering**. That weakens any argument that factor two should cut heavily against fair use. The court did not say publication alone decides fair use; it just noted that this aspect of factor two did not help Santos much.

As for the procedural point: **yes, at the motion-to-dismiss stage the court can rely on the registration records**. The opinion expressly says the Second Circuit may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry,” citing *Island Software* and *TCA Television*. That means the court can accept the existence and contents of those public records without converting the motion into summary judgment.

So the short answer is:

- **Why it mattered:** publication status is part of factor two, and published works get less protection than unpublished ones.  
- **Why the registrations were enough:** the Copyright Office records are public records the court may judicially notice on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.  
- **What the court did not do:** it did not treat the registrations as conclusive of every factual issue, only as reliable evidence that the videos were published when created.
