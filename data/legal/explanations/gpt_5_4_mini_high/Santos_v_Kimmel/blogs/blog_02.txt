Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# After *Warhol*, Purpose Is Objective: *Santos v. Kimmel* and the Limits of “I Meant It That Way”

## Why this case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is, on its face, a celebrity copyright skirmish. But its real significance is doctrinal: it shows how lower courts are reading *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* in the real world.

The panel held that Jimmy Kimmel’s use of George Santos’s Cameo videos was fair use, even though Kimmel obtained the videos by posing under false names and had, in Santos’s view, effectively “instigated” their creation. The court rejected that framing. What mattered was not who set the trap, or what the plaintiff says he subjectively meant, but what a reasonable observer would understand the original videos and the later *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* segments to be doing. That move has consequences beyond this case. It affects commissioned works, prompted speech, parody, and any dispute where a defendant obtains material through deception and later reuses it for commentary.

## Objective purpose, not subjective spin

The core fair-use holding is straightforward. The court concluded that the first factor—the purpose and character of the use—strongly favored fair use because Kimmel’s segments were transformative: they used the videos to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos tried to avoid that conclusion by arguing that Kimmel had “designed” the original purpose of the videos by soliciting them under false pretenses. But the panel declined to let that characterization control the analysis.

That is the key *Warhol* lesson. The Supreme Court said the relevant inquiry is objective: what is the purpose of the secondary use as a reasonable observer would understand it? The subjective intent of either party does not decide the question. The Second Circuit applied that principle here by pointing out that Santos’s own complaint described the original videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” not as commentary on shamelessness or absurdity. By contrast, the *JKL* segments were plainly mocking commentary. Different purpose, different character, different use.

This matters because it rejects a common litigation move: “the defendant caused the work to exist, so the defendant owns the work’s purpose.” That is not how fair use works after *Warhol*. The court treated purpose as a function of the work’s communicative role in context, not the defendant’s hidden motive in procuring it.

## Deception does not automatically defeat fair use

Santos also argued that Kimmel’s false identities showed bad faith and should nullify fair use. The panel was unmoved. It acknowledged the old line from *Harper & Row* that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but noted that the Supreme Court has since backed away from treating that statement as controlling. More importantly, the complaint itself undercut any claim that the defendants were trying to usurp Santos’s market for the videos. On the face of the pleadings, their object was sarcastic criticism and commentary.

That is a significant signal. It suggests that deception in obtaining a work does not, by itself, defeat the defense where the later use is plainly transformative and non-substitutive. In other words, a sting operation is not automatically an anti-fair-use operation. The court did not bless dishonesty as a matter of ethics or contract law; it simply refused to treat deception as a categorical bar to fair use.

The court’s treatment of the fourth factor reinforces that point. It relied on the familiar rule that parody and criticism do not create cognizable market harm just because they may devalue the original in the eyes of the audience. Fair use asks whether the secondary use usurps the market for the first by serving as a competing substitute, not whether it embarrasses or mocks the first work. That distinction, from *Campbell* through *Google* and *Hachette*, did the work here.

## What this means for commissioned and prompted works

The opinion is especially relevant to commissioned content and other prompted speech. Platforms like Cameo produce personalized expression at the request of a paying customer. That makes disputes over later reuse tricky, because the requester has usually played an active role in bringing the original speech into being. *Santos* says that role does not answer the fair-use question. Even if the defendant engineered the request, the court still asks what the resulting work appears to be for, and whether the later use changes that function.

That is a useful clarification for parody and commentary cases. It means a defendant cannot be stripped of fair use merely because they helped create the raw material they later criticized. A reasonable observer still has to look at the original work’s apparent message and the later work’s actual purpose. If those are different, transformation remains on the table.

At the same time, the decision should not be read too broadly. It does not mean any use of a deceptively obtained work is fair. It matters that the secondary use here was obvious commentary, that the complaint itself conceded the original videos were positive personalized messages, and that the market-harm theory was weak on the face of the pleadings. A closer case—say, one involving a repackaged commissioned work that competes with the original market—may come out differently.

## The bottom line

*Santos v. Kimmel* is a clean application of post-*Warhol* fair-use doctrine. The Second Circuit refused to let Santos recast Kimmel’s deceptive procurement of the videos as proof that the original and secondary works shared the same purpose. Instead, it applied an objective, observer-based inquiry and held that Kimmel’s use was transformative commentary.

The opinion also reminds plaintiffs that fair use can be decided at the pleading stage when the works themselves tell the story. And while Santos’s state-law theories failed for ordinary pleading reasons, the copyright holding is the headline: after *Warhol*, “I meant it that way” is not enough. The question is what the work does, not what the litigant says he was trying to do.
