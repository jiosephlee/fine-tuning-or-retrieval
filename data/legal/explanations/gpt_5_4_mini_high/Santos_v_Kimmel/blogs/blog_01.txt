Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: Why *Santos v. Kimmel* Matters Beyond Late-Night TV

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is about more than a former congressman, a late-night comedian, and a set of Cameo videos turned into punchlines. The real significance of the case is procedural: the court again confirmed that fair use can be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint, the registered works, and the challenged use make the answer clear. That matters because it shortens the runway for copyright plaintiffs who hope to use discovery to test a fair-use defense, and it reinforces a Second Circuit trend toward early dismissal where transformation and lack of substitution are apparent from the face of the pleadings.

## The setup: criticism dressed as a copyright case

The facts were tailor-made for a fair-use fight. According to the complaint, Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, ordered personalized videos from Santos through Cameo and then aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a recurring segment mocking Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos sued for copyright infringement and several state-law claims. Judge Denise Cote dismissed at the pleading stage, and Judge Lohier’s panel affirmed.

The court’s copyright analysis is notable for how little it needed beyond the pleaded facts. Under Second Circuit law, fair use is an affirmative defense, but it can support dismissal if it is “so clearly established by a complaint” that there is nothing left to litigate. That is exactly how the panel treated this case.

## Transformative use, viewed objectively

The heart of the opinion is the first fair-use factor: purpose and character of the use. Santos argued that Kimmel could not claim transformation because he had “instigated the creation” of the videos in the first place. The panel rejected that framing and, consistent with *Warhol*, asked what a reasonable observer would understand the original videos to be and what the later use was doing with them.

That was fatal to Santos’s claim. His own complaint alleged that the Cameo videos conveyed hope, encouragement, and positivity. The JKL segments, by contrast, were aimed at mocking Santos’s readiness to utter absurd things for money. Those are different purposes, and the court treated the discrepancy as obvious enough to decide on the pleadings. The panel also brushed aside Santos’s bad-faith argument, citing the Supreme Court’s later reluctance to give Harper & Row’s “good faith and fair dealing” language much independent force. More important, the complaint itself portrayed the defendants as engaged in commentary and criticism, not as trying to appropriate the economic value of Santos’s videos.

That is a meaningful doctrinal point. The Second Circuit is not saying that every use in a comedic segment is fair, or that bad behavior by a user is irrelevant in all cases. But it is signaling that where the pleaded facts show satire, criticism, or commentary on the plaintiff’s work or persona, the first factor can be resolved without discovery.

## Why the rest of the fair-use analysis fell away

The court gave the remaining factors comparatively short shrift, but the reasoning tracks the Second Circuit’s recent fair-use cases. The videos were published the same day they were created, so the second factor did not help Santos much. And on market harm, the panel emphasized the familiar distinction between harm from criticism and harm from substitution. A parody or lampoon may reduce a work’s value, but that is not the same thing as usurping its market.

That distinction has been central since *Campbell*, and the Second Circuit leaned on it here. The court cited *Hachette* and *Swatch* for the proposition that the relevant question is whether the secondary use serves as a competing substitute, not whether it makes the original less attractive. On the facts alleged, the answer was plainly no. A mocking late-night segment is not a market replacement for a personalized Cameo video.

The panel also reiterated a point that matters for motion practice: when fair use is apparent, courts may consider only the original work and the secondary use itself. That is one reason *Cariou* remains so important in the Second Circuit. If the visual or textual comparison tells the story, plaintiffs may not get a chance to develop a fuller record.

## The doctrinal takeaway: pleading-stage fair use is now a real feature, not a rarity

*Santos* fits comfortably within a line of Second Circuit cases that make early fair-use dismissal possible when the pleadings are doing too much of the work for the plaintiff. *TCA Television* recognized that fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss. *Cariou* emphasized the side-by-side comparison. *Hachette* and *Swatch* reinforced the idea that commentary, criticism, and non-substitutive uses can support fair use even when they affect the plaintiff’s market in the colloquial sense. *Santos* does not break new ground so much as it shows that the court is willing to apply that framework even in a pop-culture dispute with a built-in adversarial narrative.

For copyright plaintiffs, the lesson is simple: if the pleaded facts themselves show a transformative, critical use that does not compete with the original market, discovery may never arrive. Conclusory allegations about market harm will not save a complaint when the challenged use is visibly a joke at the plaintiff’s expense. And if the plaintiff’s own allegations describe the original works and the secondary use in a way that highlights their different purposes, the first fair-use factor may be dispositive.

## A narrower holding, with broad practical consequences

Still, *Santos* should not be read as a universal invitation to decide fair use on the pleadings. The opinion is careful to anchor itself in the complaint and the registered works, and the court’s confidence rests on the unusually stark contrast between personalized motivational videos and a televised mockery segment. In closer cases—especially where the secondary use is less obviously critical, or where market substitution is plausibly alleged—discovery may still be necessary.

But that is precisely why the decision matters. It shows that plaintiffs cannot assume copyright claims will survive long enough to reach a jury merely because fair use is fact-intensive in the abstract. In the Second Circuit, when the complaint itself supplies the fair-use answer, courts are increasingly willing to say so early. For defendants, that is a powerful tool. For plaintiffs, it is a pleading lesson: if the theory depends on defeating fair use, the complaint has to do more than recite infringement and hope for discovery.
