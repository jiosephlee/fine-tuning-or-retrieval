Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# No Cognizable Market for Lampooning: The Fourth Factor in *Santos v. Kimmel*

## Why the case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is, at one level, a familiar late-night comedy dispute. But the court’s fair-use analysis matters far beyond the punchline. The panel held that Jimmy Kimmel’s use of George Santos’s Cameo videos in a mocking segment on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* was fair use, and it did so at the pleading stage. That combination makes the case a useful reminder that copyright law does not give a rights holder control over criticism, ridicule, or commentary simply because those uses may be embarrassing, demeaning, or commercially unwelcome.

The doctrinal centerpiece is the fourth fair-use factor: market harm. Santos’s theory was not that Kimmel’s segments competed with his Cameo business in the ordinary sense, but that Kimmel had used the videos in a way that undermined their value and Santos’s ability to monetize them. The Second Circuit rejected that move in terms that track the Supreme Court’s fair-use cases and the court’s own recent precedent: harm from parody or criticism is not the same as usurpation of a market.

## The court’s market-harm analysis

The panel’s framing is important. It quoted *Hachette* for the proposition that the relevant question is not whether the secondary use “damage[s] the market for the first” by “devaluing it through parody or criticism,” but whether it “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” That distinction did most of the work here.

On Santos’s own allegations, the Cameo videos were personalized messages that conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Kimmel’s use, by contrast, was commentary on Santos’s willingness to “say absurd things for money.” Those are different markets, different purposes, and different audiences. A consumer buying a Cameo video from Santos is not looking for a late-night comedy segment. And a viewer watching *Will Santos Say It?* is not shopping for a birthday message or a pep talk.

That is why the court was unconvinced by the claim of market harm. The injury Santos described was real in a colloquial sense: being mocked can reduce the perceived value of one’s persona and one’s content. But copyright’s fourth factor is not a reputational tort in disguise. It asks whether the secondary use substitutes for the original in the marketplace. Here, the answer was no.

The court also relied on *Campbell*’s familiar observation that copyright owners are unlikely to license “critical reviews or lampoons” of their own works, which means those uses do not ordinarily belong to a cognizable licensing market. That point is easy to miss but doctrinally crucial. If a copyright owner could define a “market” for criticism by refusing to license criticism, then fair use would be largely self-defeating. Every parody, review, or satire would become actionable simply because the rights holder would prefer to be paid for the privilege of being mocked.

## Why criticism is not a licensing market

This is the real significance of the opinion. Santos’s complaint tried to recast a familiar fair-use reality as market harm: if Kimmel could air the videos without permission, then Santos supposedly lost control over a revenue stream. But the law does not recognize a right to be paid for criticism. Nor does it permit a copyright owner to manufacture a licensing market by declaring that any negative use must be licensed.

That would invert the structure of fair use. The doctrine exists precisely to prevent copyright from swallowing commentary, criticism, and parody—the very uses that often depend on quoting or reproducing the original work. The Second Circuit’s analysis keeps that line intact. The value lost to mockery is not the kind of market harm that counts under factor four; otherwise, the more effective the satire, the stronger the infringement claim. That is not copyright law.

The court’s treatment of the amount used also fits that logic. Although Kimmel aired the full Cameo videos, the panel saw no problem with full use in context because the point of the segment was to comment on the videos themselves. In a commentary case, copying the whole work can be reasonable if the use needs the whole work to make the point. The Fourth Circuit’s reasoning in *Campbell* and the Second Circuit’s in *Cariou* both point in that direction: the amount used matters, but it does not override a clearly transformative, non-substitutive use.

## Downstream effects

*Santos* should be read as another strong signal that the Second Circuit will not let plaintiffs turn embarrassment into copyright leverage. That matters in an ecosystem where creators monetize short-form videos, personalized messages, livestreams, and other highly contextual works that can be republished or excerpted in commentary. The opinion suggests that when the complaint itself shows a secondary use aimed at ridicule or criticism, fair use may be resolved early—without waiting for discovery—if the pleaded facts make clear there is no plausible substitute market.

The broader doctrinal takeaway is straightforward: copyright protects expressive markets, not immunity from lampooning. *Santos v. Kimmel* reaffirms that criticism may devalue a work, a persona, or a political brand, but that is not the same thing as market usurpation. For the fourth factor, that distinction is everything.
