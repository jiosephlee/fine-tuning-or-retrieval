Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# When Platform Rules Don’t Travel: Third-Party Beneficiaries, Implied Contracts, and Fraud After *Santos v. Kimmel*

## Why the state-law ruling matters

The headline in *Santos v. Kimmel* was copyright, but the part that should interest lawyers who live in the platform economy is the Second Circuit’s treatment of the state-law claims. Former Congressman George Santos tried to turn a prank on Cameo into a contract-and-fraud case, arguing that the platform’s terms gave him enforceable rights against the people who bought the videos and later aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*. The panel said no, and in doing so it drew a fairly sharp line around what platform rules can do.

That line matters well beyond celebrity disputes. Creators, performers, and rightsholders increasingly assume that terms of service, platform policies, or checkout-page language can be repurposed as downstream control rights. *Santos* is a reminder that those terms do not travel automatically. If you are not a party to the contract, or if the pleading does not identify a real contract at all, ordinary state-law doctrine will do the rest.

## No third-party beneficiary status without real textual support

The easiest claim for Santos to lose was the direct breach-of-contract theory under Cameo’s Terms of Service. He was not a party to the agreement he said was breached, so he had to show he was an intended third-party beneficiary. Under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, that is a steep hill: there is a strong presumption against conferring rights on noncontracting parties, and the intent to do so must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”

The Second Circuit found nothing close to that. Santos pointed to the terms generally, but the panel emphasized that other provisions in the same Terms of Service expressly referred to third parties. That detail cut against him. It showed that when the drafters wanted to confer rights beyond the immediate signatories, they knew how to say so. The absence of comparable language in the provision Santos invoked mattered.

This is a useful doctrinal point. Courts are reluctant to infer third-party enforcement rights from the mere existence of platform rules. A service agreement is usually a private bargain between the platform and its users, not a universal charter enforceable by anyone who later dislikes what a user did with the product. If a creator wants third-party beneficiary status, the contract has to say so with real clarity.

## The implied-contract theory failed for a more basic reason: there was no pleaded contract

Santos’s implied-contract claim was governed by New York law, and here the court’s problem was not just standing but pleading. New York will recognize implied-in-fact contracts, but only where the complaint plausibly alleges mutual assent, the surrounding circumstances, and, crucially, the essential terms of the agreement. The panel quoted the familiar rule that a plaintiff must plead the specific provisions on which liability is based, not just an abstract sense that the other side should have behaved differently.

Santos did not do that. The complaint did not identify the essential terms of the alleged implied contract, and it did not plausibly allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service in the way Santos claimed. In other words, the theory collapsed because it was really an expectation of fair dealing dressed up as contract.

That distinction matters. An implied-in-fact contract is not a general morality principle, and it is not a backdoor way to convert disappointment into liability. The Second Circuit’s analysis is a reminder that contract doctrine still requires the basics: assent, terms, and a coherent bargain.

## Fraudulent inducement needs actual out-of-pocket loss

The fraud claim fared no better. Under New York law, fraudulent inducement requires out-of-pocket loss. The panel agreed with the district court that Santos did not allege that kind of actual pecuniary injury.

That holding is unglamorous, but it is important. New York fraud law is not a catchall for every deceptive act that produces embarrassment, frustration, or a feeling of being played. It compensates actual economic loss. Without a concrete loss of money or property, the claim does not get off the ground.

For future plaintiffs, that is the key lesson. If the alleged wrong is that you were tricked into doing something, you still have to plead what you lost. Absent that, fraud doctrine will not rescue a weak contract case.

## The panel’s decision not to reach preemption is itself telling

The district court had also held that Santos’s contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, but the Second Circuit did not go there. It affirmed on the simpler ground that the claims failed on ordinary state-law grounds, and left preemption unresolved.

That was a restrained move, and a sensible one. Preemption doctrine can be sticky, especially when plaintiffs frame disputes over content use as contract claims. By sidestepping the issue, the panel avoided making broader statements about when a state-law claim is “qualitatively different” from copyright. It also kept the opinion narrow: the state-law claims failed not because contract claims are always displaced, but because these particular claims were not adequately pleaded and, in the case of the fraud count, lacked a legally sufficient damages allegation.

The practical takeaway is straightforward. Platform terms are not magic. If a creator expects them to control downstream uses, the creator needs actual contractual leverage: clear beneficiary language, a plausible theory of assent, and a damages theory that fits the governing state law. Without those pieces, the terms may bind the platform and its users, but they will not automatically become a weapon in the creator’s hands against a later critic or broadcaster.

## Bottom line

*Santos v. Kimmel* is best read as a cleanup opinion on the state-law side of a broader copyright fight. But the cleanup is the lesson. The Second Circuit treated Santos’s contract and fraud theories as what they were: attempts to convert platform disappointment into enforceable legal rights. Illinois third-party-beneficiary law, New York implied-contract pleading rules, and New York’s out-of-pocket-loss requirement all stood in the way.

For creators and lawyers who work with platformed content, the message is plain. Rules inside the platform ecosystem do not automatically travel outward. If you want downstream control, you need more than a Terms of Service and a grievance. You need a contract that actually reaches the person you want to bind.
