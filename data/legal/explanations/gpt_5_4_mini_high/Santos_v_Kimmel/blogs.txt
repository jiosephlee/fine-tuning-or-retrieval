Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: Why *Santos v. Kimmel* Matters Beyond Late-Night TV

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is about more than a former congressman, a late-night comedian, and a set of Cameo videos turned into punchlines. The real significance of the case is procedural: the court again confirmed that fair use can be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint, the registered works, and the challenged use make the answer clear. That matters because it shortens the runway for copyright plaintiffs who hope to use discovery to test a fair-use defense, and it reinforces a Second Circuit trend toward early dismissal where transformation and lack of substitution are apparent from the face of the pleadings.

## The setup: criticism dressed as a copyright case

The facts were tailor-made for a fair-use fight. According to the complaint, Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, ordered personalized videos from Santos through Cameo and then aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a recurring segment mocking Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos sued for copyright infringement and several state-law claims. Judge Denise Cote dismissed at the pleading stage, and Judge Lohier’s panel affirmed.

The court’s copyright analysis is notable for how little it needed beyond the pleaded facts. Under Second Circuit law, fair use is an affirmative defense, but it can support dismissal if it is “so clearly established by a complaint” that there is nothing left to litigate. That is exactly how the panel treated this case.

## Transformative use, viewed objectively

The heart of the opinion is the first fair-use factor: purpose and character of the use. Santos argued that Kimmel could not claim transformation because he had “instigated the creation” of the videos in the first place. The panel rejected that framing and, consistent with *Warhol*, asked what a reasonable observer would understand the original videos to be and what the later use was doing with them.

That was fatal to Santos’s claim. His own complaint alleged that the Cameo videos conveyed hope, encouragement, and positivity. The JKL segments, by contrast, were aimed at mocking Santos’s readiness to utter absurd things for money. Those are different purposes, and the court treated the discrepancy as obvious enough to decide on the pleadings. The panel also brushed aside Santos’s bad-faith argument, citing the Supreme Court’s later reluctance to give Harper & Row’s “good faith and fair dealing” language much independent force. More important, the complaint itself portrayed the defendants as engaged in commentary and criticism, not as trying to appropriate the economic value of Santos’s videos.

That is a meaningful doctrinal point. The Second Circuit is not saying that every use in a comedic segment is fair, or that bad behavior by a user is irrelevant in all cases. But it is signaling that where the pleaded facts show satire, criticism, or commentary on the plaintiff’s work or persona, the first factor can be resolved without discovery.

## Why the rest of the fair-use analysis fell away

The court gave the remaining factors comparatively short shrift, but the reasoning tracks the Second Circuit’s recent fair-use cases. The videos were published the same day they were created, so the second factor did not help Santos much. And on market harm, the panel emphasized the familiar distinction between harm from criticism and harm from substitution. A parody or lampoon may reduce a work’s value, but that is not the same thing as usurping its market.

That distinction has been central since *Campbell*, and the Second Circuit leaned on it here. The court cited *Hachette* and *Swatch* for the proposition that the relevant question is whether the secondary use serves as a competing substitute, not whether it makes the original less attractive. On the facts alleged, the answer was plainly no. A mocking late-night segment is not a market replacement for a personalized Cameo video.

The panel also reiterated a point that matters for motion practice: when fair use is apparent, courts may consider only the original work and the secondary use itself. That is one reason *Cariou* remains so important in the Second Circuit. If the visual or textual comparison tells the story, plaintiffs may not get a chance to develop a fuller record.

## The doctrinal takeaway: pleading-stage fair use is now a real feature, not a rarity

*Santos* fits comfortably within a line of Second Circuit cases that make early fair-use dismissal possible when the pleadings are doing too much of the work for the plaintiff. *TCA Television* recognized that fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss. *Cariou* emphasized the side-by-side comparison. *Hachette* and *Swatch* reinforced the idea that commentary, criticism, and non-substitutive uses can support fair use even when they affect the plaintiff’s market in the colloquial sense. *Santos* does not break new ground so much as it shows that the court is willing to apply that framework even in a pop-culture dispute with a built-in adversarial narrative.

For copyright plaintiffs, the lesson is simple: if the pleaded facts themselves show a transformative, critical use that does not compete with the original market, discovery may never arrive. Conclusory allegations about market harm will not save a complaint when the challenged use is visibly a joke at the plaintiff’s expense. And if the plaintiff’s own allegations describe the original works and the secondary use in a way that highlights their different purposes, the first fair-use factor may be dispositive.

## A narrower holding, with broad practical consequences

Still, *Santos* should not be read as a universal invitation to decide fair use on the pleadings. The opinion is careful to anchor itself in the complaint and the registered works, and the court’s confidence rests on the unusually stark contrast between personalized motivational videos and a televised mockery segment. In closer cases—especially where the secondary use is less obviously critical, or where market substitution is plausibly alleged—discovery may still be necessary.

But that is precisely why the decision matters. It shows that plaintiffs cannot assume copyright claims will survive long enough to reach a jury merely because fair use is fact-intensive in the abstract. In the Second Circuit, when the complaint itself supplies the fair-use answer, courts are increasingly willing to say so early. For defendants, that is a powerful tool. For plaintiffs, it is a pleading lesson: if the theory depends on defeating fair use, the complaint has to do more than recite infringement and hope for discovery.



# After *Warhol*, Purpose Is Objective: *Santos v. Kimmel* and the Limits of “I Meant It That Way”

## Why this case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is, on its face, a celebrity copyright skirmish. But its real significance is doctrinal: it shows how lower courts are reading *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* in the real world.

The panel held that Jimmy Kimmel’s use of George Santos’s Cameo videos was fair use, even though Kimmel obtained the videos by posing under false names and had, in Santos’s view, effectively “instigated” their creation. The court rejected that framing. What mattered was not who set the trap, or what the plaintiff says he subjectively meant, but what a reasonable observer would understand the original videos and the later *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* segments to be doing. That move has consequences beyond this case. It affects commissioned works, prompted speech, parody, and any dispute where a defendant obtains material through deception and later reuses it for commentary.

## Objective purpose, not subjective spin

The core fair-use holding is straightforward. The court concluded that the first factor—the purpose and character of the use—strongly favored fair use because Kimmel’s segments were transformative: they used the videos to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos tried to avoid that conclusion by arguing that Kimmel had “designed” the original purpose of the videos by soliciting them under false pretenses. But the panel declined to let that characterization control the analysis.

That is the key *Warhol* lesson. The Supreme Court said the relevant inquiry is objective: what is the purpose of the secondary use as a reasonable observer would understand it? The subjective intent of either party does not decide the question. The Second Circuit applied that principle here by pointing out that Santos’s own complaint described the original videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” not as commentary on shamelessness or absurdity. By contrast, the *JKL* segments were plainly mocking commentary. Different purpose, different character, different use.

This matters because it rejects a common litigation move: “the defendant caused the work to exist, so the defendant owns the work’s purpose.” That is not how fair use works after *Warhol*. The court treated purpose as a function of the work’s communicative role in context, not the defendant’s hidden motive in procuring it.

## Deception does not automatically defeat fair use

Santos also argued that Kimmel’s false identities showed bad faith and should nullify fair use. The panel was unmoved. It acknowledged the old line from *Harper & Row* that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but noted that the Supreme Court has since backed away from treating that statement as controlling. More importantly, the complaint itself undercut any claim that the defendants were trying to usurp Santos’s market for the videos. On the face of the pleadings, their object was sarcastic criticism and commentary.

That is a significant signal. It suggests that deception in obtaining a work does not, by itself, defeat the defense where the later use is plainly transformative and non-substitutive. In other words, a sting operation is not automatically an anti-fair-use operation. The court did not bless dishonesty as a matter of ethics or contract law; it simply refused to treat deception as a categorical bar to fair use.

The court’s treatment of the fourth factor reinforces that point. It relied on the familiar rule that parody and criticism do not create cognizable market harm just because they may devalue the original in the eyes of the audience. Fair use asks whether the secondary use usurps the market for the first by serving as a competing substitute, not whether it embarrasses or mocks the first work. That distinction, from *Campbell* through *Google* and *Hachette*, did the work here.

## What this means for commissioned and prompted works

The opinion is especially relevant to commissioned content and other prompted speech. Platforms like Cameo produce personalized expression at the request of a paying customer. That makes disputes over later reuse tricky, because the requester has usually played an active role in bringing the original speech into being. *Santos* says that role does not answer the fair-use question. Even if the defendant engineered the request, the court still asks what the resulting work appears to be for, and whether the later use changes that function.

That is a useful clarification for parody and commentary cases. It means a defendant cannot be stripped of fair use merely because they helped create the raw material they later criticized. A reasonable observer still has to look at the original work’s apparent message and the later work’s actual purpose. If those are different, transformation remains on the table.

At the same time, the decision should not be read too broadly. It does not mean any use of a deceptively obtained work is fair. It matters that the secondary use here was obvious commentary, that the complaint itself conceded the original videos were positive personalized messages, and that the market-harm theory was weak on the face of the pleadings. A closer case—say, one involving a repackaged commissioned work that competes with the original market—may come out differently.

## The bottom line

*Santos v. Kimmel* is a clean application of post-*Warhol* fair-use doctrine. The Second Circuit refused to let Santos recast Kimmel’s deceptive procurement of the videos as proof that the original and secondary works shared the same purpose. Instead, it applied an objective, observer-based inquiry and held that Kimmel’s use was transformative commentary.

The opinion also reminds plaintiffs that fair use can be decided at the pleading stage when the works themselves tell the story. And while Santos’s state-law theories failed for ordinary pleading reasons, the copyright holding is the headline: after *Warhol*, “I meant it that way” is not enough. The question is what the work does, not what the litigant says he was trying to do.



# No Cognizable Market for Lampooning: The Fourth Factor in *Santos v. Kimmel*

## Why the case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is, at one level, a familiar late-night comedy dispute. But the court’s fair-use analysis matters far beyond the punchline. The panel held that Jimmy Kimmel’s use of George Santos’s Cameo videos in a mocking segment on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* was fair use, and it did so at the pleading stage. That combination makes the case a useful reminder that copyright law does not give a rights holder control over criticism, ridicule, or commentary simply because those uses may be embarrassing, demeaning, or commercially unwelcome.

The doctrinal centerpiece is the fourth fair-use factor: market harm. Santos’s theory was not that Kimmel’s segments competed with his Cameo business in the ordinary sense, but that Kimmel had used the videos in a way that undermined their value and Santos’s ability to monetize them. The Second Circuit rejected that move in terms that track the Supreme Court’s fair-use cases and the court’s own recent precedent: harm from parody or criticism is not the same as usurpation of a market.

## The court’s market-harm analysis

The panel’s framing is important. It quoted *Hachette* for the proposition that the relevant question is not whether the secondary use “damage[s] the market for the first” by “devaluing it through parody or criticism,” but whether it “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” That distinction did most of the work here.

On Santos’s own allegations, the Cameo videos were personalized messages that conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Kimmel’s use, by contrast, was commentary on Santos’s willingness to “say absurd things for money.” Those are different markets, different purposes, and different audiences. A consumer buying a Cameo video from Santos is not looking for a late-night comedy segment. And a viewer watching *Will Santos Say It?* is not shopping for a birthday message or a pep talk.

That is why the court was unconvinced by the claim of market harm. The injury Santos described was real in a colloquial sense: being mocked can reduce the perceived value of one’s persona and one’s content. But copyright’s fourth factor is not a reputational tort in disguise. It asks whether the secondary use substitutes for the original in the marketplace. Here, the answer was no.

The court also relied on *Campbell*’s familiar observation that copyright owners are unlikely to license “critical reviews or lampoons” of their own works, which means those uses do not ordinarily belong to a cognizable licensing market. That point is easy to miss but doctrinally crucial. If a copyright owner could define a “market” for criticism by refusing to license criticism, then fair use would be largely self-defeating. Every parody, review, or satire would become actionable simply because the rights holder would prefer to be paid for the privilege of being mocked.

## Why criticism is not a licensing market

This is the real significance of the opinion. Santos’s complaint tried to recast a familiar fair-use reality as market harm: if Kimmel could air the videos without permission, then Santos supposedly lost control over a revenue stream. But the law does not recognize a right to be paid for criticism. Nor does it permit a copyright owner to manufacture a licensing market by declaring that any negative use must be licensed.

That would invert the structure of fair use. The doctrine exists precisely to prevent copyright from swallowing commentary, criticism, and parody—the very uses that often depend on quoting or reproducing the original work. The Second Circuit’s analysis keeps that line intact. The value lost to mockery is not the kind of market harm that counts under factor four; otherwise, the more effective the satire, the stronger the infringement claim. That is not copyright law.

The court’s treatment of the amount used also fits that logic. Although Kimmel aired the full Cameo videos, the panel saw no problem with full use in context because the point of the segment was to comment on the videos themselves. In a commentary case, copying the whole work can be reasonable if the use needs the whole work to make the point. The Fourth Circuit’s reasoning in *Campbell* and the Second Circuit’s in *Cariou* both point in that direction: the amount used matters, but it does not override a clearly transformative, non-substitutive use.

## Downstream effects

*Santos* should be read as another strong signal that the Second Circuit will not let plaintiffs turn embarrassment into copyright leverage. That matters in an ecosystem where creators monetize short-form videos, personalized messages, livestreams, and other highly contextual works that can be republished or excerpted in commentary. The opinion suggests that when the complaint itself shows a secondary use aimed at ridicule or criticism, fair use may be resolved early—without waiting for discovery—if the pleaded facts make clear there is no plausible substitute market.

The broader doctrinal takeaway is straightforward: copyright protects expressive markets, not immunity from lampooning. *Santos v. Kimmel* reaffirms that criticism may devalue a work, a persona, or a political brand, but that is not the same thing as market usurpation. For the fourth factor, that distinction is everything.



# When Platform Rules Don’t Travel: Third-Party Beneficiaries, Implied Contracts, and Fraud After *Santos v. Kimmel*

## Why the state-law ruling matters

The headline in *Santos v. Kimmel* was copyright, but the part that should interest lawyers who live in the platform economy is the Second Circuit’s treatment of the state-law claims. Former Congressman George Santos tried to turn a prank on Cameo into a contract-and-fraud case, arguing that the platform’s terms gave him enforceable rights against the people who bought the videos and later aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*. The panel said no, and in doing so it drew a fairly sharp line around what platform rules can do.

That line matters well beyond celebrity disputes. Creators, performers, and rightsholders increasingly assume that terms of service, platform policies, or checkout-page language can be repurposed as downstream control rights. *Santos* is a reminder that those terms do not travel automatically. If you are not a party to the contract, or if the pleading does not identify a real contract at all, ordinary state-law doctrine will do the rest.

## No third-party beneficiary status without real textual support

The easiest claim for Santos to lose was the direct breach-of-contract theory under Cameo’s Terms of Service. He was not a party to the agreement he said was breached, so he had to show he was an intended third-party beneficiary. Under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, that is a steep hill: there is a strong presumption against conferring rights on noncontracting parties, and the intent to do so must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”

The Second Circuit found nothing close to that. Santos pointed to the terms generally, but the panel emphasized that other provisions in the same Terms of Service expressly referred to third parties. That detail cut against him. It showed that when the drafters wanted to confer rights beyond the immediate signatories, they knew how to say so. The absence of comparable language in the provision Santos invoked mattered.

This is a useful doctrinal point. Courts are reluctant to infer third-party enforcement rights from the mere existence of platform rules. A service agreement is usually a private bargain between the platform and its users, not a universal charter enforceable by anyone who later dislikes what a user did with the product. If a creator wants third-party beneficiary status, the contract has to say so with real clarity.

## The implied-contract theory failed for a more basic reason: there was no pleaded contract

Santos’s implied-contract claim was governed by New York law, and here the court’s problem was not just standing but pleading. New York will recognize implied-in-fact contracts, but only where the complaint plausibly alleges mutual assent, the surrounding circumstances, and, crucially, the essential terms of the agreement. The panel quoted the familiar rule that a plaintiff must plead the specific provisions on which liability is based, not just an abstract sense that the other side should have behaved differently.

Santos did not do that. The complaint did not identify the essential terms of the alleged implied contract, and it did not plausibly allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service in the way Santos claimed. In other words, the theory collapsed because it was really an expectation of fair dealing dressed up as contract.

That distinction matters. An implied-in-fact contract is not a general morality principle, and it is not a backdoor way to convert disappointment into liability. The Second Circuit’s analysis is a reminder that contract doctrine still requires the basics: assent, terms, and a coherent bargain.

## Fraudulent inducement needs actual out-of-pocket loss

The fraud claim fared no better. Under New York law, fraudulent inducement requires out-of-pocket loss. The panel agreed with the district court that Santos did not allege that kind of actual pecuniary injury.

That holding is unglamorous, but it is important. New York fraud law is not a catchall for every deceptive act that produces embarrassment, frustration, or a feeling of being played. It compensates actual economic loss. Without a concrete loss of money or property, the claim does not get off the ground.

For future plaintiffs, that is the key lesson. If the alleged wrong is that you were tricked into doing something, you still have to plead what you lost. Absent that, fraud doctrine will not rescue a weak contract case.

## The panel’s decision not to reach preemption is itself telling

The district court had also held that Santos’s contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, but the Second Circuit did not go there. It affirmed on the simpler ground that the claims failed on ordinary state-law grounds, and left preemption unresolved.

That was a restrained move, and a sensible one. Preemption doctrine can be sticky, especially when plaintiffs frame disputes over content use as contract claims. By sidestepping the issue, the panel avoided making broader statements about when a state-law claim is “qualitatively different” from copyright. It also kept the opinion narrow: the state-law claims failed not because contract claims are always displaced, but because these particular claims were not adequately pleaded and, in the case of the fraud count, lacked a legally sufficient damages allegation.

The practical takeaway is straightforward. Platform terms are not magic. If a creator expects them to control downstream uses, the creator needs actual contractual leverage: clear beneficiary language, a plausible theory of assent, and a damages theory that fits the governing state law. Without those pieces, the terms may bind the platform and its users, but they will not automatically become a weapon in the creator’s hands against a later critic or broadcaster.

## Bottom line

*Santos v. Kimmel* is best read as a cleanup opinion on the state-law side of a broader copyright fight. But the cleanup is the lesson. The Second Circuit treated Santos’s contract and fraud theories as what they were: attempts to convert platform disappointment into enforceable legal rights. Illinois third-party-beneficiary law, New York implied-contract pleading rules, and New York’s out-of-pocket-loss requirement all stood in the way.

For creators and lawyers who work with platformed content, the message is plain. Rules inside the platform ecosystem do not automatically travel outward. If you want downstream control, you need more than a Terms of Service and a grievance. You need a contract that actually reaches the person you want to bind.
