Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Did the officer 'cracking the door' and calling into the house before consent already amount to a Fourth Amendment entry or search?
Question:
One factual detail threw me off: after Lisa said she needed to get her shoes and did not come back outside for a while, Officer Kizer 'cracked the door' and called into the house asking if she was okay. The opinion then focuses on the later consent as the justification for entry. But wasn't opening the door at least arguably a physical intrusion into the home before consent was given? If so, why isn't that analyzed as a separate Fourth Amendment issue? Is it because the defendant did not press that point, because it was too minimal to count as an entry, or because officer-safety/community-caretaking concerns would justify it?
Answer:
No — not in the way the Seventh Circuit framed this case.

The opinion treats the **constitutionally relevant event** as the officers’ **actual entry into the house**, which happened only **after** Lisa said “Okay” in response to Officer Kizer’s request to come inside. The earlier moment when Kizer “cracked the door” and called in asking whether Lisa was okay is described as a brief welfare check after she had gone inside and did not come back out. The court does **not** describe that as a threshold-crossing entry, and it never analyzes it as a separate search.

That fits the way Coleman litigated the suppression issue. As the opinion states, his argument was that **Lisa never validly consented to the officers entering in the first place**, and that once inside they allegedly exceeded the scope of that consent. The panel rejected both points. Once it found valid consent, it said there was **no need** to reach the government’s alternative theories of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery.

So the short answer is:

- the court did **not** view the brief “cracked the door” moment as the operative Fourth Amendment entry/search;
- Coleman apparently did **not** present it as a separate suppression theory; and
- because the later **consensual entry** resolved the case, the panel had no reason to stop and decide whether that earlier door-opening, by itself, was independently problematic.

If you want to be precise, the opinion leaves the “cracked the door” moment as a background fact, not the legal basis for suppression.
