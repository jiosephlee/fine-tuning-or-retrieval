Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # The Domestic Violence Call and the Factual Record

## The 911 Report and the Risk Facing the Officers

The case began with a 911 call in the early morning hours of April 20, 2023. The report was serious: Jaison Coleman had allegedly threatened to kill Lisa Coleman and was still inside the home. That kind of call immediately places responding officers in a difficult position. They have to decide quickly how to protect possible victims, whether children are present, and whether a violent confrontation may still be unfolding inside the house. The opinion opens by stressing that domestic violence calls are among the hardest police responses because the situation is urgent, unstable, and often uncertain.

That setting matters because the later suppression dispute turned on what happened in a matter of minutes at the front door. The officers did not enter the home with a warrant in hand. Instead, the legal question later became whether Lisa voluntarily let them in and, once inside, whether they stayed within the permission she gave.

## Officer Kizer’s Arrival and the First Doorway Conversation

Officer Jamie Kizer arrived at the Coleman home at roughly 4:00 a.m. The court relies heavily on body-camera footage from Kizer and the other officers, and that footage reconstructs the encounter step by step. Kizer knocked, announced himself, and spoke with Lisa at the doorway. She told him that her son and daughter were inside. When he asked whether the children were okay, she did not give a firm answer. At first she said only that they were in her bedroom, and when Kizer asked again whether they were going to be okay, she responded, “hope so.”

Those hesitant answers are important because they show why the officer remained concerned. He knew there had been a reported threat, and Lisa was not reassuring him that the children were safe. Even so, the exchange was calm and conversational, not confrontational. That tone later mattered to the appellate court when it reviewed whether Lisa’s consent was voluntary.

## The First Request to Enter and Lisa’s Refusal

Kizer then asked where Coleman was located inside the home. Lisa said she did not know. He followed up by asking if he could enter. This was the first explicit request to go inside, and Lisa refused: “um … I’d prefer you not.”

That refusal is a key part of the factual record because it shows Lisa understood she could say no. The opinion later treats that as evidence that her later decision to let the officers enter was an actual choice, not the product of misunderstanding. In other words, the sequence itself mattered: Lisa initially declined, which confirmed that the officer was seeking permission rather than simply walking in.

## The Interruption: Lisa Goes Back Inside and Kizer Checks On Her

After refusing entry, Lisa said she needed to go inside to get her shoes. She went back into the house, but she did not promptly return. Kizer became concerned and partially opened the door, calling inside several times to check whether she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa reappeared at the doorway and stepped back outside.

This interruption is another detail the court emphasizes because it shows the officers were still trying to monitor a potentially dangerous situation, not force their way in. The record reflects concern, not escalation. It also set up the second, pivotal doorway exchange.

## The Pivotal Second Exchange and Lisa’s “Okay”

Once Lisa was back outside, Kizer again asked how things were going inside and asked for Coleman’s name. She identified him as Jaison. Kizer then asked whether Coleman was calmer and whether the kids were okay. Lisa responded in a hesitant, fragmented way. Kizer then said, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.”

That sentence became the focal point of the suppression dispute. Lisa replied, “Okay.” She then supplied more information: the children were in her bedroom, Coleman was probably in the back bedroom, and the dogs would jump on the officers but would not bite. Kizer followed up about the dogs, and Lisa reassured him they would not bite.

The appellate opinion treats that exchange as the moment of consent. What mattered was not just the single word “Okay,” but the surrounding circumstances: Kizer spoke calmly, Lisa had already shown that she could refuse entry, and her response was followed immediately by practical directions about where the children were and how the officers could safely come in. The court read the interaction as a natural continuation of a permission-based conversation, not as a coercive command.

## Entry, the Sweep of the Home, and What the Officers Learned

After that exchange, Lisa opened the door and let the officers in. She helped facilitate entry by moving the dogs aside. She then led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were. When the officers did not immediately see Coleman, they asked where he was, and Lisa said she did not know. A quick sweep of the home located him in a separate bedroom. Coleman then allowed Officer Kizer to conduct a protective pat-down.

The officers then spoke with the children. They said Lisa and Coleman had been fighting, that Coleman had been loud, aggressive, and mean, and that he had pointed a gun at their mother while threatening to shoot her and burn the house down. The children said they had seen the gun, both that night and before, though they did not know where it was at that moment.

Those statements became important because they formed the basis for the later warrant. The officers had entered first, then gathered information from the children, then used that information to obtain judicial authorization to search the home. The firearms that supported Coleman’s federal prosecution were found during that later search.

## Why the Body-Camera Footage Was So Important

The Seventh Circuit treated the body-camera footage as especially reliable because it captured the actual exchange—its timing, pauses, tone, and gestures. That mattered because the central issues on appeal depended on exactly what Lisa said, how Kizer phrased his request, and how a reasonable person would understand the interaction. The court noted that a video record can eliminate factual disagreement that might otherwise arise from testimony alone.

In this case, the footage let the court see that Kizer was calm and respectful, that Lisa initially refused entry, that the officer’s second request was not accompanied by force or threats, and that Lisa then gave directions and facilitated entry. The appellate court’s later legal analysis rested on this factual picture. The video was not just background; it was the evidence that made the consent issue concrete and, in the court’s view, resolved.
