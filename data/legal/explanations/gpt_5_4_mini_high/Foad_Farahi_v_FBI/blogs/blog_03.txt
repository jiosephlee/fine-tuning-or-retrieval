Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## Judging FOIA From Behind the Curtain: Ex Parte Declarations After *Farahi*

## Why this case matters

*Farahi v. FBI* is not a headline-grabber in the usual sense: it is a FOIA case, decided on a familiar national-security record, and the D.C. Circuit’s bottom line is unsurprising. But the opinion is important because it shows how much of modern FOIA litigation—especially under Exemption 7(A)—is decided through sealed declarations that the requester never sees, never tests, and often cannot meaningfully rebut.

That is not a bug in the D.C. Circuit’s framework so much as a feature of it. But *Farahi* illustrates the practical consequences. The court’s conclusions on both Exemption 7(A) and segregability rested heavily on ex parte submissions, and the panel was comfortable saying so. For agencies, that is reassuring. For FOIA requesters, it is a reminder that once the government invokes national security or an ongoing investigation, the real fight may move off the public record entirely.

## The court’s path to affirmance

The underlying facts help explain the result. Farahi had been in immigration proceedings for years, and the FBI had investigated alleged ties to terrorist organizations and individuals. When he sought his FBI file under FOIA, the agency withheld most of the responsive records under Exemption 7(A), which protects law-enforcement records whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

The district court initially thought the FBI “seemingly” had made its case, but it wanted fresher information because the case had been pending so long. That prompted updated declarations from Joseph Bender, including a public declaration saying the relevant investigation remained ongoing and a sealed ex parte declaration supplying more detail. The district court then granted summary judgment, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The panel’s analysis is textbook D.C. Circuit FOIA doctrine. Exemption 7(A) applies if the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The court found both showings satisfied, relying on the FBI’s public declaration, the sealed declaration, and the broader context of Farahi’s immigration and terrorism-related proceedings. It also upheld the FBI’s segregability showing, again leaning on the sealed materials to conclude that no meaningful nonexempt information was reasonably segregable.

## Ex parte declarations did the heavy lifting

What stands out is how central the ex parte declarations were to both rulings. The public Hardy declaration was enough to set the table, but the sealed declaration filled in the specifics. The court was candid that it could not disclose the contents of that filing, but it still treated the sealed material as part of the basis for concluding that the investigation was real, ongoing, and sufficiently concrete to support Exemption 7(A).

That approach is doctrinally familiar. The D.C. Circuit has long allowed in camera or ex parte review in FOIA cases when necessary to protect sensitive information, and it has repeatedly emphasized deference where the FBI invokes law-enforcement and national-security concerns. *Farahi* does not break new ground so much as it shows how little ground there sometimes is for a requester to stand on once the agency has moved into sealed territory.

The opinion is also noteworthy for the way it handles the stale-declaration problem. The district court asked for an update because years had passed since the original submission. The FBI responded with a relatively terse public update from Bender, and the court basically said that, in context, the update was enough because it was not standing alone. That is sensible as a matter of judicial administration, but it also underscores how much the outcome depends on the unshared backstory in the sealed declaration.

## Waiver did some work too

Farahi’s challenge to the use of ex parte materials was weakened by waiver and forfeiture. He had not opposed the government’s request to file the supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, and the panel said he therefore could not later complain about the district court’s reliance on those materials. Likewise, the court brushed aside his attack on the FBI’s category definitions because he did not make a specific overbreadth argument in the district court.

That part of the opinion matters beyond this case. FOIA plaintiffs often face a difficult strategic choice: object too aggressively to sealed materials and risk looking unreasonable in a case involving national security; object too little, and the issue may be waived. *Farahi* shows how those choices can shape the litigation before the merits are ever reached. Once a plaintiff has acquiesced in sealed submissions, appellate review becomes narrower still.

## The broader institutional question: is review still meaningful?

The real tension in *Farahi* is institutional. FOIA is supposed to impose a burden on the agency to justify withholding, and courts are supposed to check that burden. But how meaningful is that check when the key justifications never enter the public record?

The D.C. Circuit’s answer is essentially that meaningful review still exists because judges can examine the sealed declarations themselves. That is true as far as it goes, and in some categories of cases it may be the only workable answer. If the records really would reveal the scope of an ongoing counterterrorism investigation, then forcing disclosure of the rationale could defeat the point of the exemption.

Still, there is a cost. Ex parte review blunts the adversarial process, and FOIA cases depend heavily on adversarial testing because the requester is usually the only party pressing the presumption of disclosure. When the government’s explanation is split between a thin public declaration and a thick sealed one, the court’s confidence often rests on what it alone can see. That can be administratively necessary, but it is not the same as transparent adjudication.

The opinion’s own language hints at that discomfort. The panel acknowledged that Bender’s public update, “considered in isolation,” was “surprisingly terse.” It only survived because of the earlier sealed Hardy declaration and the context of the case. That is a respectable way to evaluate the record. But it also means that the public record, standing alone, may tell a very incomplete story about why the government gets to keep the documents secret.

## Likely downstream effects

*Farahi* likely will not change the law, but it may affect litigation strategy. Agencies will read it as a confirmation that a public declaration plus a sealed supplement can carry an Exemption 7(A) case, even after a long delay, so long as the agency can still say the investigation remains ongoing. Requesters, in turn, will need to press harder on specific deficiencies in the public record, because generalized skepticism about secrecy will not be enough.

The case also reinforces a broader D.C. Circuit trend: when national-security or terrorism-related records are at issue, the court is willing to accept a great deal of agency-managed opacity. That may be appropriate in some cases. But *Farahi* is a reminder that the question in FOIA litigation is not just whether the government has a lawful secrecy claim. It is also whether the judiciary can test that claim in a way that remains meaningfully adversarial and publicly intelligible. On that score, the answer is often: only partially.
