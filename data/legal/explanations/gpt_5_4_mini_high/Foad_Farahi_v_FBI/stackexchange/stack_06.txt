Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### When may a FOIA court rely on sealed ex parte declarations without giving the requester access to them?
Question:
A lot of the court's reasoning seems to depend on sealed, ex parte declarations that Farahi could not see, especially on the nature of the investigation and segregability. I understand the practical problem that public explanation might reveal the very information the government wants to protect, but this still seems hard on the requester. What are the limits on using ex parte submissions in FOIA litigation? Does the requester's failure to object in the district court effectively waive any challenge, or do courts still have an independent duty to be skeptical before relying on secret affidavits?
Answer:
A FOIA court may rely on sealed ex parte declarations, but only when that is necessary to decide the exemption dispute without disclosing the very material the agency is trying to withhold.

That is the rule the D.C. Circuit applied here. The court said the usual prejudice to the requester is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” It also held that the district court did not need to put its analysis of the sealed declaration on the public record.

A few limits still matter:

- **The agency still bears the burden.** FOIA is not “trust us.” The government must justify the exemption, and the court reviews summary judgment de novo. In this case, the district court actually required **updated declarations** because the earlier ones were stale and needed more detail on segregability.
- **The public record still matters.** The court relied on the public Hardy and Bender declarations plus the sealed submissions; the ex parte material was used to fill in sensitive details, not to replace any public showing.
- **Segregability must still be addressed.** The court may consider sealed declarations on whether nonexempt material is reasonably segregable, but it still has to be satisfied that the agency explained why nothing meaningful can be released.

On your waiver point: **yes, failure to object in the district court generally forfeits the complaint about the use of ex parte declarations on appeal.** Farahi “did not oppose” the motion to file the supplemental declaration under seal and ex parte, so the panel said he could not later challenge the district court’s reliance on it.

So the short answer is:

> A FOIA court may rely on sealed ex parte declarations when necessary to avoid revealing the withheld information itself; the requester does not get access just because the declaration is important. But the court still must independently test the agency’s showing, and if the requester did not object below, the appellate challenge to the use of the ex parte submission is usually forfeited.
