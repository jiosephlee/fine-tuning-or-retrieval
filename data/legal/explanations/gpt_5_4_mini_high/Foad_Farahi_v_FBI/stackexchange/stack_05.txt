Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Why was the 2023 Bender declaration enough when it sounds so conclusory about the investigation remaining ongoing?
Question:
Farahi argued that too much time had passed and that the updated Bender declaration was very terse, just saying the relevant investigations 'remain ongoing.' The court basically agreed that it looked sparse in isolation, but then treated it as an update to the earlier, more detailed Hardy declarations. Is that a normal way for courts to evaluate agency affidavits in FOIA cases? At what point does an 'update' become too conclusory to carry the government's burden, especially where years have passed since the original showing?
Answer:
Yes — that is a pretty normal way for a court to evaluate FOIA declarations.

The key point in this opinion is that the D.C. Circuit did **not** treat the 2023 Bender declaration as a standalone affidavit. It read Bender as an **update to the earlier Hardy declarations**, which had already done the heavy lifting:

- the **public Hardy declaration** said the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations of national security and terrorism violations and that disclosure could interfere with **pending law enforcement proceedings**;
- the **ex parte Hardy declaration** supplied “considerable detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation;
- the district court had already found the FBI had “seemingly” shown pending proceedings, and only asked for a **current update** because time had passed.

Against that backdrop, Bender’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing” was enough because it was answering a narrow question: **has anything changed since the earlier showing?** The court expressly says the Bender declaration “can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document.”

That is consistent with FOIA practice. Courts assess agency affidavits **in context**, not by isolating a single sentence. And in Exemption 7(A) cases, the agency may satisfy its burden by attesting to the **existence of ongoing investigations/proceedings**, especially where the FBI and national security concerns are involved. The opinion cites that principle directly, including CREW’s statement that ongoing criminal investigations count as “proceedings,” and that the government can show them by attesting to their existence.

### When is an “update” too conclusory?
This opinion does **not** set a bright-line rule. But it gives the relevant limit:

- if the update is the **only** evidence of ongoing proceedings, and it is just a bare assertion with no detail, that may be too conclusory;
- if the update is tethered to an earlier, sufficiently detailed showing, and its purpose is simply to confirm that the investigation is still live, courts may accept it.

The court acknowledged that Bender’s declaration was “surprisingly terse” when viewed alone. It still upheld it because the earlier Hardy declarations, the district court’s prior ruling, and the sealed submission together showed a continuing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.”

So the answer is: **the declaration was enough not because the sentence itself was rich, but because it functioned as a current status update to an already adequate Exemption 7(A) showing.**
