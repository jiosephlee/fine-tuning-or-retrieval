Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # FOIA’s Disclosure Mandate and the Exemption 7(A) Framework

## FOIA’s basic obligation to disclose agency records unless a statutory exemption applies

FOIA begins with a broad rule of disclosure. A federal agency must release records responsive to a proper request unless a specific statutory exemption allows it to withhold them. In this case, that basic structure matters because Farahi asked for records from his FBI file, and the FBI withheld most of what it found. The dispute therefore turned not on whether FOIA generally favors release, but on whether the FBI could fit the requested records within an exemption.

FOIA litigation also assigns the burden of proof to the government. When an agency withholds records, it must justify the withholding, and a district court reviews the dispute on summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit reviews that ruling de novo, meaning it looks at the matter anew rather than deferring to the district court’s legal conclusions. But even in de novo review, the agency usually carries its burden through declarations explaining what the records are and why they fit a claimed exemption. That is why the Hardy and Bender declarations were so important in this case.

## The text of Exemption 7(A): law-enforcement records that could interfere with enforcement proceedings

Exemption 7(A) permits withholding of records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” That language contains two separate requirements. First, the records must have a law-enforcement purpose. Second, release must create a reasonable risk of interference with enforcement proceedings. The exemption is therefore not a blanket authorization to withhold anything the FBI wants to keep secret; the agency must connect the records to law enforcement and show a concrete risk of harm from disclosure.

The opinion also reminds the reader that the FBI receives some deference on the first part of this inquiry because it is a law-enforcement agency. Still, deference is not the same as automatic acceptance. The agency’s declarations must establish a rational link between the records and an investigation or other law-enforcement duty, as well as a connection to a possible violation of federal law or a security risk. In other words, the FBI cannot simply invoke its name; it must explain why the records were created in the course of a legitimate investigative function.

## The D.C. Circuit’s gloss: pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings

The D.C. Circuit has added an important gloss to Exemption 7(A): the relevant enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” That requirement gives the statutory phrase “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” practical content. It means the exemption is tied to actual or imminent law-enforcement activity, not just a speculative possibility that some proceeding might someday exist.

This court has also treated ongoing criminal investigations as “proceedings” for purposes of Exemption 7(A). That is crucial because an investigation can be protected even before charges are filed. The reason is intuitive: disclosure during an active investigation can tip off subjects, reveal lines of inquiry, expose witnesses, or allow targets to destroy evidence or adjust their conduct. If the investigation is still gathering evidence for a possible future case, and premature disclosure would jeopardize that effort, the exemption can apply.

In this case, that gloss mattered because the FBI did not need to show a completed prosecution. It needed to show that the records related to continuing investigative activity that was still tied to possible enforcement. The opinion treats the existence of an ongoing investigation as enough, so long as the agency’s declarations make that investigation concrete rather than vague.

## How agency declarations function in FOIA litigation

FOIA cases often rise or fall on agency declarations. Because the very records at issue are usually withheld, the court cannot simply inspect them openly and may need to rely on sworn statements from agency officials. Those declarations describe the records, identify the exemption, and explain why disclosure would cause the type of harm the statute contemplates. In this case, the FBI first submitted a declaration from David Hardy, and later updated its showing through a declaration from Joseph Bender.

The court’s concern in FOIA litigation is whether those declarations are sufficiently specific. A declaration cannot rely on empty conclusions; it has to provide enough detail for the judge to evaluate the claim without revealing the sensitive information itself. That is why the district court here required the FBI to refresh its evidence when time had passed. A declaration that may have been adequate when filed can become stale if it no longer shows that the relevant proceedings are still pending or reasonably anticipated.

FOIA cases may also involve ex parte declarations, meaning submissions shown to the judge but not to the requester. That procedure is unusual in ordinary civil litigation, but it is sometimes necessary in FOIA cases because a public explanation might itself disclose the very information the agency is trying to protect. The D.C. Circuit recognizes that this creates obvious fairness problems for the requester, but it also recognizes that some disputes cannot be resolved any other way if sensitive material is to remain secret.

## FOIA’s segregability rule: release what can be separated from exempt material

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA imposes an independent duty of segregability. The agency must take reasonable steps to separate and release any nonexempt information that can be disclosed without revealing exempt material. The statute’s focus is on information, not simply on pages or words. So an agency cannot withhold an entire record just because it contains some protected material somewhere inside it.

At the same time, segregability has limits. If the nonexempt material is so intertwined with exempt material that meaningful disclosure would not be possible, the agency need not release fragments simply because they are technically nonsecret. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly said that when exempt and nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined, the agency may withhold the document in full. The point is practical: FOIA does not require the government to carve out meaningless scraps that would not convey useful information or that would reveal exempt material by context.

That is the issue Farahi pressed in this case when he argued that, given the sheer size of the FBI’s production, there had to be some segregable material somewhere. But the doctrine does not work at that level of abstraction. The agency must show, with reasonable specificity, why further segregation is not feasible, and the court may rely on declarations addressing that question. If a record consists of sensitive material with only scattered, line-by-line nonexempt phrases, those phrases may not be “reasonably segregable” in any meaningful sense.

## Category-based withholding and the use of sufficiently distinct categories

Agencies sometimes justify withholdings on a category-by-category basis rather than discussing each document individually. That method is efficient in large FOIA cases, but it must still allow meaningful judicial review. The categories must be sufficiently distinct so that a court can determine whether the claimed exemptions fit the material grouped within each category. If the categories are too vague or too broad, the court may not be able to tell whether the agency has properly applied the exemption.

This framework explains why the FBI’s declarations mattered here. The declarations did not merely announce that the withheld records were sensitive; they attempted to explain how the records were organized and why disclosure by category would still create a risk of interference. Farahi challenged that approach only briefly, and the court treated the challenge as forfeited because he did not develop a concrete argument about why the categories were overinclusive.

## Deference to the FBI, especially in national-security and counterterrorism cases

The opinion gives special emphasis to deference in this setting because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency and because the records implicated national security and counterterrorism concerns. The D.C. Circuit has long said that the FBI’s judgment under Exemption 7 deserves a fair measure of deference, and that deference becomes even more pronounced when disclosure could affect sensitive national-security investigations. The court’s role is still real—it must ensure that the agency has met its statutory burden—but it is not supposed to second-guess investigative judgments lightly.

That background helps explain the court’s willingness to credit the FBI’s updated declarations here. The agency’s public filings said the investigation remained ongoing, and the sealed materials supplied greater detail to the district court. Taken together, those submissions gave the court a basis to conclude that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that release could interfere with concrete, continuing enforcement activity. The legal framework of Exemption 7(A), segregability, and deference set the stage for the court’s ultimate decision to affirm withholding.
