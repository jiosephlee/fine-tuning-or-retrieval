Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 5: # Segregability, Ex Parte Submissions, and the Case’s Final Significance

## FOIA’s Separate Duty to Release Anything Reasonably Segregable

Even after an agency shows that an exemption applies, FOIA does not allow it to withhold a record wholesale unless the exempt and nonexempt material cannot reasonably be separated. That is why the statute independently requires agencies to take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information, and to disclose any reasonably segregable portion of a record after deleting the exempt portions. In other words, winning on Exemption 7(A) does not end the inquiry. The court still has to ask whether something meaningful can be released without undermining the protected law-enforcement interests.

That requirement matters here because the FBI withheld most of Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A), but the district court wanted a separate explanation for why the records could not be carved up any further. The opinion shows the court treating segregability as a real, independent question rather than as a formality once an exemption has been established.

## Why the District Court Asked for More on Segregability

The district court initially accepted that the FBI had “seemingly” shown that the withheld records were covered by Exemption 7(A), but it was not satisfied that the record then before it adequately addressed segregability. That concern was tied to the passage of time. The agency’s first showing was based on declarations that had become stale while the case was pending, so the court ordered updated evidence not only on whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, but also on whether any nonexempt material could be separated out.

That procedural step is important to understand. The court was not rejecting the FBI’s exemption claim; it was asking whether the claim remained current and whether the agency had explained, with enough specificity, why nothing reasonably segregable could be released. FOIA courts often require this kind of up-to-date showing when litigation drags on, especially where the existence of ongoing investigations can change over time.

## Bender’s Supplemental Ex Parte Showing and the Mead Data Principle

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. His public declaration confirmed that the relevant investigation was still ongoing, and his ex parte declaration provided additional detail both on the investigation and on why the withheld material could not be meaningfully segregated. The district court credited that explanation and concluded that the FBI had satisfied its burden.

The court’s segregation analysis rests on a familiar FOIA idea: an agency does not have to release isolated scraps of text if those scraps are so intertwined with exempt material that their disclosure would not yield meaningful information. The opinion invokes Mead Data for the proposition that nonexempt material need not be released when it is interspersed line by line with exempt material, and where the focus of FOIA is information rather than simply written words. That distinction matters. Farahi argued, in essence, that because the FBI had a huge volume of responsive material, there must be some releasable content somewhere in it. The court rejected that intuition. A large file does not automatically mean there is something “reasonably segregable” in a practical sense. If the nonexempt material consists only of scattered words or phrases that cannot be disclosed without revealing protected law-enforcement information, FOIA does not require release.

So the court accepted the FBI’s position that, while a few isolated words or phrases might be technically nonexempt, they did not amount to meaningful information that could be separated out and disclosed without harming the investigation. That is why the opinion says the district court correctly concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

## Why the Court Accepted Ex Parte Declarations

A striking feature of the case is the FBI’s use of ex parte declarations, meaning material shown to the judge but not to Farahi. The court treated that as permissible here because the point of the declaration was to explain why the withheld material had to stay secret. In a FOIA case involving sensitive law-enforcement records, an ex parte submission may be the only way to give the court enough information to decide the dispute without revealing the very material the agency claims is exempt.

The opinion also emphasizes preservation. Farahi had not opposed the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations when the FBI sought that procedure, so he could not later complain that the court relied on them. That procedural point is easy to miss but important: appellate courts generally do not rescue a party from an objection that was never properly made below. The court also noted that ex parte review is an accepted, if imperfect, feature of FOIA litigation when secrecy is at issue. The disadvantage to the requester is real, but it is inherent in cases where public disclosure would itself defeat the exemption claim.

## The Final Disposition and What the Opinion Shows

Once the court accepted the updated declarations, the rest of the case followed. The FBI had shown that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes, that the investigation remained ongoing, that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with that investigation, and that no meaningful segregable material remained. Farahi’s challenge to the category definitions of withheld records failed because he did not develop a specific argument about overbreadth, and his segregability objection failed for the reasons above. The D.C. Circuit therefore affirmed summary judgment for the FBI.

The broader lesson is not that agencies automatically win when they invoke national-security concerns. Rather, this opinion shows how FOIA litigation over active counterterrorism or other sensitive law-enforcement files often turns on the quality and freshness of the agency’s declarations. A stale or conclusory submission may not suffice, which is why the district court required updates here. But once the agency supplies a sufficiently detailed public and sealed showing, the court will defer to the FBI’s expertise, especially where disclosure could tip off targets, compromise an ongoing investigation, or reveal the very contours of the file the requester seeks.
