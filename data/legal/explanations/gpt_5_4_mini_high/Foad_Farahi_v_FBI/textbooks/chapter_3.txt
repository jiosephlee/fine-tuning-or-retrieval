Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # Appellate Review and the First Exemption 7(A) Requirement: Records Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes

## De novo appellate review of summary judgment in FOIA cases

The court began by reminding the reader how FOIA cases come up on appeal. Like any summary-judgment ruling, a FOIA summary judgment is reviewed de novo. That means the court of appeals does not defer to the district court’s legal conclusions; instead, it asks for itself whether the record entitles the government to judgment as a matter of law. In FOIA litigation, this matters because the agency is the party claiming an exemption from the statute’s general rule of disclosure, so the agency must justify withholding with evidence.

## The government’s burden to justify withholding under a FOIA exemption

FOIA starts from a presumption of disclosure. The government may keep records secret only if it proves that one of the statute’s exemptions applies. The court stated that burden explicitly: under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), the agency must show that the withheld records fall within Exemption 7(A). At this stage, that means the FBI had to establish not only that the records were part of a law-enforcement file, but also that the exemption’s interference requirement could be satisfied. In other words, the agency does not win merely by asserting secrecy; it must tie the records to the statutory language.

## The FBI’s law-enforcement status and the resulting measure of judicial deference

The court then explained the special position of the FBI. Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, courts give its Exemption 7 determinations a “fair measure” of deference. That does not eliminate judicial review, but it does affect how closely the court scrutinizes the agency’s description of its records and investigations. The court also noted that this deference is especially strong when national security is implicated. Here, the records concerned national security and international terrorism, so the court treated the FBI’s account with particular caution and respect for the executive branch’s expertise.

## The Pratt/Campbell nexus requirement: tying the investigation to an FBI law-enforcement duty and a possible security risk or violation of law

Even with deference, the FBI still had to meet the threshold requirement that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The court relied on its earlier decisions in Campbell and Pratt for the governing test. The declarations had to establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the FBI’s law-enforcement duties, and also connect the investigation to an individual or incident that posed a possible security risk or suggested a possible violation of federal law.

This requirement serves an important screening function. It prevents an agency from labeling ordinary administrative records as law-enforcement records simply because they are held by a law-enforcement agency. The question is not whether the FBI possesses the records, but whether the records were assembled in the course of a legitimate law-enforcement inquiry. For the FBI, investigations of terrorism, national security threats, and related criminal or protective matters easily fit that framework when the declarations explain the connection.

## What the Hardy declarations said about investigations involving national security, international terrorism, and related proceedings

The court found that the FBI’s declarations did exactly what the law requires. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” including pending law-enforcement proceedings and Farahi’s associations with people directly involved in those violations. That was enough to place the file within the law-enforcement category at a basic level.

The sealed, ex parte Hardy declaration went further. Although the court could not describe its contents publicly, it said the sealed submission supplied “considerable detail” about the investigation’s nature. That detail mattered because it supported the agency’s public assertion that the file was not just an immigration or administrative file, but part of an investigation into terrorism- and national security-related conduct. The court was willing to rely on that sealed material, especially because FOIA cases sometimes require ex parte review to avoid revealing the very material the government claims is exempt.

## How Farahi’s own concessions in district court reinforced the law-enforcement character of the file

The court also relied on Farahi’s own litigation position in the district court. He conceded that the FBI had investigated him for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and convicted on terrorism-related charges. Those concessions mattered because they independently confirmed the investigative context described in the FBI’s declarations.

This is a good example of how a FOIA court may use the whole record, not just the agency’s formal declarations, to decide whether the statutory threshold is met. Farahi’s admissions made it even harder to dispute that the records were gathered in a bona fide law-enforcement investigation. They showed that the file related to suspected terrorist associations, not to a routine administrative matter.

## Why the court found no serious doubt that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes

Putting the pieces together, the court had little trouble concluding that the first Exemption 7(A) requirement was satisfied. The FBI’s declarations described an investigation into national security and international terrorism violations; the sealed submission added detail; and Farahi’s own concessions confirmed the seriousness and law-enforcement nature of the inquiry. Against that background, the court had “no doubt” that the FBI met its burden to show that Farahi’s file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes.

That conclusion mattered because Exemption 7(A) does not protect every record in an FBI file. The agency first has to show that the records belong to a genuine law-enforcement compilation. Only after that does the court ask the second question: whether disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. In this part of the opinion, the court was only resolving the threshold issue, and it did so by relying on the FBI’s declarations, the national-security context, and Farahi’s own concessions.
