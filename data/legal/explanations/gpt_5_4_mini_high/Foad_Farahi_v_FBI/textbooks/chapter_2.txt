Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Farahi’s FBI File Request and the Long Path to Appeal

## Farahi’s background as an Iranian national living in the United States and his placement in removal proceedings

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. His dispute with the FBI did not arise in a vacuum. It came against the backdrop of long-running immigration proceedings that began in 2002, when the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings because he had overstayed his visa. Those proceedings stretched on for years and involved multiple requests for relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.

For a reader trying to follow the FOIA litigation, the immigration background matters because it explains why Farahi wanted his FBI file and why the government said the file remained sensitive. The FBI’s records were tied to investigations relevant to his immigration case, so the file was not a routine personnel or administrative record. It was part of a broader law-enforcement and national-security context.

## The role of terrorism-related allegations in Farahi’s immigration proceedings

The opinion notes that, in opposing relief in the immigration case, DHS relied on documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who said he had documented Farahi’s connections to “high-level terrorists.” That detail is important not because the court is resolving the truth of those allegations in this FOIA case, but because it shows the kind of investigation at issue. The FBI’s asserted reason for withholding records was that they were connected to national-security and terrorism investigations.

The opinion also references a later Eleventh Circuit decision in Farahi’s immigration litigation, which summarized the government’s allegations in more detail. Those allegations included claimed ties to designated terrorist organizations and to individuals associated with terrorism-related crimes. Again, the D.C. Circuit does not adjudicate those allegations in this opinion; it uses them to provide context for why the FBI said the records were part of an ongoing law-enforcement matter.

## Farahi’s FOIA request for his FBI file and the breadth of potentially responsive material: thousands of pages and dozens of CDs

Farahi filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. FOIA gives the public a right to request agency records, but agencies may withhold material that falls within a statutory exemption. Here, the FBI located a very large amount of potentially responsive material: about 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. That is a significant volume, and it foreshadowed the complexity of the later segregability dispute, because the agency would need to review a substantial collection of records to determine what, if anything, could be disclosed.

The FBI did not immediately turn over the file. Once Farahi sued, the agency continued processing the records over several years. In a FOIA case, this kind of staged review is common when a request is large or sensitive, but it can make the litigation slow and document-intensive. By the time the FBI finished its review, it had disclosed some records and withheld most of the material.

## The FBI’s delayed response, partial disclosures, and extensive withholdings

Farahi filed suit after the FBI missed FOIA’s statutory deadline for responding. That deadline does not necessarily mean the requester wins automatically, but it does permit litigation when the agency fails to act in time. After suit was filed, the agency reviewed the potentially responsive material, produced some records, and withheld the rest. Its position was that the withheld records fell within FOIA’s law-enforcement exemption for records compiled for law-enforcement purposes when disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

The record review was lengthy enough that timing became a recurring issue. By the time the district court first considered summary judgment, several years had passed since the FBI’s initial declarations. That passage of time later became central to the district court’s concern that its evidence about ongoing enforcement proceedings might be stale.

## The FBI’s first summary-judgment motion supported by David Hardy’s public declaration and sealed ex parte supplement

After completing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019. In FOIA litigation, summary judgment is the usual vehicle for resolving whether the agency has properly withheld records. The agency bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies. To support that burden, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Records/Information Dissemination Section.

Hardy’s public declaration said the withheld records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that releasing them could interfere with enforcement proceedings that were pending or reasonably anticipated. The FBI also moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, meaning the court could review it without disclosing its contents to Farahi. Farahi did not oppose that request, and the district court allowed it. This is an important procedural feature of many FOIA cases: if the agency says the records themselves are sensitive, it may submit classified or otherwise protected explanations to the judge alone so the court can test the exemption claim without revealing the disputed material.

## The district court’s 2022 ruling: provisional acceptance of Exemption 7(A), concern about staleness, and request for updated submissions

In November 2022, the district court addressed the FBI’s first summary-judgment motion. It concluded that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that the FBI had seemingly shown that their release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But the court was not fully satisfied with the age of the declarations. Because a significant amount of time had passed since the FBI first made its showing, the court wanted an updated assessment of whether the relevant enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated.

The court also concluded that it needed more information to assess segregability, which is FOIA’s requirement that agencies release any reasonably segregable nonexempt material. The court said it could not explain some of its concerns on the public record. As a result, it denied summary judgment without prejudice. That procedural move did not reject the FBI’s exemption claim outright; instead, it gave the agency another opportunity to update and refine its showing.

## The FBI’s renewed motion supported by Joseph Bender’s public and ex parte declarations

The FBI responded with updated declarations and renewed its summary-judgment motion. This time, it relied on Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration. In the public filing, Bender explained that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for Farahi’s file, and that agent confirmed the investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte submission supplied additional details about the ongoing investigation and also addressed the district court’s segregability concerns.

This second round of declarations mattered because it addressed the district court’s staleness concern directly. Instead of relying only on the older Hardy declarations, the FBI provided a newer confirmation that the relevant proceedings had not gone dormant while the case was pending. It also gave the court more detail, behind the ex parte process, about why the withheld material could not be meaningfully separated from nonexempt information.

## The district court’s final grant of summary judgment and Farahi’s appeal

After reviewing the renewed submissions, the district court granted summary judgment to the FBI. It credited the agency’s confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing and, based on its review of the ex parte materials, concluded that the FBI had adequately explained why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. In other words, the court held that the agency had done enough to justify withholding the records under Exemption 7(A) and to satisfy its obligation to consider releasing any nonexempt portions.

Farahi appealed. That appeal set up the D.C. Circuit’s review of whether the FBI had carried its FOIA burden, whether the agency had shown that enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, and whether the district court properly accepted the agency’s segregability showing. The opinion’s later sections address those legal questions, but this procedural history explains how the case got there.
