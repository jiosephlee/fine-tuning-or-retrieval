Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # The Second Exemption 7(A) Requirement: Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

## The interference requirement and a live enforcement proceeding

Exemption 7(A) does not protect law-enforcement records just because they are sensitive or were gathered by the FBI. The agency also has to show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” In this circuit, that means there must be a real, concrete enforcement matter still alive when the court rules. It does not have to be a filed criminal case; an ongoing investigation can qualify if it is still gathering evidence for a possible future prosecution or other enforcement action.

That is the frame the D.C. Circuit applied here. The court treated the key question as whether the FBI had shown that Farahi’s file related to an enforcement proceeding that was still ongoing, or at least reasonably anticipated, at the time of the renewed summary-judgment motion. Because FOIA litigation can last for years, the age of the declarations mattered. The district court had already been uneasy about relying on older affidavits, so it required the FBI to update its showing.

## Why disclosure would interfere

The court accepted a familiar Exemption 7(A) rationale: releasing records from an ongoing investigation can tip off targets about what the agency knows, what it is looking for, and where the investigation is headed. That kind of disclosure can let the subject conceal evidence, alter behavior, or otherwise frustrate law enforcement efforts.

The FBI’s public declaration said exactly that. It explained that disclosure of information about the ongoing investigation could reveal the scope of the inquiry and thus interfere with it. The court viewed that as a standard and legally sufficient interference theory under Exemption 7(A), especially in a counterterrorism context.

## The Hardy declaration and the importance of the ex parte detail

The first Hardy declaration did two important things. First, it said the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” including pending proceedings and Farahi’s associations with individuals involved in those violations. Second, the FBI supported that public declaration with a sealed ex parte declaration giving the district court more detail about the investigation.

That mattered because the public record alone was necessarily limited. The ex parte submission helped the court verify that the FBI was not making a bare or abstract claim of ongoing investigations. When the district court later worried that the showing might have gone stale, it was not starting from zero; it had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, and it only wanted confirmation that the investigation was still active.

## Why Bender was treated as an update, not a standalone justification

Farahi’s central attack was that the later Bender declaration was too terse. Bender said that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” which, standing alone, might sound conclusory. The court agreed that the statement was short, but it refused to read it in isolation.

Instead, the court treated Bender’s declaration as an update to the Hardy declarations. The FBI was not using Bender to establish pendency from scratch; it was using him to confirm that nothing had changed while the case was pending in district court. That procedural context mattered. The district court had already indicated that the earlier submissions had basically satisfied Exemption 7(A), and the new declaration was meant to refresh the record after time had passed.

The court also emphasized deference to the FBI, particularly where national-security and counterterrorism investigations are involved. The public record, plus the sealed submissions, made it plausible that the agency was still pursuing a concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding. The court therefore rejected the notion that the passage of time, by itself, defeated Exemption 7(A).

## Farahi’s CREW argument and the court’s response to staleness

Farahi relied on the D.C. Circuit’s warning in CREW that vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may not be enough. The court did not dispute that principle. But it distinguished this case on the ground that the FBI’s showing was not vague in the way CREW condemned.

Here, the government had already explained the nature of the investigation, the public Hardy declaration tied the records to national-security and terrorism inquiries, and the ex parte declaration supplied additional detail. Against that backdrop, Bender’s brief update was enough to show continuity. The court also pointed to the breadth of the underlying investigation—publicly described as involving alleged ties to the Muslim Brotherhood, Al Qaeda figures, convicted or indicted terrorists, and terrorism-financing charities—as further support for the FBI’s position that the matter was still live.

The bottom line was that the court was satisfied there remained a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” That satisfied the second half of Exemption 7(A).

## The challenge to the FBI’s document categories was forfeited

Farahi also briefly argued that the FBI had not adequately justified the categories it used to describe the withheld records. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged the rule that, when an agency withholds records category by category, the categories must be distinct enough for a court to assess whether the exemptions fit. But Farahi never explained which categories were overbroad or why.

Because he offered only a general objection, the court treated the argument as forfeited. This is a good reminder that FOIA plaintiffs need to make specific challenges to an agency’s withholding methodology. A bare assertion that the categories are too broad is not enough to preserve the issue for appellate review.

## The holding on Exemption 7(A)

On the interference issue, the court held that the FBI met its burden. The declarations showed that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with an ongoing, concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding. The combination of the Hardy and Bender declarations, together with the public and sealed record, was enough to support withholding under Exemption 7(A).
