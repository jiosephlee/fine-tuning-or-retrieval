Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# When Is “Ongoing” Enough? *Farahi* and the Long Shelf Life of FOIA Exemption 7(A)

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Farahi v. FBI* is a useful reminder that FOIA litigation often turns less on dramatic legal questions than on the age of the government’s proof. Exemption 7(A) is supposed to protect active law-enforcement work from premature disclosure. But what happens when the agency’s declarations are years old, the case has lingered in discovery-like FOIA litigation, and the requester argues that whatever once was “ongoing” has gone stale?

Judge Katsas’s opinion answers that question with some practical flexibility. A terse 2023 declaration saying the investigation “remains ongoing” was enough, because it updated — rather than replaced — a more detailed earlier showing. For agencies, that is a meaningful win: *Farahi* suggests that in long-running investigations, the government may preserve 7(A) withholdings by periodically refreshing the record instead of rebuilding the exemption from scratch.

## The court’s basic move

The factual setup matters. Farahi sought his FBI file; the Bureau withheld most of it under Exemption 7(A), which applies to law-enforcement records whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The catch is that the proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” That requirement has teeth in theory, because 7(A) is not supposed to become a permanent secrecy provision.

But this case had been litigating for years. The FBI’s first summary-judgment submission relied on a 2019 Hardy declaration, later supplemented under seal and ex parte. By the time the district court reached the motion in 2022, it wanted assurance that the relevant enforcement proceedings were still alive. So it ordered an update.

The FBI responded with a 2023 Bender declaration. In public, Bender said the agent overseeing Farahi’s file confirmed the investigations remained ongoing. In camera, he provided more detail. Farahi argued that this was too thin — that a brief statement of continuation could not rescue stale declarations. The D.C. Circuit disagreed.

The key passage is not that “ongoing” always suffices. It is that “ongoing” suffices here because it was tethered to a fuller record. The earlier Hardy declarations said the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national security and international terrorism, described the scope of the inquiry, and explained how disclosure could tip off subjects about what the FBI knew and what it was still trying to learn. The Bender declaration then served as a confirmation that nothing had changed while the case was pending.

In other words, the court treated Bender as a refresh, not a foundation.

## Reading *CREW* and *Juarez* together

That framing is important because Farahi relied on the D.C. Circuit’s own warning in *CREW* that a “vague” assertion of ongoing investigations may not do the job. The panel did not reject that warning. Instead, it reconciled *CREW* with *Juarez* and related cases by emphasizing context.

Under *CREW*, ongoing criminal investigations count as “proceedings,” and the government can sometimes establish their existence by attestation alone. Under *Juarez*, if an investigation is still gathering evidence for a possible future case, and disclosure would jeopardize that process, 7(A) applies. *Farahi* essentially says: yes, but the government still needs enough detail to show that its “ongoing” claim is real, not formulaic.

That is why the opinion leans so heavily on the two Hardy declarations, including the ex parte submission, and on the fact that the district court had already found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden before asking for an update. The court was not persuaded by Bender’s sentence in isolation. It was persuaded by the sentence in light of the earlier, more complete showing.

## The deference point

There is also a familiar institutional story here. The opinion reminds us that the FBI gets deference on law-enforcement judgments, and that deference is especially pronounced when national security and terrorism are in the picture. Farahi’s file involved allegations of ties to terrorist organizations and terrorism-related figures; the panel clearly regarded that backdrop as reinforcing the plausibility of an ongoing investigation.

That does not mean the court abandoned the statutory test. But it does mean the test is applied with considerable elasticity where the FBI says disclosure would alert targets, reveal investigative scope, or otherwise compromise active work. In that sense, *Farahi* continues a pattern in which 7(A) is easiest to invoke when the investigation is both prolonged and sensitive.

## What this means going forward

The downstream effect is straightforward: agencies now have a somewhat easier path to preserving 7(A) withholdings in old cases. If an investigation is still open, a current declaration may be enough to keep the exemption alive even if the core factual showing comes from earlier, more detailed submissions. That is especially true when the agency can point to national security, terrorism, or similar contexts where courts are accustomed to caution.

But the opinion also preserves an important limit. A bare “it’s ongoing” declaration, standing alone, would still be vulnerable under *CREW*. The *Farahi* panel did not jettison specificity; it credited specificity from elsewhere in the record. So the case is less a green light for conclusory declarations than a practical lesson in record-building: agencies that want to avoid staleness problems should make sure their updates are anchored to an already detailed account.

The segregability holding fits the same pattern. The court accepted the district court’s reliance on an ex parte explanation that nonexempt material was not meaningfully separable from exempt material. That is doctrinally orthodox, but it also underscores how much of a FOIA fight can become one-sided once the government persuades the court to rely on sealed submissions.

## The bottom line

*Farahi* does not announce a new rule that “ongoing” automatically defeats staleness objections. But it does show how little the government may need, in practice, to keep Exemption 7(A) standing when a case drags on: a prior detailed declaration, a current confirmation that the investigation remains open, and a court willing to treat the latter as an update rather than a restart.

For FOIA requesters, that means time alone may not erode 7(A) as quickly as one might hope. For agencies, it means long-running investigations can keep their secrecy shield — so long as they keep refreshing the record enough to convince a court that the proceedings are still real.



# FOIA in the Shadow of Counterterrorism: Deference, National Security, and the FBI’s Law-Enforcement Showing

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Farahi v. FBI* is important less because it breaks new doctrinal ground than because it shows, again, how much room the government has under FOIA when counterterrorism and national security are in the background. The court upheld the FBI’s withholding of most of Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A), which protects law-enforcement records when their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But the opinion is notable for how readily the panel accepted the FBI’s “law enforcement purposes” showing and its claim that release would interfere with ongoing investigations.

That deference is not accidental. Judge Katsas’s opinion leans heavily on the D.C. Circuit’s existing Exemption 7 line of cases—especially *Pratt*, *Campbell*, and *CNSS*—and reads them in a way that gives the FBI substantial latitude where terrorism-related investigations are involved. The result is a reminder that FOIA doctrine is not entirely uniform. In ordinary cases, agencies must do more work to justify withholding. In FBI terrorism cases, courts tend to treat the agency’s expertise and the stakes of disclosure as powerful reasons to defer.

## The court’s basic move

Farahi sought his FBI file, and the agency disclosed some material while withholding most of it. The dispute turned on Exemption 7(A), which applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when disclosure could interfere with enforcement proceedings. The district court initially thought the FBI had “seemingly” made the necessary showing, but it wanted updated declarations because time had passed. The FBI responded with a new declaration from the acting RIDS section chief, who confirmed that the relevant investigations remained ongoing. The district court then granted summary judgment, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The panel’s analysis is straightforward in structure but revealing in tone. On the first prong, the court accepted that the FBI’s file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes because the agency said the records related to investigations of national-security and international-terrorism violations, and because Farahi himself had acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for alleged ties to terrorist organizations and individuals. On the second prong, the court held that disclosure could interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings because it might tip off targets about the scope of the investigation. The court also rejected Farahi’s segregability challenge, concluding that the FBI had adequately explained why no meaningful nonexempt material could be reasonably separated out.

## Deference at both prongs

What stands out is how much the court’s reasoning relies on deference. On the “law enforcement purposes” question, the opinion cites *Campbell* and *Pratt* for the proposition that the FBI’s judgments deserve “a fair measure of deference” because the bureau is itself a law-enforcement agency. That deference is not absolute; the agency still has to show a rational nexus between the investigation and a law-enforcement duty. But the court had little trouble finding that nexus here, particularly given the terrorism and national-security backdrop.

On the interference prong, the court again leaned on D.C. Circuit precedent, especially *CREW* and *Juarez*, for the principle that ongoing investigations count as proceedings and that Exemption 7(A) applies so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future case that would be jeopardized by premature disclosure. The court also cited *CNSS* for the proposition that national-security concerns justify especially strong deference.

The opinion’s treatment of the updated Bender declaration is especially telling. Farahi argued that the declaration was too terse and too conclusory to establish ongoing proceedings. The court agreed that, viewed in isolation, the declaration was “surprisingly terse,” but held that context mattered: the earlier Hardy declarations had already established the nature and scope of the investigation, and Bender’s declaration was just an update confirming that nothing had changed. In other words, the court did not require the FBI to rebuild the entire 7(A) showing from scratch after the district court asked for a refresh. It was enough that the agency had already supplied a sufficiently detailed foundation.

## What the opinion says about “ongoing” investigations

The panel’s willingness to accept the FBI’s “ongoing investigation” representation will matter most to FOIA litigants in cases where the government’s explanations grow stale over time. The district court here was rightly concerned about the passage of years between the initial declaration and the renewed motion. But the appellate court’s response suggests that staleness is not fatal if the agency can supply a later confirmation and if the record already contains a credible, detailed account of the underlying investigation.

That approach is not irrational. Exemption 7(A) would be undermined if agencies had to reveal sensitive investigative details simply because litigation took a long time. Still, the opinion leaves the government with an important advantage: once it has made an initial, detailed showing about a terrorism-related investigation, a relatively spare follow-up declaration may suffice to preserve the exemption.

That may be especially true where, as here, the court already has reason to think the investigation is substantial. The opinion references Farahi’s immigration proceedings, in which the government alleged ties to the Muslim Brotherhood, al Qaeda figures, and known or indicted terrorists. Those allegations are not the same as findings of fact for FOIA purposes, but they give the panel a concrete backdrop against which to view the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7(A). The practical effect is that the seriousness of the alleged conduct appears to have reinforced the court’s confidence in the agency’s withholding claim.

## Segregability gets little traction

Farahi also lost on segregability. The court reiterated the usual rule: agencies must release any reasonably segregable nonexempt material, but they do not have to perform the impossible. If nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material, or if the nonexempt bits are just scattered fragments that would not convey meaningful information, full withholding is allowed.

That part of the opinion is doctrinally unremarkable, but it fits the overall pattern. The court accepted the FBI’s ex parte explanation that no meaningful material could be separated out. And it brushed aside the plaintiff’s argument that surely something in 10,750 pages and 80 CDs must be disclosable. In FOIA litigation, that intuition often has force. Here, it did not.

## The broader significance

*Farahi* does not announce a new rule, but it reinforces an old one: in the D.C. Circuit, Exemption 7(A) is especially powerful when the FBI frames the matter as a live counterterrorism investigation. The opinion draws heavily on precedents that already give the FBI substantial room to maneuver, and it shows how those cases work in practice. The agency gets deference on whether records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes. It gets deference on whether investigations are ongoing. And it gets further deference when disclosure might reveal investigative scope or strategy in a national-security setting.

That is the kernel of the “two-track” FOIA doctrine the case points toward. In ordinary law-enforcement cases, courts may insist on more detailed category definitions, more specific explanations, and a more skeptical segregability analysis. In counterterrorism cases, the same formal standards remain in place, but the burden is easier to satisfy because the courts are more willing to credit the agency’s judgments about harm.

Whether that is good policy is debatable. The strongest defense is obvious: counterterrorism investigations are exactly the sort of matters where disclosure can compromise sources, methods, and targets. The strongest criticism is equally obvious: once courts become accustomed to accepting broad agency assurances in this area, FOIA’s transparency function can recede quickly. *Farahi* does not resolve that tension. But it does show which way the D.C. Circuit is leaning.



## Judging FOIA From Behind the Curtain: Ex Parte Declarations After *Farahi*

## Why this case matters

*Farahi v. FBI* is not a headline-grabber in the usual sense: it is a FOIA case, decided on a familiar national-security record, and the D.C. Circuit’s bottom line is unsurprising. But the opinion is important because it shows how much of modern FOIA litigation—especially under Exemption 7(A)—is decided through sealed declarations that the requester never sees, never tests, and often cannot meaningfully rebut.

That is not a bug in the D.C. Circuit’s framework so much as a feature of it. But *Farahi* illustrates the practical consequences. The court’s conclusions on both Exemption 7(A) and segregability rested heavily on ex parte submissions, and the panel was comfortable saying so. For agencies, that is reassuring. For FOIA requesters, it is a reminder that once the government invokes national security or an ongoing investigation, the real fight may move off the public record entirely.

## The court’s path to affirmance

The underlying facts help explain the result. Farahi had been in immigration proceedings for years, and the FBI had investigated alleged ties to terrorist organizations and individuals. When he sought his FBI file under FOIA, the agency withheld most of the responsive records under Exemption 7(A), which protects law-enforcement records whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

The district court initially thought the FBI “seemingly” had made its case, but it wanted fresher information because the case had been pending so long. That prompted updated declarations from Joseph Bender, including a public declaration saying the relevant investigation remained ongoing and a sealed ex parte declaration supplying more detail. The district court then granted summary judgment, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The panel’s analysis is textbook D.C. Circuit FOIA doctrine. Exemption 7(A) applies if the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The court found both showings satisfied, relying on the FBI’s public declaration, the sealed declaration, and the broader context of Farahi’s immigration and terrorism-related proceedings. It also upheld the FBI’s segregability showing, again leaning on the sealed materials to conclude that no meaningful nonexempt information was reasonably segregable.

## Ex parte declarations did the heavy lifting

What stands out is how central the ex parte declarations were to both rulings. The public Hardy declaration was enough to set the table, but the sealed declaration filled in the specifics. The court was candid that it could not disclose the contents of that filing, but it still treated the sealed material as part of the basis for concluding that the investigation was real, ongoing, and sufficiently concrete to support Exemption 7(A).

That approach is doctrinally familiar. The D.C. Circuit has long allowed in camera or ex parte review in FOIA cases when necessary to protect sensitive information, and it has repeatedly emphasized deference where the FBI invokes law-enforcement and national-security concerns. *Farahi* does not break new ground so much as it shows how little ground there sometimes is for a requester to stand on once the agency has moved into sealed territory.

The opinion is also noteworthy for the way it handles the stale-declaration problem. The district court asked for an update because years had passed since the original submission. The FBI responded with a relatively terse public update from Bender, and the court basically said that, in context, the update was enough because it was not standing alone. That is sensible as a matter of judicial administration, but it also underscores how much the outcome depends on the unshared backstory in the sealed declaration.

## Waiver did some work too

Farahi’s challenge to the use of ex parte materials was weakened by waiver and forfeiture. He had not opposed the government’s request to file the supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, and the panel said he therefore could not later complain about the district court’s reliance on those materials. Likewise, the court brushed aside his attack on the FBI’s category definitions because he did not make a specific overbreadth argument in the district court.

That part of the opinion matters beyond this case. FOIA plaintiffs often face a difficult strategic choice: object too aggressively to sealed materials and risk looking unreasonable in a case involving national security; object too little, and the issue may be waived. *Farahi* shows how those choices can shape the litigation before the merits are ever reached. Once a plaintiff has acquiesced in sealed submissions, appellate review becomes narrower still.

## The broader institutional question: is review still meaningful?

The real tension in *Farahi* is institutional. FOIA is supposed to impose a burden on the agency to justify withholding, and courts are supposed to check that burden. But how meaningful is that check when the key justifications never enter the public record?

The D.C. Circuit’s answer is essentially that meaningful review still exists because judges can examine the sealed declarations themselves. That is true as far as it goes, and in some categories of cases it may be the only workable answer. If the records really would reveal the scope of an ongoing counterterrorism investigation, then forcing disclosure of the rationale could defeat the point of the exemption.

Still, there is a cost. Ex parte review blunts the adversarial process, and FOIA cases depend heavily on adversarial testing because the requester is usually the only party pressing the presumption of disclosure. When the government’s explanation is split between a thin public declaration and a thick sealed one, the court’s confidence often rests on what it alone can see. That can be administratively necessary, but it is not the same as transparent adjudication.

The opinion’s own language hints at that discomfort. The panel acknowledged that Bender’s public update, “considered in isolation,” was “surprisingly terse.” It only survived because of the earlier sealed Hardy declaration and the context of the case. That is a respectable way to evaluate the record. But it also means that the public record, standing alone, may tell a very incomplete story about why the government gets to keep the documents secret.

## Likely downstream effects

*Farahi* likely will not change the law, but it may affect litigation strategy. Agencies will read it as a confirmation that a public declaration plus a sealed supplement can carry an Exemption 7(A) case, even after a long delay, so long as the agency can still say the investigation remains ongoing. Requesters, in turn, will need to press harder on specific deficiencies in the public record, because generalized skepticism about secrecy will not be enough.

The case also reinforces a broader D.C. Circuit trend: when national-security or terrorism-related records are at issue, the court is willing to accept a great deal of agency-managed opacity. That may be appropriate in some cases. But *Farahi* is a reminder that the question in FOIA litigation is not just whether the government has a lawful secrecy claim. It is also whether the judiciary can test that claim in a way that remains meaningfully adversarial and publicly intelligible. On that score, the answer is often: only partially.



## Segregability Without Disclosure: What *Farahi* Means for “Meaningful Information” in Investigative Files

## Why this case matters

The headline in *Farahi v. FBI* is Exemption 7(A): the D.C. Circuit agreed that the FBI could withhold records from an immigration-related investigation because disclosure could interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But the more interesting part of the opinion may be the court’s segregability analysis. That is where the court gave fresh force to an old FOIA idea: the government does not have to release scraps of text that, taken alone, would disclose little or nothing of value.

That point matters because segregability is often the last real check on sweeping withholdings. Even when an agency properly invokes an exemption, FOIA still requires release of any reasonably segregable nonexempt material. Requesters therefore often try to win the margins: maybe the file is protected overall, but some portions can still come out. *Farahi* shows how difficult that argument becomes when the records are investigative, the file is large, and the agency can persuade a court that the exempt and nonexempt material are too intertwined to separate in any meaningful way.

## The court’s core move

The D.C. Circuit’s discussion of segregability leaned heavily on *Mead Data*. That case, long a staple in FOIA law, made the point that FOIA is about “information,” not merely words on paper. In *Farahi*, Judge Katsas used that principle to reject the notion that the presence of isolated nonexempt words or phrases necessarily triggers disclosure. If the material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” the court said, those fragments are not likely to be reasonably segregable. Individual words or phrases might technically be removable, but if what remains would not convey meaningful information, FOIA does not require the agency to release the husk.

That is a practical holding, and a realistic one. Investigative files rarely come neatly divided into sealed and unsealed compartments. They tend to include narrative summaries, agent notes, references to sources, leads, and cross-links among subjects and events. If an agency can show that the exempt material is woven throughout the document structure, a court is likely to accept that sorting out tiny public fragments would produce little more than a puzzle with no answer.

The FBI’s submissions mattered here. The district court first found the agency’s explanation incomplete and ordered updated declarations because the earlier ones had gone stale. The FBI then supplied a public declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender and an ex parte declaration with additional detail, which the court found sufficient on segregability. The D.C. Circuit accepted that approach. It emphasized that nothing required the court to put the substance of the ex parte materials on the public record, especially where Farahi had not objected to the use of ex parte declarations in the first place.

## “Meaningful information” as the limiting principle

The most consequential part of the opinion is not just that the court deferred to the FBI, but how it framed what counts as releasable material. The court expressly rejected the idea that the mere existence of isolated, nonexempt words or phrases is enough. That is important because it narrows the force of a common requester argument: that if a file contains any nonexempt bits, the agency must release them.

Not so, at least not if those bits are stripped of context and do not meaningfully inform the requester. The court’s logic tracks *Mead*, but it also reflects a broader judicial instinct in law-enforcement FOIA cases: courts are reluctant to force agencies to release breadcrumb-level disclosures that could still reveal how an investigation was assembled, what kinds of information it contains, or how the government is thinking about the target and associated subjects.

There is a cost to that approach. “Meaningful information” is a flexible standard, and flexibility can shade into deference. Once a court accepts that the point of disclosure is not merely textual visibility but informational value, agencies have room to argue that almost anything less than a substantive narrative is nonsegregable. In a case like *Farahi*, where the file allegedly reflects national-security and terrorism-related investigations, the court plainly saw those concerns as heightened.

## Why the size of the file mattered

This was not a small or tidy record request. The FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. That scale matters because segregation becomes exponentially harder as the volume of material grows and as the records become more internally cross-referenced. The larger the file, the easier it is for an agency to argue that the exempt and nonexempt content are too interwoven to separate without revealing the very material the exemption protects.

The opinion suggests that courts will be receptive to that claim when the agency supports it with reasonably specific declarations. It also suggests that requesters who hope to contest segregability in such cases need more than a general insistence that “somewhere in here, something must be releasable.” Farahi argued that there must be some segregable material in such a large file, but the court found that too abstract. If a requester wants to defeat categorical withholding, the challenge needs to identify why the agency’s categories are overbroad or why the explanation fails on its own terms.

That is a significant burden-shifting practical effect. The government still bears the legal burden, of course. But once it offers a plausible segregability explanation—especially one buttressed by ex parte review—requesters often need concrete objections to specific document categories or specific factual gaps. Broad skepticism will not do.

## The downstream effect for FOIA litigants

*Farahi* is likely to be cited by agencies defending withheld investigative files, especially in national-security-adjacent matters. The opinion reinforces three themes that matter a great deal in practice. First, courts will continue to give substantial deference to the FBI and similar law-enforcement agencies when they describe ongoing investigations. Second, segregability is not a license for token disclosure; courts will not require release of isolated words or phrases if they do not amount to meaningful information. Third, requesters who do not challenge the agency’s categorizations with some specificity risk forfeiting the argument altogether.

That does not mean segregability is dead. It does mean the doctrine is most useful when paired with concrete, document-specific arguments. *Farahi* teaches that in large investigative files, the segregability fight will usually be won or lost on the quality of the agency’s declarations and the requester’s ability to show that the government’s “interwoven” story is too convenient. Absent that, *Mead Data*’s promise of partial disclosure can shrink quickly into a very small thing indeed.
