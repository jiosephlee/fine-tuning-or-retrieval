Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# FOIA’s Disclosure Mandate and the Exemption 7(A) Framework

## FOIA’s basic obligation to disclose agency records unless a statutory exemption applies

FOIA begins with a broad rule of disclosure. A federal agency must release records responsive to a proper request unless a specific statutory exemption allows it to withhold them. In this case, that basic structure matters because Farahi asked for records from his FBI file, and the FBI withheld most of what it found. The dispute therefore turned not on whether FOIA generally favors release, but on whether the FBI could fit the requested records within an exemption.

FOIA litigation also assigns the burden of proof to the government. When an agency withholds records, it must justify the withholding, and a district court reviews the dispute on summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit reviews that ruling de novo, meaning it looks at the matter anew rather than deferring to the district court’s legal conclusions. But even in de novo review, the agency usually carries its burden through declarations explaining what the records are and why they fit a claimed exemption. That is why the Hardy and Bender declarations were so important in this case.

## The text of Exemption 7(A): law-enforcement records that could interfere with enforcement proceedings

Exemption 7(A) permits withholding of records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” That language contains two separate requirements. First, the records must have a law-enforcement purpose. Second, release must create a reasonable risk of interference with enforcement proceedings. The exemption is therefore not a blanket authorization to withhold anything the FBI wants to keep secret; the agency must connect the records to law enforcement and show a concrete risk of harm from disclosure.

The opinion also reminds the reader that the FBI receives some deference on the first part of this inquiry because it is a law-enforcement agency. Still, deference is not the same as automatic acceptance. The agency’s declarations must establish a rational link between the records and an investigation or other law-enforcement duty, as well as a connection to a possible violation of federal law or a security risk. In other words, the FBI cannot simply invoke its name; it must explain why the records were created in the course of a legitimate investigative function.

## The D.C. Circuit’s gloss: pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings

The D.C. Circuit has added an important gloss to Exemption 7(A): the relevant enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” That requirement gives the statutory phrase “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” practical content. It means the exemption is tied to actual or imminent law-enforcement activity, not just a speculative possibility that some proceeding might someday exist.

This court has also treated ongoing criminal investigations as “proceedings” for purposes of Exemption 7(A). That is crucial because an investigation can be protected even before charges are filed. The reason is intuitive: disclosure during an active investigation can tip off subjects, reveal lines of inquiry, expose witnesses, or allow targets to destroy evidence or adjust their conduct. If the investigation is still gathering evidence for a possible future case, and premature disclosure would jeopardize that effort, the exemption can apply.

In this case, that gloss mattered because the FBI did not need to show a completed prosecution. It needed to show that the records related to continuing investigative activity that was still tied to possible enforcement. The opinion treats the existence of an ongoing investigation as enough, so long as the agency’s declarations make that investigation concrete rather than vague.

## How agency declarations function in FOIA litigation

FOIA cases often rise or fall on agency declarations. Because the very records at issue are usually withheld, the court cannot simply inspect them openly and may need to rely on sworn statements from agency officials. Those declarations describe the records, identify the exemption, and explain why disclosure would cause the type of harm the statute contemplates. In this case, the FBI first submitted a declaration from David Hardy, and later updated its showing through a declaration from Joseph Bender.

The court’s concern in FOIA litigation is whether those declarations are sufficiently specific. A declaration cannot rely on empty conclusions; it has to provide enough detail for the judge to evaluate the claim without revealing the sensitive information itself. That is why the district court here required the FBI to refresh its evidence when time had passed. A declaration that may have been adequate when filed can become stale if it no longer shows that the relevant proceedings are still pending or reasonably anticipated.

FOIA cases may also involve ex parte declarations, meaning submissions shown to the judge but not to the requester. That procedure is unusual in ordinary civil litigation, but it is sometimes necessary in FOIA cases because a public explanation might itself disclose the very information the agency is trying to protect. The D.C. Circuit recognizes that this creates obvious fairness problems for the requester, but it also recognizes that some disputes cannot be resolved any other way if sensitive material is to remain secret.

## FOIA’s segregability rule: release what can be separated from exempt material

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA imposes an independent duty of segregability. The agency must take reasonable steps to separate and release any nonexempt information that can be disclosed without revealing exempt material. The statute’s focus is on information, not simply on pages or words. So an agency cannot withhold an entire record just because it contains some protected material somewhere inside it.

At the same time, segregability has limits. If the nonexempt material is so intertwined with exempt material that meaningful disclosure would not be possible, the agency need not release fragments simply because they are technically nonsecret. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly said that when exempt and nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined, the agency may withhold the document in full. The point is practical: FOIA does not require the government to carve out meaningless scraps that would not convey useful information or that would reveal exempt material by context.

That is the issue Farahi pressed in this case when he argued that, given the sheer size of the FBI’s production, there had to be some segregable material somewhere. But the doctrine does not work at that level of abstraction. The agency must show, with reasonable specificity, why further segregation is not feasible, and the court may rely on declarations addressing that question. If a record consists of sensitive material with only scattered, line-by-line nonexempt phrases, those phrases may not be “reasonably segregable” in any meaningful sense.

## Category-based withholding and the use of sufficiently distinct categories

Agencies sometimes justify withholdings on a category-by-category basis rather than discussing each document individually. That method is efficient in large FOIA cases, but it must still allow meaningful judicial review. The categories must be sufficiently distinct so that a court can determine whether the claimed exemptions fit the material grouped within each category. If the categories are too vague or too broad, the court may not be able to tell whether the agency has properly applied the exemption.

This framework explains why the FBI’s declarations mattered here. The declarations did not merely announce that the withheld records were sensitive; they attempted to explain how the records were organized and why disclosure by category would still create a risk of interference. Farahi challenged that approach only briefly, and the court treated the challenge as forfeited because he did not develop a concrete argument about why the categories were overinclusive.

## Deference to the FBI, especially in national-security and counterterrorism cases

The opinion gives special emphasis to deference in this setting because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency and because the records implicated national security and counterterrorism concerns. The D.C. Circuit has long said that the FBI’s judgment under Exemption 7 deserves a fair measure of deference, and that deference becomes even more pronounced when disclosure could affect sensitive national-security investigations. The court’s role is still real—it must ensure that the agency has met its statutory burden—but it is not supposed to second-guess investigative judgments lightly.

That background helps explain the court’s willingness to credit the FBI’s updated declarations here. The agency’s public filings said the investigation remained ongoing, and the sealed materials supplied greater detail to the district court. Taken together, those submissions gave the court a basis to conclude that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that release could interfere with concrete, continuing enforcement activity. The legal framework of Exemption 7(A), segregability, and deference set the stage for the court’s ultimate decision to affirm withholding.

# Farahi’s FBI File Request and the Long Path to Appeal

## Farahi’s background as an Iranian national living in the United States and his placement in removal proceedings

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. His dispute with the FBI did not arise in a vacuum. It came against the backdrop of long-running immigration proceedings that began in 2002, when the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings because he had overstayed his visa. Those proceedings stretched on for years and involved multiple requests for relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.

For a reader trying to follow the FOIA litigation, the immigration background matters because it explains why Farahi wanted his FBI file and why the government said the file remained sensitive. The FBI’s records were tied to investigations relevant to his immigration case, so the file was not a routine personnel or administrative record. It was part of a broader law-enforcement and national-security context.

## The role of terrorism-related allegations in Farahi’s immigration proceedings

The opinion notes that, in opposing relief in the immigration case, DHS relied on documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who said he had documented Farahi’s connections to “high-level terrorists.” That detail is important not because the court is resolving the truth of those allegations in this FOIA case, but because it shows the kind of investigation at issue. The FBI’s asserted reason for withholding records was that they were connected to national-security and terrorism investigations.

The opinion also references a later Eleventh Circuit decision in Farahi’s immigration litigation, which summarized the government’s allegations in more detail. Those allegations included claimed ties to designated terrorist organizations and to individuals associated with terrorism-related crimes. Again, the D.C. Circuit does not adjudicate those allegations in this opinion; it uses them to provide context for why the FBI said the records were part of an ongoing law-enforcement matter.

## Farahi’s FOIA request for his FBI file and the breadth of potentially responsive material: thousands of pages and dozens of CDs

Farahi filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. FOIA gives the public a right to request agency records, but agencies may withhold material that falls within a statutory exemption. Here, the FBI located a very large amount of potentially responsive material: about 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. That is a significant volume, and it foreshadowed the complexity of the later segregability dispute, because the agency would need to review a substantial collection of records to determine what, if anything, could be disclosed.

The FBI did not immediately turn over the file. Once Farahi sued, the agency continued processing the records over several years. In a FOIA case, this kind of staged review is common when a request is large or sensitive, but it can make the litigation slow and document-intensive. By the time the FBI finished its review, it had disclosed some records and withheld most of the material.

## The FBI’s delayed response, partial disclosures, and extensive withholdings

Farahi filed suit after the FBI missed FOIA’s statutory deadline for responding. That deadline does not necessarily mean the requester wins automatically, but it does permit litigation when the agency fails to act in time. After suit was filed, the agency reviewed the potentially responsive material, produced some records, and withheld the rest. Its position was that the withheld records fell within FOIA’s law-enforcement exemption for records compiled for law-enforcement purposes when disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

The record review was lengthy enough that timing became a recurring issue. By the time the district court first considered summary judgment, several years had passed since the FBI’s initial declarations. That passage of time later became central to the district court’s concern that its evidence about ongoing enforcement proceedings might be stale.

## The FBI’s first summary-judgment motion supported by David Hardy’s public declaration and sealed ex parte supplement

After completing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019. In FOIA litigation, summary judgment is the usual vehicle for resolving whether the agency has properly withheld records. The agency bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies. To support that burden, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Records/Information Dissemination Section.

Hardy’s public declaration said the withheld records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that releasing them could interfere with enforcement proceedings that were pending or reasonably anticipated. The FBI also moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, meaning the court could review it without disclosing its contents to Farahi. Farahi did not oppose that request, and the district court allowed it. This is an important procedural feature of many FOIA cases: if the agency says the records themselves are sensitive, it may submit classified or otherwise protected explanations to the judge alone so the court can test the exemption claim without revealing the disputed material.

## The district court’s 2022 ruling: provisional acceptance of Exemption 7(A), concern about staleness, and request for updated submissions

In November 2022, the district court addressed the FBI’s first summary-judgment motion. It concluded that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that the FBI had seemingly shown that their release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But the court was not fully satisfied with the age of the declarations. Because a significant amount of time had passed since the FBI first made its showing, the court wanted an updated assessment of whether the relevant enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated.

The court also concluded that it needed more information to assess segregability, which is FOIA’s requirement that agencies release any reasonably segregable nonexempt material. The court said it could not explain some of its concerns on the public record. As a result, it denied summary judgment without prejudice. That procedural move did not reject the FBI’s exemption claim outright; instead, it gave the agency another opportunity to update and refine its showing.

## The FBI’s renewed motion supported by Joseph Bender’s public and ex parte declarations

The FBI responded with updated declarations and renewed its summary-judgment motion. This time, it relied on Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration. In the public filing, Bender explained that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for Farahi’s file, and that agent confirmed the investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte submission supplied additional details about the ongoing investigation and also addressed the district court’s segregability concerns.

This second round of declarations mattered because it addressed the district court’s staleness concern directly. Instead of relying only on the older Hardy declarations, the FBI provided a newer confirmation that the relevant proceedings had not gone dormant while the case was pending. It also gave the court more detail, behind the ex parte process, about why the withheld material could not be meaningfully separated from nonexempt information.

## The district court’s final grant of summary judgment and Farahi’s appeal

After reviewing the renewed submissions, the district court granted summary judgment to the FBI. It credited the agency’s confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing and, based on its review of the ex parte materials, concluded that the FBI had adequately explained why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. In other words, the court held that the agency had done enough to justify withholding the records under Exemption 7(A) and to satisfy its obligation to consider releasing any nonexempt portions.

Farahi appealed. That appeal set up the D.C. Circuit’s review of whether the FBI had carried its FOIA burden, whether the agency had shown that enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, and whether the district court properly accepted the agency’s segregability showing. The opinion’s later sections address those legal questions, but this procedural history explains how the case got there.

# Appellate Review and the First Exemption 7(A) Requirement: Records Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes

## De novo appellate review of summary judgment in FOIA cases

The court began by reminding the reader how FOIA cases come up on appeal. Like any summary-judgment ruling, a FOIA summary judgment is reviewed de novo. That means the court of appeals does not defer to the district court’s legal conclusions; instead, it asks for itself whether the record entitles the government to judgment as a matter of law. In FOIA litigation, this matters because the agency is the party claiming an exemption from the statute’s general rule of disclosure, so the agency must justify withholding with evidence.

## The government’s burden to justify withholding under a FOIA exemption

FOIA starts from a presumption of disclosure. The government may keep records secret only if it proves that one of the statute’s exemptions applies. The court stated that burden explicitly: under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), the agency must show that the withheld records fall within Exemption 7(A). At this stage, that means the FBI had to establish not only that the records were part of a law-enforcement file, but also that the exemption’s interference requirement could be satisfied. In other words, the agency does not win merely by asserting secrecy; it must tie the records to the statutory language.

## The FBI’s law-enforcement status and the resulting measure of judicial deference

The court then explained the special position of the FBI. Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, courts give its Exemption 7 determinations a “fair measure” of deference. That does not eliminate judicial review, but it does affect how closely the court scrutinizes the agency’s description of its records and investigations. The court also noted that this deference is especially strong when national security is implicated. Here, the records concerned national security and international terrorism, so the court treated the FBI’s account with particular caution and respect for the executive branch’s expertise.

## The Pratt/Campbell nexus requirement: tying the investigation to an FBI law-enforcement duty and a possible security risk or violation of law

Even with deference, the FBI still had to meet the threshold requirement that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The court relied on its earlier decisions in Campbell and Pratt for the governing test. The declarations had to establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the FBI’s law-enforcement duties, and also connect the investigation to an individual or incident that posed a possible security risk or suggested a possible violation of federal law.

This requirement serves an important screening function. It prevents an agency from labeling ordinary administrative records as law-enforcement records simply because they are held by a law-enforcement agency. The question is not whether the FBI possesses the records, but whether the records were assembled in the course of a legitimate law-enforcement inquiry. For the FBI, investigations of terrorism, national security threats, and related criminal or protective matters easily fit that framework when the declarations explain the connection.

## What the Hardy declarations said about investigations involving national security, international terrorism, and related proceedings

The court found that the FBI’s declarations did exactly what the law requires. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” including pending law-enforcement proceedings and Farahi’s associations with people directly involved in those violations. That was enough to place the file within the law-enforcement category at a basic level.

The sealed, ex parte Hardy declaration went further. Although the court could not describe its contents publicly, it said the sealed submission supplied “considerable detail” about the investigation’s nature. That detail mattered because it supported the agency’s public assertion that the file was not just an immigration or administrative file, but part of an investigation into terrorism- and national security-related conduct. The court was willing to rely on that sealed material, especially because FOIA cases sometimes require ex parte review to avoid revealing the very material the government claims is exempt.

## How Farahi’s own concessions in district court reinforced the law-enforcement character of the file

The court also relied on Farahi’s own litigation position in the district court. He conceded that the FBI had investigated him for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and convicted on terrorism-related charges. Those concessions mattered because they independently confirmed the investigative context described in the FBI’s declarations.

This is a good example of how a FOIA court may use the whole record, not just the agency’s formal declarations, to decide whether the statutory threshold is met. Farahi’s admissions made it even harder to dispute that the records were gathered in a bona fide law-enforcement investigation. They showed that the file related to suspected terrorist associations, not to a routine administrative matter.

## Why the court found no serious doubt that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes

Putting the pieces together, the court had little trouble concluding that the first Exemption 7(A) requirement was satisfied. The FBI’s declarations described an investigation into national security and international terrorism violations; the sealed submission added detail; and Farahi’s own concessions confirmed the seriousness and law-enforcement nature of the inquiry. Against that background, the court had “no doubt” that the FBI met its burden to show that Farahi’s file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes.

That conclusion mattered because Exemption 7(A) does not protect every record in an FBI file. The agency first has to show that the records belong to a genuine law-enforcement compilation. Only after that does the court ask the second question: whether disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. In this part of the opinion, the court was only resolving the threshold issue, and it did so by relying on the FBI’s declarations, the national-security context, and Farahi’s own concessions.

# The Second Exemption 7(A) Requirement: Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

## The interference requirement and a live enforcement proceeding

Exemption 7(A) does not protect law-enforcement records just because they are sensitive or were gathered by the FBI. The agency also has to show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” In this circuit, that means there must be a real, concrete enforcement matter still alive when the court rules. It does not have to be a filed criminal case; an ongoing investigation can qualify if it is still gathering evidence for a possible future prosecution or other enforcement action.

That is the frame the D.C. Circuit applied here. The court treated the key question as whether the FBI had shown that Farahi’s file related to an enforcement proceeding that was still ongoing, or at least reasonably anticipated, at the time of the renewed summary-judgment motion. Because FOIA litigation can last for years, the age of the declarations mattered. The district court had already been uneasy about relying on older affidavits, so it required the FBI to update its showing.

## Why disclosure would interfere

The court accepted a familiar Exemption 7(A) rationale: releasing records from an ongoing investigation can tip off targets about what the agency knows, what it is looking for, and where the investigation is headed. That kind of disclosure can let the subject conceal evidence, alter behavior, or otherwise frustrate law enforcement efforts.

The FBI’s public declaration said exactly that. It explained that disclosure of information about the ongoing investigation could reveal the scope of the inquiry and thus interfere with it. The court viewed that as a standard and legally sufficient interference theory under Exemption 7(A), especially in a counterterrorism context.

## The Hardy declaration and the importance of the ex parte detail

The first Hardy declaration did two important things. First, it said the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” including pending proceedings and Farahi’s associations with individuals involved in those violations. Second, the FBI supported that public declaration with a sealed ex parte declaration giving the district court more detail about the investigation.

That mattered because the public record alone was necessarily limited. The ex parte submission helped the court verify that the FBI was not making a bare or abstract claim of ongoing investigations. When the district court later worried that the showing might have gone stale, it was not starting from zero; it had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, and it only wanted confirmation that the investigation was still active.

## Why Bender was treated as an update, not a standalone justification

Farahi’s central attack was that the later Bender declaration was too terse. Bender said that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” which, standing alone, might sound conclusory. The court agreed that the statement was short, but it refused to read it in isolation.

Instead, the court treated Bender’s declaration as an update to the Hardy declarations. The FBI was not using Bender to establish pendency from scratch; it was using him to confirm that nothing had changed while the case was pending in district court. That procedural context mattered. The district court had already indicated that the earlier submissions had basically satisfied Exemption 7(A), and the new declaration was meant to refresh the record after time had passed.

The court also emphasized deference to the FBI, particularly where national-security and counterterrorism investigations are involved. The public record, plus the sealed submissions, made it plausible that the agency was still pursuing a concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding. The court therefore rejected the notion that the passage of time, by itself, defeated Exemption 7(A).

## Farahi’s CREW argument and the court’s response to staleness

Farahi relied on the D.C. Circuit’s warning in CREW that vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may not be enough. The court did not dispute that principle. But it distinguished this case on the ground that the FBI’s showing was not vague in the way CREW condemned.

Here, the government had already explained the nature of the investigation, the public Hardy declaration tied the records to national-security and terrorism inquiries, and the ex parte declaration supplied additional detail. Against that backdrop, Bender’s brief update was enough to show continuity. The court also pointed to the breadth of the underlying investigation—publicly described as involving alleged ties to the Muslim Brotherhood, Al Qaeda figures, convicted or indicted terrorists, and terrorism-financing charities—as further support for the FBI’s position that the matter was still live.

The bottom line was that the court was satisfied there remained a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” That satisfied the second half of Exemption 7(A).

## The challenge to the FBI’s document categories was forfeited

Farahi also briefly argued that the FBI had not adequately justified the categories it used to describe the withheld records. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged the rule that, when an agency withholds records category by category, the categories must be distinct enough for a court to assess whether the exemptions fit. But Farahi never explained which categories were overbroad or why.

Because he offered only a general objection, the court treated the argument as forfeited. This is a good reminder that FOIA plaintiffs need to make specific challenges to an agency’s withholding methodology. A bare assertion that the categories are too broad is not enough to preserve the issue for appellate review.

## The holding on Exemption 7(A)

On the interference issue, the court held that the FBI met its burden. The declarations showed that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with an ongoing, concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding. The combination of the Hardy and Bender declarations, together with the public and sealed record, was enough to support withholding under Exemption 7(A).

# Segregability, Ex Parte Submissions, and the Case’s Final Significance

## FOIA’s Separate Duty to Release Anything Reasonably Segregable

Even after an agency shows that an exemption applies, FOIA does not allow it to withhold a record wholesale unless the exempt and nonexempt material cannot reasonably be separated. That is why the statute independently requires agencies to take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information, and to disclose any reasonably segregable portion of a record after deleting the exempt portions. In other words, winning on Exemption 7(A) does not end the inquiry. The court still has to ask whether something meaningful can be released without undermining the protected law-enforcement interests.

That requirement matters here because the FBI withheld most of Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A), but the district court wanted a separate explanation for why the records could not be carved up any further. The opinion shows the court treating segregability as a real, independent question rather than as a formality once an exemption has been established.

## Why the District Court Asked for More on Segregability

The district court initially accepted that the FBI had “seemingly” shown that the withheld records were covered by Exemption 7(A), but it was not satisfied that the record then before it adequately addressed segregability. That concern was tied to the passage of time. The agency’s first showing was based on declarations that had become stale while the case was pending, so the court ordered updated evidence not only on whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, but also on whether any nonexempt material could be separated out.

That procedural step is important to understand. The court was not rejecting the FBI’s exemption claim; it was asking whether the claim remained current and whether the agency had explained, with enough specificity, why nothing reasonably segregable could be released. FOIA courts often require this kind of up-to-date showing when litigation drags on, especially where the existence of ongoing investigations can change over time.

## Bender’s Supplemental Ex Parte Showing and the Mead Data Principle

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. His public declaration confirmed that the relevant investigation was still ongoing, and his ex parte declaration provided additional detail both on the investigation and on why the withheld material could not be meaningfully segregated. The district court credited that explanation and concluded that the FBI had satisfied its burden.

The court’s segregation analysis rests on a familiar FOIA idea: an agency does not have to release isolated scraps of text if those scraps are so intertwined with exempt material that their disclosure would not yield meaningful information. The opinion invokes Mead Data for the proposition that nonexempt material need not be released when it is interspersed line by line with exempt material, and where the focus of FOIA is information rather than simply written words. That distinction matters. Farahi argued, in essence, that because the FBI had a huge volume of responsive material, there must be some releasable content somewhere in it. The court rejected that intuition. A large file does not automatically mean there is something “reasonably segregable” in a practical sense. If the nonexempt material consists only of scattered words or phrases that cannot be disclosed without revealing protected law-enforcement information, FOIA does not require release.

So the court accepted the FBI’s position that, while a few isolated words or phrases might be technically nonexempt, they did not amount to meaningful information that could be separated out and disclosed without harming the investigation. That is why the opinion says the district court correctly concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

## Why the Court Accepted Ex Parte Declarations

A striking feature of the case is the FBI’s use of ex parte declarations, meaning material shown to the judge but not to Farahi. The court treated that as permissible here because the point of the declaration was to explain why the withheld material had to stay secret. In a FOIA case involving sensitive law-enforcement records, an ex parte submission may be the only way to give the court enough information to decide the dispute without revealing the very material the agency claims is exempt.

The opinion also emphasizes preservation. Farahi had not opposed the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations when the FBI sought that procedure, so he could not later complain that the court relied on them. That procedural point is easy to miss but important: appellate courts generally do not rescue a party from an objection that was never properly made below. The court also noted that ex parte review is an accepted, if imperfect, feature of FOIA litigation when secrecy is at issue. The disadvantage to the requester is real, but it is inherent in cases where public disclosure would itself defeat the exemption claim.

## The Final Disposition and What the Opinion Shows

Once the court accepted the updated declarations, the rest of the case followed. The FBI had shown that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes, that the investigation remained ongoing, that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with that investigation, and that no meaningful segregable material remained. Farahi’s challenge to the category definitions of withheld records failed because he did not develop a specific argument about overbreadth, and his segregability objection failed for the reasons above. The D.C. Circuit therefore affirmed summary judgment for the FBI.

The broader lesson is not that agencies automatically win when they invoke national-security concerns. Rather, this opinion shows how FOIA litigation over active counterterrorism or other sensitive law-enforcement files often turns on the quality and freshness of the agency’s declarations. A stale or conclusory submission may not suffice, which is why the district court required updates here. But once the agency supplies a sufficiently detailed public and sealed showing, the court will defer to the FBI’s expertise, especially where disclosure could tip off targets, compromise an ongoing investigation, or reveal the very contours of the file the requester seeks.
