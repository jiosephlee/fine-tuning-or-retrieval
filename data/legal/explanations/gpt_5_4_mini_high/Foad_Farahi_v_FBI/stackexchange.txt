Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Why didn't the FBI's failure to meet FOIA's response deadline require disclosure here?
Question:
The opinion says Farahi sued after the FBI missed FOIA's statutory deadline, but the case still turned entirely on whether Exemption 7(A) applied. I thought FOIA imposed strict time limits on agencies. If the FBI blew the deadline, why wasn't that enough to force production of the records? Is the only consequence that the requester can sue immediately, rather than any waiver of exemptions?
Answer:
Because FOIA’s deadline is a **procedural trigger**, not a **substantive forfeiture rule**.

Under FOIA, if the agency misses the statutory response deadline, the requester may treat administrative remedies as **constructively exhausted** and go straight to court. That is why Farahi was able to sue when the FBI was late. But the deadline violation does **not** mean the agency automatically must disclose the records, and it does **not** waive otherwise valid exemptions.

That is exactly why this opinion focuses on **Exemption 7(A)**. Once the case is in court, the question becomes the usual FOIA question: has the agency carried its burden to show that the records fall within an exemption? The court says the FBI did so here by showing that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

So the short answer is:

- **Missing the deadline lets the requester sue sooner**
- **It does not eliminate the agency’s ability to invoke FOIA exemptions**
- **If an exemption applies, the records may still be withheld despite the late response**

The opinion is consistent with that framework throughout: Farahi sued because the FBI was late, but the merits turned entirely on whether **Exemption 7(A)** applied, not on the missed deadline itself.

### How can appellate review be de novo if FOIA courts also defer to FBI and national-security declarations?
Question:
The D.C. Circuit says it reviews summary judgment de novo, but then it also says the FBI gets deference because it is a law-enforcement agency, and even more deference when national security is involved. I'm having trouble reconciling those ideas. If review is truly de novo, what exactly is the court independently deciding, and what parts of the agency's affidavit-based showing are effectively taken at face value?
Answer:
The short answer is: **“de novo” describes the appellate court’s review of the district court’s judgment, not a refusal to credit agency expertise or sworn declarations.** In FOIA cases, those are different questions.

### 1. What “de novo” means here
When the D.C. Circuit says it reviews summary judgment **de novo**, it means it independently asks whether the **record** shows that the FBI carried its burden under FOIA. It does **not** defer to the district court’s legal conclusions.

So the appellate court itself decides:

- whether the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes”;
- whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings;
- whether the agency adequately addressed **segregability**.

That is exactly how the opinion frames the inquiry in Parts III–V.

### 2. What the “deference” is to
The deference is **not to the district court**. It is to the **FBI’s sworn declarations** because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency and the case implicates national security.

The opinion says:

- “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.”
- “Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.”

That means courts will generally credit agency affidavits on matters within the agency’s expertise—especially predictive judgments about investigative harm—so long as the affidavits are sufficiently specific.

### 3. Deference does not mean “rubber stamp”
The court is still independently checking whether the declarations are good enough. The opinion makes that clear by saying the declarations must:

- establish a **“rational nexus”** between the investigation and a law-enforcement duty; and
- provide **“reasonable specificity”**.

And the court did not just accept anything the FBI said. The district court initially thought the record might be stale and required **updated declarations**. The D.C. Circuit also noted that the later Bender declaration, “considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse,” but it was adequate **in context** with the earlier Hardy declarations.

### 4. What the court effectively accepts at face value
The court is willing to accept, absent contradiction or obvious deficiency, the FBI’s sworn factual representations about things like:

- that an investigation is ongoing;
- that disclosure could tip off targets or interfere with that investigation;
- that the withheld material is tied to law-enforcement activity;
- that nonexempt material is not reasonably segregable from exempt material.

But it accepts those points only because the declarations are detailed enough and fit the surrounding record. The court is **not** accepting bare conclusions. That is why the opinion distinguishes between a conclusory declaration and one supported by “considerable detail” and corroborating context.

### Bottom line
So the reconciliation is:

- **De novo review** = the court independently decides whether the FBI has legally justified withholding.
- **Deference to FBI/national-security declarations** = within that independent review, the court gives weight to the agency’s specialized, affidavit-based judgments, but only if they are specific and plausible enough to meet FOIA’s burden.

That is the standard the opinion applies in upholding the FBI’s Exemption 7(A) withholding.

### What does 'compiled for law enforcement purposes' mean when the FBI says the file involves national security and terrorism?
Question:
The court says Exemption 7(A) first requires the records to be 'compiled for law enforcement purposes,' and it cites the Pratt/Campbell requirement of a rational nexus to law-enforcement duties and a connection to a possible violation of law or security risk. In this case, the FBI referred to investigations of national security and international terrorism, plus Farahi's associations. Is that enough because terrorism is inherently criminal, or did the court need something more concrete than broad national-security concerns? I'm confused about where the line is between intelligence gathering and law enforcement for Exemption 7 purposes.
Answer:
Short answer: **the court required more than a bare “national security” label, but not a full merits showing of criminality.** It accepted the FBI’s showing because the records were tied to a **specific terrorism/national-security investigation** involving possible violations of law and the subject’s associations with individuals involved in those violations.

### The doctrinal point

For Exemption 7(A), the FBI had to show two things:

1. the records were **“compiled for law enforcement purposes,”** and  
2. disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with **pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings**.

On the first prong, the D.C. Circuit reiterated the usual **Pratt/Campbell** requirement: the FBI must show a **“rational nexus”** between the records and one of its law-enforcement duties, plus a connection between the investigation and a **possible violation of federal law or security risk**.

### What the court thought was enough here

The court did **not** say that “terrorism is inherently criminal, therefore everything labeled terrorism is automatically Exemption 7 material.” Instead, it relied on several concrete points:

- the public Hardy declaration said the records were compiled **“in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism”**;
- those investigations included **pending law-enforcement proceedings**;
- the records concerned **Farahi’s association with individuals directly involved** in those violations;
- the ex parte declaration added **considerable detail** about the investigation; and
- the broader context showed Farahi had been linked to terrorist organizations and individuals accused or convicted of terrorism-related conduct.

That is why the court said: **“We have no doubt that the FBI carried its burden”** on the law-enforcement-purpose requirement.

### So where is the line?

The line is between:

- **general intelligence or national-security interest**, which by itself is not enough, and
- records compiled as part of a **particular investigative or enforcement function** aimed at possible legal or security violations.

Here, the FBI crossed that line because it identified a **specific ongoing investigation into violations of national security and international terrorism**, not merely a broad intelligence-gathering mission.

### Bottom line

So the answer is: **the court needed something more concrete than broad national-security concerns, and it got it.** The opinion treats “national security and international terrorism” as sufficient **only because** the FBI tied that language to an actual investigation, identified associations relevant to that investigation, and supplied more detail in sealed declarations. Deference helped, but it did not replace the nexus requirement.

### Can an ongoing investigation by itself count as an 'enforcement proceeding' under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
I usually think of an 'enforcement proceeding' as a prosecution, an administrative case, or maybe immigration removal proceedings. But this opinion says that ongoing criminal investigations themselves count as proceedings for Exemption 7(A), so long as they are pending or reasonably anticipated. Is that a D.C. Circuit-specific rule, and how concrete does the future case have to be? For example, does the government need to identify a likely charge or tribunal, or is it enough to say it is still gathering evidence for a possible future case?
Answer:
Yes—in the D.C. Circuit, an ongoing investigation can itself be the “enforcement proceeding” for Exemption 7(A), even before any indictment or formal case is filed.

That is not new in *Farahi*; the court is applying its earlier cases, especially *CREW* and *Juarez*. The opinion says:

> “Ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes,” and  
> “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.”

So the government does **not** have to identify a specific charge, indictment, or tribunal. What it must show is a **concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding**—meaning a real, ongoing investigation aimed at a possible future case, plus a plausible explanation of how disclosure would interfere.

But the standard is not satisfied by a bare, conclusory “trust us, it’s ongoing.” *CREW* cautions that a “vague” assertion of an ongoing investigation may be insufficient. In *Farahi*, the FBI did more than that: it said the investigation was ongoing, supplied sealed detail about its scope, and explained publicly that disclosure could tip off targets about the investigation.

So the short answer is:

- **Yes, in the D.C. Circuit an ongoing investigation can count as a 7(A) proceeding.**
- **No, the agency does not need to name the eventual charge or forum.**
- **But yes, it must show more than speculation; it needs a real, ongoing investigation and a concrete interference theory.**

### Why was the 2023 Bender declaration enough when it sounds so conclusory about the investigation remaining ongoing?
Question:
Farahi argued that too much time had passed and that the updated Bender declaration was very terse, just saying the relevant investigations 'remain ongoing.' The court basically agreed that it looked sparse in isolation, but then treated it as an update to the earlier, more detailed Hardy declarations. Is that a normal way for courts to evaluate agency affidavits in FOIA cases? At what point does an 'update' become too conclusory to carry the government's burden, especially where years have passed since the original showing?
Answer:
Yes — that is a pretty normal way for a court to evaluate FOIA declarations.

The key point in this opinion is that the D.C. Circuit did **not** treat the 2023 Bender declaration as a standalone affidavit. It read Bender as an **update to the earlier Hardy declarations**, which had already done the heavy lifting:

- the **public Hardy declaration** said the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations of national security and terrorism violations and that disclosure could interfere with **pending law enforcement proceedings**;
- the **ex parte Hardy declaration** supplied “considerable detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation;
- the district court had already found the FBI had “seemingly” shown pending proceedings, and only asked for a **current update** because time had passed.

Against that backdrop, Bender’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing” was enough because it was answering a narrow question: **has anything changed since the earlier showing?** The court expressly says the Bender declaration “can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document.”

That is consistent with FOIA practice. Courts assess agency affidavits **in context**, not by isolating a single sentence. And in Exemption 7(A) cases, the agency may satisfy its burden by attesting to the **existence of ongoing investigations/proceedings**, especially where the FBI and national security concerns are involved. The opinion cites that principle directly, including CREW’s statement that ongoing criminal investigations count as “proceedings,” and that the government can show them by attesting to their existence.

### When is an “update” too conclusory?
This opinion does **not** set a bright-line rule. But it gives the relevant limit:

- if the update is the **only** evidence of ongoing proceedings, and it is just a bare assertion with no detail, that may be too conclusory;
- if the update is tethered to an earlier, sufficiently detailed showing, and its purpose is simply to confirm that the investigation is still live, courts may accept it.

The court acknowledged that Bender’s declaration was “surprisingly terse” when viewed alone. It still upheld it because the earlier Hardy declarations, the district court’s prior ruling, and the sealed submission together showed a continuing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.”

So the answer is: **the declaration was enough not because the sentence itself was rich, but because it functioned as a current status update to an already adequate Exemption 7(A) showing.**

### When may a FOIA court rely on sealed ex parte declarations without giving the requester access to them?
Question:
A lot of the court's reasoning seems to depend on sealed, ex parte declarations that Farahi could not see, especially on the nature of the investigation and segregability. I understand the practical problem that public explanation might reveal the very information the government wants to protect, but this still seems hard on the requester. What are the limits on using ex parte submissions in FOIA litigation? Does the requester's failure to object in the district court effectively waive any challenge, or do courts still have an independent duty to be skeptical before relying on secret affidavits?
Answer:
A FOIA court may rely on sealed ex parte declarations, but only when that is necessary to decide the exemption dispute without disclosing the very material the agency is trying to withhold.

That is the rule the D.C. Circuit applied here. The court said the usual prejudice to the requester is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” It also held that the district court did not need to put its analysis of the sealed declaration on the public record.

A few limits still matter:

- **The agency still bears the burden.** FOIA is not “trust us.” The government must justify the exemption, and the court reviews summary judgment de novo. In this case, the district court actually required **updated declarations** because the earlier ones were stale and needed more detail on segregability.
- **The public record still matters.** The court relied on the public Hardy and Bender declarations plus the sealed submissions; the ex parte material was used to fill in sensitive details, not to replace any public showing.
- **Segregability must still be addressed.** The court may consider sealed declarations on whether nonexempt material is reasonably segregable, but it still has to be satisfied that the agency explained why nothing meaningful can be released.

On your waiver point: **yes, failure to object in the district court generally forfeits the complaint about the use of ex parte declarations on appeal.** Farahi “did not oppose” the motion to file the supplemental declaration under seal and ex parte, so the panel said he could not later challenge the district court’s reliance on it.

So the short answer is:

> A FOIA court may rely on sealed ex parte declarations when necessary to avoid revealing the withheld information itself; the requester does not get access just because the declaration is important. But the court still must independently test the agency’s showing, and if the requester did not object below, the appellate challenge to the use of the ex parte submission is usually forfeited.

### What is a valid 'category-by-category' withholding explanation in FOIA, and why did the court say Farahi forfeited this issue?
Question:
The opinion briefly mentions that when an agency withholds documents by category instead of document-by-document, the categories must be 'sufficiently distinct' to let the court test the claimed exemptions. Farahi argued that the FBI had not adequately justified its categories, but the court said he forfeited that argument because he did not specifically explain why the categories were overbroad. What would a proper challenge have looked like here? Does a requester need to attack each category one by one, almost like arguing a Vaughn index entry?
Answer:
A valid **category-by-category** FOIA justification is one that groups records only when the group is **homogeneous enough** that the court can still tell whether the claimed exemption really fits.

In this opinion, the D.C. Circuit restated the rule from **CREW v. DOJ** and **Gallant**: if an agency withholds by category rather than document-by-document, the categories must be **“sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied”** to each category. That is the point of the grouping: not to avoid judicial review, but to make review manageable without losing the connection between the records and the exemption.

### What a proper challenge would look like
A requester would need to do more than say, in the abstract, “the categories are overbroad.” He would need to **identify the specific category or categories** and explain **why the grouping is not meaningfully distinct** for the claimed exemption.

For example, the argument would need to sound like this:

- “Category A lumps together materially different types of records, but the declaration does not explain why disclosure of all of them would interfere with the same ongoing investigation.”
- “Category B combines documents with different functions or sensitivity levels, so the court cannot tell whether each item actually falls within Exemption 7(A).”
- “The agency’s category description is so broad that it hides nonexempt material inside exempt material, defeating segregability review.”

That is basically the categorical equivalent of attacking a Vaughn index entry: you are not required to litigate every page individually, but you **do** need a concrete, category-specific reason why the agency’s grouping prevents meaningful exemption review.

### Why Farahi forfeited the issue
The court said Farahi forfeited the argument because he **did not give any specific explanation** for why the FBI’s categories were overbroad. The opinion says exactly that: he raised only a brief, generalized objection, but **“provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad.”** Under **Khine v. DHS**, that kind of undeveloped assertion is forfeited.

So the problem was not that a category-by-category challenge is impossible; it was that Farahi **did not actually make one in a developed way**. The panel had nothing concrete to review.

### Short answer
Yes, functionally it is like attacking a Vaughn index entry: **you need a targeted objection to the particular category, with reasons why that category is not sufficiently distinct or does not support the exemption**. But you do **not** necessarily have to contest every category one by one if your argument goes to the agency’s overall categorization method. What you cannot do is offer only a conclusory complaint and leave the court to figure out the defect for you.

### How can 10,000+ pages and 80 CDs contain no reasonably segregable nonexempt information?
Question:
The segregability holding is what confuses me most. The FBI located about 10,750 pages and 80 CDs, and the court still accepted that no meaningful nonexempt information was reasonably segregable from the withheld material. I understand that isolated words or phrases don't have to be released if they are just random scraps, but it still seems intuitive that some factual material should be separable from exempt material in a file that large. How do courts decide when nonexempt information is too intertwined to require disclosure, and how much explanation must the agency give to justify withholding entire records?
Answer:
The short answer is: **FOIA segregability is not a volume test**. A giant file does not create a presumption that something releasable must be in it.

Under FOIA, an agency must release **“any reasonably segregable portion”** of a record after deleting exempt material. But the key word is **reasonably**. If the nonexempt material is so intertwined with exempt material that separating it would leave only scraps, phrases, or fragments with no real informational value, the agency may withhold the record in full.

That is exactly the doctrine the court invoked here:

- **Whole records may be withheld** when nonexempt portions are **“inextricably intertwined”** with exempt portions.
- A court may rely on agency declarations that explain, with **reasonable specificity**, why further segregation is not possible.
- And as the opinion notes, the FOIA inquiry is about **information**, not just isolated words on a page.

So the fact that the FBI located **10,750 pages and 80 CDs** does not by itself mean there must be meaningful segregable material. Those records can still consist largely of ongoing-investigation material, repetitive entries, or fragments that would be meaningless if stripped of the exempt context. The D.C. Circuit specifically said that while **individual words or phrases** might technically be severable, that is “neither here nor there” if what remains is not **meaningful information**.

On the explanation point: the agency does **not** have to give a public page-by-page explanation for every withheld document. What it must do is give enough detail for the court to assess segregability. Here, the district court initially wanted more information, and the FBI provided updated public and **ex parte** declarations; after reviewing those, the court was satisfied that no meaningful nonexempt information was reasonably segregable.

So the holding is not “nothing in a huge file can ever be segregated.” It is: **on this record, and based on the sealed and public declarations, the FBI showed that any remaining nonexempt material was too intertwined and too trivial to require disclosure.**
