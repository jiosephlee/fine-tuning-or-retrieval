Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic, Not Propensity? The Eighth Circuit’s Broad View of Prior Gun Possession Evidence

## A prosecution-friendly ruling in a familiar kind of case

United States v. Cutbank is, on the facts, a very serious case: a violent domestic confrontation, a standoff with police, and a defendant with a long criminal record. But the opinion matters beyond its ugly facts because it shows how easily prior-sighting testimony about the *same firearm* can come in without being treated as “other acts” evidence under Rule 404(b). In a felon-in-possession prosecution, that distinction is huge. Once evidence is labeled “intrinsic,” the government often avoids the special suspicion that attends propensity evidence, and the district court gets a wider berth to admit it.

That is exactly what happened here. Two witnesses, D.F. and C.F., testified that they had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle before the charged incident. The district court allowed that testimony as intrinsic evidence, relevant to proving possession and what the court called “continuous possession” of the firearm. Judge Loken’s panel opinion endorsed that approach, citing longstanding Eighth Circuit precedent for the proposition that prior possession of the same gun is directly relevant to later possession because it helps show ownership or control.

## Why the panel had little trouble

The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning is brisk, and that is revealing. Rather than walking through Rule 404(b)’s usual admissibility framework — permissible purpose, relevance, and Rule 403 balancing — the court treated the testimony as part of the charged offense’s factual narrative. That move matters because it reframes the evidence. The government was not, on the panel’s view, trying to prove that Cutbank was a bad person who liked guns. It was trying to prove that he possessed *this gun* on the charged night, and testimony that he had been seen with that same gun before tended to make that point more likely.

There is a logic to that. In a case like this, prior possession of the identical weapon is more than generic character evidence. It can help identify the gun, connect the defendant to it, and make “possession” less contestable. The gun here was distinctive — a sawed-off rifle with an altered barrel and stock — and the witnesses’ testimony was not abstract. It was about the very weapon recovered along the path Cutbank likely took from D.F.’s house to the garage where police found him.

The panel also brushed aside the related procedural objections. Cutbank wanted a limiting instruction before the jury heard D.F.’s statements to police, but the court noted that D.F. later testified in person and that the district court was not required to stop the trial to instruct the jury about every item of evidence as it came in. Cutbank also challenged testimony that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, but the court viewed that as responsive to cross-examination about why D.F. had made inconsistent statements. Those rulings fit the opinion’s broader tone: the defense’s objections were treated as largely undeveloped, and the government’s evidence was strong enough that any marginal evidentiary error would have been harmless anyway.

## The bigger doctrinal picture

The more interesting question is not whether this particular evidence was powerful — it plainly was — but whether the “intrinsic evidence” label is doing too much work. Critics of the doctrine have long argued that “intrinsic” can become a sorting device that swallows Rule 404(b). After all, almost any evidence the government wants to offer can be described as part of the story of the offense. The risk is that the label lets courts admit evidence with obvious propensity flavor while skipping the discipline that 404(b) is supposed to impose.

Cutbank illustrates both the appeal and the danger of that approach. The appeal is obvious: if a witness saw the defendant with the same rifle before the charged offense, that fact does seem probative of later possession. But the danger is just as obvious: the jury may also infer, “he had guns before, so he probably had this one too.” That is the very inference Rule 404(b) is designed to police.

The Eighth Circuit’s answer is not to deny that risk, but to treat it as tolerable where the evidence concerns the same weapon and goes directly to possession. That makes this a relatively strong case for admissibility. A weaker case — one involving a different gun, a more attenuated time frame, or less reliable witnesses — would present harder questions. Still, the opinion signals that in the Eighth Circuit, prosecutors in § 922(g) cases have a ready path to admit same-weapon testimony as “intrinsic” evidence, especially when possession is disputed and the government can tie the defendant to the firearm before the arrest.

That has real downstream effects. Defense counsel will likely have to shift more of the fight from Rule 404(b) to Rule 403, emphasizing unfair prejudice, the risk of mini-trials, and whether the evidence truly proves possession rather than bad character. But Cutbank suggests that those arguments may be uphill in this circuit when the prior sightings involve the same gun. The practical lesson is that “intrinsic” remains a robust doctrine in the Eighth Circuit, and in firearm cases it continues to be a meaningful prosecution shortcut.

The sentence issues in Cutbank are important in their own right, but the evidentiary ruling is what will travel. For future felon-in-possession cases, the opinion reinforces a familiar Eighth Circuit theme: if the government can show that the defendant had *this exact firearm* before the charged event, the court is likely to treat that evidence as part of the case itself, not as forbidden propensity proof.



## Context Without Constraint: Police-Testimony Hearsay, Later Confrontation, and the Minimal Role of Limiting Instructions

The most interesting part of *United States v. Cutbank* is not the felon-in-possession conviction or the ACCA sentence. It is the court’s fairly brisk treatment of a familiar trial tactic: the government introduces an out-of-court statement not for its truth, but for “context” — here, to explain why officers did what they did — and the defense asks for an immediate limiting instruction to keep the jury from treating the statement as substantive proof. The Eighth Circuit’s answer was basically that the problem never fully materialized, because the declarant later testified and the record did not show hearsay being admitted in the first place.

That may sound procedural, but it reflects a broader practical reality in criminal trials. Once a statement is characterized as contextual rather than testimonial evidence, the hearsay and Confrontation Clause objections often become much harder to press effectively, especially if the declarant eventually takes the stand. *Cutbank* shows how little room remains for a defendant to demand a contemporaneous curative instruction when the court thinks the issue can be handled later — or may not need to be handled at all.

## The court’s move: “context” first, confrontation later

Cutbank asked the district court to instruct the jury, before D.F. testified, that it could not treat her statements to police as substantive evidence of what he had done. His concern was straightforward: police officers were expected to recount what they were told, and without a limiting instruction the jury might simply assume those statements were true. The government responded that the statements would explain the officers’ actions and knowledge during the investigation, not prove the truth of D.F.’s allegations. The district court declined to give an advance instruction, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The panel framed the issue under abuse-of-discretion review, relying on its own precedent that a trial judge need not give a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is admitted so long as an appropriate instruction can be given at the close of trial. But the court went further: it said the record “demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial.” That is doing a lot of work. If the challenged statements were truly admitted only for context, then the hearsay objection loses force; and once D.F. took the stand, the Confrontation Clause problem largely evaporated under the court’s citation to *Charboneau*, which says confrontation is satisfied when the declarant appears and testifies in person.

So the Eighth Circuit’s reasoning was not just “no harm, no foul.” It was closer to: there was no hearsay ruling to reverse, and any confrontation concern was cured by the declarant’s live testimony.

## Why that matters for trial practice

This is a useful reminder that “context” is a powerful evidentiary label. Courts routinely allow officers to explain why they took certain steps — why they went to a particular location, why they detained someone, why they searched a car, why they escalated a call — and that often means the jury hears out-of-court accusations or descriptions of criminal conduct before the declarant appears. In theory, the evidence is admitted for a narrow nonhearsay purpose. In practice, jurors may be slow to compartmentalize it.

That is why defendants often push for an immediate limiting instruction. The concern is not abstract. If a jury hears that a victim told police the defendant had a gun, had assaulted her, or had threatened her, that information can easily color the rest of the trial, even if the statement is later “formally” tied to officer conduct. A timely instruction can at least signal that the statement is not itself proof.

But *Cutbank* shows the limits of that strategy when the declarant will testify anyway. Once the declarant is on the witness stand, the judge can reasonably view the earlier statement as a temporary evidentiary placeholder rather than an independent confrontation problem. And if the record does not clearly show the statement was admitted for its truth, appellate review becomes especially deferential.

## The opinion’s understated doctrinal point

The Eighth Circuit did not announce any new rule about contextual statements, and it did not bless open-ended use of hearsay dressed up as background. Instead, it relied on a cluster of familiar propositions: trial courts have discretion over limiting instructions; they need not micromanage each item of evidence as it comes in; and the Confrontation Clause is ordinarily satisfied when the declarant testifies and can be cross-examined.

Still, the opinion reinforces a practical doctrinal trend. Courts are increasingly willing to treat disputed out-of-court statements as nonhearsay when they are tied to officer conduct, and they are increasingly comfortable postponing cautionary instructions until the end of trial. That approach tends to favor the government because the immediate prejudicial sting of the statement is front-loaded, while the limiting instruction is delayed and often less effective. If the declarant later testifies, the appellate court may deem any objection functionally cured.

That does not mean defendants are powerless. If a statement is truly introduced for its truth, or if “context” is being used as a pretext to smuggle in accusatory hearsay, the objection remains viable. But *Cutbank* suggests that in the Eighth Circuit, the combination of later live testimony and a context rationale will make it difficult to win reversal absent a very clear record of misuse.

## Bottom line

The case’s larger lesson is not about one instruction in one trial. It is about how little Confrontation Clause doctrine sometimes does in the face of ordinary evidentiary discretion. If the witness later appears, and if the trial judge is willing to describe the early statements as contextual rather than substantive, the defendant’s best protections are likely to be cross-examination, careful record-making, and a request for a closing instruction — not an appellate claim that the jury should have been warned sooner.

In that sense, *Cutbank* is a reminder that hearsay and confrontation objections often turn less on grand constitutional principles than on sequencing, framing, and the trial judge’s sense that jurors can sort it out later. Sometimes that is true. Sometimes it is wishful thinking. But this opinion makes clear that, at least in the Eighth Circuit, the appellate court will give the district judge substantial room to decide which side of that line the case falls on.



## Harmless Error as Appellate Gravity: What *Cutbank* Says About Evidentiary Appeals in the Eighth Circuit

## Why this case matters

*United States v. Cutbank* is not a major doctrinal reset on the Federal Rules of Evidence. Its significance is more practical, and in some ways more telling: it shows how appellate courts, especially in the Eighth Circuit, process criminal trial claims when the defendant’s briefing is thin, the evidentiary objections are familiar, and the government’s proof is overwhelming. The result is a kind of appellate gravity. Once the panel decides the district court stayed within the wide bounds of discretion, harmless error does the rest of the work.

That matters because many criminal appeals are built less on a single dramatic mistake than on a stack of smaller trial complaints. *Cutbank* is a reminder that this kind of appeal will rarely succeed unless the appellant can do more than gesture at prejudice. The opinion also offers a useful cautionary note for prosecutors: the panel was not impressed by the government’s decision to broaden one of the issues on appeal unnecessarily.

## A record that made the hard questions easy

The facts were not sympathetic to the defendant. According to the opinion, Cutbank terrorized his girlfriend while intoxicated and high, held her in a closet at gunpoint, struck her with the gun, and fled with both the firearm and her cellphone. Police later found him barricaded in another garage, where he resisted arrest, bit an officer, and grabbed for the officer’s gun. Along the route between the two locations, officers found the phone, a knife, and a sawed-off rifle bearing Cutbank’s DNA. Two witnesses identified the rifle as one they had previously seen him with.

Against that backdrop, the panel had little difficulty treating the evidentiary disputes as garden-variety abuse-of-discretion issues, not trial-threatening errors. That is important doctrinally. In the Eighth Circuit’s hands, abuse-of-discretion review is not just deferential in theory; it becomes especially unforgiving when the evidentiary point is modest and the overall proof is strong.

## “Intrinsic” evidence and the limits of Rule 404(b)

The most straightforward ruling involved testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. The district court treated that evidence as intrinsic, not as other-acts evidence governed by Rule 404(b). The panel agreed, relying on precedent holding that prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession because it helps establish ownership or control.

That is a familiar move in felon-in-possession cases, but *Cutbank* illustrates how little room there is, on appeal, once the government can tie the earlier sightings to the very weapon charged in the indictment. The panel did not engage in a deep 404(b) balancing exercise because the evidence was not being offered to prove character or propensity in the first place. It was part of the possession story.

The practical lesson is that defense counsel facing this kind of evidence need a more ambitious theory than “prior bad act.” If the government can frame the testimony as proof of identity, continuity, or control of the same gun, the intrinsic-evidence label will often carry the day.

## Limiting instructions, hearsay, and a clean Confrontation Clause answer

Cutbank also wanted a limiting instruction to prevent D.F.’s statements to police from being used substantively before she took the stand. The panel rejected that argument for a simple reason: the record did not show that inadmissible hearsay was actually admitted, and D.F. later testified in court. The court reiterated that trial judges need not issue a limiting instruction for each item of evidence as it comes in if a proper instruction is given at the end, and it invoked the standard Confrontation Clause rule that the problem disappears when the declarant appears and testifies.

Again, the doctrinal point is less dramatic than the appellate method. The panel did not need to resolve a fine-grained hearsay dispute because the claimed prejudice evaporated once the witness testified. This is a recurring pattern in appellate criminal law: where the alleged evidentiary misstep is procedural and the witness is available for confrontation, the constitutional argument often collapses into an ordinary evidentiary one.

## Harmless error as the backstop

The opinion’s most important theme is not any one evidentiary ruling. It is the court’s repeated insistence that, even if a ruling were imperfect, reversal would require a showing that the jury may have been substantially swayed. The panel concluded that Cutbank could not make that showing because the government’s evidence was substantial, both testimonial and physical. That finding effectively shut down the cumulative-error theory as well.

This is the real appellate lesson. In a case with DNA, contemporaneous victim testimony, corroborating eyewitnesses, and resisting-arrest conduct, an appellate court can afford to be generous to the district judge. Harmless error becomes the gravitational force that keeps the conviction in orbit. The more robust the proof, the less likely an evidentiary blemish will matter.

For defense lawyers, that means error preservation is necessary but not sufficient. The appellate brief has to explain why the error mattered in a case where the jury already had a lot to work with. *Cutbank* makes plain that simply stacking objections will not overcome the force of the record.

## A rare—and useful—aside directed at the government

One of the more interesting moments in the opinion comes when the panel notes, pointedly, “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” That is an unusually candid rebuke, especially in a routine criminal case. The government had suggested that Cutbank was also challenging a cross-examination ruling he did not actually brief.

The aside is small, but it matters. Appellate courts dislike unnecessary issue inflation, whether by appellants or appellees. For prosecutors, the message is to resist the temptation to turn a narrow defense argument into a broader imagined one. That kind of over-reading can irritate the panel and distract from the stronger parts of the government’s case. For defense counsel, the same point cuts the other way: precision matters. If you mean to challenge a ruling, say so clearly. If you do not, do not leave the court and opposing counsel guessing.

## Sentencing: the guidelines fight Cutbank could not win

The sentencing portion of the opinion is secondary, but it fits the same pattern. Cutbank argued that the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was inconsistent with § 3553(a)’s “not greater than necessary” principle. The panel rejected that argument because the district court adopted the PSR’s facts without objection, and because the guidelines themselves direct courts to use the higher offense level where the Armed Career Criminal Guideline and the enhancement produce different ranges.

The court then had no trouble affirming the within-guidelines sentence as substantively reasonable. Once again, the combination of a deferential standard and an ugly factual record left little room for appellate intervention.

## The bottom line

*Cutbank* is less a case about the fine points of evidence law than about how appellate courts decide criminal appeals when the defense brief is underdeveloped and the trial record is strong. The opinion shows the Eighth Circuit’s basic method in these cases: give the district court broad discretion, treat familiar evidence issues as settled unless the appellant makes a serious argument, and fall back on harmless error when the proof of guilt is overwhelming.

That makes the case a useful study in appellate realism. In the Eighth Circuit, evidentiary error is not enough; prejudice is the price of reversal.



## ACCA, Relevant Conduct, and the Parsimony Principle: Why a Four-Level Firearm Enhancement Still Counts

## Why this case matters

*United States v. Cutbank* is not a headline-grabbing decision, but it is a very useful one for understanding how federal sentencing actually works when a defendant is already facing the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Eighth Circuit had little trouble affirming Justin Cutbank’s 292-month sentence, and the most important part of the opinion is not the conviction analysis but the way the panel handled the interaction between ACCA, the Guidelines, and the parsimony command of § 3553(a).

The short version is this: even in an ACCA case, the district court still has to calculate the Guidelines correctly, and that includes applying a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) if the firearm was possessed “in connection with another felony offense.” A defendant cannot convert § 3553(a)’s “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” language into a rule that the enhancement itself should disappear. That argument goes to variance, not to the Guidelines calculation.

## How the panel framed the issue

Cutbank’s sentencing argument was straightforward enough. He said that layering the four-level enhancement onto an already severe ACCA sentence conflicted with the statute’s parsimony principle. In other words, once ACCA already triggers a draconian sentencing range, adding more offense-specific punishment for conduct outside the gun-possession count overstates the need for punishment.

The panel rejected that framing. It emphasized the familiar sequencing from *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*: a court must first correctly calculate the advisory range, and only then decide whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a departure or variance. That matters because it keeps two distinct inquiries separate. The enhancement question asks whether the Guidelines, as written, apply. The parsimony question asks whether the resulting advisory range is too much for this defendant’s particular case.

That distinction did most of the work here. The court noted that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) applies even if the other felony is uncharged and unconvicted, and it accepted the district court’s reliance on the PSR’s unobjected-to facts. Once those facts were in the record, the panel saw no clear error in applying the enhancement. Cutbank’s policy objection, the court said in substance, did not make the enhancement legally wrong.

## Why the enhancement still mattered even with ACCA

There is a tempting intuition that ACCA should swallow everything else. After all, once a defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal, the case already sits in a sentencing category marked by a 15-year mandatory minimum and a much higher advisory range. But the Guidelines do not treat ACCA as a ceiling. They treat it as one input, and USSG § 4B1.4(b) expressly says that the greater offense level controls.

That is exactly what happened here. The PSR calculated a total offense level of 35 after the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement and the other adjustments, while the ACCA guideline would have yielded offense level 34. Because the Guidelines direct the court to use the greater number, the enhancement moved the range upward. So this was not an academic dispute about sentencing theory; it altered the advisory range from the baseline ACCA range to a slightly higher one.

That point has practical significance. Defense lawyers sometimes assume that once ACCA is in play, offense-specific enhancements are just noise. *Cutbank* is a reminder that they are not. The Guidelines can still ratchet the range upward through conduct tied to the offense episode, including conduct that is not separately charged so long as the record supports it.

## The parsimony argument is not a shortcut around the Guidelines

The more interesting doctrinal move in the opinion is the court’s treatment of the parsimony clause. Cutbank tried to use § 3553(a) almost as a backdoor challenge to the enhancement itself. The Eighth Circuit would not have it. The court treated the argument as one for a lower sentence after the range is calculated, not as a basis to rewrite the range.

That is a fairly orthodox way to read the post-*Booker* sentencing structure, but it has real consequences. If the enhancement is legally available and supported by the record, the defendant’s remedy is not to re-label the issue as a statutory command and hope to skip the Guidelines step. The remedy is to persuade the judge that, notwithstanding the range, the particular case calls for a variance.

Cutbank did not get traction there either. The district court had reviewed the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, considered the relevant § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a sentence within the advisory range. The Eighth Circuit therefore applied the usual presumption of reasonableness to the within-range sentence and found no abuse of discretion. The panel also pointed to the seriousness of the underlying conduct: domestic violence, kidnapping-like conduct in the victim’s home, threats at gunpoint, and resistance to arrest that injured an officer. Those facts made the variance argument a hard sell.

## The downstream lesson

The opinion’s broader lesson is that sentencing litigation in ACCA cases remains very much a two-step fight. First, the defendant must contest the factual and legal basis for every enhancement, including the content of the PSR. If those facts go unchallenged, they can do a lot of work. Second, if the enhancement is properly applied, the fight shifts to § 3553(a), where the defendant must persuade the court that the resulting range is too high for this case.

For future cases, *Cutbank* will likely be cited less for any novel doctrinal gloss than for its practical insistence on that sequence. The Eighth Circuit is signaling that the parsimony principle does not collapse guideline calculation into sentencing discretion. It operates after the range is calculated, not before. In a system where offense-level differences can still add years to a sentence, that is a meaningful line.
