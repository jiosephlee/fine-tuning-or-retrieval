Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why wasn't testimony about the witness's sister being killed by a gun excluded under Rule 403?
Question:
During cross-examination, D.F. was being questioned about inconsistent statements and said she was not familiar with guns and did not like guns because her sister was killed by one. The defense moved to strike that part, but the district court refused. I would think that kind of testimony could create sympathy for the witness or emotional prejudice against the defendant even though it had nothing to do with this crime. Was the idea that the defense 'opened the door' by asking about her prior inconsistent descriptions of the gun, or is there some other Rule 403 logic here?
Answer:
Because, in the court’s view, the testimony had **real probative value** on the credibility issue the defense had just raised, and there was **no showing of unfair prejudice that substantially outweighed that value**.

Under Rule 403, evidence is not excluded just because it is emotional or sympathetic. It must create a danger of **unfair prejudice**—that is, an improper tendency to inflame the jury or lead it to decide the case on an emotional basis—**substantially outweighing** its probative value.

Here, the panel treated D.F.’s statement as a **direct response to cross-examination** about her inconsistent prior statements regarding the gun. She was explaining why she was inconsistent and why her descriptions might be unreliable: she was “not familiar with guns” and did not like them because her sister had been killed by one. That goes to context and credibility, not to an unrelated attack on the defendant.

The opinion says exactly that:

> “This testimony responded to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements. Cutbank points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, and none is apparent.”

So the logic was not simply “the defense opened the door” in the abstract, but more specifically:

1. **The defense made her familiarity with guns and her prior statements an issue.**
2. **Her explanation was relevant to that issue.**
3. **The court saw no concrete unfair prejudice or confusion.**

Also, the panel noted that **C.F. later mentioned the same fact without objection**, which further undercuts the claim that this brief remark was the kind of prejudicial matter Rule 403 is meant to keep out.

In short: the district court kept the testimony because it was **responsive, explanatory, and only minimally prejudicial**, and Rule 403 does not require exclusion on sympathy alone.
