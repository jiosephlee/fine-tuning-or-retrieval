Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Does the Confrontation Clause issue go away if the out-of-court declarant later testifies at trial?
Question:
Before trial, the defense asked for a limiting instruction so the jury would not treat D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence until D.F. herself testified. The government said the officers would only mention the statements to explain their actions and knowledge. The court of appeals then said there was no Confrontation Clause problem because D.F. later testified in person. Is the rule really that a prior out-of-court statement is fine under the Confrontation Clause as long as the declarant appears at trial, even if the jury heard the police version first?
Answer:
**Short answer: yes, at least as the court applied it here.**  

In **United States v. Cutbank**, the Eighth Circuit treated the Confrontation Clause issue as cured once **D.F. actually testified at trial**. The court quoted its earlier rule that the Clause is satisfied **“when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.”** So the fact that the jury heard the police account first did **not** create a Confrontation Clause problem by itself.

The key point is that the Confrontation Clause is about **the opportunity to confront and cross-examine the declarant at trial**. It is **not** a sequencing rule requiring the jury to hear the declarant’s own testimony before hearing any prior statement. In Cutbank, because D.F. later took the stand, the defendant had that confrontation opportunity, and the court said there was no abuse of discretion in denying the requested limiting instruction.

That said, this does **not** mean every prior out-of-court statement automatically becomes admissible just because the declarant later testifies. **Hearsay objections can still matter**, and a limiting instruction may still be relevant if the statement is admitted for a non-hearsay purpose, like explaining police conduct. But on the **Confrontation Clause** issue specifically, the later in-court testimony was enough for the court.

So the opinion’s answer is essentially: **yes, the constitutional concern goes away once the declarant appears and testifies; the order in which the jury heard the evidence did not matter here.**
