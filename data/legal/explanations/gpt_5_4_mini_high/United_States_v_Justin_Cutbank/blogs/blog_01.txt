Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic, Not Propensity? The Eighth Circuit’s Broad View of Prior Gun Possession Evidence

## A prosecution-friendly ruling in a familiar kind of case

United States v. Cutbank is, on the facts, a very serious case: a violent domestic confrontation, a standoff with police, and a defendant with a long criminal record. But the opinion matters beyond its ugly facts because it shows how easily prior-sighting testimony about the *same firearm* can come in without being treated as “other acts” evidence under Rule 404(b). In a felon-in-possession prosecution, that distinction is huge. Once evidence is labeled “intrinsic,” the government often avoids the special suspicion that attends propensity evidence, and the district court gets a wider berth to admit it.

That is exactly what happened here. Two witnesses, D.F. and C.F., testified that they had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle before the charged incident. The district court allowed that testimony as intrinsic evidence, relevant to proving possession and what the court called “continuous possession” of the firearm. Judge Loken’s panel opinion endorsed that approach, citing longstanding Eighth Circuit precedent for the proposition that prior possession of the same gun is directly relevant to later possession because it helps show ownership or control.

## Why the panel had little trouble

The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning is brisk, and that is revealing. Rather than walking through Rule 404(b)’s usual admissibility framework — permissible purpose, relevance, and Rule 403 balancing — the court treated the testimony as part of the charged offense’s factual narrative. That move matters because it reframes the evidence. The government was not, on the panel’s view, trying to prove that Cutbank was a bad person who liked guns. It was trying to prove that he possessed *this gun* on the charged night, and testimony that he had been seen with that same gun before tended to make that point more likely.

There is a logic to that. In a case like this, prior possession of the identical weapon is more than generic character evidence. It can help identify the gun, connect the defendant to it, and make “possession” less contestable. The gun here was distinctive — a sawed-off rifle with an altered barrel and stock — and the witnesses’ testimony was not abstract. It was about the very weapon recovered along the path Cutbank likely took from D.F.’s house to the garage where police found him.

The panel also brushed aside the related procedural objections. Cutbank wanted a limiting instruction before the jury heard D.F.’s statements to police, but the court noted that D.F. later testified in person and that the district court was not required to stop the trial to instruct the jury about every item of evidence as it came in. Cutbank also challenged testimony that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, but the court viewed that as responsive to cross-examination about why D.F. had made inconsistent statements. Those rulings fit the opinion’s broader tone: the defense’s objections were treated as largely undeveloped, and the government’s evidence was strong enough that any marginal evidentiary error would have been harmless anyway.

## The bigger doctrinal picture

The more interesting question is not whether this particular evidence was powerful — it plainly was — but whether the “intrinsic evidence” label is doing too much work. Critics of the doctrine have long argued that “intrinsic” can become a sorting device that swallows Rule 404(b). After all, almost any evidence the government wants to offer can be described as part of the story of the offense. The risk is that the label lets courts admit evidence with obvious propensity flavor while skipping the discipline that 404(b) is supposed to impose.

Cutbank illustrates both the appeal and the danger of that approach. The appeal is obvious: if a witness saw the defendant with the same rifle before the charged offense, that fact does seem probative of later possession. But the danger is just as obvious: the jury may also infer, “he had guns before, so he probably had this one too.” That is the very inference Rule 404(b) is designed to police.

The Eighth Circuit’s answer is not to deny that risk, but to treat it as tolerable where the evidence concerns the same weapon and goes directly to possession. That makes this a relatively strong case for admissibility. A weaker case — one involving a different gun, a more attenuated time frame, or less reliable witnesses — would present harder questions. Still, the opinion signals that in the Eighth Circuit, prosecutors in § 922(g) cases have a ready path to admit same-weapon testimony as “intrinsic” evidence, especially when possession is disputed and the government can tie the defendant to the firearm before the arrest.

That has real downstream effects. Defense counsel will likely have to shift more of the fight from Rule 404(b) to Rule 403, emphasizing unfair prejudice, the risk of mini-trials, and whether the evidence truly proves possession rather than bad character. But Cutbank suggests that those arguments may be uphill in this circuit when the prior sightings involve the same gun. The practical lesson is that “intrinsic” remains a robust doctrine in the Eighth Circuit, and in firearm cases it continues to be a meaningful prosecution shortcut.

The sentence issues in Cutbank are important in their own right, but the evidentiary ruling is what will travel. For future felon-in-possession cases, the opinion reinforces a familiar Eighth Circuit theme: if the government can show that the defendant had *this exact firearm* before the charged event, the court is likely to treat that evidence as part of the case itself, not as forbidden propensity proof.
