Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Harmless Error as Appellate Gravity: What *Cutbank* Says About Evidentiary Appeals in the Eighth Circuit

## Why this case matters

*United States v. Cutbank* is not a major doctrinal reset on the Federal Rules of Evidence. Its significance is more practical, and in some ways more telling: it shows how appellate courts, especially in the Eighth Circuit, process criminal trial claims when the defendant’s briefing is thin, the evidentiary objections are familiar, and the government’s proof is overwhelming. The result is a kind of appellate gravity. Once the panel decides the district court stayed within the wide bounds of discretion, harmless error does the rest of the work.

That matters because many criminal appeals are built less on a single dramatic mistake than on a stack of smaller trial complaints. *Cutbank* is a reminder that this kind of appeal will rarely succeed unless the appellant can do more than gesture at prejudice. The opinion also offers a useful cautionary note for prosecutors: the panel was not impressed by the government’s decision to broaden one of the issues on appeal unnecessarily.

## A record that made the hard questions easy

The facts were not sympathetic to the defendant. According to the opinion, Cutbank terrorized his girlfriend while intoxicated and high, held her in a closet at gunpoint, struck her with the gun, and fled with both the firearm and her cellphone. Police later found him barricaded in another garage, where he resisted arrest, bit an officer, and grabbed for the officer’s gun. Along the route between the two locations, officers found the phone, a knife, and a sawed-off rifle bearing Cutbank’s DNA. Two witnesses identified the rifle as one they had previously seen him with.

Against that backdrop, the panel had little difficulty treating the evidentiary disputes as garden-variety abuse-of-discretion issues, not trial-threatening errors. That is important doctrinally. In the Eighth Circuit’s hands, abuse-of-discretion review is not just deferential in theory; it becomes especially unforgiving when the evidentiary point is modest and the overall proof is strong.

## “Intrinsic” evidence and the limits of Rule 404(b)

The most straightforward ruling involved testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. The district court treated that evidence as intrinsic, not as other-acts evidence governed by Rule 404(b). The panel agreed, relying on precedent holding that prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession because it helps establish ownership or control.

That is a familiar move in felon-in-possession cases, but *Cutbank* illustrates how little room there is, on appeal, once the government can tie the earlier sightings to the very weapon charged in the indictment. The panel did not engage in a deep 404(b) balancing exercise because the evidence was not being offered to prove character or propensity in the first place. It was part of the possession story.

The practical lesson is that defense counsel facing this kind of evidence need a more ambitious theory than “prior bad act.” If the government can frame the testimony as proof of identity, continuity, or control of the same gun, the intrinsic-evidence label will often carry the day.

## Limiting instructions, hearsay, and a clean Confrontation Clause answer

Cutbank also wanted a limiting instruction to prevent D.F.’s statements to police from being used substantively before she took the stand. The panel rejected that argument for a simple reason: the record did not show that inadmissible hearsay was actually admitted, and D.F. later testified in court. The court reiterated that trial judges need not issue a limiting instruction for each item of evidence as it comes in if a proper instruction is given at the end, and it invoked the standard Confrontation Clause rule that the problem disappears when the declarant appears and testifies.

Again, the doctrinal point is less dramatic than the appellate method. The panel did not need to resolve a fine-grained hearsay dispute because the claimed prejudice evaporated once the witness testified. This is a recurring pattern in appellate criminal law: where the alleged evidentiary misstep is procedural and the witness is available for confrontation, the constitutional argument often collapses into an ordinary evidentiary one.

## Harmless error as the backstop

The opinion’s most important theme is not any one evidentiary ruling. It is the court’s repeated insistence that, even if a ruling were imperfect, reversal would require a showing that the jury may have been substantially swayed. The panel concluded that Cutbank could not make that showing because the government’s evidence was substantial, both testimonial and physical. That finding effectively shut down the cumulative-error theory as well.

This is the real appellate lesson. In a case with DNA, contemporaneous victim testimony, corroborating eyewitnesses, and resisting-arrest conduct, an appellate court can afford to be generous to the district judge. Harmless error becomes the gravitational force that keeps the conviction in orbit. The more robust the proof, the less likely an evidentiary blemish will matter.

For defense lawyers, that means error preservation is necessary but not sufficient. The appellate brief has to explain why the error mattered in a case where the jury already had a lot to work with. *Cutbank* makes plain that simply stacking objections will not overcome the force of the record.

## A rare—and useful—aside directed at the government

One of the more interesting moments in the opinion comes when the panel notes, pointedly, “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” That is an unusually candid rebuke, especially in a routine criminal case. The government had suggested that Cutbank was also challenging a cross-examination ruling he did not actually brief.

The aside is small, but it matters. Appellate courts dislike unnecessary issue inflation, whether by appellants or appellees. For prosecutors, the message is to resist the temptation to turn a narrow defense argument into a broader imagined one. That kind of over-reading can irritate the panel and distract from the stronger parts of the government’s case. For defense counsel, the same point cuts the other way: precision matters. If you mean to challenge a ruling, say so clearly. If you do not, do not leave the court and opposing counsel guessing.

## Sentencing: the guidelines fight Cutbank could not win

The sentencing portion of the opinion is secondary, but it fits the same pattern. Cutbank argued that the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was inconsistent with § 3553(a)’s “not greater than necessary” principle. The panel rejected that argument because the district court adopted the PSR’s facts without objection, and because the guidelines themselves direct courts to use the higher offense level where the Armed Career Criminal Guideline and the enhancement produce different ranges.

The court then had no trouble affirming the within-guidelines sentence as substantively reasonable. Once again, the combination of a deferential standard and an ugly factual record left little room for appellate intervention.

## The bottom line

*Cutbank* is less a case about the fine points of evidence law than about how appellate courts decide criminal appeals when the defense brief is underdeveloped and the trial record is strong. The opinion shows the Eighth Circuit’s basic method in these cases: give the district court broad discretion, treat familiar evidence issues as settled unless the appellant makes a serious argument, and fall back on harmless error when the proof of guilt is overwhelming.

That makes the case a useful study in appellate realism. In the Eighth Circuit, evidentiary error is not enough; prejudice is the price of reversal.
