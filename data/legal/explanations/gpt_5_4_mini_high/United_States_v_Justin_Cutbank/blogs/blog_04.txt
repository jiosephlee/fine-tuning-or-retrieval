Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## ACCA, Relevant Conduct, and the Parsimony Principle: Why a Four-Level Firearm Enhancement Still Counts

## Why this case matters

*United States v. Cutbank* is not a headline-grabbing decision, but it is a very useful one for understanding how federal sentencing actually works when a defendant is already facing the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Eighth Circuit had little trouble affirming Justin Cutbank’s 292-month sentence, and the most important part of the opinion is not the conviction analysis but the way the panel handled the interaction between ACCA, the Guidelines, and the parsimony command of § 3553(a).

The short version is this: even in an ACCA case, the district court still has to calculate the Guidelines correctly, and that includes applying a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) if the firearm was possessed “in connection with another felony offense.” A defendant cannot convert § 3553(a)’s “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” language into a rule that the enhancement itself should disappear. That argument goes to variance, not to the Guidelines calculation.

## How the panel framed the issue

Cutbank’s sentencing argument was straightforward enough. He said that layering the four-level enhancement onto an already severe ACCA sentence conflicted with the statute’s parsimony principle. In other words, once ACCA already triggers a draconian sentencing range, adding more offense-specific punishment for conduct outside the gun-possession count overstates the need for punishment.

The panel rejected that framing. It emphasized the familiar sequencing from *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*: a court must first correctly calculate the advisory range, and only then decide whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a departure or variance. That matters because it keeps two distinct inquiries separate. The enhancement question asks whether the Guidelines, as written, apply. The parsimony question asks whether the resulting advisory range is too much for this defendant’s particular case.

That distinction did most of the work here. The court noted that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) applies even if the other felony is uncharged and unconvicted, and it accepted the district court’s reliance on the PSR’s unobjected-to facts. Once those facts were in the record, the panel saw no clear error in applying the enhancement. Cutbank’s policy objection, the court said in substance, did not make the enhancement legally wrong.

## Why the enhancement still mattered even with ACCA

There is a tempting intuition that ACCA should swallow everything else. After all, once a defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal, the case already sits in a sentencing category marked by a 15-year mandatory minimum and a much higher advisory range. But the Guidelines do not treat ACCA as a ceiling. They treat it as one input, and USSG § 4B1.4(b) expressly says that the greater offense level controls.

That is exactly what happened here. The PSR calculated a total offense level of 35 after the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement and the other adjustments, while the ACCA guideline would have yielded offense level 34. Because the Guidelines direct the court to use the greater number, the enhancement moved the range upward. So this was not an academic dispute about sentencing theory; it altered the advisory range from the baseline ACCA range to a slightly higher one.

That point has practical significance. Defense lawyers sometimes assume that once ACCA is in play, offense-specific enhancements are just noise. *Cutbank* is a reminder that they are not. The Guidelines can still ratchet the range upward through conduct tied to the offense episode, including conduct that is not separately charged so long as the record supports it.

## The parsimony argument is not a shortcut around the Guidelines

The more interesting doctrinal move in the opinion is the court’s treatment of the parsimony clause. Cutbank tried to use § 3553(a) almost as a backdoor challenge to the enhancement itself. The Eighth Circuit would not have it. The court treated the argument as one for a lower sentence after the range is calculated, not as a basis to rewrite the range.

That is a fairly orthodox way to read the post-*Booker* sentencing structure, but it has real consequences. If the enhancement is legally available and supported by the record, the defendant’s remedy is not to re-label the issue as a statutory command and hope to skip the Guidelines step. The remedy is to persuade the judge that, notwithstanding the range, the particular case calls for a variance.

Cutbank did not get traction there either. The district court had reviewed the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, considered the relevant § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a sentence within the advisory range. The Eighth Circuit therefore applied the usual presumption of reasonableness to the within-range sentence and found no abuse of discretion. The panel also pointed to the seriousness of the underlying conduct: domestic violence, kidnapping-like conduct in the victim’s home, threats at gunpoint, and resistance to arrest that injured an officer. Those facts made the variance argument a hard sell.

## The downstream lesson

The opinion’s broader lesson is that sentencing litigation in ACCA cases remains very much a two-step fight. First, the defendant must contest the factual and legal basis for every enhancement, including the content of the PSR. If those facts go unchallenged, they can do a lot of work. Second, if the enhancement is properly applied, the fight shifts to § 3553(a), where the defendant must persuade the court that the resulting range is too high for this case.

For future cases, *Cutbank* will likely be cited less for any novel doctrinal gloss than for its practical insistence on that sequence. The Eighth Circuit is signaling that the parsimony principle does not collapse guideline calculation into sentencing discretion. It operates after the range is calculated, not before. In a system where offense-level differences can still add years to a sentence, that is a meaningful line.
