Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# From Violent Night to Federal Firearm Case

## The events inside D.F.’s home: methamphetamine use, threats, confinement in a closet, and striking D.F. with the gun

The case begins with a late-night domestic episode that quickly became the factual core of the federal firearm prosecution. According to the evidence at trial, Justin Cutbank had been drinking and using methamphetamine when he woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. While holding her there, he acted paranoid, accusing people in and around the house of listening to him and waiting to harm him. D.F. testified that he kept her in the closet at gunpoint for what felt like hours, threatened to kill her, and struck her across the face with the gun. Those facts mattered not only because they showed violence and intimidation, but because they helped explain why the later discovery of the gun and the testimony of the witnesses were so important at trial.

## Other witnesses in the house, including D.F.’s sons and housemate C.F., and why their presence mattered

Cutbank eventually ordered D.F. to wake up the other people in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Their presence mattered in two ways. First, it showed the seriousness of the threat and the broader danger in the home, because the confrontation was not limited to a private argument with D.F. Second, both D.F. and C.F. later became key witnesses identifying the gun. Their testimony linked Cutbank to the firearm before the night in question and helped establish that the weapon found later was the same one he had possessed earlier.

## Cutbank’s flight from the home with D.F.’s phone and the firearm

At some point after the confrontation, Cutbank fled from the house with both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. That fact became important because it connected the domestic incident to the later search for physical evidence and to the possession charge itself. In a felon-in-possession case, the government must prove that the defendant knowingly possessed a firearm. Evidence that the same gun was seen with him before, during, and after the incident helped the government prove possession rather than mere proximity or accident.

## The second police call: Cutbank barricading himself in a nearby garage and making erratic statements

Police later received another call, this time from a house about a half mile from D.F.’s home, reporting that a man was in the garage and refusing to leave. When officers arrived, they found Cutbank there. He barricaded himself inside the garage and over the next several hours made erratic statements while claiming to have a weapon. This second scene mattered because it placed Cutbank in a different location within a short time after the home incident and gave officers and the jury more evidence about his behavior during the relevant time period. The officers’ response to this standoff also generated some of the procedural and evidentiary issues raised on appeal.

## Resistance to arrest, including biting an officer and grabbing for the officer’s gun

When officers tried to detain him, Cutbank resisted violently. The opinion notes that he bit an officer in the face and neck and grabbed the officer’s gun. These facts were significant for the sentencing discussion as well as the narrative of the offense. They showed that the episode did not end with his flight from D.F.’s house; it escalated into a confrontation with law enforcement that increased the danger to others. At sentencing, the district court also relied on the broader conduct surrounding the offense, including his resistance to arrest, in assessing the seriousness of the crime and the appropriate punishment.

## Physical evidence recovered along the likely path of flight: D.F.’s phone, a knife, and the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle

Officers searched the route Cutbank likely took between D.F.’s home and the garage and found several pieces of physical evidence: D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. This recovery was a major evidentiary anchor for the government’s case. The phone supported the theory that Cutbank had fled the home carrying items from the confrontation. The knife suggested the path between the two locations was not incidental or harmless. Most importantly, the rifle was the firearm at issue in the felon-in-possession charge. Its altered condition also mattered later at sentencing, because the Presentence Investigation Report treated it as a sawed-off firearm described in the Guidelines’ definition of a firearm subject to enhanced treatment.

## DNA evidence and witness identification linking Cutbank to the rifle

The government strengthened the connection between Cutbank and the rifle in two ways. First, DNA on the gun matched Cutbank. Second, both D.F. and C.F. identified the weapon as one they had seen him with before that night. This combination of scientific and eyewitness evidence made the possession case stronger than a case based on a recovered firearm alone. It also explains why the appellate opinion later treated the evidentiary disputes as largely immaterial: the government had substantial proof that the gun belonged to the same chain of events as the domestic incident and that Cutbank had possessed it.

## The federal charge under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and the enhanced penalty framework of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)

Cutbank was charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). That statute makes it a federal crime for a person previously convicted of a felony to possess a firearm. The case also implicated 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), the Armed Career Criminal Act, because the government alleged that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions. The practical importance of § 924(e) is that it raises the penalty range significantly for qualifying defendants. In other words, the case was not just about whether he possessed a gun; it was also about whether his criminal history triggered a much harsher sentencing regime.

## The jury’s conviction on the possession charge and its finding of at least three prior violent felony convictions

The jury convicted Cutbank of the felon-in-possession offense. The opinion also states that the jury found he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which triggered the enhanced penalty provisions of § 924(e). That finding mattered because it moved the case into Armed Career Criminal territory and affected the sentencing calculation. Once the enhancement applied, the district court had to sentence him under a much higher statutory and Guidelines framework than would apply to an ordinary § 922(g)(1) conviction.

## The district court’s sentence of 292 months and the basic structure of the advisory Guidelines calculation

Before sentencing, the Presentence Investigation Report proposed a detailed advisory Guidelines calculation. It recommended a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm and Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence. It then added three levels for an altered or obliterated serial number, two levels for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police, and four levels for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). That produced a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory range was 292 to 365 months. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, the bottom of that range. The opinion also notes that although the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a lower offense level in one respect, the Guidelines direct courts to use the higher offense level when multiple provisions apply.

## The issues on appeal: multiple evidentiary and procedural objections plus a challenge to the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement

On appeal, Cutbank challenged both his conviction and his sentence. He argued that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes during trial, including rulings about prior firearm possession testimony, limiting instructions, references to D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun, and authentication of a police report used for impeachment. He also challenged the four-level sentencing enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which increased his advisory Guidelines range. The appellate court emphasized that he offered almost no supporting authority or analysis for these claims.

## The appellate court’s framing of the appeal as a series of largely unsupported claims

The Eighth Circuit’s opening assessment of the appeal is important for reading the rest of the opinion. The court signaled that the arguments were weak from the start by saying Cutbank provided “no cases and almost no analysis” and that none of the contentions had merit. That framing helps explain the opinion’s structure: the court deals with each issue briefly, relying on established rules such as abuse-of-discretion review for evidentiary rulings, harmless-error principles, and deferential review of sentencing decisions. The bottom-line result is that the conviction and sentence were affirmed because the trial evidence was substantial, the challenged rulings were within the district court’s discretion, and the sentence was both procedurally and substantively reasonable.

# Pretrial Evidentiary Rulings: Prior Sightings of the Rifle, Hearsay Concerns, and the Confrontation Clause

## The motion in limine and the prior-sighting testimony

Cutbank’s first pretrial fight was over testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen him with the same sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the night of the charged offense. He moved in limine to keep that testimony out, characterizing it as impermissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). That rule generally bars evidence of other acts when its real purpose is to suggest that because a person acted badly before, he probably acted badly again. The district court rejected that framing and agreed with the government that the testimony was intrinsic to the charged felon-in-possession offense.

That distinction matters. If evidence is intrinsic, it is treated as part of the story of the crime itself rather than as evidence of some separate “other act.” Here, the government’s theory was not that Cutbank was a bad person who happened to like guns; it was that he possessed this particular rifle and had possessed it before the incident. Prior sightings of the same weapon were therefore directly probative of who owned or controlled it. The Eighth Circuit relied on its earlier decision in United States v. Battle, which explains that prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control. In other words, the prior sightings helped identify the rifle as Cutbank’s gun and supported the inference that his possession was continuous rather than accidental or momentary.

The testimony at trial fit that theory neatly. D.F. told the jury that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a “handful of times” before the charged episode, and C.F. testified that she had seen the gun used during the incident “once or twice” before. Those statements were not offered simply to show character. They were part of the government’s proof that the firearm recovered after the incident was the same gun Cutbank had already been using and displaying, making his possession on the charged date far more likely.

## D.F.’s statements to police and the request for a limiting instruction

Cutbank also tried to limit the jury’s use of D.F.’s out-of-court statements to police. Before trial, he asked the court to instruct the jury that it could not treat those statements as substantive evidence of what Cutbank had done until D.F. herself testified. His concern was that officers might repeat what D.F. said to them, creating hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems if the statements were treated as proof of the underlying events rather than merely as background for police conduct.

The government responded that the officers’ testimony would be used to explain the context of the investigation and what the officers knew when they acted. It also argued that if a limiting instruction were needed, the court could give one later, rather than immediately each time evidence was admitted. The Eighth Circuit reviewed the refusal to give the requested instruction for abuse of discretion, which is a deferential standard. Under that standard, the appellate court asks not whether it would have handled the issue differently, but whether the district court’s choice was outside the range of reasonable options.

The court found no abuse of discretion. One important reason was that the record did not show the trial court actually admitted hearsay during the trial in the first place. Another was timing: a district court does not have to give a limiting instruction at the exact moment evidence is admitted if it provides an appropriate instruction later, such as at the close of trial. That is a practical trial-management rule. It recognizes that juries can be told how to use evidence in a final instruction without interrupting the flow of testimony each time a potentially sensitive exhibit or statement comes in. On this record, the court saw no reason to require the more restrictive instruction Cutbank requested.

## Why the Confrontation Clause issue disappeared once D.F. testified

Cutbank framed the issue partly as a Confrontation Clause problem, but that concern did not survive once D.F. took the stand. The Confrontation Clause protects a criminal defendant’s right to confront the witnesses against him, especially when the prosecution relies on testimonial out-of-court statements from a declarant who does not appear at trial. But the problem is cured when the declarant actually appears in court and testifies in person. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Charboneau for that basic point.

That is why D.F.’s eventual testimony mattered so much. Whatever concern Cutbank had about police recounting her statements before she testified was eliminated when she came before the jury and was available for examination. The defense could question her directly about what she had told police, what she remembered, and what she did not remember. In that setting, the constitutional concern that a defendant is being forced to confront an absent accuser simply was not present.

## How these rulings fit the government’s proof of possession

These evidentiary rulings were not minor side issues; they supported the core of the government’s possession case. The prior-sighting testimony helped tie Cutbank to the rifle before the charged night, reinforcing the conclusion that the gun recovered along his likely path of travel was the same weapon he had been using in the home. The ruling on D.F.’s police statements allowed the government to present the investigation without turning routine background testimony into a hearsay dispute, while D.F.’s own in-court testimony satisfied confrontation concerns.

Taken together, the Eighth Circuit treated these issues as ordinary exercises of trial-court discretion, not as errors that distorted the trial. The result was to leave the jury with a coherent picture: Cutbank had been seen with the rifle before, he used it during the charged incident, and the physical evidence recovered afterward matched that testimony. That context helped make the firearm-possession charge easier for the government to prove and gave the appellate court little reason to disturb the conviction on evidentiary grounds.

# At Trial: Renewed Objections, Explanatory Testimony, Impeachment Limits, and Harmless Error

## The renewed objection to prior-sighting testimony

One of Cutbank’s central trial complaints was that the jury heard from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin rifle before the charged night. Before trial, Cutbank tried to keep that testimony out as impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court rejected that theory, treating the testimony as intrinsic evidence: evidence that directly helped prove the firearm-possession charge and showed that Cutbank had continued control over the same weapon before the incident.

That pretrial ruling largely controlled the issue at trial. When D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the rifle a “handful of times” before the incident, and when C.F. testified that she had seen it “once or twice” before, Cutbank renewed the same objection. The Eighth Circuit treated those objections as falling under the earlier intrinsic-evidence ruling. The key point is that prior possession of the same firearm is not being offered simply to show bad character; it is offered to show ownership, control, and continuity of possession. In a felon-in-possession case, evidence that the defendant had the same weapon before the charged event can be direct proof of the offense itself.

## D.F.’s cross-examination and her explanation about guns

Cutbank also challenged testimony that arose during cross-examination of D.F. She had been questioned about earlier inconsistent statements, including statements suggesting she did not remember seeing the gun on the night in question. In response, she explained that some of her confusion came from the fact that she was not familiar with guns and did not like them because her sister had been killed by one.

The district court refused to strike that testimony, and the court of appeals saw no abuse of discretion. The explanation was responsive to the defense’s impeachment effort. Once the defense attacked D.F.’s credibility by pointing to prior inconsistent statements, the witness was allowed to answer with context that helped the jury understand why her earlier accounts may have differed. The court also saw no unfair prejudice or jury confusion under Rule 403. That matters because Rule 403 does not permit exclusion merely because evidence is emotionally charged; the question is whether its unfair prejudice or confusion substantially outweighs its probative value. Here, the panel saw nothing close to that. The point was reinforced by the fact that C.F. later mentioned the same family tragedy without any defense objection, making it harder for Cutbank to claim that the jury had been improperly inflamed by the reference.

## Impeaching C.F. with a police report

Cutbank then tried to impeach C.F. by using statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court allowed defense counsel to question C.F. about what she told police, but it would not let counsel simply read portions of the resulting police report into the record without proper foundation. The reason was straightforward: the report was not written by C.F., so counsel could not treat the report itself as if it were her statement without first authenticating it.

That ruling reflects a basic impeachment principle. Prior inconsistent statements can be used to challenge a witness, but if the lawyer wants to introduce extrinsic proof of the inconsistency, the evidence must still be authenticated. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Cumbie for that point. In other words, the defense was not barred from impeachment; it was required to use an admissible method. The court permitted questions about the substance of C.F.’s prior statements but prevented counsel from bypassing the foundation rules by reading from a document that had not been shown to be a reliable, properly supported exhibit. The panel also took a swipe at the government for trying to recast the issue on appeal as if Cutbank had complained that the court had “thwarted” cross-examination altogether. The real issue was narrower, and the court said it did not appreciate appellees unnecessarily expanding the appeal.

## Harmless error, cumulative error, and the lesson of broad trial-court discretion

Even when a defendant can point to an arguably mistaken evidentiary ruling, reversal is not automatic. The Eighth Circuit repeated the harmless-error rule: an evidentiary mistake warrants reversal only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by it. Put differently, if the error did not influence the verdict, or had only a slight influence, the conviction stands. Cutbank could not meet that standard because the government’s proof was substantial, including both testimony and physical evidence connecting him to the rifle.

The court also rejected any cumulative-error theory. Even if one looks at the combined effect of multiple alleged trial errors, reversal is appropriate only when the defendant shows substantial prejudice. The panel concluded that threshold was not met. The testimony supporting possession was strong, the physical evidence was powerful, and the disputed evidentiary rulings were either correct or at most minor. Taken together, this part of the opinion shows how much discretion trial judges have over evidentiary management, especially when a witness is being impeached or rehabilitated in real time. On appeal, the question is not whether the reviewing court would have managed the evidence differently, but whether the district court abused its broad discretion and whether any misstep actually affected the verdict. Here, the answer to both was no.

# Sentencing: ACCA Status, the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement, and Appellate Review of a Within-Guidelines Sentence

## Reconstructing the Guidelines calculation

The sentencing discussion begins with the Presentence Investigation Report, because federal sentencing still starts with a correctly calculated Guidelines range. The PSR assigned Cutbank a base offense level of 26 and then added three separate enhancements: three levels because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number, two levels because he recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and four levels because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. Those calculations produced a total offense level of 35. With Criminal History Category VI, the advisory range was 292 to 365 months.

For a law student, the important lesson is that the Guidelines calculation is built in layers. The base offense level sets the starting point, and specific offense characteristics then raise or lower the range depending on the facts found by the sentencing court. Here, the PSR’s starting point was elevated because the firearm was treated as a sawed-off weapon under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), which the Guidelines regard as especially dangerous. That matters because the weapon’s classification affects how serious the possession offense is for Guidelines purposes, even before any enhancements are added.

## ACCA and the armed career criminal guideline

Cutbank was not sentenced as an ordinary felon-in-possession defendant. The jury also found that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). ACCA is a statutory sentencing enhancement, not just a Guidelines adjustment, and it greatly increases punishment for repeat offenders who unlawfully possess firearms.

The opinion also explains the relationship between ACCA and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4. That guideline produces its own offense level, but the key rule is simple: when multiple guideline calculations are possible, the greater offense level controls. So although the armed career criminal guideline would have yielded offense level 34, the district court used the higher total offense level of 35 from the PSR’s other calculations. That is why the dispute over the four-level enhancement mattered. If the enhancement fell away, the guideline math could have changed.

## The challenged § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement

Cutbank’s main sentencing objection was to the four-level enhancement in USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense.” The guideline commentary makes clear that the other felony does not have to be separately charged or result in a conviction. Application Note 14(C) says the enhancement applies regardless of whether the defendant was actually prosecuted for that other felony.

That rule is important because it explains why the district court could rely on the facts set out in the PSR and the evidence at trial. The opinion notes that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection. Procedurally, that matters a great deal: when a defendant does not challenge the PSR’s facts at sentencing, the court may treat them as established for sentencing purposes. On appeal, that makes it much harder to argue that the district court lacked a factual basis for the enhancement.

## Why the court rejected Cutbank’s challenge to the enhancement

The Eighth Circuit had little trouble affirming the enhancement. The court said that, under the facts in the PSR, it was not error—“much less clear error”—to find that the firearm was used in connection with another felony. The opinion does not spend much time spelling out the exact felony theory, but the record described a violent domestic episode in which Cutbank pointed the gun at D.F., held her in a closet at gunpoint, threatened her, struck her with the gun, and fled with the weapon. Those facts plainly supported a conclusion that the firearm was part of other felony conduct, not merely coincidentally present.

The court also emphasized the standard of review. Factual determinations supporting a Guidelines enhancement are reviewed deferentially, and the appellate court will not reverse unless the district court clearly erred. Here, because the PSR facts were adopted without objection and the record strongly supported the enhancement, Cutbank had no realistic path to reversal on factual grounds.

## Procedural correctness versus substantive reasonableness

Cutbank’s real complaint was not just about the facts. He argued that adding the enhancement produced a sentence that was “greater than necessary” under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). That is a substantive-reasonableness argument, not merely a technical objection to the Guidelines calculation. The opinion therefore walks through the proper sequence from Gall v. United States and Rosales-Mireles v. United States: the district court must first calculate the Guidelines range correctly, and only then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a variance or departure.

Once the district court sentenced Cutbank within the correctly calculated range, the sentence received a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. The district court said it had reviewed the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda and had considered the § 3553(a) factors. The Eighth Circuit treated that as enough. A sentencing judge does not have to recite every factor mechanically, so long as the record shows the judge actually considered them. The appellate court therefore presumed that the district court heard Cutbank’s mitigation arguments and rejected them.

## Why the 292-month sentence was affirmed

The substantive facts made the sentence easy to defend. Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms. The offense itself was exceptionally dangerous: he terrorized D.F. in her home, in front of children and others in the house, while intoxicated and using methamphetamine, and later injured a police officer while resisting arrest. On that record, a 292-month sentence at the bottom of the advisory range was not outside the district court’s discretion.

The bottom line is that the Eighth Circuit found no procedural error in the guideline calculation and no substantive unreasonableness in the sentence chosen. This case is a good illustration of how federal sentencing review works in practice: once the district court makes a supported Guidelines calculation, considers the § 3553(a) factors, and imposes a within-range sentence, the court of appeals is unlikely to disturb the result.
