Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 4: # Sentencing: ACCA Status, the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement, and Appellate Review of a Within-Guidelines Sentence

## Reconstructing the Guidelines calculation

The sentencing discussion begins with the Presentence Investigation Report, because federal sentencing still starts with a correctly calculated Guidelines range. The PSR assigned Cutbank a base offense level of 26 and then added three separate enhancements: three levels because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number, two levels because he recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and four levels because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. Those calculations produced a total offense level of 35. With Criminal History Category VI, the advisory range was 292 to 365 months.

For a law student, the important lesson is that the Guidelines calculation is built in layers. The base offense level sets the starting point, and specific offense characteristics then raise or lower the range depending on the facts found by the sentencing court. Here, the PSR’s starting point was elevated because the firearm was treated as a sawed-off weapon under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), which the Guidelines regard as especially dangerous. That matters because the weapon’s classification affects how serious the possession offense is for Guidelines purposes, even before any enhancements are added.

## ACCA and the armed career criminal guideline

Cutbank was not sentenced as an ordinary felon-in-possession defendant. The jury also found that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). ACCA is a statutory sentencing enhancement, not just a Guidelines adjustment, and it greatly increases punishment for repeat offenders who unlawfully possess firearms.

The opinion also explains the relationship between ACCA and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4. That guideline produces its own offense level, but the key rule is simple: when multiple guideline calculations are possible, the greater offense level controls. So although the armed career criminal guideline would have yielded offense level 34, the district court used the higher total offense level of 35 from the PSR’s other calculations. That is why the dispute over the four-level enhancement mattered. If the enhancement fell away, the guideline math could have changed.

## The challenged § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement

Cutbank’s main sentencing objection was to the four-level enhancement in USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense.” The guideline commentary makes clear that the other felony does not have to be separately charged or result in a conviction. Application Note 14(C) says the enhancement applies regardless of whether the defendant was actually prosecuted for that other felony.

That rule is important because it explains why the district court could rely on the facts set out in the PSR and the evidence at trial. The opinion notes that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection. Procedurally, that matters a great deal: when a defendant does not challenge the PSR’s facts at sentencing, the court may treat them as established for sentencing purposes. On appeal, that makes it much harder to argue that the district court lacked a factual basis for the enhancement.

## Why the court rejected Cutbank’s challenge to the enhancement

The Eighth Circuit had little trouble affirming the enhancement. The court said that, under the facts in the PSR, it was not error—“much less clear error”—to find that the firearm was used in connection with another felony. The opinion does not spend much time spelling out the exact felony theory, but the record described a violent domestic episode in which Cutbank pointed the gun at D.F., held her in a closet at gunpoint, threatened her, struck her with the gun, and fled with the weapon. Those facts plainly supported a conclusion that the firearm was part of other felony conduct, not merely coincidentally present.

The court also emphasized the standard of review. Factual determinations supporting a Guidelines enhancement are reviewed deferentially, and the appellate court will not reverse unless the district court clearly erred. Here, because the PSR facts were adopted without objection and the record strongly supported the enhancement, Cutbank had no realistic path to reversal on factual grounds.

## Procedural correctness versus substantive reasonableness

Cutbank’s real complaint was not just about the facts. He argued that adding the enhancement produced a sentence that was “greater than necessary” under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). That is a substantive-reasonableness argument, not merely a technical objection to the Guidelines calculation. The opinion therefore walks through the proper sequence from Gall v. United States and Rosales-Mireles v. United States: the district court must first calculate the Guidelines range correctly, and only then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a variance or departure.

Once the district court sentenced Cutbank within the correctly calculated range, the sentence received a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. The district court said it had reviewed the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda and had considered the § 3553(a) factors. The Eighth Circuit treated that as enough. A sentencing judge does not have to recite every factor mechanically, so long as the record shows the judge actually considered them. The appellate court therefore presumed that the district court heard Cutbank’s mitigation arguments and rejected them.

## Why the 292-month sentence was affirmed

The substantive facts made the sentence easy to defend. Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms. The offense itself was exceptionally dangerous: he terrorized D.F. in her home, in front of children and others in the house, while intoxicated and using methamphetamine, and later injured a police officer while resisting arrest. On that record, a 292-month sentence at the bottom of the advisory range was not outside the district court’s discretion.

The bottom line is that the Eighth Circuit found no procedural error in the guideline calculation and no substantive unreasonableness in the sentence chosen. This case is a good illustration of how federal sentencing review works in practice: once the district court makes a supported Guidelines calculation, considers the § 3553(a) factors, and imposes a within-range sentence, the court of appeals is unlikely to disturb the result.
