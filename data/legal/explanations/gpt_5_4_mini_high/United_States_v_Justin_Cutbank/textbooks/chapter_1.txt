Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 1: # From Violent Night to Federal Firearm Case

## The events inside D.F.’s home: methamphetamine use, threats, confinement in a closet, and striking D.F. with the gun

The case begins with a late-night domestic episode that quickly became the factual core of the federal firearm prosecution. According to the evidence at trial, Justin Cutbank had been drinking and using methamphetamine when he woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. While holding her there, he acted paranoid, accusing people in and around the house of listening to him and waiting to harm him. D.F. testified that he kept her in the closet at gunpoint for what felt like hours, threatened to kill her, and struck her across the face with the gun. Those facts mattered not only because they showed violence and intimidation, but because they helped explain why the later discovery of the gun and the testimony of the witnesses were so important at trial.

## Other witnesses in the house, including D.F.’s sons and housemate C.F., and why their presence mattered

Cutbank eventually ordered D.F. to wake up the other people in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Their presence mattered in two ways. First, it showed the seriousness of the threat and the broader danger in the home, because the confrontation was not limited to a private argument with D.F. Second, both D.F. and C.F. later became key witnesses identifying the gun. Their testimony linked Cutbank to the firearm before the night in question and helped establish that the weapon found later was the same one he had possessed earlier.

## Cutbank’s flight from the home with D.F.’s phone and the firearm

At some point after the confrontation, Cutbank fled from the house with both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. That fact became important because it connected the domestic incident to the later search for physical evidence and to the possession charge itself. In a felon-in-possession case, the government must prove that the defendant knowingly possessed a firearm. Evidence that the same gun was seen with him before, during, and after the incident helped the government prove possession rather than mere proximity or accident.

## The second police call: Cutbank barricading himself in a nearby garage and making erratic statements

Police later received another call, this time from a house about a half mile from D.F.’s home, reporting that a man was in the garage and refusing to leave. When officers arrived, they found Cutbank there. He barricaded himself inside the garage and over the next several hours made erratic statements while claiming to have a weapon. This second scene mattered because it placed Cutbank in a different location within a short time after the home incident and gave officers and the jury more evidence about his behavior during the relevant time period. The officers’ response to this standoff also generated some of the procedural and evidentiary issues raised on appeal.

## Resistance to arrest, including biting an officer and grabbing for the officer’s gun

When officers tried to detain him, Cutbank resisted violently. The opinion notes that he bit an officer in the face and neck and grabbed the officer’s gun. These facts were significant for the sentencing discussion as well as the narrative of the offense. They showed that the episode did not end with his flight from D.F.’s house; it escalated into a confrontation with law enforcement that increased the danger to others. At sentencing, the district court also relied on the broader conduct surrounding the offense, including his resistance to arrest, in assessing the seriousness of the crime and the appropriate punishment.

## Physical evidence recovered along the likely path of flight: D.F.’s phone, a knife, and the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle

Officers searched the route Cutbank likely took between D.F.’s home and the garage and found several pieces of physical evidence: D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. This recovery was a major evidentiary anchor for the government’s case. The phone supported the theory that Cutbank had fled the home carrying items from the confrontation. The knife suggested the path between the two locations was not incidental or harmless. Most importantly, the rifle was the firearm at issue in the felon-in-possession charge. Its altered condition also mattered later at sentencing, because the Presentence Investigation Report treated it as a sawed-off firearm described in the Guidelines’ definition of a firearm subject to enhanced treatment.

## DNA evidence and witness identification linking Cutbank to the rifle

The government strengthened the connection between Cutbank and the rifle in two ways. First, DNA on the gun matched Cutbank. Second, both D.F. and C.F. identified the weapon as one they had seen him with before that night. This combination of scientific and eyewitness evidence made the possession case stronger than a case based on a recovered firearm alone. It also explains why the appellate opinion later treated the evidentiary disputes as largely immaterial: the government had substantial proof that the gun belonged to the same chain of events as the domestic incident and that Cutbank had possessed it.

## The federal charge under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and the enhanced penalty framework of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)

Cutbank was charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). That statute makes it a federal crime for a person previously convicted of a felony to possess a firearm. The case also implicated 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), the Armed Career Criminal Act, because the government alleged that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions. The practical importance of § 924(e) is that it raises the penalty range significantly for qualifying defendants. In other words, the case was not just about whether he possessed a gun; it was also about whether his criminal history triggered a much harsher sentencing regime.

## The jury’s conviction on the possession charge and its finding of at least three prior violent felony convictions

The jury convicted Cutbank of the felon-in-possession offense. The opinion also states that the jury found he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which triggered the enhanced penalty provisions of § 924(e). That finding mattered because it moved the case into Armed Career Criminal territory and affected the sentencing calculation. Once the enhancement applied, the district court had to sentence him under a much higher statutory and Guidelines framework than would apply to an ordinary § 922(g)(1) conviction.

## The district court’s sentence of 292 months and the basic structure of the advisory Guidelines calculation

Before sentencing, the Presentence Investigation Report proposed a detailed advisory Guidelines calculation. It recommended a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm and Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence. It then added three levels for an altered or obliterated serial number, two levels for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police, and four levels for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). That produced a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory range was 292 to 365 months. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, the bottom of that range. The opinion also notes that although the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a lower offense level in one respect, the Guidelines direct courts to use the higher offense level when multiple provisions apply.

## The issues on appeal: multiple evidentiary and procedural objections plus a challenge to the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement

On appeal, Cutbank challenged both his conviction and his sentence. He argued that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes during trial, including rulings about prior firearm possession testimony, limiting instructions, references to D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun, and authentication of a police report used for impeachment. He also challenged the four-level sentencing enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which increased his advisory Guidelines range. The appellate court emphasized that he offered almost no supporting authority or analysis for these claims.

## The appellate court’s framing of the appeal as a series of largely unsupported claims

The Eighth Circuit’s opening assessment of the appeal is important for reading the rest of the opinion. The court signaled that the arguments were weak from the start by saying Cutbank provided “no cases and almost no analysis” and that none of the contentions had merit. That framing helps explain the opinion’s structure: the court deals with each issue briefly, relying on established rules such as abuse-of-discretion review for evidentiary rulings, harmless-error principles, and deferential review of sentencing decisions. The bottom-line result is that the conviction and sentence were affirmed because the trial evidence was substantial, the challenged rulings were within the district court’s discretion, and the sentence was both procedurally and substantively reasonable.
