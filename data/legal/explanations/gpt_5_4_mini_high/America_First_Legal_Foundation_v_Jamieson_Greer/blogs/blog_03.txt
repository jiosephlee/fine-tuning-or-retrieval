Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Informational Standing Has Limits: Why a Hope for Investigative “Fruits” Is Not a Right to Information

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is not just another standing ruling. It is a useful reminder that “informational injury” has a real doctrinal boundary: a plaintiff must be denied information the statute actually entitles it to receive. A hope that an agency investigation might eventually generate helpful notices, reports, or discipline is not the same thing.

That distinction mattered here because America First Legal tried to turn the Office of Special Counsel’s refusal to investigate a FOIA policy into a disclosure case. Judge Katsas, writing for the court, was not persuaded. The court vacated the district court’s merits ruling and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, holding that AFL lacked Article III standing. The most important part of the opinion is its rejection of AFL’s second standing theory: the notion that a contingent benefit from an investigation can itself count as an informational injury.

## A right to information, not a right to a process that might produce information

The core of AFL’s argument was deceptively simple. If OSC were required to investigate DOJ under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, AFL said, then OSC might have to provide notices and information that are associated with investigations under § 1214. So, AFL argued, OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of information.

The court saw through that move. Informational standing depends on being denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive. That is the lesson of cases like *FEC v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*. In those cases, Congress had created a direct right of access to information, and the injury was the denial of that statutory entitlement.

Here, by contrast, § 1216 does not create an entitlement to receive information. Even assuming it requires OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, that is only a duty to investigate. It is not a promise that the requester will get the notices, updates, or procedural protections associated with a different statutory regime. AFL tried to bridge that gap by pointing to § 1214, but the court noted that AFL did not argue — and the case law does not support — the idea that OSC must automatically import § 1214’s notice requirements into § 1216 investigations. Indeed, the court cited *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* for the proposition that OSC’s decision whether to follow those procedures in this setting is discretionary.

That distinction does a lot of work. It means AFL was not denied information it had a legal right to receive. It was denied an investigative process that might, under some circumstances, have generated information. That is too speculative to qualify as informational standing.

## Why *Akins* and *Public Citizen* did not save AFL

This is where the opinion is most useful doctrinally. Litigants increasingly invoke “informational injury” to challenge agency inaction, and the instinct is understandable: if an agency won’t act, perhaps it has denied the public information that action would have produced. But *Akins* and *Public Citizen* do not go that far.

In both cases, the plaintiffs were within the class of people Congress had entitled to receive information. The injury was concrete because the statute itself guaranteed access. Here, AFL was not denied a statutory disclosure. It was denied the chance to trigger an investigation that might or might not have led to notices, records, or enforcement. The court treated that as a step too far — and rightly so.

That analysis also helps explain the court’s redressability discussion. Even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, that would not necessarily produce the information AFL wanted. OSC might still decline to adopt § 1214-style procedures. And even if OSC pursued enforcement, that would not guarantee disclosure of DOJ records. The chain runs through too many independent actors and too many discretionary decisions to support standing.

## The broader doctrinal signal

The opinion should make it harder to recast agency-process complaints as informational standing claims. That matters well beyond this FOIA dispute. Any time a plaintiff says, in effect, “if the agency had done its job, maybe it would have created or uncovered information I want,” courts now have a clean D.C. Circuit precedent for answering: that is not the same as a statutory entitlement to information.

The decision does not diminish informational standing in its proper lane. If Congress creates a disclosure right, courts can enforce it. But *Greer* insists on the right kind of right: a concrete statutory entitlement, not a contingent hope that an investigation will produce useful fruits. That is a sensible limit, and one that will likely be cited whenever litigants try to convert nonenforcement grievances into disclosure cases.

## Bottom line

Judge Katsas’s opinion is careful and narrow, but its message is clear. Informational standing does not extend to everything that might eventually lead to information. A statute that requires an agency to investigate is not the same as a statute that entitles a plaintiff to receive the investigation’s byproducts. For standing purposes, that difference is decisive.
