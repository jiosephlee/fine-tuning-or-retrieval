Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Redressability Through a Chain of Agency Discretion: OSC, MSPB, DOJ, and the Post-Chaney Standing Problem

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is important less for what it says about FOIA than for what it says about standing in modern administrative litigation. Judge Katsas’s opinion is a sharp reminder that Article III gets skeptical very quickly when the plaintiff’s requested relief depends on a chain of decisions by multiple agencies, only some of which are even before the court. If the plaintiff needs Agency A to investigate, Agency B to sanction, and Agency C to change its conduct, the case is likely to run straight into redressability trouble.

That matters because a lot of administrative-law suits are really indirect enforcement actions. Plaintiffs often do not just want a court order against the agency that hurt them; they want a court order that will set off pressure on another agency, or on an employee, or on a future enforcement decision. This opinion shows the limits of that strategy.

## What the court actually held

America First Legal asked the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged DOJ FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. OSC declined, reading the statute as requiring the predicates found in FOIA’s own disciplinary provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL then sued.

The district court reached the merits, agreed with AFL that OSC had misread the statute, but still refused to order an investigation. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit never got to that statutory question. Instead, it held that AFL lacked Article III standing.

That holding turned on causation and redressability. AFL tried two theories. First, it argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate had kept DOJ from producing the records AFL wanted. But that injury, the court said, was traceable to DOJ’s own conduct, not OSC’s. And even if OSC were forced to investigate, the hoped-for relief would depend on a speculative sequence: OSC might choose corrective or disciplinary action; the MSPB might impose sanctions; DOJ might then decide to produce records. That is too many steps, too much discretion, and too much speculation to satisfy Article III.

Second, AFL said it had an informational injury because OSC failed to give it the notices and information it would receive in an investigation. The court rejected that too. Even assuming § 1216 requires OSC to investigate certain FOIA complaints, the statute does not itself entitle AFL to receive information. And AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to follow the notice provisions applicable to § 1214 investigations is discretionary in the § 1216 setting. Without a statutory entitlement to receive the information, there is no concrete informational injury.

## The opinion’s doctrinal move: standing as a filter for indirect enforcement suits

The most interesting part of the opinion is how it uses familiar standing cases to police a very contemporary litigation tactic: suing one agency in hopes of forcing another agency to act.

The court’s reliance on *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* is classic procedure-in-vacuo doctrine. A plaintiff cannot get into federal court by invoking a procedural violation unless that violation affects some concrete interest. AFL tried to turn OSC’s alleged failure to investigate into a procedural injury, but the court said that framing does not do enough work unless the procedure is tied to a concrete, legally protected interest.

The more consequential move comes from *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, *Haaland v. Brackeen*, and *Hecate Energy*. Those cases emphasize that redressability is not satisfied by a story about how a judgment might persuade or influence third parties. The judgment must bind the defendant in a way that makes relief likely. That distinction is especially important in cases like this one, where the plaintiff wants an order against a watchdog agency in the hope that the order will pressure someone else to change course.

That is where *Heckler v. Chaney* enters the picture. *Chaney* is usually remembered for nonreviewability of enforcement discretion, but its deeper significance here is structural: if the path to relief runs through discretionary enforcement choices, courts are understandably reluctant to treat that path as sufficiently certain. Judge Katsas’s opinion treats those downstream choices as too contingent to ground standing.

## What this means for future litigation

The practical lesson is straightforward. If your injury is really that another agency is withholding records, compelling a supervisory or investigative agency to “look into it” is unlikely to establish standing unless the statute creates a direct, concrete entitlement that the plaintiff can actually invoke.

That should make plaintiffs think carefully about their forum and their cause of action. If the target is records, sue the records holder. If the claim is that a statute creates a right to receive information, identify the statutory entitlement with precision. And if the only path to relief runs through multiple layers of agency discretion, expect redressability to become the main battleground.

The decision also has broader implications for oversight statutes. Congress sometimes creates mandatory investigative duties without creating a private right to force the investigation, and plaintiffs then try to use standing doctrine to bridge the gap. This opinion suggests that bridge may be narrower than some litigants hoped. A statutory command to investigate does not automatically mean a private plaintiff can sue to compel that investigation, especially if the real harm is the conduct of a different agency and the requested remedy depends on what a chain of other actors might do later.

## The bigger picture

There is a tension here, and the opinion is candid about it without dwelling on it. If Congress wants an investigation to happen, but the statute gives no direct enforcement mechanism and no concrete right to information, then plaintiffs may be left with a remedial gap. But that is a consequence of Article III’s insistence on a real case or controversy, not merely a policy complaint about agency inaction.

So the case is best read as a standing decision with teeth. It does not decide whether OSC misread the statute. It decides something more structural: when the requested judicial relief only starts a sequence of discretionary decisions by agencies not before the court, the causal chain is often too attenuated to support standing. For administrative litigants, that is the lesson to take away.
