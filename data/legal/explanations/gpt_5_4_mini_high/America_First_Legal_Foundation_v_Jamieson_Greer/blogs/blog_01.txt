Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# A Statutory “Shall” Without a Plaintiff: What *America First Legal* Says About Procedural Rights and Article III

## Why the case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is important for a reason that goes well beyond FOIA. The court held that even when Congress uses mandatory language — here, that the Office of Special Counsel “shall … conduct an investigation” of certain alleged FOIA violations — a plaintiff still needs its own concrete Article III injury to get into federal court. In other words, a statutory command directed to an agency is not automatically a judicially enforceable entitlement for anyone who wants the command carried out.

That may sound technical, but it has real consequences. Litigants often try to transform statutory oversight provisions into leverage for broader disputes with the executive branch. The D.C. Circuit’s answer here is that the Constitution does not let a plaintiff sue just because it thinks an agency should investigate someone else. The plaintiff must show an injury that is concrete, particularized, and redressable by a court order against the defendant before it.

## How the court got there

The key move in Judge Katsas’s opinion is to separate two very different things: a statutory duty to investigate, and a plaintiff’s own right to receive information or other concrete benefits from that investigation. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s FOIA practice injured it in two ways. First, DOJ had not produced the records it wanted. Second, OSC had not given it the notices and information that would accompany an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1214.

The court rejected both theories. On the FOIA side, the injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. And the route from an OSC investigation to disclosure is too indirect to satisfy causation and redressability: OSC would have to decide to pursue enforcement, the MSPB would have to act, and DOJ would have to change course. That is exactly the kind of chain of contingent decisions the Supreme Court has warned against, especially where the choices of independent actors and agency enforcement discretion are doing the work.

The second theory fared no better because § 1216, even if it imposes a duty to investigate, does not by itself create a right to receive information. The court treated AFL’s claim as an attempt to bootstrap a procedural entitlement into informational standing. But as the standing cases teach, a procedural violation alone is not enough. There must be some concrete interest the procedure protects. Here, the court said, AFL had not identified any statutory entitlement to the notices it wanted. And because OSC’s procedures under § 1214 are discretionary in the § 1216 setting, a court order compelling an investigation would not necessarily produce the information AFL sought anyway.

## The doctrinal significance

This is a fairly strict application of the Supreme Court’s recent standing decisions, especially *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. The throughline is familiar: federal courts are increasingly reluctant to let plaintiffs sue over alleged statutory noncompliance unless the statute gives them something concrete — access to information, a legal status, a payment, a disclosure, a permit, or some comparable interest — that the challenged conduct actually invades.

The opinion also fits a pattern in the D.C. Circuit. The court has been careful in recent years to police causation and redressability in suits that depend on agency action by third parties or on downstream enforcement choices. Here, Judge Katsas emphasized that even a mandatory “shall” does not solve the constitutional problem if the plaintiff cannot show that the relief requested would likely redress its own injury. That is a meaningful limitation on attempts to use oversight statutes as litigation tools against agencies that are not the direct source of the underlying harm.

There is also a quiet but important structural point here. OSC is an oversight and enforcement body, but the court would not let AFL convert that institutional role into a private right of action to force investigation. That distinction matters because Congress often creates duties for agencies without creating private enforcement rights. *America First Legal* reads § 1216 as one of those provisions: a directive to the agency, not an invitation to litigants to sue whenever they think OSC should act.

## Bottom line

The opinion is less about whether DOJ’s FOIA practices were lawful than about who gets to demand that OSC investigate them. The D.C. Circuit’s answer is that Article III does not permit a plaintiff to sue over a statutory command in the abstract. A “shall” aimed at an agency is not enough unless the plaintiff can tie it to a concrete personal injury and show that a court order would likely redress that injury.

For litigants, the lesson is straightforward: if the real grievance is a FOIA withholding, sue the withholding agency. If the grievance is that an oversight body declined to investigate, standing will be hard to come by unless Congress has given the plaintiff a direct informational or other concrete entitlement. This decision suggests the D.C. Circuit is prepared to use all three standing elements — injury, causation, and redressability — to keep private parties from converting statutory oversight duties into freestanding judicial claims.
