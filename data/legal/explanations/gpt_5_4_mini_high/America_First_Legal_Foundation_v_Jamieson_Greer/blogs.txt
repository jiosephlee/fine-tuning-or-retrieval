Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# A Statutory “Shall” Without a Plaintiff: What *America First Legal* Says About Procedural Rights and Article III

## Why the case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is important for a reason that goes well beyond FOIA. The court held that even when Congress uses mandatory language — here, that the Office of Special Counsel “shall … conduct an investigation” of certain alleged FOIA violations — a plaintiff still needs its own concrete Article III injury to get into federal court. In other words, a statutory command directed to an agency is not automatically a judicially enforceable entitlement for anyone who wants the command carried out.

That may sound technical, but it has real consequences. Litigants often try to transform statutory oversight provisions into leverage for broader disputes with the executive branch. The D.C. Circuit’s answer here is that the Constitution does not let a plaintiff sue just because it thinks an agency should investigate someone else. The plaintiff must show an injury that is concrete, particularized, and redressable by a court order against the defendant before it.

## How the court got there

The key move in Judge Katsas’s opinion is to separate two very different things: a statutory duty to investigate, and a plaintiff’s own right to receive information or other concrete benefits from that investigation. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s FOIA practice injured it in two ways. First, DOJ had not produced the records it wanted. Second, OSC had not given it the notices and information that would accompany an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1214.

The court rejected both theories. On the FOIA side, the injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. And the route from an OSC investigation to disclosure is too indirect to satisfy causation and redressability: OSC would have to decide to pursue enforcement, the MSPB would have to act, and DOJ would have to change course. That is exactly the kind of chain of contingent decisions the Supreme Court has warned against, especially where the choices of independent actors and agency enforcement discretion are doing the work.

The second theory fared no better because § 1216, even if it imposes a duty to investigate, does not by itself create a right to receive information. The court treated AFL’s claim as an attempt to bootstrap a procedural entitlement into informational standing. But as the standing cases teach, a procedural violation alone is not enough. There must be some concrete interest the procedure protects. Here, the court said, AFL had not identified any statutory entitlement to the notices it wanted. And because OSC’s procedures under § 1214 are discretionary in the § 1216 setting, a court order compelling an investigation would not necessarily produce the information AFL sought anyway.

## The doctrinal significance

This is a fairly strict application of the Supreme Court’s recent standing decisions, especially *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. The throughline is familiar: federal courts are increasingly reluctant to let plaintiffs sue over alleged statutory noncompliance unless the statute gives them something concrete — access to information, a legal status, a payment, a disclosure, a permit, or some comparable interest — that the challenged conduct actually invades.

The opinion also fits a pattern in the D.C. Circuit. The court has been careful in recent years to police causation and redressability in suits that depend on agency action by third parties or on downstream enforcement choices. Here, Judge Katsas emphasized that even a mandatory “shall” does not solve the constitutional problem if the plaintiff cannot show that the relief requested would likely redress its own injury. That is a meaningful limitation on attempts to use oversight statutes as litigation tools against agencies that are not the direct source of the underlying harm.

There is also a quiet but important structural point here. OSC is an oversight and enforcement body, but the court would not let AFL convert that institutional role into a private right of action to force investigation. That distinction matters because Congress often creates duties for agencies without creating private enforcement rights. *America First Legal* reads § 1216 as one of those provisions: a directive to the agency, not an invitation to litigants to sue whenever they think OSC should act.

## Bottom line

The opinion is less about whether DOJ’s FOIA practices were lawful than about who gets to demand that OSC investigate them. The D.C. Circuit’s answer is that Article III does not permit a plaintiff to sue over a statutory command in the abstract. A “shall” aimed at an agency is not enough unless the plaintiff can tie it to a concrete personal injury and show that a court order would likely redress that injury.

For litigants, the lesson is straightforward: if the real grievance is a FOIA withholding, sue the withholding agency. If the grievance is that an oversight body declined to investigate, standing will be hard to come by unless Congress has given the plaintiff a direct informational or other concrete entitlement. This decision suggests the D.C. Circuit is prepared to use all three standing elements — injury, causation, and redressability — to keep private parties from converting statutory oversight duties into freestanding judicial claims.



# The Question the D.C. Circuit Left Open: How Section 1216 Interacts With FOIA’s Own OSC Referral Mechanism

## Why the case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is important not because it answers the FOIA question at the center of the case, but because it leaves that question unanswered. The panel held that America First Legal Foundation lacked Article III standing to force the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. That means the court never reached the statutory issue the case was really about: whether 5 U.S.C. § 1216 gives OSC independent authority — or even a duty — to investigate allegedly arbitrary FOIA withholdings outside FOIA’s own narrower referral mechanism in § 552(a)(4)(F).

That unresolved question matters. If § 1216 is freestanding, OSC can be used as an additional oversight channel for FOIA misconduct, even before a court has found a violation. If it is not, FOIA enforcement stays far more court-centered, with OSC entering the picture only after the specific conditions Congress laid out in § 552(a)(4)(F) are met. Either way, the case touches the architecture of FOIA accountability.

## What the D.C. Circuit actually decided

The panel never blessed the district court’s merits ruling. It vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The standing analysis is straightforward, if unforgiving: AFL’s real injury, the court said, is DOJ’s alleged withholding of records, not OSC’s refusal to investigate. And the causal chain from an OSC investigation to eventual disclosure runs through too many independent actors — OSC, then perhaps the MSPB, then DOJ — to satisfy redressability.

That same problem doomed AFL’s attempt to frame the case as a procedural-injury suit. Even assuming § 1216 creates some procedural entitlement, a plaintiff still needs a concrete interest that the procedure protects. The court held AFL had not shown that OSC’s failure to investigate deprived it of any statutorily guaranteed information. In other words, the panel treated the asserted injury as too indirect and too speculative to get into federal court.

## The merits question the panel left on the table

The unresolved interpretive issue is the one the district court had already wrestled with: how, exactly, does § 1216 fit with FOIA’s own OSC discipline provision in § 552(a)(4)(F)?

On one reading, § 1216(a)(3) is broad and mandatory on its face. It says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” That language does not obviously depend on a prior court order, a fee award, or a judicial finding that the agency acted arbitrarily. Read that way, § 1216 supplies a freestanding investigative authority over alleged FOIA misconduct. FOIA’s own § 552(a)(4)(F), meanwhile, would operate as a separate and narrower mechanism: once a court has ordered disclosure, awarded fees, and specifically questioned arbitrariness, OSC must initiate a disciplinary proceeding.

But there is another plausible reading. Congress may have meant § 552(a)(4)(F) to be the exclusive FOIA-specific trigger for OSC discipline, with § 1216 serving as a more general provision about OSC’s ability to use its existing enforcement tools once a matter is already properly before it. That reading draws force from the structure of FOIA itself, which ordinarily channels disputes into direct judicial review rather than executive-branch discipline. It also avoids turning OSC into a parallel FOIA tribunal for every disputed withholding decision.

The district court came down on the first side of that divide, concluding that § 552(a)(4)(F) does not limit OSC’s power under § 1216. But because the D.C. Circuit vacated for lack of standing, that interpretation now has no appellate force. The statutory question remains genuinely open.

## Why the uncertainty matters for agencies and requesters

For requesters, the difference is significant. If § 1216 is freestanding, a requester could try to use OSC as an additional pressure point when an agency adopts a categorical FOIA practice — here, DOJ’s alleged refusal to search employee emails absent consent. That would not replace a FOIA suit, but it would create another lever for oversight and potential discipline. If § 1216 is not freestanding, that avenue largely disappears, and the requester is left with the ordinary FOIA remedy: sue for the records.

For agencies, the stakes run in the other direction. A broad reading of § 1216 would expose FOIA practices to an executive-branch enforcement mechanism that is not tied to a court finding. That could matter not just in one-off disputes, but for internal policies that affect many requests at once. A narrow reading keeps the disciplinary consequences of FOIA violations tethered to judicial findings and preserves a cleaner separation between disclosure litigation and personnel discipline.

The D.C. Circuit’s standing analysis also has a broader doctrinal effect. It reinforces the idea that a plaintiff cannot manufacture Article III standing by chaining together a series of contingent enforcement steps by agencies that are not before the court. That point may be obvious in the abstract, but the opinion applies it rigorously. For future plaintiffs, the message is clear: wanting an agency to investigate is not the same thing as having a concrete, redressable injury from the failure to investigate.

## What comes next

For now, FOIA’s disciplinary architecture remains unsettled at precisely the point AFL wanted the court to clarify it. The D.C. Circuit did not say whether § 1216 creates independent OSC authority, and it certainly did not say that it doesn’t. It said only that AFL is not the right plaintiff to press that question in federal court.

That leaves the statute’s practical operation uncertain. OSC may continue to take the view that its FOIA-investigation authority is limited, or at least discretionary, unless § 552(a)(4)(F)’s specific trigger has been satisfied. A future case with a different plaintiff, or a different injury theory, may force the merits question into the open. Until then, the governing precedent is mostly negative: no standing, no merits decision, and no appellate resolution of how these two FOIA-related provisions fit together.



# Informational Standing Has Limits: Why a Hope for Investigative “Fruits” Is Not a Right to Information

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is not just another standing ruling. It is a useful reminder that “informational injury” has a real doctrinal boundary: a plaintiff must be denied information the statute actually entitles it to receive. A hope that an agency investigation might eventually generate helpful notices, reports, or discipline is not the same thing.

That distinction mattered here because America First Legal tried to turn the Office of Special Counsel’s refusal to investigate a FOIA policy into a disclosure case. Judge Katsas, writing for the court, was not persuaded. The court vacated the district court’s merits ruling and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, holding that AFL lacked Article III standing. The most important part of the opinion is its rejection of AFL’s second standing theory: the notion that a contingent benefit from an investigation can itself count as an informational injury.

## A right to information, not a right to a process that might produce information

The core of AFL’s argument was deceptively simple. If OSC were required to investigate DOJ under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, AFL said, then OSC might have to provide notices and information that are associated with investigations under § 1214. So, AFL argued, OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of information.

The court saw through that move. Informational standing depends on being denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive. That is the lesson of cases like *FEC v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*. In those cases, Congress had created a direct right of access to information, and the injury was the denial of that statutory entitlement.

Here, by contrast, § 1216 does not create an entitlement to receive information. Even assuming it requires OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, that is only a duty to investigate. It is not a promise that the requester will get the notices, updates, or procedural protections associated with a different statutory regime. AFL tried to bridge that gap by pointing to § 1214, but the court noted that AFL did not argue — and the case law does not support — the idea that OSC must automatically import § 1214’s notice requirements into § 1216 investigations. Indeed, the court cited *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* for the proposition that OSC’s decision whether to follow those procedures in this setting is discretionary.

That distinction does a lot of work. It means AFL was not denied information it had a legal right to receive. It was denied an investigative process that might, under some circumstances, have generated information. That is too speculative to qualify as informational standing.

## Why *Akins* and *Public Citizen* did not save AFL

This is where the opinion is most useful doctrinally. Litigants increasingly invoke “informational injury” to challenge agency inaction, and the instinct is understandable: if an agency won’t act, perhaps it has denied the public information that action would have produced. But *Akins* and *Public Citizen* do not go that far.

In both cases, the plaintiffs were within the class of people Congress had entitled to receive information. The injury was concrete because the statute itself guaranteed access. Here, AFL was not denied a statutory disclosure. It was denied the chance to trigger an investigation that might or might not have led to notices, records, or enforcement. The court treated that as a step too far — and rightly so.

That analysis also helps explain the court’s redressability discussion. Even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, that would not necessarily produce the information AFL wanted. OSC might still decline to adopt § 1214-style procedures. And even if OSC pursued enforcement, that would not guarantee disclosure of DOJ records. The chain runs through too many independent actors and too many discretionary decisions to support standing.

## The broader doctrinal signal

The opinion should make it harder to recast agency-process complaints as informational standing claims. That matters well beyond this FOIA dispute. Any time a plaintiff says, in effect, “if the agency had done its job, maybe it would have created or uncovered information I want,” courts now have a clean D.C. Circuit precedent for answering: that is not the same as a statutory entitlement to information.

The decision does not diminish informational standing in its proper lane. If Congress creates a disclosure right, courts can enforce it. But *Greer* insists on the right kind of right: a concrete statutory entitlement, not a contingent hope that an investigation will produce useful fruits. That is a sensible limit, and one that will likely be cited whenever litigants try to convert nonenforcement grievances into disclosure cases.

## Bottom line

Judge Katsas’s opinion is careful and narrow, but its message is clear. Informational standing does not extend to everything that might eventually lead to information. A statute that requires an agency to investigate is not the same as a statute that entitles a plaintiff to receive the investigation’s byproducts. For standing purposes, that difference is decisive.



# Redressability Through a Chain of Agency Discretion: OSC, MSPB, DOJ, and the Post-Chaney Standing Problem

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is important less for what it says about FOIA than for what it says about standing in modern administrative litigation. Judge Katsas’s opinion is a sharp reminder that Article III gets skeptical very quickly when the plaintiff’s requested relief depends on a chain of decisions by multiple agencies, only some of which are even before the court. If the plaintiff needs Agency A to investigate, Agency B to sanction, and Agency C to change its conduct, the case is likely to run straight into redressability trouble.

That matters because a lot of administrative-law suits are really indirect enforcement actions. Plaintiffs often do not just want a court order against the agency that hurt them; they want a court order that will set off pressure on another agency, or on an employee, or on a future enforcement decision. This opinion shows the limits of that strategy.

## What the court actually held

America First Legal asked the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged DOJ FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. OSC declined, reading the statute as requiring the predicates found in FOIA’s own disciplinary provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL then sued.

The district court reached the merits, agreed with AFL that OSC had misread the statute, but still refused to order an investigation. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit never got to that statutory question. Instead, it held that AFL lacked Article III standing.

That holding turned on causation and redressability. AFL tried two theories. First, it argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate had kept DOJ from producing the records AFL wanted. But that injury, the court said, was traceable to DOJ’s own conduct, not OSC’s. And even if OSC were forced to investigate, the hoped-for relief would depend on a speculative sequence: OSC might choose corrective or disciplinary action; the MSPB might impose sanctions; DOJ might then decide to produce records. That is too many steps, too much discretion, and too much speculation to satisfy Article III.

Second, AFL said it had an informational injury because OSC failed to give it the notices and information it would receive in an investigation. The court rejected that too. Even assuming § 1216 requires OSC to investigate certain FOIA complaints, the statute does not itself entitle AFL to receive information. And AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to follow the notice provisions applicable to § 1214 investigations is discretionary in the § 1216 setting. Without a statutory entitlement to receive the information, there is no concrete informational injury.

## The opinion’s doctrinal move: standing as a filter for indirect enforcement suits

The most interesting part of the opinion is how it uses familiar standing cases to police a very contemporary litigation tactic: suing one agency in hopes of forcing another agency to act.

The court’s reliance on *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* is classic procedure-in-vacuo doctrine. A plaintiff cannot get into federal court by invoking a procedural violation unless that violation affects some concrete interest. AFL tried to turn OSC’s alleged failure to investigate into a procedural injury, but the court said that framing does not do enough work unless the procedure is tied to a concrete, legally protected interest.

The more consequential move comes from *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, *Haaland v. Brackeen*, and *Hecate Energy*. Those cases emphasize that redressability is not satisfied by a story about how a judgment might persuade or influence third parties. The judgment must bind the defendant in a way that makes relief likely. That distinction is especially important in cases like this one, where the plaintiff wants an order against a watchdog agency in the hope that the order will pressure someone else to change course.

That is where *Heckler v. Chaney* enters the picture. *Chaney* is usually remembered for nonreviewability of enforcement discretion, but its deeper significance here is structural: if the path to relief runs through discretionary enforcement choices, courts are understandably reluctant to treat that path as sufficiently certain. Judge Katsas’s opinion treats those downstream choices as too contingent to ground standing.

## What this means for future litigation

The practical lesson is straightforward. If your injury is really that another agency is withholding records, compelling a supervisory or investigative agency to “look into it” is unlikely to establish standing unless the statute creates a direct, concrete entitlement that the plaintiff can actually invoke.

That should make plaintiffs think carefully about their forum and their cause of action. If the target is records, sue the records holder. If the claim is that a statute creates a right to receive information, identify the statutory entitlement with precision. And if the only path to relief runs through multiple layers of agency discretion, expect redressability to become the main battleground.

The decision also has broader implications for oversight statutes. Congress sometimes creates mandatory investigative duties without creating a private right to force the investigation, and plaintiffs then try to use standing doctrine to bridge the gap. This opinion suggests that bridge may be narrower than some litigants hoped. A statutory command to investigate does not automatically mean a private plaintiff can sue to compel that investigation, especially if the real harm is the conduct of a different agency and the requested remedy depends on what a chain of other actors might do later.

## The bigger picture

There is a tension here, and the opinion is candid about it without dwelling on it. If Congress wants an investigation to happen, but the statute gives no direct enforcement mechanism and no concrete right to information, then plaintiffs may be left with a remedial gap. But that is a consequence of Article III’s insistence on a real case or controversy, not merely a policy complaint about agency inaction.

So the case is best read as a standing decision with teeth. It does not decide whether OSC misread the statute. It decides something more structural: when the requested judicial relief only starts a sequence of discretionary decisions by agencies not before the court, the causal chain is often too attenuated to support standing. For administrative litigants, that is the lesson to take away.
