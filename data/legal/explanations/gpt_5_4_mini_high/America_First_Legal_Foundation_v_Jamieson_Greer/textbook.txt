Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Chapter 1: Statutory Architecture Behind the Dispute

## The Office of Special Counsel in the Civil Service Reform Act

This case sits inside a specialized federal employment regime. The Office of Special Counsel, or OSC, is an investigative and prosecutorial agency within that system. Its basic function is to police certain kinds of misconduct in the federal civil service and, when appropriate, to take enforcement steps rather than merely investigate and walk away. The opinion describes OSC as an agency meant to promote a “fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” That framing matters because AFL’s lawsuit assumed OSC had an enforceable duty to investigate one kind of alleged misconduct involving FOIA; OSC responded that its power to act was narrower.

The statutory framework matters even before you reach the case’s standing problem. AFL did not sue DOJ only to force production under FOIA. It also tried to use the OSC statutes to compel an investigation into DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent. To understand why that claim was contested, a law student needs to know how the Civil Service Reform Act channels complaints through OSC and how that channel differs depending on the kind of wrongdoing alleged.

## Section 1214: Prohibited Personnel Practices, Corrective Action, and Notice

The core OSC enforcement provision is 5 U.S.C. § 1214. That section deals with “prohibited personnel practices,” a category of federal employee misconduct that OSC is authorized to investigate. If OSC finds reason to proceed, it can bring an enforcement action before the Merit Systems Protection Board, or MSPB. The statute thus creates an administrative enforcement path: OSC investigates, and the MSPB adjudicates the resulting corrective or disciplinary proceeding.

The word “corrective” is important. A corrective action is aimed at fixing the underlying wrong, not simply punishing the employee who caused it. Section 1214 also contains procedural protections for the person who complained to OSC. The opinion notes that these include notice provisions requiring OSC to keep the complaining party informed about the progress of the investigation. Those notice obligations became relevant because AFL argued that if OSC had to investigate DOJ under the FOIA-related provision, then it should also have to give AFL the sort of information that a § 1214 complainant receives.

For a student reading the opinion, the key point is that § 1214 is not just a substantive authorization to investigate. It also comes with procedures, and those procedures helped AFL frame its claimed injury. But § 1214 by its terms deals with prohibited personnel practices, not every kind of agency wrongdoing.

## Section 1215: Disciplinary Action Before the MSPB

Section 1215 supplies the other enforcement track. Where § 1214 is about corrective action, § 1215 is about disciplinary action. In the opinion’s terms, OSC may bring a disciplinary proceeding before the MSPB against an offending employee. The distinction is functional: corrective action addresses the problem caused by the agency’s conduct, while disciplinary action addresses the employee responsible for it.

This matters because the statutes do not simply authorize OSC to investigate. They also define what OSC may ask the MSPB to do after the investigation. AFL’s theory assumed that if OSC investigated DOJ under the FOIA-related provision, it could lead to the same sort of enforcement machinery that applies in prohibited-personnel-practice cases. That is why the relationship between §§ 1214 and 1215 mattered so much. The lawsuit was not just about whether OSC could look into DOJ’s behavior; it was also about whether the investigation could be tied to the usual remedial and disciplinary apparatus of the CSRA.

## Section 1216: A Separate OSC Power for Certain Other Misconduct

Section 1216 is the statute that brought FOIA into the OSC scheme. It gives OSC authority over certain misconduct that is not itself a prohibited personnel practice. Most importantly here, § 1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations concerning “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of information prohibited under FOIA. That mandatory “shall” is the statutory language AFL relied on. In AFL’s view, once it alleged an arbitrary FOIA withholding, OSC had to investigate.

Section 1216(c) then says OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That phrase is the source of much of the statutory tension. It suggests that FOIA-withholding allegations can be handled through the same enforcement machinery used for prohibited personnel practices, but it does not spell out exactly which § 1214 procedures automatically carry over and which remain discretionary.

That ambiguity matters. AFL treated § 1216 as a stand-alone mandate to investigate FOIA misconduct. OSC read the statute more narrowly and said it lacked authority to proceed unless the separate FOIA-specific trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had been satisfied. The district court thought § 1216 did not depend on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), but it also thought the choice whether to employ certain § 1214-style procedures in a § 1216 investigation was discretionary. The statutory text therefore created a layered scheme: a mandatory investigation provision on one level, and more open-ended enforcement language on another.

## FOIA’s Own OSC Provision and the Three Predicates

FOIA contains a separate OSC enforcement mechanism in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This provision is narrower and more specific than § 1216. It applies only when three things happen: a court orders production of agency records improperly withheld under FOIA, the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and the court specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records. If all three predicates are met, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the responsible officer or employee.

That provision is not a general complaint process. It is a court-triggered disciplinary referral mechanism. In other words, Congress did not say that every allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding automatically requires OSC action under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). Instead, the statute ties OSC’s obligation to a prior judicial determination and to a particular kind of FOIA litigation outcome.

This distinction was central to the dispute. OSC said the three statutory predicates had not been satisfied, so it did not have to investigate. AFL said that even if FOIA’s own referral provision was not triggered, § 1216 independently required OSC to investigate the alleged withholding practice. The district court accepted the broader reading of § 1216, but the court of appeals ultimately did not resolve the statutory merits because it found AFL lacked Article III standing.

## The Tension Between the Two FOIA-Related Pathways

The heart of the statutory problem is that FOIA-related misconduct appears in two different provisions, and they do not line up perfectly. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) gives OSC a specific role when a court has already found a FOIA violation and has questioned the agency’s conduct. Section 1216, by contrast, appears to let OSC investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding even without those judicial predicates. The question is whether the FOIA-specific referral provision limits OSC’s broader authority or merely adds one more enforcement route.

That question framed the entire lawsuit. AFL wanted to use § 1216 to force OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy, while OSC argued that no investigation was required absent the more specific FOIA triggers. Understanding this statutory architecture is essential to reading the opinion, because the later standing analysis only makes sense once you see what AFL was trying to obtain from OSC in the first place.

# Chapter 2: Facts, Claims, and the Case’s Procedural Path

## The FOIA request and the underlying records dispute

America First Legal Foundation’s dispute began with an effort to investigate judicial recusals. To do that, AFL submitted a Freedom of Information Act request to the Department of Justice seeking certain emails between an outside attorney and DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL believed that DOJ had denied the request on an improper rationale—specifically, that the Civil Division would not search employee email accounts for responsive records unless the employee consented. That alleged policy became the foundation for AFL’s later challenge.

The key point for reading the opinion is that AFL’s lawsuit against DOJ under FOIA was separate from the suit against the Office of Special Counsel. The FOIA suit was the ordinary records-disclosure case, and DOJ was already producing responsive records in that case while the appeal in this case was pending. The D.C. Circuit makes a point of saying that the sufficiency of DOJ’s ongoing production was not the issue before it here. This case instead concerns whether AFL could force OSC to investigate DOJ’s conduct.

## AFL’s separate request to the Office of Special Counsel

AFL next asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to investigate allegations of “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information” prohibited under FOIA. In AFL’s view, this statute required OSC to look into DOJ’s alleged FOIA practice even if the ordinary FOIA enforcement mechanism was not triggered.

OSC disagreed. It said it lacked authority to investigate because the conditions that trigger the separate FOIA enforcement provision in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. That provision applies only when a court has already ordered disclosure, awarded attorneys’ fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records. OSC’s position was that without those predicates, it had no duty to open an investigation.

## The district court proceedings

AFL then sued OSC in district court to compel the investigation. The district court reached an unusual split result. It agreed with AFL on the statutory interpretation question and held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s investigative authority under § 1216. In other words, the court concluded that OSC had been wrong to treat the absence of the FOIA-specific predicates as a reason not to investigate.

But the district court stopped there. It refused to order OSC to investigate, reasoning that § 1216(c) left the decision whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action to OSC’s discretion. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration rather than issuing the coercive relief AFL sought. After OSC again declined to investigate, AFL asked the district court to enter final judgment, and the court dismissed the remaining claims.

That procedural sequence matters because it shows why the appeal reached the D.C. Circuit in the posture it did. AFL was appealing from a case in which it had won a favorable statutory ruling in part, but had still failed to obtain the investigation it wanted.

## The appeal and the court of appeals’ threshold review

AFL appealed, and the D.C. Circuit reviewed the case de novo. That means the court of appeals considered the legal issues anew, without deferring to the district court’s legal conclusions. The panel initially noted that neither party had addressed Article III standing, but because standing is part of subject-matter jurisdiction, the court had to satisfy itself that it had power to hear the case before reaching the merits. It therefore ordered supplemental briefing on standing.

This is an important procedural lesson for reading the opinion. Even though the district court had addressed the statutory merits, the court of appeals first asked whether AFL had a sufficient injury, causation, and redressability to invoke federal judicial power at all. The ultimate appellate holding was that AFL lacked standing, so the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Chapter 3: Article III Standing as a Threshold Barrier

## Why the court addressed standing before the merits

This opinion is a good example of a basic but crucial point in federal litigation: a court must be sure it has Article III jurisdiction before it can decide the merits. Even though the district court had already ruled on AFL’s statutory claims, the court of appeals had an independent duty to confirm that AFL had standing to sue. That is why the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on standing even though neither party had initially focused on the issue. If standing is missing, the federal courts lack power to issue a merits judgment at all, and any such judgment must be vacated.

That is what happened here. The panel never reached the correctness of AFL’s interpretation of the Office of Special Counsel’s statutory duties. Instead, it held that AFL had not shown a concrete Article III injury fairly traceable to OSC and likely to be redressed by a favorable court order. Because standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite, the proper disposition was to vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

## The standing framework the court applied

The opinion begins with the familiar Article III test from *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*: a plaintiff must show an injury in fact, causation, and redressability. The injury must be concrete and particularized, and it must be actual or imminent rather than speculative. “Concrete” means the harm is real, not abstract; “particularized” means it affects the plaintiff itself, not the public at large. Causation asks whether the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct. Redressability asks whether a favorable judgment is likely to remedy the injury.

At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the plaintiff does not need to prove standing with evidence, but it must plausibly allege facts supporting each element. That matters in an opinion like this one because AFL’s standing theory depended on a chain of inferences about what OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ might each do if a court ordered OSC to investigate. The court treated that chain skeptically, as Article III requires.

## AFL’s procedural-injury theory

AFL’s lawsuit was built around the idea that OSC had unlawfully refused to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. In the court’s words, AFL claimed that OSC violated a “procedural right” under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. That framing was important because courts sometimes relax standing requirements in procedural-injury cases. If Congress gives a plaintiff a procedure designed to protect a concrete interest, the plaintiff may not need to show the same degree of imminence or redressability that would otherwise be required.

But the opinion emphasizes an important limit: a bare procedural violation is not enough. Under *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, a “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation” does not create standing. In other words, a plaintiff cannot sue simply because an agency failed to follow a statutory procedure in the abstract. The plaintiff still must connect that failure to some real injury to itself.

AFL tried to make that connection in two different ways. First, it said OSC’s refusal to investigate had helped deprive AFL of the FOIA records it wanted from DOJ. Second, it said OSC had denied AFL the notices and information that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214. The court evaluated those theories separately.

## AFL’s first standing theory: the missing FOIA records

AFL’s first theory was that if OSC had investigated DOJ under § 1216, that investigation might have led to sanctions or corrective action, and DOJ then might have produced the requested emails. The court rejected this theory because the injury AFL identified was really the ordinary FOIA injury caused by DOJ’s alleged nonproduction, not by OSC’s refusal to investigate. The real source of the alleged informational harm was DOJ, not OSC.

More importantly, the connection between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s continued withholding was too speculative. AFL’s theory depended on several contingent steps: OSC would have to choose to investigate; OSC would then have to decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action; the MSPB would have to impose sanctions; and DOJ would then have to respond by producing records. The court pointed out that this causal chain requires speculation about independent actors not before the court, and Article III generally does not allow standing to rest on such speculation. It also depends on predicting how OSC would exercise enforcement discretion, which courts usually cannot compel.

The redressability problem followed the same logic. A judicial order telling OSC to investigate would not itself bind DOJ or the MSPB. AFL therefore could not show that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary” to get the records. The court also invoked *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the proposition that redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether its reasoning might persuade outsiders. On this theory, then, AFL lacked standing because the alleged injury was not sufficiently traceable to OSC and not likely to be redressed by an order against OSC.

## AFL’s second standing theory: the alleged right to OSC-generated information

AFL’s second theory was more directly tied to the investigation itself. It argued that even if the investigation would not necessarily produce the DOJ records, OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the notices and information that would come with an investigation. That sounds like an informational injury, and the D.C. Circuit recognizes informational standing when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive certain information and the plaintiff is denied it.

The problem for AFL was that § 1216 does not create a right to receive information. Even assuming that § 1216 requires OSC to investigate certain FOIA allegations, the statute does not say that the complainant is entitled to the kinds of notices found in § 1214. AFL tried to rely on the possibility that OSC might choose to apply § 1214-style procedures in a § 1216 investigation, but that was not enough. AFL did not claim a statutory entitlement to those procedures, and the court cited cases recognizing that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary.

That meant AFL could not identify a concrete informational injury of the kind required for standing. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, there was no legally protected informational interest that OSC had invaded. And if OSC was not required to provide those notices in the first place, then an order requiring OSC to investigate would not likely redress the alleged injury either.

## Why the court did not decide whether the injuries were procedural or substantive

AFL tried to fit its claims into the procedural-injury framework, because that framework can ease the plaintiff’s burden on imminence and redressability. The court, however, said it did not need to decide whether AFL’s asserted injuries were best characterized as procedural or substantive. That is because AFL failed even under the more forgiving procedural-injury standard.

This is a useful teaching point. The label “procedural” does not itself create standing. A plaintiff still needs a concrete, personal interest affected by the procedural violation, and the injury still has to be traceable to the defendant. If those elements are missing, it does not matter how the plaintiff characterizes the claim.

## The opinion’s structure: two theories, both rejected

The D.C. Circuit’s standing analysis is organized around AFL’s two asserted injuries. First, the court asked whether OSC’s noninvestigation caused AFL to lose access to the DOJ records it requested under FOIA. It held that the answer was no, because the connection was too attenuated and depended on speculation about third parties. Second, the court asked whether AFL was denied information that § 1216 or related provisions entitled it to receive. It held that the answer was also no, because § 1216 does not itself confer a right to receive investigation-related notices or reports.

Once both theories failed, the rest of the case fell away. The court did not reach whether OSC had interpreted the statutes correctly, whether the district court was right on the merits, or whether § 1216 independently authorizes the investigation AFL wanted. The threshold defect in standing prevented the federal courts from deciding those issues. The final disposition followed the jurisdictional conclusion: the judgment was vacated, and the case was remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Chapter 4: AFL’s First Theory of Standing—Loss of the Requested FOIA Records

## AFL’s argument that OSC’s inaction deprived it of the records it wanted

AFL’s first standing theory was that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under § 1216 caused AFL to lose access to the records it had requested under FOIA. AFL tried to treat OSC’s inaction as the source of its injury: if OSC had done what AFL said the statute required, DOJ would have been pressured to produce the requested emails. That framing matters because standing depends on identifying the injury, the defendant’s role in causing it, and whether a court order against that defendant could realistically fix it.

The court understood AFL to be claiming an informational injury. But the key question was not simply whether AFL wanted information. It was whether the loss of information was fairly traceable to OSC’s failure to investigate, and whether a favorable judgment against OSC would likely lead to disclosure of the DOJ records.

## Why the court said the real source of the injury was DOJ’s withholding

The court’s first and most basic point was that the records were being withheld by DOJ, not OSC. That means the immediate cause of AFL’s injury was DOJ’s response to the FOIA request. OSC did not deny the request, and OSC did not possess the records. So even if OSC had an obligation to investigate, its failure to do so was not the direct source of AFL’s inability to obtain the documents.

This is the traceability problem in standing doctrine. A plaintiff must show that the injury is “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct. AFL could not do that by pointing to OSC’s silence when the actual withholding decision was made by another agency. The court treated that as a crucial distinction: a defendant’s failure to investigate a separate agency’s conduct is not the same thing as causing the underlying withholding.

## Why the causal chain was too speculative

AFL’s theory also depended on a long chain of future events. The court laid that chain out step by step. First, OSC would have to conduct the investigation that AFL said was mandatory under § 1216. Then OSC would have to decide to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. After that, the Merit Systems Protection Board would have to take action against the responsible employees. Finally, DOJ would have to decide to produce the records.

That chain was too speculative to support standing. It depended on the choices of several actors, including two independent actors who were not parties to the suit: the MSPB and DOJ. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that causation for standing cannot rest on speculation about what independent actors might do. The court cited FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for that principle. There, too, the injury depended on a sequence of uncertain decisions by third parties, and the Court refused to treat that as adequate causation.

The court also emphasized that OSC’s own decisionmaking is discretionary in important respects. Under Heckler v. Chaney, courts generally do not compel an agency to take enforcement action. So even before reaching DOJ or the MSPB, AFL’s theory assumed that OSC would choose to investigate and then choose to press enforcement. That kind of assumption is not enough for Article III standing.

## Why redressability also failed

Even if one assumed some causal connection, AFL still had to show redressability: a favorable judicial decision must likely remedy the injury. The court held that AFL could not make that showing either. An order directing OSC to investigate would not bind DOJ to release the records, and it would not bind the MSPB to impose sanctions. At most, it might influence those actors. But standing does not turn on whether a court’s reasoning might persuade outsiders.

The court relied on Haaland v. Brackeen for that point. Redressability depends on the legal effect of the judgment on the parties before the court, not on whether the judgment might influence others who are not bound by it. The court also cited Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC to stress that the plaintiff must show a favorable decision would very likely set in motion the steps needed to cure the injury. Here, AFL could not do that. The best it could offer was a chain of possibilities.

## Why a relaxed procedural-injury theory did not save AFL

AFL tried to reframe the problem as a procedural injury, because standing doctrine sometimes relaxes the redressability and imminence requirements when a statute gives a plaintiff a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest. But that doctrine did not help AFL. Even under a relaxed procedural-injury framework, AFL still had to show that the alleged procedural violation affected a concrete interest. Its concrete interest here was getting the DOJ records, and that interest was not actually caused by OSC’s refusal to investigate.

In other words, AFL could not use the label “procedural injury” to bypass the basic requirements of causation and redressability. The court therefore rejected AFL’s first standing theory: the loss of DOJ records was too directly attributable to DOJ, and too indirectly related to OSC, to support Article III standing.

# Chapter 5: AFL’s Second Theory of Standing, the Court’s Disposition, and the Case’s Significance

## AFL’s Second Theory: A Lost Chance at Notices or Information

After rejecting AFL’s first standing theory, the court turned to AFL’s fallback position: that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the informational benefits that might come from an OSC investigation itself. This was a different kind of injury from the one tied to DOJ’s failure to produce FOIA records. AFL argued that if OSC had conducted the investigation it says § 1216 requires, AFL would have received notices and other information associated with an OSC enforcement proceeding under § 1214.

The court treated this theory as a claim of informational injury. But the key problem was that AFL was not pointing to a concrete statutory right to receive information from OSC. Instead, AFL was relying on the idea that an investigation might generate information, and that information might be shared. That is too uncertain to count as an Article III injury unless the statute actually entitles the plaintiff to receive it.

## Informational Standing Requires a Statutory Entitlement to Receive Information

The court’s discussion of informational standing follows a familiar line from Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit precedent. A plaintiff can suffer a concrete injury when a statute gives it a right to information and the government withholds that information. The opinion cites cases such as *Federal Election Commission v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. Department of Justice*, which recognized standing where the plaintiff was denied access to information the law required to be disclosed. The D.C. Circuit’s own cases say the same thing: informational standing exists when a plaintiff is denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive.

That requirement matters here because it limits informational standing to situations where Congress has actually created an enforceable informational entitlement. Merely wanting more information, or believing that a government process should generate information, is not enough. The plaintiff must point to a legal right to receive that information. Without that right, there is no concrete injury in fact.

## Why § 1216(a)(3) Does Not Itself Create an Entitlement to Information

AFL’s difficulty was that § 1216(a)(3), even if it requires OSC to investigate arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings, does not say that the complainant is entitled to receive the byproducts of that investigation. The statute directs OSC to “conduct an investigation,” but that is not the same thing as requiring OSC to give the requester notices, updates, or investigative materials.

The court therefore separated two ideas that AFL tried to merge. One is the existence of an investigative duty. The other is the existence of an information right. A statutory duty to investigate does not automatically create a right to be informed about how that investigation proceeds. AFL’s theory depended on converting the first into the second, and the court refused to do that.

That distinction also explains why the court found no injury in fact under this theory. If the statute does not entitle AFL to receive the notices or information it wants, then OSC’s failure to provide them is not a deprivation of a legally protected interest. AFL may have wanted the information, but wanting information is not the same as having a statutory right to it.

## AFL’s Reliance on § 1214 and the Problem of Discretion

To bridge that gap, AFL argued that once OSC undertakes a § 1216 investigation, it should provide the same kind of notices and information that § 1214 requires in investigations of prohibited personnel practices. That was AFL’s fallback theory: if § 1216 requires an investigation, perhaps it also imports § 1214’s procedural protections.

The court rejected that move, relying in part on AFL’s own concession. AFL did not argue that § 1214’s notice provisions are mandatory in every § 1216 investigation. Instead, it acknowledged authority holding that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in the § 1216 setting is discretionary. The opinion cites *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* for that proposition.

That mattered for both injury and redressability. If OSC is not legally required to give § 1214-style notices in a § 1216 investigation, then AFL has no statutory entitlement to those notices. And if no such entitlement exists, a court order directing OSC to investigate would not likely produce the information AFL wants. In other words, the requested injunction would not solve the problem because the missing notices are not something the statute guarantees.

## Failure of Injury in Fact and Redressability

The court’s standing analysis thus failed on two related grounds. First, there was no injury in fact because AFL was not denied information to which it had a legal right. Second, there was no redressability because even if the court ordered OSC to conduct an investigation, that order would not likely force OSC to provide the notices AFL sought.

This is an important feature of the opinion. The court did not say only that AFL’s theory was weak on the merits. It said the theory did not satisfy Article III at all. That means the problem was jurisdictional: AFL had not shown a case or controversy that federal courts are empowered to decide. The court also emphasized that redressability is especially weak where the requested judicial relief depends on discretionary choices by a non-party agency. Here, OSC’s choice whether to use § 1214-style procedures in a § 1216 matter was not something the court could simply assume or command.

## The Appellate Disposition: Vacatur and Remand to Dismiss for Lack of Jurisdiction

Because AFL lacked standing, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. That disposition is significant. A dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is not a ruling on the merits of the statutory dispute. It means the court lacked power to decide whether AFL was right about the meaning of § 1216 or whether OSC had erred under the statute.

That is why the appellate court did not leave the district court’s merits ruling in place. If the case should never have been heard in federal court, then the merits judgment cannot stand. The proper remedy is to erase that judgment and enter a jurisdictional dismissal instead.

## The Broader Lesson of the Case

The practical lesson of the opinion is that statutory language saying an agency “shall” act does not by itself create Article III standing. Even mandatory language does not eliminate the Constitution’s separate requirement that the plaintiff suffer a concrete, particularized, and redressable injury. AFL wanted a court order compelling an investigation, but the injury it claimed depended on a chain of uncertain events and on information it was not legally entitled to receive.

The case also illustrates a recurring problem in suits seeking to compel agency enforcement or investigation. When the plaintiff’s asserted harm depends on what one agency might do, what another agency might decide, or what an investigation might incidentally reveal, standing becomes difficult to establish. Federal courts do not decide abstract disagreements over whether the government should investigate. They decide concrete disputes where the plaintiff has a legally protected injury that a judicial order can likely fix.

In that sense, the opinion’s doctrinal message is simple but important: a statutory command to investigate is not the same as a judicially enforceable right to receive the fruits of that investigation, and Article III still requires more than a desire to see the government act.
