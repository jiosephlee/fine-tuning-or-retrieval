Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How can the D.C. Circuit throw out the case for lack of standing after the district court already ruled on the merits?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The district court interpreted the statutes, said OSC made a legal error, remanded to the agency, and only later entered final judgment. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit says AFL lacked Article III standing all along and vacates the district court's judgment with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Can an appellate court raise standing on its own even if neither party briefed it at first? And if standing was missing from the start, does that mean the district court had no power to issue any merits ruling at all?
Answer:
Yes. Article III standing is a **jurisdictional** requirement, so a federal court must satisfy itself that standing exists **before** reaching the merits, and it can do so **on its own initiative** even if the parties did not raise the issue.

That is exactly what the D.C. Circuit did here:

> “Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subjectmatter jurisdiction. … Neither party initially addressed standing, so we ordered supplemental briefing on that question.”

Because standing goes to **subject-matter jurisdiction**, it is not waived by silence and cannot be created by the district court’s later merits analysis. The court’s duty is to determine whether there is a live “case” or “controversy” at all. If the plaintiff lacked standing when the suit was filed, the district court never had constitutional power to decide the statutory issues on the merits.

So the procedural posture is:

1. District court addressed the merits.
2. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit independently checked standing.
3. Finding no standing, it held the district court lacked jurisdiction.
4. It therefore **vacated** the judgment and remanded with instructions to **dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction**.

That does **not** mean the district court’s merits ruling was just “wrong on the law”; it means the district court had no authority to issue a binding merits ruling in the first place. In other words, the merits discussion is wiped out once the appellate court concludes there was no Article III standing from the start.

So the short answer is: **yes, the appellate court can raise standing sua sponte, and yes, if standing was absent ab initio, the district court had no power to decide the merits.**

### Why doesn't a statute saying OSC 'shall conduct an investigation' automatically give the requester standing to sue?
Question:
Section 1216(a)(3) uses mandatory language: OSC 'shall' investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding. My instinct was that if Congress creates that duty, then the person who filed the allegation should be able to sue when OSC refuses. But the court says a 'procedural right in vacuo' is not enough under Article III. What exactly is missing here? Is the problem that Congress can create a statutory duty, but cannot eliminate the need for a separate concrete injury?
Answer:
Because **a statutory duty is not the same thing as Article III standing**.

The key point in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is that § 1216(a)(3) may tell OSC what it **must do** (“shall … conduct an investigation”), but that does **not automatically create a concrete injury to the requester** sufficient to invoke federal jurisdiction.

## What Article III requires

Standing still requires the familiar three elements:

1. **injury in fact**
2. **causation**
3. **redressability**

The court emphasizes that a plaintiff cannot sue based only on “the deprivation of a procedural right in vacuo.” That phrase comes from *Summers v. Earth Island*: a procedural violation by itself is not enough unless it affects some **concrete, particularized interest**.

So the missing piece is not the statutory command; it is the **separate constitutional injury**.

## Why the mandatory “shall” is not enough here

The court treats AFL’s claim as essentially this:

- OSC had a duty to investigate;
- OSC did not investigate;
- therefore AFL can sue.

But that skips the Article III question: **how was AFL concretely harmed by OSC’s failure to investigate?**

The court says AFL’s alleged harms do not work:

### 1. The FOIA records injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC
AFL’s real complaint is that DOJ did not give it the records. But that injury comes from DOJ’s withholding, not from OSC’s inaction.

To connect OSC’s failure to investigate to the missing records, AFL would have to speculate that:

- OSC would investigate;
- OSC would pursue corrective or disciplinary action;
- the MSPB would act;
- DOJ would then disclose the records.

That is too attenuated to satisfy causation and redressability.

### 2. OSC’s own investigation does not entitle AFL to information
AFL also argued that it was injured because it did not get notices and information that would accompany a § 1214-style investigation. The court rejects that because § 1216 does **not** itself give AFL a right to receive that information.

So even if OSC had a duty to investigate, AFL still lacked a statutory entitlement to the information it claimed as an injury.

## The deeper doctrinal point

Yes: **Congress can create statutory duties and procedural rights**, and it can sometimes make the denial of a statutory entitlement itself a concrete injury. But Congress **cannot abolish Article III’s injury requirement** by simply labeling something a “right.”

That is why the court says a statutory violation must affect a concrete interest. A bare right to have an agency do something, without a corresponding personal stake or informational entitlement, is not enough.

## Bottom line

The court is not saying § 1216(a)(3) is meaningless. It is saying:

- § 1216 may impose a duty on OSC,
- but AFL still had to show a **concrete injury caused by OSC’s failure to act**, and
- it could not do that.

So the problem is exactly what you suspected: **Congress can create a statutory obligation, but that does not eliminate the need for a separate Article III injury-in-fact.**

### Why did the court say AFL's FOIA-records injury was caused by DOJ rather than by OSC's refusal to investigate?
Question:
AFL argued that OSC's failure to investigate prevented it from getting the records it requested from DOJ. The court rejects that on causation grounds and says any informational injury from nondisclosure is 'plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.' I'm having trouble with that because AFL's whole theory is that OSC enforcement might pressure DOJ employees to comply. Is the rule basically that the immediate withholder of records is the legally relevant cause, and a regulator's failure to police that conduct is too indirect?
Answer:
Yes — that is basically the point the court is making, but in Article III terms rather than as a categorical “immediate actor” rule.

### What injury did AFL actually claim?
For the first standing theory, AFL said it was injured because it **did not receive DOJ records it requested under FOIA**.

That is an injury caused, in the court’s view, by **DOJ’s own withholding of the records**. OSC did not withhold the records; it allegedly failed to investigate DOJ’s withholding.

### Why isn’t OSC’s inaction enough?
Because causation for standing requires the injury to be **fairly traceable** to the defendant. Here, the court thought AFL’s theory depended on a chain like this:

1. OSC investigates,
2. OSC may decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action,
3. MSPB may impose sanctions,
4. DOJ may then decide to produce the records.

The court says that chain is too speculative and attenuated. It involves:
- OSC’s **enforcement discretion**,
- possible actions by **MSPB**,
- and a possible response by **DOJ**.

As the opinion puts it, any informational injury from nondisclosure is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.”

### So is the rule that the immediate withholder is the relevant cause?
In this context, yes, that is the practical effect.

A plaintiff generally cannot create standing by saying:
> “If the regulator had enforced the law, the third party might have complied.”

That is usually too indirect, especially when the hoped-for compliance depends on discretionary decisions by multiple actors not before the court.

The court cites that principle directly: causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”

### Why does redressability matter too?
Even if OSC were ordered to investigate, that would not necessarily get AFL the DOJ records. The court says the order would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury, because it would still take several discretionary steps by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ.

### Bottom line
The court is saying that AFL’s claimed harm is **the denial of DOJ records**, so the legally relevant cause is **DOJ’s withholding**. OSC’s failure to investigate is, at most, an indirect regulatory omission whose effect on DOJ’s conduct is too speculative to satisfy Article III causation and redressability.

### Why wasn't redressability satisfied if the requested remedy was only an order requiring OSC to investigate?
Question:
The opinion says AFL's theory depended on a speculative chain: OSC might investigate, might pursue corrective or disciplinary action, MSPB might impose sanctions, and then DOJ might produce records. I understand that chain is long, but in procedural-right cases I thought courts relax redressability somewhat. So why wasn't that enough here? Is it mainly because the result depended on discretionary choices by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ, all of which are independent actors not before the court?
Answer:
No. The D.C. Circuit’s point was that **an order requiring OSC to investigate would not likely redress AFL’s actual injuries**—the missing FOIA records and the missing investigation-related notices.

Under Article III, redressability is relaxed in procedural-right cases, but it is **not eliminated**. The plaintiff still has to show that the requested procedure is tied to a **concrete interest** and that a favorable judgment is **likely** to protect that interest. The court said AFL could not do that here.

## 1. The requested remedy was only the first step in a speculative chain

For AFL’s FOIA-records theory, the court laid out the chain:

1. OSC investigates;
2. OSC might decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action;
3. MSPB might impose sanctions;
4. DOJ might then decide to produce the records.

The court held that this is too speculative to satisfy redressability because it depends on the **unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court**—especially MSPB and DOJ—and on OSC’s own **enforcement discretion**, which courts generally cannot compel. The opinion explicitly says a favorable judgment must “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury, and AFL could not show that.

So yes, your instinct is right: **the involvement of discretionary decisions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ was central**.

## 2. An order to investigate does not itself produce the information AFL said it wanted

AFL also tried to recast the injury as a procedural/informational one: that OSC was supposed to investigate and provide notices or information. But the court rejected that too, because even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate FOIA allegations, **it does not create an entitlement to receive information**. And AFL did not show that OSC was legally required to apply § 1214’s notice rules in a § 1216 investigation.

That mattered for redressability because if AFL has **no statutory entitlement** to the information it wants from OSC, then an order to investigate would not likely remedy that alleged injury.

## 3. The procedural-right doctrine did not save AFL

The court quoted the usual procedural-standing rule: redressability is relaxed, but only where the procedural violation threatens a concrete interest. Here, the court found AFL lacked that link. It characterized AFL’s claim as either:

- too **attenuated** (for the FOIA-records theory), or
- not tied to any concrete informational entitlement (for the OSC-notice theory).

## Bottom line

Yes—**the fact that redress depended on discretionary decisions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ was a major reason redressability failed**. But the deeper reason is that **the relief AFL requested—an OSC investigation—was only a predicate to possible later events, not a remedy likely to itself produce the records or notices AFL claimed were missing**. The court therefore held that even under the relaxed procedural-injury standard, AFL did not show redressability.

### How are 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and FOIA's disciplinary provision in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) supposed to interact?
Question:
OSC refused to investigate because FOIA's own enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), is triggered only when a court orders production, awards fees, and specifically questions whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious. But AFL argued that § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate alleged FOIA violations even without those predicates, and the district court agreed. What is the best way to read these two provisions together? Is § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) a narrow mandatory trigger that supplements a broader § 1216 authority, or is it supposed to be the exclusive route for OSC involvement in FOIA withholding cases?
Answer:
The best reading, as reflected in the opinion’s statutory description, is that **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not the exclusive route for OSC involvement** in FOIA withholding cases. Rather, it is a **narrow, specific, mandatory disciplinary trigger** that applies only after a court has already:  
1. ordered production,  
2. awarded attorneys’ fees, and  
3. specifically questioned whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious.

By contrast, **§ 1216(a)(3) is broader and independent**. It says OSC **“shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”** And § 1216(c) then says OSC **may** pursue corrective or disciplinary action **“in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”** That structure suggests a two-step regime:

- **§ 1216** creates OSC’s general investigative authority over alleged arbitrary/capricious FOIA withholdings;  
- **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** creates an additional, court-triggered duty to commence a disciplinary proceeding in the narrower situation where litigation has already produced the specified judicial findings.

That is how the district court read the statutes, and the D.C. Circuit’s opinion describes that reading without rejecting it on the merits. The court of appeals ultimately **did not decide the statutory question**, because it held AFL lacked Article III standing and vacated for dismissal on jurisdictional grounds.

So, in short: **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplements § 1216; it does not displace it.**

### Why didn't AFL have informational standing based on the notices and updates it said OSC should have provided?
Question:
The court says informational standing exists only when a plaintiff is denied information that a statute actually entitles him to receive. AFL argued that if OSC had investigated under § 1216, it could have received the kinds of notices and information described in § 1214. But the court says that isn't enough because OSC was not required to graft § 1214's notice provisions onto a § 1216 investigation. Is the key distinction here between a guaranteed disclosure right and a mere possibility of receiving information if the agency chooses to follow a certain procedure?
Answer:
Yes — that is the key distinction, and it is exactly why AFL lost on informational standing.

The D.C. Circuit used the ordinary informational-standing rule: a plaintiff suffers a concrete informational injury only when it is denied information that a statute **entitles it to receive**. The court quoted that standard from *ASPCA v. Feld*: a plaintiff must be denied information a “statute entitled him to receive.”

Here, AFL’s problem was that **§ 1214 does not itself give AFL a right to those notices in a § 1216 investigation**. Section 1214 contains notice-and-update procedures for investigations of prohibited personnel practices. Section 1216 authorizes OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings and says OSC **may** investigate and seek corrective action “under section 1214” and disciplinary action “under section 1215” “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” But, as the court emphasized, AFL did **not** claim that OSC was legally required to import § 1214’s notice provisions into every § 1216 investigation. In fact, the opinion notes that cases like *Carson* treat OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 matter as **discretionary**.

So the distinction is:

- **Guaranteed statutory entitlement to information** → informational standing can exist.
- **Possible information if the agency chooses a discretionary procedure** → no informational standing.

That is why the court rejected AFL’s theory. AFL was not denied information it had a legal right to receive; it was denied information it hoped OSC might provide if OSC voluntarily used § 1214-style procedures during a § 1216 investigation. That is too contingent to count as an informational injury.

The court also pointed out the redressability problem: even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, that still would not make it likely that AFL would receive the § 1214 notices, because OSC need not provide them in a § 1216 investigation.

So your instinct is right: the case turns on the difference between a **statutory disclosure right** and a **mere possibility of disclosure**.

### Does § 1216(c)'s phrase 'in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved' import § 1214 procedures or only the available remedies?
Question:
I'm stuck on the statutory language in § 1216(c). It says OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 'in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.' AFL seemed to rely on that language to argue for investigation-related rights, while the court cites cases saying OSC's choice whether to follow § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Does 'in the same way' mean the whole § 1214 procedural framework comes along, or does it just mean OSC can use the same enforcement mechanisms without being bound to all the same procedural steps?
Answer:
Short answer: **no, not wholesale**. In this opinion, § 1216(c)’s “**in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved**” is read as authorizing OSC to use the **same enforcement machinery**—i.e., to **seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215**—but **not as automatically importing every § 1214 procedural step** into a § 1216 investigation.

The D.C. Circuit’s discussion is the key clue. It explains that:

- § 1214 “sets forth procedures” for prohibited-personnel-practice cases, including notice to the complaining party; but
- in a § 1216 FOIA-misconduct matter, AFL itself “acknowledges cases holding that OSC’s *decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements* … is *discretionary*.” The court cites **Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)** for that proposition.

That tells you how the phrase is doing work in the statute:

1. **It imports the remedial/enforcement authority** — OSC can pursue the same kinds of outcomes it could pursue in a PPP case.
2. **It does not, by itself, make § 1214’s procedural framework mandatory** in every § 1216 investigation.
3. So a party cannot claim a statutory entitlement to all the **notice/information rights** that attach in a § 1214 proceeding simply because OSC is investigating under § 1216.

The court’s standing analysis reinforces that reading. AFL argued it was entitled to the notices and information that would be required if OSC were proceeding under § 1214, but the court rejected that theory because AFL “does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations.” Absent such an entitlement, there was no concrete informational injury.

So, as this opinion reads the statute, **“in the same way” means same enforcement options, not automatic incorporation of all § 1214 procedures**. If OSC chooses to use § 1214-type procedures, it may do so; but § 1216(c) does not itself force OSC to follow every procedural step that would apply in a true § 1214 prohibited-personnel-practice case.
