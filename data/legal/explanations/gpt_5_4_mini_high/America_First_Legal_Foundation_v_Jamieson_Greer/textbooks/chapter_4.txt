Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: AFL’s First Theory of Standing—Loss of the Requested FOIA Records

## AFL’s argument that OSC’s inaction deprived it of the records it wanted

AFL’s first standing theory was that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under § 1216 caused AFL to lose access to the records it had requested under FOIA. AFL tried to treat OSC’s inaction as the source of its injury: if OSC had done what AFL said the statute required, DOJ would have been pressured to produce the requested emails. That framing matters because standing depends on identifying the injury, the defendant’s role in causing it, and whether a court order against that defendant could realistically fix it.

The court understood AFL to be claiming an informational injury. But the key question was not simply whether AFL wanted information. It was whether the loss of information was fairly traceable to OSC’s failure to investigate, and whether a favorable judgment against OSC would likely lead to disclosure of the DOJ records.

## Why the court said the real source of the injury was DOJ’s withholding

The court’s first and most basic point was that the records were being withheld by DOJ, not OSC. That means the immediate cause of AFL’s injury was DOJ’s response to the FOIA request. OSC did not deny the request, and OSC did not possess the records. So even if OSC had an obligation to investigate, its failure to do so was not the direct source of AFL’s inability to obtain the documents.

This is the traceability problem in standing doctrine. A plaintiff must show that the injury is “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct. AFL could not do that by pointing to OSC’s silence when the actual withholding decision was made by another agency. The court treated that as a crucial distinction: a defendant’s failure to investigate a separate agency’s conduct is not the same thing as causing the underlying withholding.

## Why the causal chain was too speculative

AFL’s theory also depended on a long chain of future events. The court laid that chain out step by step. First, OSC would have to conduct the investigation that AFL said was mandatory under § 1216. Then OSC would have to decide to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. After that, the Merit Systems Protection Board would have to take action against the responsible employees. Finally, DOJ would have to decide to produce the records.

That chain was too speculative to support standing. It depended on the choices of several actors, including two independent actors who were not parties to the suit: the MSPB and DOJ. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that causation for standing cannot rest on speculation about what independent actors might do. The court cited FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for that principle. There, too, the injury depended on a sequence of uncertain decisions by third parties, and the Court refused to treat that as adequate causation.

The court also emphasized that OSC’s own decisionmaking is discretionary in important respects. Under Heckler v. Chaney, courts generally do not compel an agency to take enforcement action. So even before reaching DOJ or the MSPB, AFL’s theory assumed that OSC would choose to investigate and then choose to press enforcement. That kind of assumption is not enough for Article III standing.

## Why redressability also failed

Even if one assumed some causal connection, AFL still had to show redressability: a favorable judicial decision must likely remedy the injury. The court held that AFL could not make that showing either. An order directing OSC to investigate would not bind DOJ to release the records, and it would not bind the MSPB to impose sanctions. At most, it might influence those actors. But standing does not turn on whether a court’s reasoning might persuade outsiders.

The court relied on Haaland v. Brackeen for that point. Redressability depends on the legal effect of the judgment on the parties before the court, not on whether the judgment might influence others who are not bound by it. The court also cited Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC to stress that the plaintiff must show a favorable decision would very likely set in motion the steps needed to cure the injury. Here, AFL could not do that. The best it could offer was a chain of possibilities.

## Why a relaxed procedural-injury theory did not save AFL

AFL tried to reframe the problem as a procedural injury, because standing doctrine sometimes relaxes the redressability and imminence requirements when a statute gives a plaintiff a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest. But that doctrine did not help AFL. Even under a relaxed procedural-injury framework, AFL still had to show that the alleged procedural violation affected a concrete interest. Its concrete interest here was getting the DOJ records, and that interest was not actually caused by OSC’s refusal to investigate.

In other words, AFL could not use the label “procedural injury” to bypass the basic requirements of causation and redressability. The court therefore rejected AFL’s first standing theory: the loss of DOJ records was too directly attributable to DOJ, and too indirectly related to OSC, to support Article III standing.
