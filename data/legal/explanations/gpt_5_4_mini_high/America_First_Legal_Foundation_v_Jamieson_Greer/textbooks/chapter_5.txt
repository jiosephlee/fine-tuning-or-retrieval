Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: AFL’s Second Theory of Standing, the Court’s Disposition, and the Case’s Significance

## AFL’s Second Theory: A Lost Chance at Notices or Information

After rejecting AFL’s first standing theory, the court turned to AFL’s fallback position: that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the informational benefits that might come from an OSC investigation itself. This was a different kind of injury from the one tied to DOJ’s failure to produce FOIA records. AFL argued that if OSC had conducted the investigation it says § 1216 requires, AFL would have received notices and other information associated with an OSC enforcement proceeding under § 1214.

The court treated this theory as a claim of informational injury. But the key problem was that AFL was not pointing to a concrete statutory right to receive information from OSC. Instead, AFL was relying on the idea that an investigation might generate information, and that information might be shared. That is too uncertain to count as an Article III injury unless the statute actually entitles the plaintiff to receive it.

## Informational Standing Requires a Statutory Entitlement to Receive Information

The court’s discussion of informational standing follows a familiar line from Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit precedent. A plaintiff can suffer a concrete injury when a statute gives it a right to information and the government withholds that information. The opinion cites cases such as *Federal Election Commission v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. Department of Justice*, which recognized standing where the plaintiff was denied access to information the law required to be disclosed. The D.C. Circuit’s own cases say the same thing: informational standing exists when a plaintiff is denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive.

That requirement matters here because it limits informational standing to situations where Congress has actually created an enforceable informational entitlement. Merely wanting more information, or believing that a government process should generate information, is not enough. The plaintiff must point to a legal right to receive that information. Without that right, there is no concrete injury in fact.

## Why § 1216(a)(3) Does Not Itself Create an Entitlement to Information

AFL’s difficulty was that § 1216(a)(3), even if it requires OSC to investigate arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings, does not say that the complainant is entitled to receive the byproducts of that investigation. The statute directs OSC to “conduct an investigation,” but that is not the same thing as requiring OSC to give the requester notices, updates, or investigative materials.

The court therefore separated two ideas that AFL tried to merge. One is the existence of an investigative duty. The other is the existence of an information right. A statutory duty to investigate does not automatically create a right to be informed about how that investigation proceeds. AFL’s theory depended on converting the first into the second, and the court refused to do that.

That distinction also explains why the court found no injury in fact under this theory. If the statute does not entitle AFL to receive the notices or information it wants, then OSC’s failure to provide them is not a deprivation of a legally protected interest. AFL may have wanted the information, but wanting information is not the same as having a statutory right to it.

## AFL’s Reliance on § 1214 and the Problem of Discretion

To bridge that gap, AFL argued that once OSC undertakes a § 1216 investigation, it should provide the same kind of notices and information that § 1214 requires in investigations of prohibited personnel practices. That was AFL’s fallback theory: if § 1216 requires an investigation, perhaps it also imports § 1214’s procedural protections.

The court rejected that move, relying in part on AFL’s own concession. AFL did not argue that § 1214’s notice provisions are mandatory in every § 1216 investigation. Instead, it acknowledged authority holding that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in the § 1216 setting is discretionary. The opinion cites *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* for that proposition.

That mattered for both injury and redressability. If OSC is not legally required to give § 1214-style notices in a § 1216 investigation, then AFL has no statutory entitlement to those notices. And if no such entitlement exists, a court order directing OSC to investigate would not likely produce the information AFL wants. In other words, the requested injunction would not solve the problem because the missing notices are not something the statute guarantees.

## Failure of Injury in Fact and Redressability

The court’s standing analysis thus failed on two related grounds. First, there was no injury in fact because AFL was not denied information to which it had a legal right. Second, there was no redressability because even if the court ordered OSC to conduct an investigation, that order would not likely force OSC to provide the notices AFL sought.

This is an important feature of the opinion. The court did not say only that AFL’s theory was weak on the merits. It said the theory did not satisfy Article III at all. That means the problem was jurisdictional: AFL had not shown a case or controversy that federal courts are empowered to decide. The court also emphasized that redressability is especially weak where the requested judicial relief depends on discretionary choices by a non-party agency. Here, OSC’s choice whether to use § 1214-style procedures in a § 1216 matter was not something the court could simply assume or command.

## The Appellate Disposition: Vacatur and Remand to Dismiss for Lack of Jurisdiction

Because AFL lacked standing, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. That disposition is significant. A dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is not a ruling on the merits of the statutory dispute. It means the court lacked power to decide whether AFL was right about the meaning of § 1216 or whether OSC had erred under the statute.

That is why the appellate court did not leave the district court’s merits ruling in place. If the case should never have been heard in federal court, then the merits judgment cannot stand. The proper remedy is to erase that judgment and enter a jurisdictional dismissal instead.

## The Broader Lesson of the Case

The practical lesson of the opinion is that statutory language saying an agency “shall” act does not by itself create Article III standing. Even mandatory language does not eliminate the Constitution’s separate requirement that the plaintiff suffer a concrete, particularized, and redressable injury. AFL wanted a court order compelling an investigation, but the injury it claimed depended on a chain of uncertain events and on information it was not legally entitled to receive.

The case also illustrates a recurring problem in suits seeking to compel agency enforcement or investigation. When the plaintiff’s asserted harm depends on what one agency might do, what another agency might decide, or what an investigation might incidentally reveal, standing becomes difficult to establish. Federal courts do not decide abstract disagreements over whether the government should investigate. They decide concrete disputes where the plaintiff has a legally protected injury that a judicial order can likely fix.

In that sense, the opinion’s doctrinal message is simple but important: a statutory command to investigate is not the same as a judicially enforceable right to receive the fruits of that investigation, and Article III still requires more than a desire to see the government act.
