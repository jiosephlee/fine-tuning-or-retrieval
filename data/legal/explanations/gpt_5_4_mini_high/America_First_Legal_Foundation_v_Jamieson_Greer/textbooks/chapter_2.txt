Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Facts, Claims, and the Case’s Procedural Path

## The FOIA request and the underlying records dispute

America First Legal Foundation’s dispute began with an effort to investigate judicial recusals. To do that, AFL submitted a Freedom of Information Act request to the Department of Justice seeking certain emails between an outside attorney and DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL believed that DOJ had denied the request on an improper rationale—specifically, that the Civil Division would not search employee email accounts for responsive records unless the employee consented. That alleged policy became the foundation for AFL’s later challenge.

The key point for reading the opinion is that AFL’s lawsuit against DOJ under FOIA was separate from the suit against the Office of Special Counsel. The FOIA suit was the ordinary records-disclosure case, and DOJ was already producing responsive records in that case while the appeal in this case was pending. The D.C. Circuit makes a point of saying that the sufficiency of DOJ’s ongoing production was not the issue before it here. This case instead concerns whether AFL could force OSC to investigate DOJ’s conduct.

## AFL’s separate request to the Office of Special Counsel

AFL next asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to investigate allegations of “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information” prohibited under FOIA. In AFL’s view, this statute required OSC to look into DOJ’s alleged FOIA practice even if the ordinary FOIA enforcement mechanism was not triggered.

OSC disagreed. It said it lacked authority to investigate because the conditions that trigger the separate FOIA enforcement provision in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. That provision applies only when a court has already ordered disclosure, awarded attorneys’ fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records. OSC’s position was that without those predicates, it had no duty to open an investigation.

## The district court proceedings

AFL then sued OSC in district court to compel the investigation. The district court reached an unusual split result. It agreed with AFL on the statutory interpretation question and held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s investigative authority under § 1216. In other words, the court concluded that OSC had been wrong to treat the absence of the FOIA-specific predicates as a reason not to investigate.

But the district court stopped there. It refused to order OSC to investigate, reasoning that § 1216(c) left the decision whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action to OSC’s discretion. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration rather than issuing the coercive relief AFL sought. After OSC again declined to investigate, AFL asked the district court to enter final judgment, and the court dismissed the remaining claims.

That procedural sequence matters because it shows why the appeal reached the D.C. Circuit in the posture it did. AFL was appealing from a case in which it had won a favorable statutory ruling in part, but had still failed to obtain the investigation it wanted.

## The appeal and the court of appeals’ threshold review

AFL appealed, and the D.C. Circuit reviewed the case de novo. That means the court of appeals considered the legal issues anew, without deferring to the district court’s legal conclusions. The panel initially noted that neither party had addressed Article III standing, but because standing is part of subject-matter jurisdiction, the court had to satisfy itself that it had power to hear the case before reaching the merits. It therefore ordered supplemental briefing on standing.

This is an important procedural lesson for reading the opinion. Even though the district court had addressed the statutory merits, the court of appeals first asked whether AFL had a sufficient injury, causation, and redressability to invoke federal judicial power at all. The ultimate appellate holding was that AFL lacked standing, so the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
