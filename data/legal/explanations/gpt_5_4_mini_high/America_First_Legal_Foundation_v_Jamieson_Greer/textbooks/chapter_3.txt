Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Article III Standing as a Threshold Barrier

## Why the court addressed standing before the merits

This opinion is a good example of a basic but crucial point in federal litigation: a court must be sure it has Article III jurisdiction before it can decide the merits. Even though the district court had already ruled on AFL’s statutory claims, the court of appeals had an independent duty to confirm that AFL had standing to sue. That is why the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on standing even though neither party had initially focused on the issue. If standing is missing, the federal courts lack power to issue a merits judgment at all, and any such judgment must be vacated.

That is what happened here. The panel never reached the correctness of AFL’s interpretation of the Office of Special Counsel’s statutory duties. Instead, it held that AFL had not shown a concrete Article III injury fairly traceable to OSC and likely to be redressed by a favorable court order. Because standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite, the proper disposition was to vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

## The standing framework the court applied

The opinion begins with the familiar Article III test from *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*: a plaintiff must show an injury in fact, causation, and redressability. The injury must be concrete and particularized, and it must be actual or imminent rather than speculative. “Concrete” means the harm is real, not abstract; “particularized” means it affects the plaintiff itself, not the public at large. Causation asks whether the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct. Redressability asks whether a favorable judgment is likely to remedy the injury.

At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the plaintiff does not need to prove standing with evidence, but it must plausibly allege facts supporting each element. That matters in an opinion like this one because AFL’s standing theory depended on a chain of inferences about what OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ might each do if a court ordered OSC to investigate. The court treated that chain skeptically, as Article III requires.

## AFL’s procedural-injury theory

AFL’s lawsuit was built around the idea that OSC had unlawfully refused to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. In the court’s words, AFL claimed that OSC violated a “procedural right” under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. That framing was important because courts sometimes relax standing requirements in procedural-injury cases. If Congress gives a plaintiff a procedure designed to protect a concrete interest, the plaintiff may not need to show the same degree of imminence or redressability that would otherwise be required.

But the opinion emphasizes an important limit: a bare procedural violation is not enough. Under *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, a “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation” does not create standing. In other words, a plaintiff cannot sue simply because an agency failed to follow a statutory procedure in the abstract. The plaintiff still must connect that failure to some real injury to itself.

AFL tried to make that connection in two different ways. First, it said OSC’s refusal to investigate had helped deprive AFL of the FOIA records it wanted from DOJ. Second, it said OSC had denied AFL the notices and information that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214. The court evaluated those theories separately.

## AFL’s first standing theory: the missing FOIA records

AFL’s first theory was that if OSC had investigated DOJ under § 1216, that investigation might have led to sanctions or corrective action, and DOJ then might have produced the requested emails. The court rejected this theory because the injury AFL identified was really the ordinary FOIA injury caused by DOJ’s alleged nonproduction, not by OSC’s refusal to investigate. The real source of the alleged informational harm was DOJ, not OSC.

More importantly, the connection between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s continued withholding was too speculative. AFL’s theory depended on several contingent steps: OSC would have to choose to investigate; OSC would then have to decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action; the MSPB would have to impose sanctions; and DOJ would then have to respond by producing records. The court pointed out that this causal chain requires speculation about independent actors not before the court, and Article III generally does not allow standing to rest on such speculation. It also depends on predicting how OSC would exercise enforcement discretion, which courts usually cannot compel.

The redressability problem followed the same logic. A judicial order telling OSC to investigate would not itself bind DOJ or the MSPB. AFL therefore could not show that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary” to get the records. The court also invoked *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the proposition that redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether its reasoning might persuade outsiders. On this theory, then, AFL lacked standing because the alleged injury was not sufficiently traceable to OSC and not likely to be redressed by an order against OSC.

## AFL’s second standing theory: the alleged right to OSC-generated information

AFL’s second theory was more directly tied to the investigation itself. It argued that even if the investigation would not necessarily produce the DOJ records, OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the notices and information that would come with an investigation. That sounds like an informational injury, and the D.C. Circuit recognizes informational standing when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive certain information and the plaintiff is denied it.

The problem for AFL was that § 1216 does not create a right to receive information. Even assuming that § 1216 requires OSC to investigate certain FOIA allegations, the statute does not say that the complainant is entitled to the kinds of notices found in § 1214. AFL tried to rely on the possibility that OSC might choose to apply § 1214-style procedures in a § 1216 investigation, but that was not enough. AFL did not claim a statutory entitlement to those procedures, and the court cited cases recognizing that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary.

That meant AFL could not identify a concrete informational injury of the kind required for standing. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, there was no legally protected informational interest that OSC had invaded. And if OSC was not required to provide those notices in the first place, then an order requiring OSC to investigate would not likely redress the alleged injury either.

## Why the court did not decide whether the injuries were procedural or substantive

AFL tried to fit its claims into the procedural-injury framework, because that framework can ease the plaintiff’s burden on imminence and redressability. The court, however, said it did not need to decide whether AFL’s asserted injuries were best characterized as procedural or substantive. That is because AFL failed even under the more forgiving procedural-injury standard.

This is a useful teaching point. The label “procedural” does not itself create standing. A plaintiff still needs a concrete, personal interest affected by the procedural violation, and the injury still has to be traceable to the defendant. If those elements are missing, it does not matter how the plaintiff characterizes the claim.

## The opinion’s structure: two theories, both rejected

The D.C. Circuit’s standing analysis is organized around AFL’s two asserted injuries. First, the court asked whether OSC’s noninvestigation caused AFL to lose access to the DOJ records it requested under FOIA. It held that the answer was no, because the connection was too attenuated and depended on speculation about third parties. Second, the court asked whether AFL was denied information that § 1216 or related provisions entitled it to receive. It held that the answer was also no, because § 1216 does not itself confer a right to receive investigation-related notices or reports.

Once both theories failed, the rest of the case fell away. The court did not reach whether OSC had interpreted the statutes correctly, whether the district court was right on the merits, or whether § 1216 independently authorizes the investigation AFL wanted. The threshold defect in standing prevented the federal courts from deciding those issues. The final disposition followed the jurisdictional conclusion: the judgment was vacated, and the case was remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
