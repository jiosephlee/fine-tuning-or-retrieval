Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Statutory Architecture Behind the Dispute

## The Office of Special Counsel in the Civil Service Reform Act

This case sits inside a specialized federal employment regime. The Office of Special Counsel, or OSC, is an investigative and prosecutorial agency within that system. Its basic function is to police certain kinds of misconduct in the federal civil service and, when appropriate, to take enforcement steps rather than merely investigate and walk away. The opinion describes OSC as an agency meant to promote a “fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” That framing matters because AFL’s lawsuit assumed OSC had an enforceable duty to investigate one kind of alleged misconduct involving FOIA; OSC responded that its power to act was narrower.

The statutory framework matters even before you reach the case’s standing problem. AFL did not sue DOJ only to force production under FOIA. It also tried to use the OSC statutes to compel an investigation into DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent. To understand why that claim was contested, a law student needs to know how the Civil Service Reform Act channels complaints through OSC and how that channel differs depending on the kind of wrongdoing alleged.

## Section 1214: Prohibited Personnel Practices, Corrective Action, and Notice

The core OSC enforcement provision is 5 U.S.C. § 1214. That section deals with “prohibited personnel practices,” a category of federal employee misconduct that OSC is authorized to investigate. If OSC finds reason to proceed, it can bring an enforcement action before the Merit Systems Protection Board, or MSPB. The statute thus creates an administrative enforcement path: OSC investigates, and the MSPB adjudicates the resulting corrective or disciplinary proceeding.

The word “corrective” is important. A corrective action is aimed at fixing the underlying wrong, not simply punishing the employee who caused it. Section 1214 also contains procedural protections for the person who complained to OSC. The opinion notes that these include notice provisions requiring OSC to keep the complaining party informed about the progress of the investigation. Those notice obligations became relevant because AFL argued that if OSC had to investigate DOJ under the FOIA-related provision, then it should also have to give AFL the sort of information that a § 1214 complainant receives.

For a student reading the opinion, the key point is that § 1214 is not just a substantive authorization to investigate. It also comes with procedures, and those procedures helped AFL frame its claimed injury. But § 1214 by its terms deals with prohibited personnel practices, not every kind of agency wrongdoing.

## Section 1215: Disciplinary Action Before the MSPB

Section 1215 supplies the other enforcement track. Where § 1214 is about corrective action, § 1215 is about disciplinary action. In the opinion’s terms, OSC may bring a disciplinary proceeding before the MSPB against an offending employee. The distinction is functional: corrective action addresses the problem caused by the agency’s conduct, while disciplinary action addresses the employee responsible for it.

This matters because the statutes do not simply authorize OSC to investigate. They also define what OSC may ask the MSPB to do after the investigation. AFL’s theory assumed that if OSC investigated DOJ under the FOIA-related provision, it could lead to the same sort of enforcement machinery that applies in prohibited-personnel-practice cases. That is why the relationship between §§ 1214 and 1215 mattered so much. The lawsuit was not just about whether OSC could look into DOJ’s behavior; it was also about whether the investigation could be tied to the usual remedial and disciplinary apparatus of the CSRA.

## Section 1216: A Separate OSC Power for Certain Other Misconduct

Section 1216 is the statute that brought FOIA into the OSC scheme. It gives OSC authority over certain misconduct that is not itself a prohibited personnel practice. Most importantly here, § 1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations concerning “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of information prohibited under FOIA. That mandatory “shall” is the statutory language AFL relied on. In AFL’s view, once it alleged an arbitrary FOIA withholding, OSC had to investigate.

Section 1216(c) then says OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That phrase is the source of much of the statutory tension. It suggests that FOIA-withholding allegations can be handled through the same enforcement machinery used for prohibited personnel practices, but it does not spell out exactly which § 1214 procedures automatically carry over and which remain discretionary.

That ambiguity matters. AFL treated § 1216 as a stand-alone mandate to investigate FOIA misconduct. OSC read the statute more narrowly and said it lacked authority to proceed unless the separate FOIA-specific trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had been satisfied. The district court thought § 1216 did not depend on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), but it also thought the choice whether to employ certain § 1214-style procedures in a § 1216 investigation was discretionary. The statutory text therefore created a layered scheme: a mandatory investigation provision on one level, and more open-ended enforcement language on another.

## FOIA’s Own OSC Provision and the Three Predicates

FOIA contains a separate OSC enforcement mechanism in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This provision is narrower and more specific than § 1216. It applies only when three things happen: a court orders production of agency records improperly withheld under FOIA, the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and the court specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding the records. If all three predicates are met, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the responsible officer or employee.

That provision is not a general complaint process. It is a court-triggered disciplinary referral mechanism. In other words, Congress did not say that every allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding automatically requires OSC action under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). Instead, the statute ties OSC’s obligation to a prior judicial determination and to a particular kind of FOIA litigation outcome.

This distinction was central to the dispute. OSC said the three statutory predicates had not been satisfied, so it did not have to investigate. AFL said that even if FOIA’s own referral provision was not triggered, § 1216 independently required OSC to investigate the alleged withholding practice. The district court accepted the broader reading of § 1216, but the court of appeals ultimately did not resolve the statutory merits because it found AFL lacked Article III standing.

## The Tension Between the Two FOIA-Related Pathways

The heart of the statutory problem is that FOIA-related misconduct appears in two different provisions, and they do not line up perfectly. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) gives OSC a specific role when a court has already found a FOIA violation and has questioned the agency’s conduct. Section 1216, by contrast, appears to let OSC investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding even without those judicial predicates. The question is whether the FOIA-specific referral provision limits OSC’s broader authority or merely adds one more enforcement route.

That question framed the entire lawsuit. AFL wanted to use § 1216 to force OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy, while OSC argued that no investigation was required absent the more specific FOIA triggers. Understanding this statutory architecture is essential to reading the opinion, because the later standing analysis only makes sense once you see what AFL was trying to obtain from OSC in the first place.
