Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### When a federal indictment is dismissed for failure to state an offense, why does the appellate court assume the alleged facts are true?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedure here. The district court dismissed the indictment before trial, but the Fifth Circuit says review is de novo and that it must take the indictment's allegations as true. Is the court only asking whether the indictment alleges every element of the offense, rather than whether the government can actually prove those allegations? If so, how is this different from reviewing evidentiary sufficiency after trial, and why is the government allowed to appeal this kind of pretrial dismissal?
Answer:
Yes. On a motion to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the court is not deciding whether the government can *prove* the charge. It is deciding whether the indictment, **on its face**, alleges a crime.

That is exactly what the Fifth Circuit said in **Constantinescu**:

> “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”

So the question is purely legal: **Do the pleaded facts, assumed true, satisfy the statutory elements?** If yes, the indictment stands; if no, it can be dismissed.

### Why assume the facts are true?
Because there has been no trial yet. At the indictment stage, factual disputes belong to the jury, not the judge. The court is not weighing evidence or deciding credibility. It is only checking whether the charging document:

1. alleges each material element of the offense,
2. informs the defendant of the charge, and
3. protects against double jeopardy.

That is why the Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* focused on whether the superseding indictment alleged a **scheme to defraud** and **intent to defraud**, not whether the defendants actually did it.

### How is this different from evidentiary sufficiency after trial?
After trial, the question changes from **“Did the indictment allege a crime?”** to **“Did the evidence prove the crime?”**

So:

- **Pretrial dismissal:** facial sufficiency of the indictment.
- **Post-trial sufficiency review:** sufficiency of the evidence presented at trial.

In *Constantinescu*, the district court had dismissed before trial, so the Fifth Circuit did **not** ask whether the government could ultimately prove the pump-and-dump allegations. It only asked whether those allegations, if true, described securities fraud. The court held they did.

### Why can the government appeal?
Because a pretrial dismissal of an indictment is a legal ruling that terminates the prosecution before jeopardy attaches. Federal law generally allows the government to appeal that kind of dismissal, and the Double Jeopardy Clause is not a bar when the defendant has not yet been tried.

So the appeal here was proper because the district court’s ruling was not an acquittal on the merits; it was a pretrial legal dismissal.

### Bottom line
The Fifth Circuit assumes the indictment’s allegations are true **only for the limited purpose of testing legal sufficiency**. It is asking:

> If everything the indictment says happened did happen, would that be a federal offense?

In *Constantinescu*, the answer was yes, because the indictment alleged that defendants used false statements to induce investors to buy securities and part with money. That was enough to state an offense, even though the government still had to prove those facts at trial.

### Why did the Fifth Circuit analyze Section 1348 securities fraud using mail and wire fraud cases instead of ordinary Rule 10b-5 doctrine?
Question:
The opinion says there is scant caselaw on 18 U.S.C. Section 1348 in the Fifth Circuit, so it borrows the concepts of a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud from mail and wire fraud cases. I thought securities fraud usually involved concepts like reliance, loss causation, and the Rule 10b-5 line of cases. Is Section 1348 a different criminal statute with different elements? What exactly did the government have to allege here under Section 1348 and the conspiracy statute, Section 1349?
Answer:
Because **§ 1348 is a criminal fraud statute, not the civil Rule 10b-5 regime**.

The Fifth Circuit says exactly why it did this: there is “**scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit**,” and § 1348 “**borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes**,” so courts have given those terms “similar treatment.” *United States v. Constantinescu* (citing *United States v. Greenlaw*). In other words, the court was not deciding a private securities-fraud case; it was interpreting the elements of a federal criminal fraud offense.

### What § 1348 required here
Under the panel’s formulation, securities fraud under § 1348 required:

1. a **scheme to defraud**, and  
2. **intent to defraud**.

The opinion further explains:

- A **scheme to defraud** requires a **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money.
- **Intent to defraud** means an intent to **deceive** and to **cause some harm** from the deceit.

That is why the court discusses mail/wire fraud cases: those cases supply the ordinary meaning of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud.”

### Why not Rule 10b-5 concepts like reliance and loss causation?
Because those are **not the elements the court was applying**.

Rule 10b-5 doctrine is mainly the law of **civil securities-fraud liability**. By contrast, § 1348, as used here, is a **criminal statute**. The Fifth Circuit therefore focused on whether the indictment alleged that defendants:

- made false or misleading statements,
- used those statements to induce investors to buy securities,
- thereby caused investors to part with money/property,
- and did so with fraudulent intent.

The opinion expressly rejects the idea that the government had to prove a **right-to-control** or a **net economic loss**. Relying on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the court says the fraud statutes require a scheme to obtain **money or property** by deceit; they do **not** require proof that the victims suffered an overall economic loss.

### What the government had to allege in this case
For the **substantive § 1348 counts**, the indictment had to allege that defendants, in connection with securities, used false or misleading statements as part of a scheme to obtain money or property. The court held the indictment did that by alleging a classic pump-and-dump scheme: defendants falsely touted securities to their followers, induced purchases, inflated the price, and then sold for profit.

For **§ 1349 conspiracy**, the indictment had to allege that the defendants **agreed to commit securities fraud**. Here, the same pump-and-dump allegations did that work: multiple defendants allegedly coordinated the scheme together.

So the short answer is:

- **§ 1348 is a different criminal statute from Rule 10b-5 doctrine.**
- The Fifth Circuit used **mail/wire fraud cases** because § 1348 is interpreted through the general federal fraud framework.
- The government had to allege a **deceptive scheme aimed at obtaining money or property in connection with securities**, plus **fraudulent intent**.
- For **§ 1349**, it had to allege an **agreement to carry out that securities-fraud scheme**.

### Why wasn't this pump-and-dump indictment just a forbidden right-to-control theory under Ciminelli?
Question:
The defendants argued that, at most, they deprived their followers of accurate market information about the defendants' own trading positions and intentions. That sounds a lot like depriving someone of valuable economic information, which Ciminelli said is not a traditional property interest. But the Fifth Circuit says this case is different because it alleges fraudulent inducement to buy securities. I'm having trouble seeing the line. If the lie only affected the investors' decision-making, why isn't this still just an information-deprivation theory in disguise?
Answer:
Because the indictment does **not** treat the followers’ “right to information” as the property being protected. It treats their **money** as the property being obtained by fraud.

That is the key line in the opinion.

### 1. What Ciminelli forbids
In **Ciminelli**, the government’s theory was that the victim was deprived of **valuable economic information** needed to make a decision. The Supreme Court said that is not a traditional property interest, so you cannot turn a fraud case into a “right to control” case just by saying the defendant hid information relevant to a business decision.

So if the only alleged injury is:  
> “the victim was denied accurate information,”  
that is not enough.

### 2. What this indictment alleges instead
The Fifth Circuit says this indictment goes further. It alleges that defendants:

- posted false and misleading statements,
- **to induce followers to buy securities**, and
- thereby caused followers to **part with money**,
- while defendants later profited by dumping their own holdings.

That is classic **fraudulent inducement**: using lies to get someone to hand over money or property. The opinion quotes **Kousisis** for the point that fraudulent inducement “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.”

### 3. Why the “information” point does not win
You are right that the lies affected investors’ decision-making. But that does not make the case a forbidden right-to-control theory.

Almost every fraud case affects decision-making. The difference is **what the lie is used to obtain**:

- **Ciminelli**: lie deprives victim of information itself; no traditional property interest alleged.
- **Constantinescu**: lie is used to **cause purchases of securities**, i.e. to extract money from victims.

So the “information” is the means, not the object, of the scheme.

### 4. The opinion’s own formulation
The court is explicit:

> “The indictment does not mention the right-to-control theory. Rather, it alleges that defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property.”

That is why the court says this case is “unlike” Ciminelli.

### Bottom line
This was not dismissed as a forbidden right-to-control theory because the indictment alleged **fraudulent inducement to transfer money**, not merely deprivation of market information. The misstatements mattered because they were used to get investors to buy securities, and that is a traditional property-fraud theory under the opinion’s reading of **Ciminelli** and **Kousisis**.

### How is a victim deprived of money or property when they buy stock and receive stock in return?
Question:
This is the part I find most counterintuitive. The followers allegedly paid money to buy securities because of the misleading posts, but they still got shares when they paid. The court says that is enough because they were induced by fraud to part with their money, even if they received market-value securities in exchange. How does that qualify as property fraud after Ciminelli? Doesn't fraud usually require that the victim lose the thing the defendant is trying to obtain?
Answer:
Because the fraud here is not that the victims were left with *nothing*; it is that they were induced by lies to **part with money**.

That is the key move in **Constantinescu**. The Fifth Circuit says the indictment alleges a classic **fraudulent-inducement theory**: defendants used false posts to trick followers into buying securities, i.e. to hand over money in exchange for the purchase. That is a deprivation of **money/property**, which is a traditional property interest after **Ciminelli**.

What **Ciminelli** rejected was a theory that the victim was deprived only of the **right to make informed economic decisions** or the value of economic information itself. That is not property. But this case is different because the indictment does not rest on “they were kept in the dark.” It rests on “they were lied to so they would pay money.”

And the fact that the followers received stock back does not defeat fraud. As the opinion explains, under **Kousisis** a defendant can commit fraud even if he “provides something of equal value in return.” The fraud statutes do **not** require the victim to suffer a net economic loss. They require that the defendant, by deception, **obtain the victim’s money or property**. The Court even quotes Kousisis for the proposition that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses.”

So the answer is:

- **The property taken is the money paid for the stock.**
- **Receiving shares in return does not erase the fraud.**
- **After Ciminelli, the government still must show traditional property deprivation — and this indictment does, because the victims were induced to transfer money.**
- **What is not enough is merely depriving them of information or decision-making autonomy.**

In short, this is not “fraud because they bought a bad investment.” It is “fraud because they were lied to in order to make the purchase and transfer money.”

### After Kousisis, does intent to defraud require intending to make the victim economically worse off?
Question:
The defendants argued that they intended to profit from the scheme, not to injure their followers. The Fifth Circuit says that distinction does not matter because the object was to obtain money by deception. Does that mean criminal fraud no longer requires any intended economic harm at all, so long as the defendant lies to induce a transaction involving money or property? If actual or intended net loss is unnecessary, what is the limiting principle?
Answer:
**No.** After *Kousisis*—as applied by the Fifth Circuit in **United States v. Constantinescu**—an intent to defraud **does not require an intent to leave the victim economically worse off overall**.

The court is quite explicit on this point. It says that under *Kousisis*, the fraud statutes reach a defendant who is **“schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”** And it rejects the defendants’ “we wanted to profit, not harm anyone” argument as **“a distinction without a difference.”**

## What intent to defraud still requires

The opinion keeps the traditional fraud elements intact:

- a **scheme to defraud**;
- a **material misrepresentation**;
- **intent to deceive**; and
- an intent that **some harm result from the deceit**.

But after *Kousisis*, that “harm” need **not** be a net economic loss. It is enough that the deception is used to induce the victim to part with **money or property**.

That is why the Fifth Circuit says the indictment was sufficient: it alleged that defendants **misled followers so they would buy securities**, thereby parting with money, and that defendants profited by later dumping those securities.

## The limiting principle

The limiting principle is **not** “any lie connected to money.” It is still limited by the fraud statutes’ traditional property focus.

So, under *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* as discussed in *Constantinescu*:

1. **Mere deprivation of valuable economic information is not enough**  
   *Ciminelli* rejected the “right-to-control” theory.

2. **There must be deception aimed at obtaining money or property**  
   *Kousisis* approved the fraudulent-inducement theory because the object was to obtain the victim’s money or property.

3. **The victim need not suffer net pecuniary loss**  
   The fact that the victim receives something of value in return does not defeat fraud.

So the line is: **fraud law punishes deceptive schemes to obtain money or property, not every dishonest statement or every transaction where the defendant profits.** The defendant must be trying to induce a transfer of money or property by material falsehoods.

In short, **intent to defraud no longer means intending net economic harm**; it means intending to deceive in order to obtain money or property. The fraud statutes still require a protected property interest and a deceptive scheme aimed at that transfer.

### For federal fraud, does the victim's money have to go directly to the defendant?
Question:
In a pump-and-dump scheme, the deceived followers may be buying in the market from whoever happens to be selling, while the defendants profit by unloading their own shares into the inflated market. That seems less direct than a classic fraud where the victim hands money straight to the fraudster. The opinion cites Baker to say there is no mirror-image requirement between the victim's loss and the defendant's gain. How indirect can the transfer be before it stops counting as obtaining the victim's money or property by fraud?
Answer:
No. **Under Constantinescu, the victim’s money does not have to be handed directly to the defendant.**

The Fifth Circuit rejected the idea that federal fraud requires a **one-to-one, mirror-image transfer** from victim to wrongdoer. Citing **Baker**, the court explained that there is **“no ‘mirror image’ requirement”** between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain. What matters is that the defendant, **by materially false pretenses**, **induces the victim to part with money or property**, and that **obtaining that money or property is an object of the scheme**.

That is why the pump-and-dump indictment survived even though followers may have bought shares **from other market sellers**, not by paying the defendants directly. The court treated that as still alleging a scheme to defraud because the defendants allegedly **used false statements to induce purchases of securities** and then profited by **dumping their own shares** into the inflated market.

So the answer is:

- **Direct payment to the defendant is not required**
- **A fraud can be indirect**
- But there still must be a scheme to **obtain money or property by deception**, not merely to deprive victims of information

The opinion does **not** give a precise cutoff for how indirect is too indirect. Its point is narrower: **the fact that the victim’s money passed through the market, rather than straight into the defendant’s hands, does not by itself defeat federal fraud liability.**
