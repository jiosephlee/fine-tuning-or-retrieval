Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### When a federal indictment is dismissed for failure to state an offense, why does the appellate court assume the alleged facts are true?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedure here. The district court dismissed the indictment before trial, but the Fifth Circuit says review is de novo and that it must take the indictment's allegations as true. Is the court only asking whether the indictment alleges every element of the offense, rather than whether the government can actually prove those allegations? If so, how is this different from reviewing evidentiary sufficiency after trial, and why is the government allowed to appeal this kind of pretrial dismissal?
Answer:
Yes. On a motion to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the court is not deciding whether the government can *prove* the charge. It is deciding whether the indictment, **on its face**, alleges a crime.

That is exactly what the Fifth Circuit said in **Constantinescu**:

> “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”

So the question is purely legal: **Do the pleaded facts, assumed true, satisfy the statutory elements?** If yes, the indictment stands; if no, it can be dismissed.

### Why assume the facts are true?
Because there has been no trial yet. At the indictment stage, factual disputes belong to the jury, not the judge. The court is not weighing evidence or deciding credibility. It is only checking whether the charging document:

1. alleges each material element of the offense,
2. informs the defendant of the charge, and
3. protects against double jeopardy.

That is why the Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* focused on whether the superseding indictment alleged a **scheme to defraud** and **intent to defraud**, not whether the defendants actually did it.

### How is this different from evidentiary sufficiency after trial?
After trial, the question changes from **“Did the indictment allege a crime?”** to **“Did the evidence prove the crime?”**

So:

- **Pretrial dismissal:** facial sufficiency of the indictment.
- **Post-trial sufficiency review:** sufficiency of the evidence presented at trial.

In *Constantinescu*, the district court had dismissed before trial, so the Fifth Circuit did **not** ask whether the government could ultimately prove the pump-and-dump allegations. It only asked whether those allegations, if true, described securities fraud. The court held they did.

### Why can the government appeal?
Because a pretrial dismissal of an indictment is a legal ruling that terminates the prosecution before jeopardy attaches. Federal law generally allows the government to appeal that kind of dismissal, and the Double Jeopardy Clause is not a bar when the defendant has not yet been tried.

So the appeal here was proper because the district court’s ruling was not an acquittal on the merits; it was a pretrial legal dismissal.

### Bottom line
The Fifth Circuit assumes the indictment’s allegations are true **only for the limited purpose of testing legal sufficiency**. It is asking:

> If everything the indictment says happened did happen, would that be a federal offense?

In *Constantinescu*, the answer was yes, because the indictment alleged that defendants used false statements to induce investors to buy securities and part with money. That was enough to state an offense, even though the government still had to prove those facts at trial.
