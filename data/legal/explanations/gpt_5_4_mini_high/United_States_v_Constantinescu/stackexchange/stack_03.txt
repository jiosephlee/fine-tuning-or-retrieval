Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why wasn't this pump-and-dump indictment just a forbidden right-to-control theory under Ciminelli?
Question:
The defendants argued that, at most, they deprived their followers of accurate market information about the defendants' own trading positions and intentions. That sounds a lot like depriving someone of valuable economic information, which Ciminelli said is not a traditional property interest. But the Fifth Circuit says this case is different because it alleges fraudulent inducement to buy securities. I'm having trouble seeing the line. If the lie only affected the investors' decision-making, why isn't this still just an information-deprivation theory in disguise?
Answer:
Because the indictment does **not** treat the followers’ “right to information” as the property being protected. It treats their **money** as the property being obtained by fraud.

That is the key line in the opinion.

### 1. What Ciminelli forbids
In **Ciminelli**, the government’s theory was that the victim was deprived of **valuable economic information** needed to make a decision. The Supreme Court said that is not a traditional property interest, so you cannot turn a fraud case into a “right to control” case just by saying the defendant hid information relevant to a business decision.

So if the only alleged injury is:  
> “the victim was denied accurate information,”  
that is not enough.

### 2. What this indictment alleges instead
The Fifth Circuit says this indictment goes further. It alleges that defendants:

- posted false and misleading statements,
- **to induce followers to buy securities**, and
- thereby caused followers to **part with money**,
- while defendants later profited by dumping their own holdings.

That is classic **fraudulent inducement**: using lies to get someone to hand over money or property. The opinion quotes **Kousisis** for the point that fraudulent inducement “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.”

### 3. Why the “information” point does not win
You are right that the lies affected investors’ decision-making. But that does not make the case a forbidden right-to-control theory.

Almost every fraud case affects decision-making. The difference is **what the lie is used to obtain**:

- **Ciminelli**: lie deprives victim of information itself; no traditional property interest alleged.
- **Constantinescu**: lie is used to **cause purchases of securities**, i.e. to extract money from victims.

So the “information” is the means, not the object, of the scheme.

### 4. The opinion’s own formulation
The court is explicit:

> “The indictment does not mention the right-to-control theory. Rather, it alleges that defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property.”

That is why the court says this case is “unlike” Ciminelli.

### Bottom line
This was not dismissed as a forbidden right-to-control theory because the indictment alleged **fraudulent inducement to transfer money**, not merely deprivation of market information. The misstatements mattered because they were used to get investors to buy securities, and that is a traditional property-fraud theory under the opinion’s reading of **Ciminelli** and **Kousisis**.
