Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why did the Fifth Circuit analyze Section 1348 securities fraud using mail and wire fraud cases instead of ordinary Rule 10b-5 doctrine?
Question:
The opinion says there is scant caselaw on 18 U.S.C. Section 1348 in the Fifth Circuit, so it borrows the concepts of a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud from mail and wire fraud cases. I thought securities fraud usually involved concepts like reliance, loss causation, and the Rule 10b-5 line of cases. Is Section 1348 a different criminal statute with different elements? What exactly did the government have to allege here under Section 1348 and the conspiracy statute, Section 1349?
Answer:
Because **§ 1348 is a criminal fraud statute, not the civil Rule 10b-5 regime**.

The Fifth Circuit says exactly why it did this: there is “**scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit**,” and § 1348 “**borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes**,” so courts have given those terms “similar treatment.” *United States v. Constantinescu* (citing *United States v. Greenlaw*). In other words, the court was not deciding a private securities-fraud case; it was interpreting the elements of a federal criminal fraud offense.

### What § 1348 required here
Under the panel’s formulation, securities fraud under § 1348 required:

1. a **scheme to defraud**, and  
2. **intent to defraud**.

The opinion further explains:

- A **scheme to defraud** requires a **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money.
- **Intent to defraud** means an intent to **deceive** and to **cause some harm** from the deceit.

That is why the court discusses mail/wire fraud cases: those cases supply the ordinary meaning of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud.”

### Why not Rule 10b-5 concepts like reliance and loss causation?
Because those are **not the elements the court was applying**.

Rule 10b-5 doctrine is mainly the law of **civil securities-fraud liability**. By contrast, § 1348, as used here, is a **criminal statute**. The Fifth Circuit therefore focused on whether the indictment alleged that defendants:

- made false or misleading statements,
- used those statements to induce investors to buy securities,
- thereby caused investors to part with money/property,
- and did so with fraudulent intent.

The opinion expressly rejects the idea that the government had to prove a **right-to-control** or a **net economic loss**. Relying on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the court says the fraud statutes require a scheme to obtain **money or property** by deceit; they do **not** require proof that the victims suffered an overall economic loss.

### What the government had to allege in this case
For the **substantive § 1348 counts**, the indictment had to allege that defendants, in connection with securities, used false or misleading statements as part of a scheme to obtain money or property. The court held the indictment did that by alleging a classic pump-and-dump scheme: defendants falsely touted securities to their followers, induced purchases, inflated the price, and then sold for profit.

For **§ 1349 conspiracy**, the indictment had to allege that the defendants **agreed to commit securities fraud**. Here, the same pump-and-dump allegations did that work: multiple defendants allegedly coordinated the scheme together.

So the short answer is:

- **§ 1348 is a different criminal statute from Rule 10b-5 doctrine.**
- The Fifth Circuit used **mail/wire fraud cases** because § 1348 is interpreted through the general federal fraud framework.
- The government had to allege a **deceptive scheme aimed at obtaining money or property in connection with securities**, plus **fraudulent intent**.
- For **§ 1349**, it had to allege an **agreement to carry out that securities-fraud scheme**.
