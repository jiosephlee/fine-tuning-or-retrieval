Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### After Kousisis, does intent to defraud require intending to make the victim economically worse off?
Question:
The defendants argued that they intended to profit from the scheme, not to injure their followers. The Fifth Circuit says that distinction does not matter because the object was to obtain money by deception. Does that mean criminal fraud no longer requires any intended economic harm at all, so long as the defendant lies to induce a transaction involving money or property? If actual or intended net loss is unnecessary, what is the limiting principle?
Answer:
**No.** After *Kousisis*—as applied by the Fifth Circuit in **United States v. Constantinescu**—an intent to defraud **does not require an intent to leave the victim economically worse off overall**.

The court is quite explicit on this point. It says that under *Kousisis*, the fraud statutes reach a defendant who is **“schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”** And it rejects the defendants’ “we wanted to profit, not harm anyone” argument as **“a distinction without a difference.”**

## What intent to defraud still requires

The opinion keeps the traditional fraud elements intact:

- a **scheme to defraud**;
- a **material misrepresentation**;
- **intent to deceive**; and
- an intent that **some harm result from the deceit**.

But after *Kousisis*, that “harm” need **not** be a net economic loss. It is enough that the deception is used to induce the victim to part with **money or property**.

That is why the Fifth Circuit says the indictment was sufficient: it alleged that defendants **misled followers so they would buy securities**, thereby parting with money, and that defendants profited by later dumping those securities.

## The limiting principle

The limiting principle is **not** “any lie connected to money.” It is still limited by the fraud statutes’ traditional property focus.

So, under *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* as discussed in *Constantinescu*:

1. **Mere deprivation of valuable economic information is not enough**  
   *Ciminelli* rejected the “right-to-control” theory.

2. **There must be deception aimed at obtaining money or property**  
   *Kousisis* approved the fraudulent-inducement theory because the object was to obtain the victim’s money or property.

3. **The victim need not suffer net pecuniary loss**  
   The fact that the victim receives something of value in return does not defeat fraud.

So the line is: **fraud law punishes deceptive schemes to obtain money or property, not every dishonest statement or every transaction where the defendant profits.** The defendant must be trying to induce a transfer of money or property by material falsehoods.

In short, **intent to defraud no longer means intending net economic harm**; it means intending to deceive in order to obtain money or property. The fraud statutes still require a protected property interest and a deceptive scheme aimed at that transfer.
