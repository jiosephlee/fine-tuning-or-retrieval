Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# The Alleged Pump-and-Dump Scheme and the District Court’s Dismissal

## The defendants and why their social-media presence mattered

The indictment alleged that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight were not ordinary retail traders. According to the government, each had a substantial following on social media and publicly presented himself as knowledgeable about stock trading. That mattered because the scheme allegedly depended on trust and influence: if followers believed these defendants were skilled and were sharing genuine trading information, the followers would be more likely to act on their posts. In other words, the defendants’ online personas were allegedly part of the mechanism that made the scheme work.

## How the alleged pump-and-dump scheme worked

The government’s account describes a classic “pump and dump” operation. First, the defendants allegedly bought a security. Then they allegedly used social media to “pump” the price by posting false or misleading claims about the security and about their own trading positions. Those posts were said to induce followers and other investors to buy the security, which would artificially drive the price upward. Once the price rose, the defendants allegedly “dumped” their holdings by secretly selling at the inflated price. The key idea is that the alleged misrepresentations were not random falsehoods; they were designed to move the market in a way that let the defendants profit.

The indictment says the scheme produced $114 million in profits. That figure is important because it shows the scale of the alleged fraud and helps explain why the government viewed the case as a serious securities-fraud prosecution rather than a mere dispute over misleading commentary or poor investment advice.

## The charges and the procedural posture

The defendants were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with substantive securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Section 1348 is the core securities-fraud statute at issue, and § 2 is the aiding-and-abetting statute. Knight pleaded guilty, but the other defendants moved to dismiss the indictment before trial.

At that stage, the question was not whether the government could prove the scheme at trial. The question was whether the indictment, as written, alleged a crime at all. That procedural distinction is crucial in criminal practice: a motion to dismiss challenges the legal sufficiency of the charging document, not the truth of the allegations. The district court therefore had to accept the indictment’s factual assertions as true and decide whether those facts described conduct that federal law makes criminal.

## Why the district court dismissed the indictment

The district court concluded that the indictment did not state an offense. Its reasoning was narrow and formalistic: it viewed the indictment as alleging only that the defendants sought to deprive their followers of valuable economic information, not of a traditional property interest. In the district court’s view, the fraud statutes do not cover a scheme that merely withholds or distorts information unless that scheme is tied to a recognized property interest.

That framing was the centerpiece of the dismissal. The court treated the indictment as if it charged only deception about information, rather than deception used to induce victims to part with money. The distinction matters because federal fraud statutes traditionally require more than dishonesty in the abstract; they require a scheme to defraud someone of money or property, or to obtain money or property by false pretenses.

## The government’s appeal and the Fifth Circuit’s task

The government appealed, arguing that the district court read the indictment too narrowly. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo, meaning it considered the issue fresh and without deference to the district court’s legal conclusion. The appellate court also had to take the indictment’s allegations as true.

That set up the core appellate question: did the indictment allege only deprivation of information, or did it allege a fraudulent scheme to induce followers to buy securities and thereby part with money? That question framed the rest of the Fifth Circuit’s opinion. The panel’s later analysis focused on whether the indictment alleged a cognizable scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud under the securities-fraud statute, not merely a plan to mislead followers in the abstract.

# How to Evaluate an Indictment: Review Standard and the Securities Fraud Framework

## De Novo Review and the Posture of a Motion to Dismiss

The Fifth Circuit begins with posture. When a district court dismisses an indictment, the court of appeals reviews that decision de novo, meaning it decides the legal question independently rather than deferring to the district court’s view. At this stage, the court does not ask whether the government can prove its case at trial. It asks only whether the indictment, on its face, charges a federal offense.

That is why the court also repeats a basic but crucial rule: the allegations in the indictment are taken as true for purposes of the dismissal motion. The court is not weighing evidence, resolving factual disputes, or judging whether the alleged conduct is likely to be proved. It is only deciding whether the grand jury has alleged facts that, if true, amount to the crime charged.

## What an Indictment Must Contain

An indictment serves three core functions. First, it must plead the elements of the offense so the defendant and the court can see what crime is being charged. Second, it must give the defendant fair notice of the accusations so the defendant can prepare a defense. Third, it must be specific enough to protect the defendant from being prosecuted again for the same offense, which is the double-jeopardy function.

The Fifth Circuit frames this requirement in terms of “material elements.” That means the indictment must include each element that matters to the offense; it cannot leave out a necessary part of the crime. The government does not need to set out all of its evidence in the indictment, but it does need to allege the facts that make the offense complete. In this case, that standard matters because the defendants argued that the indictment described deceptive conduct without alleging the kind of property deprivation needed for fraud.

## Section 1348 and Why the Court Looks to Mail and Wire Fraud Cases

The securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, is written in two related ways. It prohibits schemes to defraud any person in connection with any security, and it also prohibits schemes to obtain money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of any security. The opinion treats those formulations as part of the same basic fraud framework.

The Fifth Circuit also explains that there is relatively little Fifth Circuit case law interpreting § 1348. For that reason, the court looks to mail and wire fraud precedents, because § 1348 borrows key concepts from those statutes. In practice, that means the court uses the same general ideas about deception, property, and intent that appear in the older fraud cases. The opinion relies especially on recent Supreme Court decisions, including Ciminelli and Kousisis, to clarify what counts as fraud after those cases narrowed certain theories and confirmed others.

## The Two Operative Elements: Scheme to Defraud and Intent to Defraud

The court says securities fraud, as relevant here, requires two elements: a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. That is the basic analytical structure the court uses before turning to the defendants’ specific arguments.

A scheme to defraud involves a material misrepresentation used to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money. In other words, fraud is not just about dishonesty in the abstract; it is about deceptive conduct directed at getting value from the victim. The court also describes the scheme element in terms of an objective to obtain the victim’s money or property.

The intent to defraud element requires more than a false statement. It requires an intent to deceive and an intent to cause some harm to result from the deceit. The court’s framing is important because the defendants tried to recast the alleged scheme as merely a plan to profit from public misinformation. The court’s framework asks whether the allegations go further and show deception aimed at obtaining money or property.

## The Baseline Definitions the Court Uses

The opinion’s definitions matter because they set the boundaries of the later merits discussion. The court says a defendant commits securities fraud when he engaged in deception and obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the fraud. That phrasing comes from the Supreme Court’s recent fraud cases and helps the Fifth Circuit distinguish permissible fraud theories from impermissible ones.

Ciminelli is used to show what fraud is not. The Supreme Court rejected a theory that rested only on depriving victims of valuable economic information, because mere information about how to make an economic decision is not itself a traditional property interest. By contrast, Kousisis is used to show what fraud can be: a fraudulent-inducement theory, in which the defendant uses a lie to get the victim to hand over money or property, even if the victim receives something in return. The Fifth Circuit uses these cases to explain that the relevant question is not simply whether the victims were misinformed, but whether the indictment alleges that deception was used to obtain money or property.

That framework is what the court applies to the indictment in Constantinescu. The court is not yet deciding whether the government will ultimately prove a pump-and-dump scheme, only whether the indictment alleges enough to state securities fraud. On the court’s reading, an indictment alleging that defendants used false social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities, thereby parting with money and enriching the defendants, does allege the essential fraud elements.

# Scheme to Defraud: Traditional Property, Fraudulent Inducement, and the Limits of Ciminelli

## The defendants’ property-based objection to the indictment

The defendants’ first major argument was that the superseding indictment did not allege a fraud offense at all because, in their view, it described only an attempt to deprive followers of useful market information. The district court accepted that framing and dismissed the indictment on the theory that federal fraud requires an aim to take a traditional property interest, not merely to influence someone’s economic decision-making. The Fifth Circuit reversed because it read the indictment differently. In the court’s view, the government did not charge a bare informational deprivation. It charged a classic fraud theory: the defendants allegedly used false social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities and thereby part with money.

That distinction matters because the federal fraud statutes protect property, not just honest markets or accurate information. A defendant can mislead people in many ways, but not every lie is fraud under these statutes. The key question is what the lie is supposed to obtain. If the object of the scheme is the victim’s money or property, the statute is implicated; if the object is only to deprive the victim of information in the abstract, the statute is not.

## Ciminelli’s rejection of the right-to-control theory

The court’s analysis starts with **Ciminelli v. United States**, which rejected the so-called right-to-control theory. Under that theory, the government had argued that a defendant could commit fraud by depriving a victim of “potentially valuable economic information” needed to make discretionary economic choices. The Supreme Court said that approach goes too far because the federal fraud statutes reach only traditional property interests, and the “right to valuable economic information” is not itself property in the relevant sense.

The Fifth Circuit emphasized that Ciminelli is about the theory of harm, not merely about deception. A fraud prosecution cannot rest on the idea that the victim was kept in the dark or denied a better-informed choice, standing alone. There must be a scheme to obtain money or property. That is why the district court’s reliance on “economic information” was not enough to dispose of this indictment unless the indictment truly stopped at that point.

## What the right-to-control theory means, and why it was not the theory here

The right-to-control theory treats the victim’s property as the supposed “right to control” assets through informed decision-making. In practical terms, it says the victim was cheated because he lacked information that would have affected whether he spent money, entered a deal, or made an investment. Ciminelli rejected that approach because it converts fraud into a general anti-deception law and stretches “property” beyond its traditional meaning.

The Fifth Circuit said the indictment here did not proceed on that basis. It did not charge the defendants merely with making followers less informed about securities or with interfering with some abstract decision-making right. Instead, it alleged that the defendants made false and misleading statements “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” That allegation points to a concrete transfer of money: followers allegedly bought securities because they were tricked into doing so. The victims were not just deprived of information; they were induced to hand over funds.

## Fraudulent inducement as a valid fraud theory

The court relied heavily on **Kousisis v. United States**, which clarified that fraudulent inducement is a legitimate fraud theory and is distinct from the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli. Under fraudulent inducement, a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into parting with money or property, even if the victim receives something in return and even if the defendant did not intend to leave the victim worse off in a net economic sense.

That point was central to the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning. The defendants argued that they intended to profit, not to injure anyone, and that their followers may have received securities of market value. But Kousisis makes clear that fraud does not require proof that the victim ended up economically worse off overall. What matters is that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by false pretenses. Here, the indictment alleged exactly that: the defendants posted false and misleading information to induce followers to buy securities, and the defendants profited when the price was artificially inflated and they later sold.

## Why the indictment stated a cognizable scheme to defraud

With that framework, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment alleged more than a deprivation of information. It alleged that the defendants used deceptive social-media posts to cause followers to purchase securities, which means to part with money. That is a traditional property interest, and it is enough to support a fraud charge even if the victims received securities in exchange and even if the defendants’ goal was to enrich themselves rather than to cause a net loss.

The court’s bottom line was that the indictment described a fraudulent inducement scheme, not a right-to-control theory. Because the alleged object of the scheme was the followers’ money, the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud. That is why the Fifth Circuit rejected the district court’s property-based dismissal and allowed the securities fraud prosecution to go forward.

# Intent to Defraud: Why Profit-Seeking Can Satisfy the Statute Even Without Net Economic Loss

## The defendants’ argument that intent to profit is different from intent to harm

The defendants’ second main argument was that the indictment failed to allege an “intent to defraud” because, in their view, they were trying only to make money for themselves, not to cause anyone else harm. They framed the scheme as one aimed at enriching the defendants through trading profits, while leaving their followers with securities that still had value. On that theory, the case would be about self-enrichment, not fraud.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing. In fraud cases, the key question is not whether the defendant hoped to profit, but whether the defendant used deception to obtain money or property. The court treated the profit motive as fully consistent with fraudulent intent. If the defendants induced investors to part with money by means of false and misleading statements, then the fact that the defendants wanted to cash out at a gain does not save them.

## Kousisis and the modern fraudulent-inducement theory

The court’s analysis depended heavily on the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. That case clarified that federal fraud statutes reach “fraudulent inducement” schemes: a defendant lies in order to induce a victim to enter a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The important point is that the lie is used to get the transaction done.

*Kousisis* also made clear that fraudulent inducement is not just a renamed version of the “right-to-control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*. Under *Ciminelli*, the government cannot satisfy fraud statutes by showing only that the defendant deprived the victim of valuable economic information. But *Kousisis* explained that when a defendant lies to obtain money or property, the fraud statutes are satisfied even if the victim receives something in return.

That distinction mattered here. The Fifth Circuit said the indictment did not rely on a right-to-control theory at all. Instead, it alleged that defendants used false statements to induce followers to buy securities. That is a straightforward fraudulent-inducement theory: the victims were induced to hand over money based on deception.

## Fraud does not require proof that the defendant sought to inflict net pecuniary loss

The defendants argued that fraud requires an intent to make the victim economically worse off. The Fifth Circuit said *Kousisis* forecloses that idea. Under *Kousisis*, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, even if he does not intend to leave the victim worse off in net economic terms.

That point is central to understanding the opinion. A fraud case does not have to show that the victim ended up poorer after the transaction in a bookkeeping sense. The statute is violated when the defendant uses deception to induce the transfer of money or property. In the court’s words, the scheme is complete once the defendant has induced that transfer under materially false pretenses. The defendant’s purpose to profit from the transaction is therefore not a defense; it can itself help show that the object of the scheme was to get the victim’s money.

The court quoted *Kousisis* for the idea that the fraud statutes are satisfied where the defendant had “money in mind.” That phrase captures the Fifth Circuit’s point: if the scheme’s design was to trick investors into buying securities so the defendants could sell at a profit, then the object of the fraud was to obtain money through deception.

## Why receiving securities of some value did not defeat the fraud theory

The defendants also emphasized that the followers bought securities, so they did not receive nothing. The Fifth Circuit held that this did not defeat the indictment. Under *Kousisis*, a fraud defendant may still be liable even if he gives the victim something in return, including something of equal value. The statute does not require that the victim suffer a net loss or that the defendant’s gain be the exact mirror image of the victim’s loss.

That is why the court rejected the argument that the followers’ receipt of securities somehow erased the injury. The alleged injury was the induced transfer of money under false pretenses. In fraud law, the harm is the deceptive procurement of the victim’s money or property, not necessarily a showing that the victim ended up with a worthless asset or an overall bad bargain. The indictment’s allegation that defendants posted false and misleading information “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” was enough at the pleading stage to allege that kind of injury.

## The indictment’s allegations showing intent to defraud

The indictment gave concrete allegations supporting intent. It said the defendants posted false and misleading information about the securities in order to induce other investors to buy. That allegation matters because it ties the deception directly to the intended transfer of money. The supposed social-media “pump” was not just chatter or puffery; according to the indictment, it was the mechanism by which defendants caused others to buy the securities and inflate the price.

The court treated the alleged pump-and-dump profits as further evidence of intent. The defendants were alleged to have profited $114 million from the scheme, and the court viewed that as consistent with an intent to defraud because the defendants were using deceit to generate those profits. In other words, their goal was not merely to express false opinions or to misstate market conditions in the abstract. Their goal was to induce purchases from which they could later benefit.

## Why there is no strict mirror-image requirement between victim loss and defendant gain

The Fifth Circuit also relied on its own precedent in *United States v. Baker* to reject any requirement that the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain be a strict mirror image. *Baker* explained that fraud statutes do not demand a one-to-one symmetry between what the victim loses and what the defendant gains. That principle fits this case because the defendants’ profit from the scheme does not need to match, dollar for dollar, a corresponding measurable loss suffered by each follower.

The court’s brief citation to the Seventh Circuit’s *United States v. Smith* served the same purpose. It showed that other courts have applied *Kousisis* in comparable settings to reject arguments that fraud disappears whenever the defendant provides some value in exchange for the victim’s money. The focus remains on the deceptive inducement of the payment or transfer, not on whether the victim later received something with some market value.

The bottom line is that the indictment adequately alleged intent to defraud because it alleged that the defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce investors to buy securities, thereby causing them to part with money. Under *Kousisis*, that is enough even if the defendants were primarily motivated by profit and even if the victims received securities that had value. The alleged scheme was not just about misleading investors; it was about getting their money through deception.

# Holding, Remand, and the Case’s Place in Post-Ciminelli Securities Fraud Law

## The holding: the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud

The Fifth Circuit held that the superseding indictment was legally sufficient. At the pleading stage, the government had alleged more than just false statements in the abstract: it alleged that the defendants used misleading social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities, thereby causing them to part with money, while the defendants secretly sold into the resulting price inflation and profited. In the court’s view, those allegations described a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme, not merely a deprivation of “valuable economic information.”

That distinction mattered because securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, as the court explained, requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The indictment satisfied that standard by alleging material misrepresentations designed to deceive investors into purchasing securities. The court emphasized that the object of the alleged scheme was money: the defendants allegedly used lies to get others to buy, which is enough to plead a fraud offense.

## The disposition: reversal of the district court’s dismissal and remand for further proceedings

Because the indictment was sufficient, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded the case for further proceedings. That means the prosecution was allowed to go forward in the district court; the appellate court did not terminate the case, but instead restored the indictment and left the next steps to the trial court.

For a law student, this disposition is a reminder that an appellate decision on indictment sufficiency is not a merits decision. The court was deciding only whether the charging document alleged an offense adequately, not whether the government would ultimately prove the charges.

## What the court did not decide

The opinion does not decide whether the defendants are guilty, whether the government can prove its allegations at trial, or whether the social-media posts were in fact false or misleading. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court must take the indictment’s allegations as true and ask only whether they state an offense. That procedural posture is important: a defendant can lose a dismissal motion even though the evidence at trial may later be contested or insufficient.

The court also did not announce a broad rule that every misleading market comment is securities fraud. Its holding is narrower. The indictment survived because it alleged a deceptive scheme aimed at inducing purchases and obtaining money, not simply a failure to provide useful information to followers.

## How the opinion reads Ciminelli and Kousisis

The defendants relied on **Ciminelli** for the proposition that fraud statutes do not criminalize the deprivation of “valuable economic information” alone. The Fifth Circuit accepted that point, but said it did not help the defendants because this indictment went further. In **Ciminelli**, the Supreme Court rejected the “right-to-control” theory, under which the government treated the victim’s interest in making informed economic decisions as the property interest itself. The Fifth Circuit said this case was different because the indictment did not rest on that theory. Instead, it alleged that the defendants used lies to induce investors to hand over money for securities.

That move brought **Kousisis** to the foreground. The Fifth Circuit relied on Kousisis for the idea that fraud does not require a showing that the victim suffered a net economic loss. A defendant can commit fraud even if the victim receives something of value in return, so long as the defendant used materially false statements to obtain money or property. In other words, the law focuses on whether the defendant schemed to get the victim’s money by deceit, not on whether the victim was left worse off in a balance-sheet sense.

## Why the opinion matters for pump-and-dump prosecutions on social media

The opinion is especially significant because it applies those fraud principles to a modern pump-and-dump scheme carried out through social media. The alleged mechanism was straightforward: defendants built credibility with large followings, publicly touted securities, posted false or misleading claims to drive demand, let the price rise, and then sold their positions for a profit. The court treated that as a scheme to obtain money through deception, even though the fraud was implemented through posts, followers, and online market hype rather than a traditional face-to-face solicitation.

That makes the case an important signal for prosecutors and defense lawyers alike. When the government pleads that false statements were used to induce securities purchases, it is not enough for a defendant to argue that the scheme involved only misinformation or that the victims may have received something of market value. If the indictment connects the deception to the defendants’ effort to obtain money or property, the fraud charge can survive dismissal.

## The broader pleading lesson

The opinion’s practical lesson is that a federal fraud indictment must allege more than withholding useful information; it must allege a deceptive plan to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit’s analysis shows how post-**Ciminelli** fraud pleading works: an indictment is vulnerable if it relies on a right-to-control or information-only theory, but it is sufficient if it alleges that false statements induced the victim to part with money or property.

For students reading fraud opinions, that is the key takeaway. The court did not ask whether the defendants were merely “misinforming” people in some general sense. It asked whether the alleged misinformation was the means of getting investors’ money. Because the indictment said yes, the dismissal could not stand.
