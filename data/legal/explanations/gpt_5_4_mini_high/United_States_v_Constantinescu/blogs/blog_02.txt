Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Profit Without Proving Loss: How *Constantinescu* Imports *Kousisis* into Securities Fraud

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* matters because it answers a question that keeps resurfacing in fraud cases after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*: does the government have to show that the defendant meant to cause the victim a net economic loss? The court’s answer is no. In a “pump-and-dump” prosecution, the indictment can survive so long as it plausibly alleges that the defendants used material deception to induce victims to part with money or property. That remains true even if the victims received something of market value in return, and even if the defendants’ own goal was simply to profit.

That is an important move. The district court had treated the case as if the government were charging little more than the deprivation of “valuable economic information.” The Fifth Circuit said the indictment goes further: it alleges that defendants lied to induce their followers to buy securities, thereby obtaining the followers’ money. That distinction is now central to post-*Ciminelli* fraud doctrine, and *Constantinescu* is one of the clearest appellate applications of *Kousisis* to the securities context.

## The court’s basic move

The opinion starts from familiar ground. Securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the panel explains, borrows heavily from mail and wire fraud principles. So the familiar two-part frame applies: a scheme to defraud, and an intent to defraud. The defendants conceded deception, but argued that deception alone is not enough. In their telling, they were not trying to deprive followers of a traditional property interest; they were trying to influence followers’ economic decisions, which is precisely the sort of “right-to-control” theory the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli*.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing because it misdescribes the indictment. The indictment did not simply allege that followers were deprived of information. It alleged that defendants falsely touted their positions and prospects in securities in order to induce purchases, which let defendants “dump” their own holdings for profit. That is classic fraudulent inducement: tricking a victim into handing over money by means of a lie.

That matters because *Ciminelli* left room for ordinary fraud. The Supreme Court disapproved the notion that the fraud statutes protect a generalized “right to control” one’s assets through access to information. But *Ciminelli* did not eliminate fraud cases where false statements are used to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit emphasized that distinction. The alleged object of the scheme here was not merely to deprive investors of information; it was to get them to buy securities and part with money.

## Why “we only meant to profit” is not a defense

The more interesting part of the opinion is the court’s treatment of intent. The defendants argued that even if they lied, they lacked an intent to defraud because they wanted to profit, not to harm their followers. The Fifth Circuit treated that as squarely foreclosed by *Kousisis*.

That is the key doctrinal import of the case. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court rejected the idea that fraud requires proof the defendant sought to inflict net economic loss. A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain money or property through material deception, even if the victim gets something back that has value. The Fifth Circuit imported that logic directly. It held that the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, and do not require a “mirror image” of the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss. The fact that victims received market-value securities does not defeat the charge if they were induced by fraud to make the purchase.

This is a meaningful clarification. Defendants in market-manipulation cases often try to reframe the issue as one of profit-seeking rather than harm. But *Constantinescu* says that distinction is not dispositive. If the object of the scheme was to obtain the victims’ money through material lies, the intent element is satisfied. The court’s formulation is blunt: the indictment alleges defendants “had money in mind,” and that is enough.

## What the opinion gets right — and what it leaves open

Doctrinally, the opinion is on solid footing. Securities fraud has always been about deception used to extract money in connection with a security. A pump-and-dump scheme is almost the paradigmatic case of fraud by fraudulent inducement: false market signals are deployed to induce purchases, after which the schemer exits with the proceeds. The Fifth Circuit was right not to let that become a right-to-control case in disguise.

Still, the opinion’s reach should not be overstated. This was a motion-to-dismiss decision, and the court was careful to take the indictment’s allegations as true. The government still has to prove at trial that the defendants made material misrepresentations, that they acted with fraudulent intent, and that the scheme was in connection with securities transactions. The court did not say every exaggerated stock tip is fraud, or that ordinary promotional speech becomes criminal whenever prices move.

But the downstream effect is real. After *Constantinescu*, defendants in the Fifth Circuit will have a harder time arguing that fraud fails because the victim got “something of value” or because the defendant merely sought profit. That pushes disputes away from abstract property theory and toward the facts: Were the statements false? Were they material? Were they designed to induce purchases? For prosecutors, that is a very favorable terrain.

## The bigger implication

The broader implication is that *Kousisis* may prove to be a much more general fraud case than many initially thought. *Constantinescu* shows how quickly its reasoning can migrate from government-contract fraud into securities fraud, especially in schemes built around social media and retail investors. If the government can allege that false statements were used to induce money to change hands, the “no intended loss” defense looks increasingly weak.

That does not mean fraud law now covers every deceptive market statement. But it does mean the old instinct — that criminal fraud requires a showing that the defendant intended to leave victims economically worse off — has lost much of its force. In the Fifth Circuit, at least, the core question is simpler: was money or property obtained by material deception? If yes, the absence of intended net loss is not a get-out-of-indictment card.
