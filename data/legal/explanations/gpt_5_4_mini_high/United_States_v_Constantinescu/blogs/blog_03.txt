Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Section 1348 as Mail-and-Wire Fraud’s Securities Cousin

## Why Constantinescu matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* matters because it shows how federal securities-fraud charging is increasingly being built out of ordinary fraud doctrine. The panel reversed dismissal of a superseding indictment alleging a social-media “pump and dump” scheme, and in doing so treated 18 U.S.C. § 1348 much like the mail- and wire-fraud statutes: the key questions were whether the indictment alleged a scheme to defraud, an intent to defraud, and a deprivation of money or property. That may sound routine, but it is doctrinally significant. In a circuit with relatively thin § 1348 precedent, the court made clear that prosecutors do not need to plead some freestanding, securities-specific theory of injury when the alleged scheme induced investors to part with money.

The district court had taken a narrower view, reasoning that the indictment amounted to an allegation that defendants deprived followers of valuable economic information, not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing. On the government’s allegations, the defendants did not merely distort information in the abstract; they used false and misleading social-media posts to induce purchases of securities, thereby causing followers to part with money. That distinction—between a pure right-to-control theory and a fraudulent-inducement theory—drives the opinion.

## The court’s fraud analysis: Ciminelli out, Kousisis in

The panel’s reasoning is anchored in two recent Supreme Court decisions. First, *Ciminelli v. United States* forecloses the idea that fraud can rest solely on depriving a victim of “valuable economic information” or the “right to control” assets. That matters because defense counsel understandably tried to cast the case as one about misinformation and market manipulation, not property. But the Fifth Circuit said that characterization stops short of what the indictment actually alleges. The indictment is not about a mere informational deprivation; it alleges that defendants tricked followers into buying securities. That is a classic fraudulent-inducement theory.

Second, the court relies heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, which reaffirmed that the fraud statutes are violated when the defendant schemes to obtain money or property by deceit, even if the victim receives something of equal value in return and even if the defendant’s primary motive is profit rather than injury for its own sake. That is a big point for securities cases. Defendants argued that they intended to enrich themselves, not to harm investors, and that followers may have received securities at market value. The Fifth Circuit responded that fraud does not require net economic loss. If the object of the scheme was to obtain the victims’ money through misrepresentation, the statute is satisfied.

That is an important doctrinal choice, but it is also fairly orthodox after *Kousisis*. The opinion is careful to say that a defendant cannot be convicted for depriving someone of economic information alone; what saves the indictment is the allegation that the misinformation was used to induce securities purchases. In other words, the court does not erase *Ciminelli*; it locates this case on the other side of the line.

## The securities-fraud implications

What makes *Constantinescu* notable is not just that the indictment survives, but how easily § 1348 is being assimilated to general fraud law. The court candidly notes that Fifth Circuit precedent on § 1348 is sparse, and says the statute borrows “key concepts” from mail and wire fraud. That borrowing has real consequences. It means that developments in general fraud doctrine—especially the Supreme Court’s recent efforts to cabin “right-to-control” theories while preserving fraudulent inducement—will shape securities fraud almost automatically.

For prosecutors, that is a feature, not a bug. Pump-and-dump schemes are a natural fit for a statute that reaches schemes to defraud “in connection with” securities. The opinion gives the government substantial flexibility to charge social-media-driven manipulation without having to prove a separate, technical property theory about market information. The practical message is clear: if the allegation is that defendants lied to get people to buy securities so that defendants could sell at a profit, § 1348 is available.

For defendants, the decision narrows one of the more promising post-*Ciminelli* arguments. A theory that collapses securities fraud into “they lied and distorted the market” may run into trouble. But once the government pleads that the lies induced actual purchases, the fraud statutes become much harder to evade. The opinion also underscores that an indictment need not negate every possible innocent explanation or establish actual loss at the pleading stage.

## The broader doctrinal picture

My read is that *Constantinescu* is both sensible and revealing. It is sensible because the allegations here are far removed from a pure information-right theory; this is the archetypal kind of scheme federal fraud law has long targeted. But it is revealing because it shows how little independent content § 1348 may have in practice. Rather than developing a distinct body of securities-fraud doctrine, the Fifth Circuit is effectively importing the latest mail- and wire-fraud cases wholesale. That may promote consistency, but it also means the meaning of § 1348 will likely continue to track the Supreme Court’s general fraud decisions.

The downstream effect is likely more charging latitude in influencer-driven market-manipulation cases and less room for defendants to argue that the government must prove some special securities-specific property loss. At the same time, *Constantinescu* leaves the outer boundaries unresolved. It does not say that every false stock tip is fraud, or that mere market misinformation suffices. It says something narrower and more defensible: when false posts are alleged to have induced investors to hand over money for securities, the indictment states a fraud offense. That is a significant point—and, for the government, a very usable one.
