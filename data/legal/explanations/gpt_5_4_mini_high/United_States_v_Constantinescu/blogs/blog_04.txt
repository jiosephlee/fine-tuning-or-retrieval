Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# From Finfluencer Hype to Federal Fraud: What *Constantinescu* Means for Social-Media Market Manipulation

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is an important signal that federal fraud law still reaches classic market manipulation even when it is dressed up in influencer culture and social-media branding. The defendants allegedly used their online followings to “pump and dump” securities: they posted false or misleading claims about their own positions and about where a stock was headed, drew in followers to buy, and then secretly sold into the spike. The district court thought the indictment fell short because it described a deprivation of “valuable economic information,” not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit disagreed, and for good reason. The indictment, the court held, alleged something more concrete than information harm: it alleged that followers were fraudulently induced to part with money to buy securities.

That distinction is now central to a lot of modern fraud litigation. After *Ciminelli* rejected the right-to-control theory, defense lawyers have understandably pressed hard on the proposition that fraud cannot rest on some generalized loss of informational autonomy. But *Constantinescu*, following *Kousisis*, shows the other side of the ledger: when the scheme is to trick victims into handing over money or property, the fraud statutes still do the work, even if the victims receive something of nominal value in return.

## The court’s reasoning

The panel’s analysis is straightforward but consequential. It begins with the familiar proposition that an indictment is enough if it alleges each material element of the offense and gives fair notice of the charges. On the merits, the court treats securities fraud as borrowing heavily from mail and wire fraud doctrine, and it frames the two key elements as a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud.

The defendants’ principal move was to recast the case as one about withheld information, not property. That argument borrowed from *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court made clear that the federal fraud statutes do not criminalize schemes to deprive victims of “potentially valuable economic information” standing alone. But the Fifth Circuit said that was not what the indictment alleged here. According to the government, the defendants did not merely manipulate their followers’ decision-making environment; they used false statements to induce purchases of securities. That is the classic fraudulent-inducement theory that the Supreme Court expressly preserved in *Kousisis*.

The intent issue follows the same logic. The defendants argued that they meant to make money, not to harm anyone. But *Kousisis* rejected the notion that fraud requires a scheme aimed at leaving the victim economically worse off on a net basis. What matters is whether the defendant schemed to obtain money or property by deception. On the allegations here, the answer was yes: the defendants allegedly lied to get followers to buy, then profited from the resulting price movement. The court’s point is that profiting from the victim’s purchase is not a benign side effect; it is the object of the scheme.

## Where the line is drawn

The most useful feature of the opinion is the line it tries to preserve between ordinary bullish commentary and actionable deception. Not every loud, optimistic, self-interested stock tip is fraud. The securities markets are full of hype, bad calls, and aggressive promotion, and the First Amendment and the securities laws do not convert all of that into a federal case.

What makes *Constantinescu* different is the alleged package of lies: false claims about the promoters’ own trading positions, misleading signals intended to induce purchase, and undisclosed dumping on the back end. That combination matters because it turns the speech from opinion or advocacy into a mechanism for obtaining money by false pretenses. The opinion does not say that saying “I like this stock” is enough. It says that saying, in substance, “I am aligned with you and believe in the trade” while secretly using followers as exit liquidity can support securities fraud charges.

That is a manageable line, even if it will be litigated hard in future cases. Prosecutors will need to prove material misstatements or omissions, not just bad vibes. Defense counsel, in turn, will likely focus on whether the statements were actually false, whether they were material, and whether the government can tie each defendant to the allegedly deceptive posts and trades. The opinion makes clear that those are trial issues, not grounds for dismissing a facially sufficient indictment.

## Broader implications

The downstream effects are likely to be significant for both enforcement and defense strategy. For prosecutors, *Constantinescu* is a template for charging influencer-driven market manipulation without getting trapped in the vocabulary of “informational harm.” If the theory is that victims were induced to buy with false pretenses, the indictment can survive even if the victims received securities of some value or even if the defendant’s object was to enrich himself rather than to impose a net loss.

For traders and social-media personalities, the message is obvious: undisclosed self-dealing is not just a reputational risk; it can be a criminal one. The case also reinforces that online communities are not a fraud-free zone simply because the communication happens in posts, videos, or chats rather than in a traditional brokerage setting.

At the doctrinal level, the opinion helps settle an emerging post-*Ciminelli* and post-*Kousisis* equilibrium. *Ciminelli* narrowed fraud by rejecting the right-to-control theory, but *Kousisis* prevented that narrowing from swallowing fraudulent-inducement cases. *Constantinescu* is a clean example of how those two decisions fit together: no fraud liability for mere information deprivation, but full liability where deception is used to obtain money or property.

The case is only at the indictment stage, so the real fight is still ahead. On remand, the government will have to prove that the defendants’ posts were materially false, that the misrepresentations were part of a scheme, and that the trading was tied to the alleged pump-and-dump operation. But the Fifth Circuit has now made one thing clear: dressing a scheme in influencer language does not take it outside the federal fraud statutes.
