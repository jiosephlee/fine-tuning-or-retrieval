Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Ciminelli Did Not Kill the Pump-and-Dump Case: The Fifth Circuit’s Property Theory in *Constantinescu*

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is a useful reminder that the Supreme Court’s recent narrowing of federal fraud law did not erase every deception-based securities prosecution. What it did do was force courts to be precise about what, exactly, the defendant was trying to obtain. Here, that mattered because the district court read *Ciminelli v. United States* to mean that the government had alleged only a deprivation of “valuable economic information,” not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit disagreed and revived the indictment, holding that the alleged pump-and-dump scheme was aimed at the followers’ money—the dollars used to buy the securities—not just their right to make informed decisions.

That distinction is now central to fraud doctrine. After *Ciminelli* rejected the “right-to-control” theory, defense lawyers have had a strong argument that many fraud cases are really about bad information, not property. *Constantinescu* shows how prosecutors can still survive that objection when the indictment alleges that false statements were used to induce victims to part with money in a transaction.

## The court’s doctrinal move

The opinion’s key move is to separate two very different theories of fraud. The first, rejected in *Ciminelli*, treats the victim’s interest in accurate information as the property right itself. The second, which the Fifth Circuit treated as controlling here, is the classic fraudulent-inducement theory: a defendant lies to get the victim to hand over money or property.

That distinction does a lot of work in the opinion. The indictment alleged that the defendants told their social-media followers false things about their own trading positions and the likely price of the stock, pumped up demand, and then sold into the spike. In the court’s view, those allegations do not describe a scheme to deprive followers merely of market information. They describe a scheme to induce those followers to buy securities with their money. That is enough to allege fraud because the object of the scheme was money or property, even if the victims received securities in exchange.

The court leaned heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, which clarified that the fraud statutes do not require the government to prove the victim ended up economically worse off overall. That point is important in pump-and-dump cases. A defendant might argue that buyers received something of market value and therefore were not “injured” in the ordinary sense. The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing, emphasizing that fraud is complete when money or property is obtained by materially false pretenses. In other words, the fact that the victims got stock certificates back does not cleanse the deceit that induced the purchases.

The court also disposed of the intent issue in the same way. Defendants argued that they meant to enrich themselves, not to harm followers. But the opinion treats that as beside the point. Under *Kousisis*, the fraud statutes do not require proof of a net-loss objective; what matters is the intent to deceive in order to obtain money or property. The indictment’s allegations that the defendants posted false statements “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” were enough.

## Why the distinction is persuasive — and where the opinion draws the line

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis is persuasive because it keeps *Ciminelli* in its lane. The Supreme Court rejected the idea that federal fraud law reaches a bare right to accurate economic information. But that does not mean every fraud case involving misinformation collapses. Most classic frauds are information cases in one sense or another; the question is whether the deception is used to obtain something traditionally recognized as property. Here, the answer was yes: the followers’ trading dollars.

That said, the opinion is careful, and rightly so, not to hold that any misleading market commentary is fraud. The indictment alleged more than optimistic stock-picking or puffery. It alleged false statements about the defendants’ own positions and a coordinated scheme to inflate prices before dumping the holdings. That matters. Without those allegations, the case would look much more like an information-rights dispute, especially in a world where online traders, influencers, and meme-stock promoters constantly mix opinion, hype, and advice.

The decision also fits comfortably within securities-fraud doctrine because the statute reaches schemes “to defraud” and schemes to obtain money or property “in connection with” securities transactions. By grounding the property interest in the followers’ purchases, the Fifth Circuit avoids turning securities fraud into a right-to-control case while still preserving room for the government to prosecute garden-variety market manipulation.

## The downstream effects

The practical effect of *Constantinescu* is to make it harder for defendants in modern market-manipulation cases to invoke *Ciminelli* as a universal shield. That will matter beyond traditional pump-and-dump schemes. The opinion will likely be cited in prosecutions involving social-media stock promoters, crypto influencers, and other online actors who monetize trust while allegedly making false statements to move trading volume.

At the same time, the decision may encourage prosecutors to draft indictments with more care. If the theory is that false statements induced victims to spend money, say that explicitly. If the theory instead sounds like “they were deprived of accurate information,” expect a *Ciminelli* challenge. *Constantinescu* suggests the Fifth Circuit will look past labels and focus on whether the indictment pleads a real transfer of money or property.

The broader doctrinal lesson is that *Ciminelli* narrowed fraud, but did not de-federalize deception. After *Kousisis*, the government can still plead fraud where lies are used to extract money, even if the victim gets something back and even if the defendant’s motive is pure profit rather than spite. For securities cases, that is a significant clarification: the property interest is often not the market information itself, but the trading dollars that falsehoods induce investors to part with.



# Profit Without Proving Loss: How *Constantinescu* Imports *Kousisis* into Securities Fraud

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* matters because it answers a question that keeps resurfacing in fraud cases after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*: does the government have to show that the defendant meant to cause the victim a net economic loss? The court’s answer is no. In a “pump-and-dump” prosecution, the indictment can survive so long as it plausibly alleges that the defendants used material deception to induce victims to part with money or property. That remains true even if the victims received something of market value in return, and even if the defendants’ own goal was simply to profit.

That is an important move. The district court had treated the case as if the government were charging little more than the deprivation of “valuable economic information.” The Fifth Circuit said the indictment goes further: it alleges that defendants lied to induce their followers to buy securities, thereby obtaining the followers’ money. That distinction is now central to post-*Ciminelli* fraud doctrine, and *Constantinescu* is one of the clearest appellate applications of *Kousisis* to the securities context.

## The court’s basic move

The opinion starts from familiar ground. Securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the panel explains, borrows heavily from mail and wire fraud principles. So the familiar two-part frame applies: a scheme to defraud, and an intent to defraud. The defendants conceded deception, but argued that deception alone is not enough. In their telling, they were not trying to deprive followers of a traditional property interest; they were trying to influence followers’ economic decisions, which is precisely the sort of “right-to-control” theory the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli*.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing because it misdescribes the indictment. The indictment did not simply allege that followers were deprived of information. It alleged that defendants falsely touted their positions and prospects in securities in order to induce purchases, which let defendants “dump” their own holdings for profit. That is classic fraudulent inducement: tricking a victim into handing over money by means of a lie.

That matters because *Ciminelli* left room for ordinary fraud. The Supreme Court disapproved the notion that the fraud statutes protect a generalized “right to control” one’s assets through access to information. But *Ciminelli* did not eliminate fraud cases where false statements are used to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit emphasized that distinction. The alleged object of the scheme here was not merely to deprive investors of information; it was to get them to buy securities and part with money.

## Why “we only meant to profit” is not a defense

The more interesting part of the opinion is the court’s treatment of intent. The defendants argued that even if they lied, they lacked an intent to defraud because they wanted to profit, not to harm their followers. The Fifth Circuit treated that as squarely foreclosed by *Kousisis*.

That is the key doctrinal import of the case. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court rejected the idea that fraud requires proof the defendant sought to inflict net economic loss. A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain money or property through material deception, even if the victim gets something back that has value. The Fifth Circuit imported that logic directly. It held that the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, and do not require a “mirror image” of the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss. The fact that victims received market-value securities does not defeat the charge if they were induced by fraud to make the purchase.

This is a meaningful clarification. Defendants in market-manipulation cases often try to reframe the issue as one of profit-seeking rather than harm. But *Constantinescu* says that distinction is not dispositive. If the object of the scheme was to obtain the victims’ money through material lies, the intent element is satisfied. The court’s formulation is blunt: the indictment alleges defendants “had money in mind,” and that is enough.

## What the opinion gets right — and what it leaves open

Doctrinally, the opinion is on solid footing. Securities fraud has always been about deception used to extract money in connection with a security. A pump-and-dump scheme is almost the paradigmatic case of fraud by fraudulent inducement: false market signals are deployed to induce purchases, after which the schemer exits with the proceeds. The Fifth Circuit was right not to let that become a right-to-control case in disguise.

Still, the opinion’s reach should not be overstated. This was a motion-to-dismiss decision, and the court was careful to take the indictment’s allegations as true. The government still has to prove at trial that the defendants made material misrepresentations, that they acted with fraudulent intent, and that the scheme was in connection with securities transactions. The court did not say every exaggerated stock tip is fraud, or that ordinary promotional speech becomes criminal whenever prices move.

But the downstream effect is real. After *Constantinescu*, defendants in the Fifth Circuit will have a harder time arguing that fraud fails because the victim got “something of value” or because the defendant merely sought profit. That pushes disputes away from abstract property theory and toward the facts: Were the statements false? Were they material? Were they designed to induce purchases? For prosecutors, that is a very favorable terrain.

## The bigger implication

The broader implication is that *Kousisis* may prove to be a much more general fraud case than many initially thought. *Constantinescu* shows how quickly its reasoning can migrate from government-contract fraud into securities fraud, especially in schemes built around social media and retail investors. If the government can allege that false statements were used to induce money to change hands, the “no intended loss” defense looks increasingly weak.

That does not mean fraud law now covers every deceptive market statement. But it does mean the old instinct — that criminal fraud requires a showing that the defendant intended to leave victims economically worse off — has lost much of its force. In the Fifth Circuit, at least, the core question is simpler: was money or property obtained by material deception? If yes, the absence of intended net loss is not a get-out-of-indictment card.



# Section 1348 as Mail-and-Wire Fraud’s Securities Cousin

## Why Constantinescu matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* matters because it shows how federal securities-fraud charging is increasingly being built out of ordinary fraud doctrine. The panel reversed dismissal of a superseding indictment alleging a social-media “pump and dump” scheme, and in doing so treated 18 U.S.C. § 1348 much like the mail- and wire-fraud statutes: the key questions were whether the indictment alleged a scheme to defraud, an intent to defraud, and a deprivation of money or property. That may sound routine, but it is doctrinally significant. In a circuit with relatively thin § 1348 precedent, the court made clear that prosecutors do not need to plead some freestanding, securities-specific theory of injury when the alleged scheme induced investors to part with money.

The district court had taken a narrower view, reasoning that the indictment amounted to an allegation that defendants deprived followers of valuable economic information, not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing. On the government’s allegations, the defendants did not merely distort information in the abstract; they used false and misleading social-media posts to induce purchases of securities, thereby causing followers to part with money. That distinction—between a pure right-to-control theory and a fraudulent-inducement theory—drives the opinion.

## The court’s fraud analysis: Ciminelli out, Kousisis in

The panel’s reasoning is anchored in two recent Supreme Court decisions. First, *Ciminelli v. United States* forecloses the idea that fraud can rest solely on depriving a victim of “valuable economic information” or the “right to control” assets. That matters because defense counsel understandably tried to cast the case as one about misinformation and market manipulation, not property. But the Fifth Circuit said that characterization stops short of what the indictment actually alleges. The indictment is not about a mere informational deprivation; it alleges that defendants tricked followers into buying securities. That is a classic fraudulent-inducement theory.

Second, the court relies heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, which reaffirmed that the fraud statutes are violated when the defendant schemes to obtain money or property by deceit, even if the victim receives something of equal value in return and even if the defendant’s primary motive is profit rather than injury for its own sake. That is a big point for securities cases. Defendants argued that they intended to enrich themselves, not to harm investors, and that followers may have received securities at market value. The Fifth Circuit responded that fraud does not require net economic loss. If the object of the scheme was to obtain the victims’ money through misrepresentation, the statute is satisfied.

That is an important doctrinal choice, but it is also fairly orthodox after *Kousisis*. The opinion is careful to say that a defendant cannot be convicted for depriving someone of economic information alone; what saves the indictment is the allegation that the misinformation was used to induce securities purchases. In other words, the court does not erase *Ciminelli*; it locates this case on the other side of the line.

## The securities-fraud implications

What makes *Constantinescu* notable is not just that the indictment survives, but how easily § 1348 is being assimilated to general fraud law. The court candidly notes that Fifth Circuit precedent on § 1348 is sparse, and says the statute borrows “key concepts” from mail and wire fraud. That borrowing has real consequences. It means that developments in general fraud doctrine—especially the Supreme Court’s recent efforts to cabin “right-to-control” theories while preserving fraudulent inducement—will shape securities fraud almost automatically.

For prosecutors, that is a feature, not a bug. Pump-and-dump schemes are a natural fit for a statute that reaches schemes to defraud “in connection with” securities. The opinion gives the government substantial flexibility to charge social-media-driven manipulation without having to prove a separate, technical property theory about market information. The practical message is clear: if the allegation is that defendants lied to get people to buy securities so that defendants could sell at a profit, § 1348 is available.

For defendants, the decision narrows one of the more promising post-*Ciminelli* arguments. A theory that collapses securities fraud into “they lied and distorted the market” may run into trouble. But once the government pleads that the lies induced actual purchases, the fraud statutes become much harder to evade. The opinion also underscores that an indictment need not negate every possible innocent explanation or establish actual loss at the pleading stage.

## The broader doctrinal picture

My read is that *Constantinescu* is both sensible and revealing. It is sensible because the allegations here are far removed from a pure information-right theory; this is the archetypal kind of scheme federal fraud law has long targeted. But it is revealing because it shows how little independent content § 1348 may have in practice. Rather than developing a distinct body of securities-fraud doctrine, the Fifth Circuit is effectively importing the latest mail- and wire-fraud cases wholesale. That may promote consistency, but it also means the meaning of § 1348 will likely continue to track the Supreme Court’s general fraud decisions.

The downstream effect is likely more charging latitude in influencer-driven market-manipulation cases and less room for defendants to argue that the government must prove some special securities-specific property loss. At the same time, *Constantinescu* leaves the outer boundaries unresolved. It does not say that every false stock tip is fraud, or that mere market misinformation suffices. It says something narrower and more defensible: when false posts are alleged to have induced investors to hand over money for securities, the indictment states a fraud offense. That is a significant point—and, for the government, a very usable one.



# From Finfluencer Hype to Federal Fraud: What *Constantinescu* Means for Social-Media Market Manipulation

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is an important signal that federal fraud law still reaches classic market manipulation even when it is dressed up in influencer culture and social-media branding. The defendants allegedly used their online followings to “pump and dump” securities: they posted false or misleading claims about their own positions and about where a stock was headed, drew in followers to buy, and then secretly sold into the spike. The district court thought the indictment fell short because it described a deprivation of “valuable economic information,” not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit disagreed, and for good reason. The indictment, the court held, alleged something more concrete than information harm: it alleged that followers were fraudulently induced to part with money to buy securities.

That distinction is now central to a lot of modern fraud litigation. After *Ciminelli* rejected the right-to-control theory, defense lawyers have understandably pressed hard on the proposition that fraud cannot rest on some generalized loss of informational autonomy. But *Constantinescu*, following *Kousisis*, shows the other side of the ledger: when the scheme is to trick victims into handing over money or property, the fraud statutes still do the work, even if the victims receive something of nominal value in return.

## The court’s reasoning

The panel’s analysis is straightforward but consequential. It begins with the familiar proposition that an indictment is enough if it alleges each material element of the offense and gives fair notice of the charges. On the merits, the court treats securities fraud as borrowing heavily from mail and wire fraud doctrine, and it frames the two key elements as a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud.

The defendants’ principal move was to recast the case as one about withheld information, not property. That argument borrowed from *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court made clear that the federal fraud statutes do not criminalize schemes to deprive victims of “potentially valuable economic information” standing alone. But the Fifth Circuit said that was not what the indictment alleged here. According to the government, the defendants did not merely manipulate their followers’ decision-making environment; they used false statements to induce purchases of securities. That is the classic fraudulent-inducement theory that the Supreme Court expressly preserved in *Kousisis*.

The intent issue follows the same logic. The defendants argued that they meant to make money, not to harm anyone. But *Kousisis* rejected the notion that fraud requires a scheme aimed at leaving the victim economically worse off on a net basis. What matters is whether the defendant schemed to obtain money or property by deception. On the allegations here, the answer was yes: the defendants allegedly lied to get followers to buy, then profited from the resulting price movement. The court’s point is that profiting from the victim’s purchase is not a benign side effect; it is the object of the scheme.

## Where the line is drawn

The most useful feature of the opinion is the line it tries to preserve between ordinary bullish commentary and actionable deception. Not every loud, optimistic, self-interested stock tip is fraud. The securities markets are full of hype, bad calls, and aggressive promotion, and the First Amendment and the securities laws do not convert all of that into a federal case.

What makes *Constantinescu* different is the alleged package of lies: false claims about the promoters’ own trading positions, misleading signals intended to induce purchase, and undisclosed dumping on the back end. That combination matters because it turns the speech from opinion or advocacy into a mechanism for obtaining money by false pretenses. The opinion does not say that saying “I like this stock” is enough. It says that saying, in substance, “I am aligned with you and believe in the trade” while secretly using followers as exit liquidity can support securities fraud charges.

That is a manageable line, even if it will be litigated hard in future cases. Prosecutors will need to prove material misstatements or omissions, not just bad vibes. Defense counsel, in turn, will likely focus on whether the statements were actually false, whether they were material, and whether the government can tie each defendant to the allegedly deceptive posts and trades. The opinion makes clear that those are trial issues, not grounds for dismissing a facially sufficient indictment.

## Broader implications

The downstream effects are likely to be significant for both enforcement and defense strategy. For prosecutors, *Constantinescu* is a template for charging influencer-driven market manipulation without getting trapped in the vocabulary of “informational harm.” If the theory is that victims were induced to buy with false pretenses, the indictment can survive even if the victims received securities of some value or even if the defendant’s object was to enrich himself rather than to impose a net loss.

For traders and social-media personalities, the message is obvious: undisclosed self-dealing is not just a reputational risk; it can be a criminal one. The case also reinforces that online communities are not a fraud-free zone simply because the communication happens in posts, videos, or chats rather than in a traditional brokerage setting.

At the doctrinal level, the opinion helps settle an emerging post-*Ciminelli* and post-*Kousisis* equilibrium. *Ciminelli* narrowed fraud by rejecting the right-to-control theory, but *Kousisis* prevented that narrowing from swallowing fraudulent-inducement cases. *Constantinescu* is a clean example of how those two decisions fit together: no fraud liability for mere information deprivation, but full liability where deception is used to obtain money or property.

The case is only at the indictment stage, so the real fight is still ahead. On remand, the government will have to prove that the defendants’ posts were materially false, that the misrepresentations were part of a scheme, and that the trading was tied to the alleged pump-and-dump operation. But the Fifth Circuit has now made one thing clear: dressing a scheme in influencer language does not take it outside the federal fraud statutes.
