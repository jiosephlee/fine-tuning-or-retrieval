Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 1: # The Dispute, the Marks, and the TTAB Proceeding

## CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration: credit card services, 1998 registration, and 1996 priority date

This dispute begins with CC Serve’s federal registration of the word mark **ASPIRE** for **credit card services**. The registration issued in 1998, but its effective priority date was October 17, 1996. In trademark law, that earlier priority date matters because it establishes CC Serve as the prior user for purposes of comparing its rights against a later applicant. In this case, Apex’s applications were filed much later, so CC Serve’s registration served as the earlier mark that Apex had to avoid under the Lanham Act’s likelihood-of-confusion standard.

The opinion treats the ASPIRE registration as the anchor for the entire opposition. The mark is a standard character word mark, meaning the registration protects the word itself rather than a particular font, style, or logo treatment. That makes the scope of the registration important, because the analysis later asks whether Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks are too close to CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark in light of the services each side offers.

## How CC Serve’s business model works: partnering with banks that issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards while CC Serve services the accounts

CC Serve does not itself operate like a traditional card issuer in the ordinary consumer sense. According to the record, it partners with banks, and those banks issue **ASPIRE-branded credit cards** and related accounts to customers. CC Serve and its affiliates then service the accounts. That business model matters because the court later has to decide how to describe CC Serve’s services for purposes of the trademark analysis.

The TTAB and the Federal Circuit looked not only at the label “credit card services,” but also at what that phrase covers in practice. The Board understood CC Serve’s services to include activities associated with issuing and managing credit card accounts, such as processing transactions, handling account administration, and related consumer support. Even though CC Serve itself works through bank partners, the trademark question is still whether consumers would likely think Apex’s proposed banking marks and CC Serve’s ASPIRE-marked services come from the same source.

## Apex Bank’s existing retail banking business and its plan to launch an internet bank under ASPIRE BANK

Apex Bank is a Tennessee retail bank with 18 branch locations. Its current business includes personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Importantly, Apex does **not** offer credit cards. The case arises because Apex planned to expand into a different business line: an internet bank using the **ASPIRE BANK** word and design marks.

This distinction between Apex’s existing retail banking business and its proposed online banking brand is part of the background, but it does not control the trademark inquiry. For the TTAB, the key point was that Apex wanted to use a mark containing the word **ASPIRE** in connection with **banking and financing services**. That placed Apex’s proposed marks in the same broad commercial space as CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark, triggering the question whether consumers would likely assume a common source or affiliation.

## The two applications at issue: ASPIRE BANK word mark and design mark for "banking and financing services"

In August 2019, Apex filed **intent-to-use applications** to register two marks: the **ASPIRE BANK** word mark and the **ASPIRE BANK** design mark. Both applications identified the goods or services as **“banking and financing services.”** An intent-to-use application allows an applicant to seek registration before actual use, so long as the applicant has a good-faith intent to use the mark in commerce.

The choice of services is crucial. Trademark rights and registration review depend not just on the words in the mark, but on the goods or services listed in the application and registration. Here, Apex’s broad description of “banking and financing services” later became a central issue because the Board had to compare those services against CC Serve’s credit card services. The court’s later likelihood-of-confusion analysis turns in significant part on how similar those services are as a matter of trademark law.

## The letter of protest during examination and the examining attorney’s decision to approve the applications for publication

During USPTO examination, CC Serve filed a **letter of protest** arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to its ASPIRE registration. A letter of protest is a way for a third party to bring potentially relevant evidence to the attention of the examining attorney before the application is published. In this case, the protest did not stop the applications from moving forward.

The examining attorney nonetheless approved the applications for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. Publication is the stage at which a trademark application becomes visible to third parties and opens the door to formal opposition. The fact that the examining attorney approved publication despite CC Serve’s protest shows that the Office did not, at that stage, find the conflict so obvious as to bar publication outright. But publication did not end the dispute; it simply shifted it into the adversarial opposition process before the TTAB.

## CC Serve’s opposition under Lanham Act Section 2(d) based on likelihood of confusion with ASPIRE

After publication, CC Serve filed a formal **opposition** in February 2020. It opposed both of Apex’s applications on the ground that they were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE mark. The opposition was brought under **Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act**, the statutory provision that bars registration of a mark that is likely to cause confusion with a prior mark.

This is the core trademark-registration dispute. In an opposition, the question is not whether Apex is already infringing in a broad sense, but whether the USPTO should allow Apex to register the marks. The governing test is likelihood of confusion, which asks whether consumers encountering the junior mark are likely to believe the parties’ services come from the same source, are affiliated, or are otherwise connected. CC Serve’s theory was that ASPIRE BANK would be too close to ASPIRE, especially because both sides operate in financial services.

## The Board’s decision sustaining the opposition and refusing registration

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained the opposition and refused registration. The Board concluded that consumer confusion was likely under Section 2(d). That meant the Board agreed with CC Serve that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK word and design marks could not be registered because of the existing ASPIRE registration.

At this stage, the Board’s role was to evaluate the evidence through the familiar DuPont likelihood-of-confusion framework. Even in this introductory portion of the case, it is useful to remember that the Board was not deciding abstract trademark similarity in the abstract; it was applying the registration bar to a specific pair of marks and a specific set of services. Its final refusal set up the appeal to the Federal Circuit.

## The appeal to the Federal Circuit and the court’s jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)

Apex appealed the TTAB’s decision to the Federal Circuit, which has exclusive appellate jurisdiction over appeals from the TTAB under **28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)**. That jurisdictional provision is why this case came to the Federal Circuit rather than a regional circuit. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence, but the substance of that review comes later in the opinion.

For purposes of understanding the case at the outset, the key procedural point is that the dispute moved from trademark prosecution, to formal opposition, to appellate review. The opinion ultimately addresses whether the Board got the likelihood-of-confusion analysis right, but this opening segment supplies the essential background: CC Serve had the earlier ASPIRE registration; Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services; CC Serve objected; the Board agreed with CC Serve; and Apex sought reversal in the Federal Circuit.
