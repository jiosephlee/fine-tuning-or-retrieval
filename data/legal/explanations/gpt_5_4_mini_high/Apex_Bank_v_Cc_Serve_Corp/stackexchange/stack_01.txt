Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How can likelihood of confusion be a 'question of law' if the DuPont factors are treated as factual findings?
Question:
I'm confused by the standard of review section. The Federal Circuit says likelihood of confusion is a question of law, but that the individual DuPont factors are questions of fact reviewed for substantial evidence. In practice, what does that distinction do on appeal from the TTAB? Does the court independently re-weigh the factors once it accepts the Board's fact findings, or is it mostly limited to checking whether the Board applied the correct legal framework?
Answer:
The short answer is: **the Federal Circuit does not re-try the facts, but it does review the Board’s ultimate confusion analysis to make sure the Board applied the right legal rules to those facts.**

That is exactly the distinction the opinion draws:

- **Underlying DuPont factor findings = fact findings**, reviewed for **substantial evidence**.
- **Likelihood of confusion overall = legal conclusion**, reviewed **de novo**, but only *after* accepting the Board’s supported factual findings.

So in practice, the court’s role is **not** to freely re-weigh the evidence like a fresh factfinder. Instead, it asks:

1. Did the Board make supported findings on the relevant facts?
2. Did the Board apply the correct legal framework to those facts?
3. Do those facts, under the correct framework, support the legal conclusion of likely confusion?

### How Apex v. CC Serve shows this

The court says:

> “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.”

And also:

> “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence.”

That means the court treated the **individual factor findings** as factual. For example, on the **second factor** (similarity of the services), the court said substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that the services were highly similar, so it **affirmed** that finding.

But on the **sixth factor**, the court did **not** just say, “we would have weighed the evidence differently.” Instead, it held the Board’s analysis was **legally flawed** because the Board used too narrow a universe of similar marks after already finding the parties’ services were highly similar under factor two. The court’s point was that the Board used the wrong legal scope for the factor, so it **vacated and remanded** for reconsideration.

### So does the court re-weigh the factors?

**Not in the sense of substituting its own factual judgment.**  
The court generally will **not** say, “we think factor six should have counted more heavily for Apex” based on its own appraisal of the evidence.

But the court **will** intervene if:

- the Board misdefines a factor,
- ignores legally relevant evidence,
- applies a stricter or looser standard than the law permits, or
- draws the overall likelihood-of-confusion conclusion from an improper framework.

That is why the opinion remands for reconsideration of factors six and one rather than simply announcing the final confusion result itself.

### Bottom line

The distinction matters because:

- **Fact findings** get deference.
- **The legal conclusion** of likelihood of confusion does not.
- But the court usually reaches that legal conclusion **through** the Board’s fact findings, not by redoing the case from scratch.

So the Federal Circuit is **more than a mere framework checker**, but **less than a de novo factfinder**. It reviews the Board’s factual findings for support, then ensures those findings were legally evaluated under the correct DuPont analysis.
