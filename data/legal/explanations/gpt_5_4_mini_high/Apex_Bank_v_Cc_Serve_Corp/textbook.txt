Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Dispute, the Marks, and the TTAB Proceeding

## CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration: credit card services, 1998 registration, and 1996 priority date

This dispute begins with CC Serve’s federal registration of the word mark **ASPIRE** for **credit card services**. The registration issued in 1998, but its effective priority date was October 17, 1996. In trademark law, that earlier priority date matters because it establishes CC Serve as the prior user for purposes of comparing its rights against a later applicant. In this case, Apex’s applications were filed much later, so CC Serve’s registration served as the earlier mark that Apex had to avoid under the Lanham Act’s likelihood-of-confusion standard.

The opinion treats the ASPIRE registration as the anchor for the entire opposition. The mark is a standard character word mark, meaning the registration protects the word itself rather than a particular font, style, or logo treatment. That makes the scope of the registration important, because the analysis later asks whether Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks are too close to CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark in light of the services each side offers.

## How CC Serve’s business model works: partnering with banks that issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards while CC Serve services the accounts

CC Serve does not itself operate like a traditional card issuer in the ordinary consumer sense. According to the record, it partners with banks, and those banks issue **ASPIRE-branded credit cards** and related accounts to customers. CC Serve and its affiliates then service the accounts. That business model matters because the court later has to decide how to describe CC Serve’s services for purposes of the trademark analysis.

The TTAB and the Federal Circuit looked not only at the label “credit card services,” but also at what that phrase covers in practice. The Board understood CC Serve’s services to include activities associated with issuing and managing credit card accounts, such as processing transactions, handling account administration, and related consumer support. Even though CC Serve itself works through bank partners, the trademark question is still whether consumers would likely think Apex’s proposed banking marks and CC Serve’s ASPIRE-marked services come from the same source.

## Apex Bank’s existing retail banking business and its plan to launch an internet bank under ASPIRE BANK

Apex Bank is a Tennessee retail bank with 18 branch locations. Its current business includes personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Importantly, Apex does **not** offer credit cards. The case arises because Apex planned to expand into a different business line: an internet bank using the **ASPIRE BANK** word and design marks.

This distinction between Apex’s existing retail banking business and its proposed online banking brand is part of the background, but it does not control the trademark inquiry. For the TTAB, the key point was that Apex wanted to use a mark containing the word **ASPIRE** in connection with **banking and financing services**. That placed Apex’s proposed marks in the same broad commercial space as CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark, triggering the question whether consumers would likely assume a common source or affiliation.

## The two applications at issue: ASPIRE BANK word mark and design mark for "banking and financing services"

In August 2019, Apex filed **intent-to-use applications** to register two marks: the **ASPIRE BANK** word mark and the **ASPIRE BANK** design mark. Both applications identified the goods or services as **“banking and financing services.”** An intent-to-use application allows an applicant to seek registration before actual use, so long as the applicant has a good-faith intent to use the mark in commerce.

The choice of services is crucial. Trademark rights and registration review depend not just on the words in the mark, but on the goods or services listed in the application and registration. Here, Apex’s broad description of “banking and financing services” later became a central issue because the Board had to compare those services against CC Serve’s credit card services. The court’s later likelihood-of-confusion analysis turns in significant part on how similar those services are as a matter of trademark law.

## The letter of protest during examination and the examining attorney’s decision to approve the applications for publication

During USPTO examination, CC Serve filed a **letter of protest** arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to its ASPIRE registration. A letter of protest is a way for a third party to bring potentially relevant evidence to the attention of the examining attorney before the application is published. In this case, the protest did not stop the applications from moving forward.

The examining attorney nonetheless approved the applications for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. Publication is the stage at which a trademark application becomes visible to third parties and opens the door to formal opposition. The fact that the examining attorney approved publication despite CC Serve’s protest shows that the Office did not, at that stage, find the conflict so obvious as to bar publication outright. But publication did not end the dispute; it simply shifted it into the adversarial opposition process before the TTAB.

## CC Serve’s opposition under Lanham Act Section 2(d) based on likelihood of confusion with ASPIRE

After publication, CC Serve filed a formal **opposition** in February 2020. It opposed both of Apex’s applications on the ground that they were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE mark. The opposition was brought under **Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act**, the statutory provision that bars registration of a mark that is likely to cause confusion with a prior mark.

This is the core trademark-registration dispute. In an opposition, the question is not whether Apex is already infringing in a broad sense, but whether the USPTO should allow Apex to register the marks. The governing test is likelihood of confusion, which asks whether consumers encountering the junior mark are likely to believe the parties’ services come from the same source, are affiliated, or are otherwise connected. CC Serve’s theory was that ASPIRE BANK would be too close to ASPIRE, especially because both sides operate in financial services.

## The Board’s decision sustaining the opposition and refusing registration

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained the opposition and refused registration. The Board concluded that consumer confusion was likely under Section 2(d). That meant the Board agreed with CC Serve that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK word and design marks could not be registered because of the existing ASPIRE registration.

At this stage, the Board’s role was to evaluate the evidence through the familiar DuPont likelihood-of-confusion framework. Even in this introductory portion of the case, it is useful to remember that the Board was not deciding abstract trademark similarity in the abstract; it was applying the registration bar to a specific pair of marks and a specific set of services. Its final refusal set up the appeal to the Federal Circuit.

## The appeal to the Federal Circuit and the court’s jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)

Apex appealed the TTAB’s decision to the Federal Circuit, which has exclusive appellate jurisdiction over appeals from the TTAB under **28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)**. That jurisdictional provision is why this case came to the Federal Circuit rather than a regional circuit. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence, but the substance of that review comes later in the opinion.

For purposes of understanding the case at the outset, the key procedural point is that the dispute moved from trademark prosecution, to formal opposition, to appellate review. The opinion ultimately addresses whether the Board got the likelihood-of-confusion analysis right, but this opening segment supplies the essential background: CC Serve had the earlier ASPIRE registration; Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services; CC Serve objected; the Board agreed with CC Serve; and Apex sought reversal in the Federal Circuit.

# The Governing Legal Framework: Section 2(d), DuPont, and Appellate Review

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act and refusal of registration based on likely confusion with a prior mark

This case is an opposition proceeding under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, which bars registration when a new mark is likely to cause confusion with a prior user’s mark. The basic idea is straightforward: even if an applicant wants to register a mark, the PTO and the Board must refuse registration if consumers are likely to think the applicant’s goods or services come from the same source as, or are affiliated with, the earlier mark owner. That is the core issue here. CC Serve owned the prior registration for ASPIRE for credit card services, while Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained CC Serve’s opposition because it concluded that the marks, as used on the respective services, were likely to be confused.

For a law student reading the opinion, it is important to see that the dispute is not about whether Apex was actually using its mark in the marketplace at the time of the opposition. It was applying on an intent-to-use basis. The legal question instead was whether the applied-for marks, if registered for the identified services, would create a likelihood of confusion with the prior ASPIRE registration. That is the statutory question the Board had to answer and the Federal Circuit had to review.

## Likelihood of confusion as a legal conclusion built on underlying factual findings

The opinion emphasizes that “likelihood of confusion” is a legal conclusion, but one that rests on specific factual findings. The court cites its precedent for the idea that the ultimate confusion determination is a legal issue, while the underlying considerations—such as how similar the services are, how similar the marks are, and whether third-party use makes a mark weak—are factual findings. This distinction matters because it determines the standard of appellate review.

The practical consequence is that the Board does not merely announce whether confusion is likely in the abstract. It must make findings about the marketplace facts that inform that legal conclusion. In this case, those findings included the nature of CC Serve’s credit card services, the nature of Apex’s banking and financing services, the extent of third-party use of ASPIRE-formative marks, and the overall commercial impression of the marks. Once those facts are found, the Board applies the legal standard of likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit then asks whether the Board made legal errors and whether its factual findings are supported by substantial evidence.

## The DuPont framework: why the Board considers only those factors that are relevant and supported by the record

The DuPont factors are the familiar framework for evaluating likelihood of confusion. They are not rigid elements that must all be addressed in every case. Instead, the Board considers only the factors that are relevant and supported by the record. That flexibility is important because trademark disputes vary widely: some cases turn heavily on mark similarity, others on relatedness of the goods or services, still others on the strength or weakness of the earlier mark.

In this opinion, the Board and the court focus on three factors: factor two, factor six, and factor one. Factor two addresses the similarity of the parties’ services. Factor six concerns the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods or services, which speaks to the strength or weakness of the prior mark. Factor one concerns the similarity of the marks themselves in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. These three factors mattered most because the Board found the services highly similar, rejected Apex’s claim that third-party use weakened ASPIRE, and then used those conclusions to support its overall confusion finding.

The opinion also shows how the DuPont factors interact. A finding that the services are highly similar can affect what counts as a relevant field for third-party mark evidence under factor six. And factor six, in turn, can affect how strong or weak the earlier mark appears, which can influence factor one’s commercial impression analysis. So even though the factors are analytically distinct, they are not isolated from one another.

## The distinction between legal conclusions reviewed de novo and factual findings reviewed for substantial evidence

The Federal Circuit’s review framework is central to the opinion. Legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, meaning the court gives no deference to the Board’s legal interpretation. Factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, which is a deferential standard. Under that standard, the court asks whether the Board’s factual determinations are supported by enough relevant evidence that a reasonable mind could accept them.

This division explains why the court could affirm one part of the Board’s analysis while vacating others. The court did not reweigh the evidence from scratch. Instead, it asked whether the Board applied the correct legal standard and whether the record contained substantial evidence supporting the Board’s factual findings. For factor two, the court concluded that the Board correctly identified and compared the services and that substantial evidence supported the finding that they were highly similar. For factor six, however, the court concluded that the Board used an unduly narrow legal approach to the scope of relevant third-party marks. That was a legal error, not merely a different factual view of the record. Once a legal error affects how the Board framed the evidence, appellate deference does not save the result.

## The meaning of substantial evidence in administrative trademark appeals

“Substantial evidence” is a familiar but often misunderstood standard. It does not mean overwhelming evidence, and it does not require the appellate court to agree with the Board’s finding as the best possible conclusion. It means only enough evidence that a reasonable mind could accept the finding as adequately supported. The opinion quotes the classic definition and applies it to the Board’s findings on the parties’ services.

That standard is especially important in trademark appeals because the Board often makes detailed factual judgments about marketplace conditions, consumer perceptions, and the scope of third-party use. If those judgments are reasonably supported, the Federal Circuit will not disturb them simply because a different inference could also have been drawn. That is why Apex’s argument that CC Serve did not itself offer banking services was not enough to overturn factor two. The Board’s broader conclusion—that credit card services and banking/financing services overlap enough to create confusion risk—was supported by the record and therefore survived substantial-evidence review.

## Why the opinion focuses on three particular DuPont factors: factor two, factor six, and factor one

The opinion focuses on factors two, six, and one because those were the points of disagreement that mattered to the overall confusion analysis. Factor two was about whether the parties’ services were similar enough to matter. The Board found they were highly similar, and the court affirmed that determination. That finding set the stage for the rest of the analysis because the more similar the services, the more likely consumers are to assume common source.

Factor six was about whether ASPIRE was weak because similar “Aspire” marks were used by others in the market. Apex argued that the Board should have considered many more third-party uses beyond the handful it treated as relevant. The Federal Circuit agreed that the Board’s approach was too narrow because it limited the relevant universe to credit card services alone, even though the Board had already found the parties’ services highly similar. In the court’s view, the Board should not apply a stricter similarity screen under factor six than it used under factor two.

Factor one then had to be revisited because the overall commercial impression of a mark can depend on whether the mark appears strong or weak in the marketplace. If factor six is reconsidered and the strength of ASPIRE changes, that can affect how the marks compare in their entireties. So the court vacated factor one as well, not because the Board independently committed the same kind of error there, but because the reassessment of factor six could alter the context in which factor one is evaluated.

## How the court’s review method shapes its treatment of affirmance versus vacatur and remand

The court’s disposition—affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded—follows directly from the review framework. The Board’s factor-two analysis was legally sound and supported by substantial evidence, so the court affirmed it. The Board’s factor-six analysis, however, used an incorrect legal scope for the relevant third-party marks, so the court vacated that part and sent it back for reconsideration. Because factor one depends in part on the overall commercial strength and commercial impression of the mark, the court also vacated that analysis and remanded it.

This is a good example of how appellate courts use vacatur and remand when a legal mistake may have affected the Board’s weighing of the evidence. The Federal Circuit did not itself decide the full likelihood-of-confusion issue in the first instance. Instead, it preserved the Board’s valid findings, corrected the legal error, and required the Board to redo the affected parts of the analysis under the proper framework. The disposition thus reflects the court’s limited role on appeal: it ensures the Board applied the right legal standard, but it leaves fact-intensive trademark judgments to the agency in the first instance when those judgments need to be reconsidered.

# DuPont Factor Two: Similarity of the Services

## The Legal Standard for Related Goods and Services

The second DuPont factor asks how similar the parties’ goods or services are. The key point is that they do not have to be identical. The question is whether the services are related enough that consumers could mistakenly believe they come from the same source. In trademark law, that kind of relatedness can exist even when the parties do not directly compete. Here, the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board’s similarity finding for substantial evidence, which means the court asked not whether it would have reached the same conclusion itself, but whether the record contained enough evidence for a reasonable factfinder to reach the Board’s result.

## How the Board Defined “Credit Card Services”

A threshold issue was how to understand CC Serve’s registration for “credit card services.” That phrase had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration issued, so the Board had to interpret the scope of the term from the record rather than simply relying on a current manual entry. The Board construed “credit card services” broadly. It included not just issuing cards, but also counseling on credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorizing cards, monitoring and alerting customers about card activity, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.

That broad construction mattered because the services comparison depends on what each party’s registration or application actually covers. Once the Board defined CC Serve’s services in that way, it could compare them to Apex’s identified services for “banking and financing services.”

## Comparing Apex’s and CC Serve’s Services

The Board then turned to Apex’s identification of services and used dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” to understand their scope. Those definitions were broad enough, in the Board’s view, to include extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. That led the Board to conclude that Apex’s services and CC Serve’s services overlapped in part at the legal level.

The phrase “legally identical, in part” is important. It does not mean that the two businesses are the same in every respect. It means that some of the services covered by Apex’s application fall within the same commercial category as services covered by CC Serve’s registration. Even partial overlap can be powerful in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, because consumers do not parse service descriptions with lawyerly precision. If the services are partly the same and otherwise very close, that supports a finding of relatedness.

## Why the Board Found High Similarity

The Board did not stop with the text of the identifications. It also looked to third-party registrations that covered both credit card services and banking or financing services. That evidence matters because it suggests that the market can view those kinds of services as coming from one source under one mark. In other words, if other registrations already group those services together, it is reasonable to think consumers may expect a single company to offer both.

On that basis, the Board found that the services were highly similar and that the second DuPont factor weighed heavily in favor of likelihood of confusion. The Board’s reasoning was not that the services were identical in every practical sense, but that they were close enough—given their definitions and the marketplace evidence—to create a real risk that consumers would assume a common source.

## Apex’s Argument and the Federal Circuit’s Affirmance

Apex argued on appeal that the services were not the same and were not directly competitive, especially because CC Serve worked with banks to manage credit card programs rather than itself offering banking services. The Federal Circuit rejected that argument. The court emphasized that the Board had carefully considered the service descriptions and that substantial evidence supported the Board’s conclusion that the parties’ services were highly similar.

The court therefore affirmed the Board’s ruling on the second DuPont factor. For the law student reading the opinion, the main lesson is that service similarity under DuPont factor two is a broad, practical inquiry. It is enough that the services are closely related and could reasonably be thought to come from the same source, even if one party frames its business model differently or the services are not direct competitors.

# DuPont Factor Six: Third-Party Uses, Weakness, and the Board’s Error

## The role of factor six: number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services

In a Section 2(d) opposition, the DuPont factors help the Board decide whether consumers are likely to confuse the applicant’s mark with a prior user’s mark. Factor six asks about the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” That matters because if many different businesses are already using similar marks in the same area, consumers may be less likely to assume that one mark points to a single source. In that sense, factor six is really about the strength of the prior mark in the marketplace: the more crowded the field, the narrower the protection the mark may receive.

## Why third-party use matters to mark strength

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly explained that evidence of third-party use can show a mark is relatively weak. The basic idea is consumer conditioning. If customers see similar marks used by multiple sources, they learn to distinguish among them, and similarity alone becomes less likely to cause confusion. That is why a “crowded” field can cut against a claim of likely confusion. But the evidence must be relevant to the actual universe of goods and services at issue. The point is not to count every possible use of a shared word in the abstract; it is to gauge how crowded the field is for the goods and services the parties actually provide.

## Apex’s evidence of third-party uses of ASPIRE

Apex submitted multiple exhibits showing third-party uses of ASPIRE or ASPIRE-formative marks. Some of those uses appeared in connection with credit card-related services, and others appeared more broadly in financial services. Apex’s argument was that this collection of third-party use showed that ASPIRE was not uniquely associated with CC Serve and therefore deserved only a narrow scope of protection. In other words, Apex tried to use the third-party evidence to show that CC Serve’s mark was commercially or conceptually weak.

## The Board’s definition of the relevant public and its exclusion of broader financial services marks

The Board began by defining the relevant public as consumers of “credit card services,” because it viewed that as the overlap between the two parties’ offerings. From there, it limited its factor-six analysis to third-party marks tied to credit card services. It treated marks using ASPIRE in other banking or financing contexts as essentially irrelevant. On that approach, the Board found only nine relevant ASPIRE-formative uses. It concluded that nine uses were not enough to show the kind of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” third-party use that would demonstrate weakness under cases like Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin.

## The Board’s bottom-line view of CC Serve’s mark

Having found that the number of relevant third-party uses was not large enough to establish weakness, the Board said CC Serve had not shown any particular commercial strength, but Apex also had not shown that the ASPIRE mark was commercially or conceptually weak. The Board therefore treated ASPIRE as entitled to the normal scope of protection given to an inherently distinctive mark. That conclusion mattered because a stronger mark is more likely to support a finding of confusion when another similar mark is used on related services.

## The Federal Circuit’s critique: factor six requires similar, not identical, goods or services

The Federal Circuit agreed that factor six turns on the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods or services, but it held that the Board’s analysis was too narrow. The key problem was that the Board imported a stricter similarity test into factor six than the law requires. The court emphasized that factor six does not require identical goods; it requires similar goods or services. That principle was already reflected in Federal Circuit precedent such as Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation.

## Why consistency mattered across the DuPont analysis

The court’s main criticism was one of internal consistency. Under factor two, the Board had already found that the parties’ services were highly similar, and even partially legally identical. Once the Board made that finding, it could not then shrink the universe of relevant third-party marks for factor six by acting as though only credit card services counted and broader banking and financing services did not. The Federal Circuit saw no reason to apply a more demanding similarity standard in factor six than the Board had applied in factor two. Because the services were already found to be highly similar, the Board needed to consider third-party ASPIRE uses within that broader similar-services context.

## The precedents the court used

The Federal Circuit relied on Olde Tyme Foods for the proposition that factor six reaches similar, not identical, goods. It also cited Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin for the broader point that a meaningful number of third-party uses can show weakness in a crowded field. Those cases did not require the court to decide how many uses are enough in the abstract; they supported the more basic proposition that the Board had used the wrong scope when deciding which third-party marks to count.

## The remand instruction

The court did not decide for itself whether ASPIRE is weak or strong. Instead, it vacated the Board’s factor-six finding and remanded for reconsideration. On remand, the Board must decide which third-party ASPIRE marks should be considered in light of its own earlier finding that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are highly similar. That revised factor-six analysis may then affect the Board’s view of the mark’s strength, the related first-factor analysis, and ultimately the overall likelihood-of-confusion determination.

# DuPont Factor One, the Interdependence of the Analysis, and the Final Disposition

## The setting: a likelihood-of-confusion appeal with a partial win for Apex

This appeal arose from a TTAB opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. CC Serve owned the prior ASPIRE registration for credit card services, while Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s treatment of the parties’ services under DuPont factor two, but it concluded that the Board had used too narrow a universe of third-party uses when analyzing factor six. That error mattered because factor six bears on whether the shared term ASPIRE is strong or weak in the marketplace, and that in turn affected the Board’s factor-one analysis.

The important lesson is that a TTAB likelihood-of-confusion analysis is not a set of isolated boxes. Even when one factor is analytically distinct from another, the way the Board frames the record under one factor can influence how the same evidence should be understood under the others. This opinion is a good example of that point.

## Factor one: comparing the marks as a whole

DuPont factor one asks about the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” That phrase matters. The Board is not supposed to compare only one word in the abstract or focus on a single feature of a composite mark. Instead, it must ask what the marks look like, sound like, mean, and, most importantly, what overall impression they leave on consumers.

Here, the marks were CC Serve’s ASPIRE and Apex’s ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. The opinion does not revisit the Board’s factor-one reasoning in detail, but the court’s remand makes clear that the Board must assess the entire commercial impression of Apex’s marks against ASPIRE after correcting the error in factor six. That means the Board must think about the added “BANK” element and any design features, but also about how the shared term ASPIRE functions in the relevant marketplace.

## Why commercial impression cannot be separated from evidence of strength or weakness

The court’s remand of factor one depends on a practical point about trademark perception: commercial impression is not formed in a vacuum. A mark can look and sound similar to another mark, but the way consumers perceive that similarity may depend on how distinctive the shared element is in the field. If a term is used by many third parties on similar goods or services, consumers may be accustomed to seeing it and may not assume that every use comes from the same source.

That is why the court linked factor one to factor six. Factor six concerns the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. Evidence under that factor can show that a mark is relatively weak and therefore entitled to a narrower scope of protection. If the Board undervalues or excludes that evidence, it may also overstate the commercial impression created by the disputed mark. In this case, the Federal Circuit thought that possibility was real enough to require vacatur and remand.

## Why the Board’s factor-six error could affect factor one

The Board had limited its factor-six analysis to third-party “Aspire” uses tied to credit card services, on the theory that the relevant public consisted of consumers of credit card services. The Federal Circuit held that this was too narrow because, in its factor-two analysis, the Board had already found the parties’ services highly similar and even partially legally identical. Once the Board recognized that Apex’s banking and financing services overlapped substantially with CC Serve’s credit card services, it could not confine the factor-six universe to credit card services alone and ignore Aspire-formative marks used in the broader financial-services space.

That mattered for factor one because the strength or weakness of ASPIRE affects the overall commercial impression analysis. If third-party use shows that ASPIRE is a crowded or diluted term in the relevant field, then the shared word may leave a different impression on consumers than the Board assumed. The Federal Circuit therefore did not decide factor one on the existing record. Instead, it held that the factor-one analysis had to be revisited after the Board reconsiders factor six under the correct scope of similar goods and services.

This is an important procedural move. The court was not saying that factor one necessarily fails or that Apex must win on remand. It was saying that the Board’s legal error in factor six may have tainted the way it understood commercial impression under factor one, so the Board itself must reassess the issue.

## Why the court remanded instead of resolving factor one itself

The Federal Circuit reviews legal conclusions de novo but factual findings for substantial evidence. Here, it affirmed the Board’s factor-two findings because substantial evidence supported the conclusion that the parties’ services were highly similar. But the court could not simply “fix” factor one from the appellate bench, because the correct factor-one analysis depended on the Board first reconsidering the evidence of third-party use under factor six.

That choice reflects the court’s usual role in trademark appeals. The Federal Circuit will correct a legal mistake, but if the mistake affects how the Board should weigh or frame the evidence in the first instance, the better course is remand. That allows the TTAB to reevaluate the record under the right legal standard and then determine how much weight the renewed factor-six analysis should have in the overall likelihood-of-confusion assessment.

## The remaining arguments, the disposition, and costs

The court briefly noted that it had considered the parties’ remaining arguments and found them unpersuasive. It did not discuss them further, which signals that nothing else in the appeal required reversal or changed the analysis of the challenged factors.

The final disposition was mixed: the court affirmed the Board’s analysis of factor two, vacated the Board’s findings as to factors six and one, and remanded for further proceedings. In practical terms, the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar stands, but the Board must revisit the strength/weakness evidence and then reconsider the marks’ overall commercial impression in light of that corrected analysis. The court also awarded costs to Apex as the appellant.

## The broader lesson for reading TTAB appeals

This opinion teaches a useful methodological lesson. In a DuPont analysis, the Board may make factual findings that are individually supported, but it still must use a consistent legal frame across the factors. If the Board defines the relevant marketplace too narrowly for one factor, that mistake can ripple into other factors that depend on the same marketplace evidence. For a law student, the key point is not just that the Federal Circuit affirmed some findings and vacated others, but why: the court was policing the Board’s method, not merely its conclusions.

In other words, the opinion shows how appellate review in trademark cases works in practice. A factual finding can survive substantial-evidence review, yet a legal misstep in how the evidence is organized or limited can still require remand. That is exactly what happened here.
