Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# One Marketplace, One Standard: The Federal Circuit Tells the TTAB to Keep Its DuPont Factors Aligned

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* matters less for what it says about “ASPIRE” than for what it says about how the TTAB must do trademark law. The court did not disturb the Board’s core finding that Apex’s banking and financing services are highly similar to CC Serve’s credit card services. But it did reject the Board’s attempt to turn around and cabin the sixth DuPont factor to a much narrower universe of third-party uses, limited essentially to credit-card-only marks. That mismatch, the court held, was a legal error.

The practical significance is real. Trademark oppositions often turn on how broadly or narrowly the Board frames the relevant market. If an applicant can point to a crowded field of similar marks in adjacent financial services, that evidence may now have more force when the services are closely related. In other words, once the Board says the goods or services are highly similar, it cannot then pretend that only the most exact sub-niche matters when assessing third-party use.

## The Federal Circuit’s doctrinal move

The opinion is straightforward on the second DuPont factor: the Board had substantial evidence to find that the parties’ services are highly similar. CC Serve’s services involve credit card accounts and related services; Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board reasonably read the relevant service descriptions broadly enough to conclude that the parties overlap in a meaningful way, and the Federal Circuit left that finding intact.

The real issue was the sixth factor, which asks about “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” Apex offered third-party “Aspire” and “Aspire-formative” marks, not only for credit-card services but more broadly in the financial-services space. The Board largely disregarded the broader set, reasoning that the relevant public was consumers of credit-card services and that marks outside that narrow category were “essentially irrelevant.” It then found only nine relevant third-party uses and concluded that this was not enough to show weakness.

The Federal Circuit thought that was too cramped. Its key point is doctrinally modest but important: similarity should mean the same thing across the DuPont analysis when the Board has already found the services highly similar. The court cited precedent making clear that the sixth factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. So if the Board has already found a high degree of similarity—or even partial legal identity—it should not sharply narrow the evidence set when it turns to third-party use. That inconsistency, the court held, is legal error. The court therefore vacated the sixth-factor finding and remanded for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third-party marks.

The first factor fell with the sixth, at least for now. The Federal Circuit did not hold that the Board got the marks’ appearance, sound, or connotation wrong on the merits. Instead, it reasoned that commercial strength or weakness influences commercial impression, and because the Board may reach a different weakness determination on remand, the first factor also needs another look. That is a fairly cautious move, but a sensible one: in trademark cases, these factors are not hermetically sealed compartments.

## What the court did not do

The court did not announce a new rule that every third-party financial-services mark is automatically relevant whenever one side offers banking services and the other offers credit cards. Nor did it say the Board must count Apex’s proffered 42 additional “Aspire” marks. The remand leaves room for the TTAB to decide exactly how broad the relevant market should be, so long as the analysis is consistent with its own similarity finding.

That distinction matters. The opinion is not about inflating third-party evidence for its own sake. It is about methodological coherence. The TTAB cannot, in one breath, say the services are highly similar and then, in the next, define the relevant field so narrowly that only nearly identical services count for weakness purposes. Trademark law has always been sensitive to market context, and the Federal Circuit is reminding the Board that context cuts both ways.

## Broader implications

This decision should help applicants in crowded or semi-crowded service markets, especially financial services, where product lines overlap and branding often stretches across adjacent offerings. Third-party-use evidence has long been a route to showing that a mark is weak and deserves a narrower scope of protection. *Apex Bank* makes that route more usable when the Board has already found the underlying services to be closely related.

The downstream effect may be a more disciplined TTAB record. Oppositions and appeals will likely feature more careful arguments about the breadth of the relevant market at each DuPont factor, and the Board will have to explain why the same similarity finding does or does not carry over from factor to factor. That should reduce the temptation to slice the market too finely when doing so would preserve a confusion finding.

At the same time, the opinion should not be overstated. The Federal Circuit did not weaken *DuPont*; it enforced it. The case reinforces a simple but consequential principle: likelihood-of-confusion analysis must be internally consistent. If the services are closely related enough to support a strong confusion case under the second factor, the Board cannot then shrink the relevant evidence universe under the sixth factor in a way that ignores that very finding. In a market as interconnected as banking and credit services, that is a meaningful doctrinal check.



# Old Identifications, New Industries: Why “Credit Card Services” Carried So Much Weight in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*

## Why this case matters

*APEX Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is a reminder that trademark disputes in financial services often turn less on branding flourish than on the old words buried in a registration. The Federal Circuit’s bottom-line ruling was modest: it affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s finding that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were highly similar, in services, to CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration. But the opinion matters because it shows how a legacy service identification—here, “credit card services,” a phrase no longer in the Trademark ID Manual—can still drive the likelihood-of-confusion analysis in a modern fintech setting.

That has real consequences. Financial services are increasingly modular: banks issue cards, processors service accounts, platform partners handle customer interfaces, and financing functions are split across multiple entities. A registration from the 1990s can therefore cast a long shadow over a market that looks very different today. The court’s opinion is a good illustration of how trademark doctrine, especially the DuPont factors, can keep up with that evolution without pretending that service categories have stayed frozen in time.

## The court’s treatment of “credit card services”

The pivotal issue on the second DuPont factor was whether Apex’s “banking and financing services” were sufficiently similar to CC Serve’s “credit card services.” The Board answered yes, and the Federal Circuit upheld that conclusion under substantial-evidence review.

What makes the analysis interesting is the way the Board defined the older term. Because “credit card services” had been deleted from the ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration issued, the Board did not simply take the phrase at face value as a narrow label for one discrete offering. Instead, it construed the term to include issuing credit cards for purchases, managing card accounts, processing payments and transactions, monitoring cards, handling alerts, counseling on credit card debt, and providing access to credit scores. Once the Board adopted that reading, the overlap with Apex’s banking and financing services became obvious. The Board concluded the services were “legally identical, in part,” and the Federal Circuit had little trouble saying that finding was supported by substantial evidence.

That is the opinion’s most important doctrinal move. The court did not hold that “banking” and “credit card services” are always the same thing. Nor did it say that a bank that does not itself issue credit cards is automatically offering identical services to a credit card company’s affiliate model. Rather, it accepted the Board’s practical point: in trademark law, the scope of a service identification is not limited to the narrowest imaginable business model, but to the ordinary meaning of the words in the registration as understood in the marketplace. “Credit card services,” even if now outdated as a catalog term, still describes a cluster of financial functions that sit very close to “banking and financing services.”

Apex’s argument that CC Serve was only partnering with banks, not itself “providing banking services,” did not carry the day because the inquiry is not whether the businesses are structured identically. It is whether consumers could reasonably think the services come from the same source. In the court’s view, the Board reasonably found they could.

## Why the sixth-factor error mattered so much

The more interesting part of the opinion may be what the court did with the sixth DuPont factor, which looks at the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods or services. Apex had submitted third-party Aspire-formative uses, but the Board limited its comparison to marks used for credit card services alone and treated other financial-services uses as irrelevant. The Federal Circuit said that was too narrow.

This is where the court’s reasoning becomes especially important for fintech trademark disputes. The panel tied the sixth factor back to the second factor: if the Board has already found the parties’ services highly similar—indeed, partly legally identical—it should not then define the relevant field for third-party uses more grudgingly than that finding allows. In other words, similarity should mean something consistent across the DuPont analysis. The Board could not say, on the one hand, that credit card services and banking/financing services are highly similar, and then, on the other hand, shrink the third-party-use universe to only the smallest slice of credit-card-specific evidence.

That does not mean Apex wins the weakness argument. The court did not hold that the 42 other Aspire-formative marks Apex identified must count, or that the record necessarily proves weakness. It held only that the Board’s legal framework was too restrictive and needed to be reconsidered. But the remand is still meaningful. If the relevant market is broader than the Board assumed, then Apex’s evidence of third-party use may look more substantial, and CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark may look less commercially strong than the Board found.

The court’s vacatur of the first factor follows naturally. Commercial impression is affected by the strength of the mark, and strength in turn depends on how crowded the field is. So once the sixth factor is reopened, the first factor has to be revisited too.

## The broader lesson for older marks in modern finance

The opinion’s practical message is that legacy trademark registrations can remain powerful even when the industry around them has changed. That is especially true in financial services, where older service descriptions often predate the current division of labor among banks, processors, servicers, and fintech platforms. A phrase like “credit card services” may sound dated, but in trademark law dated does not mean narrow.

For applicants, the lesson is to draft identifications carefully and assume that old registrations may be read expansively if their wording naturally reaches adjacent functions. For challengers, the case shows that old, flexible service terms can be valuable assets in opposition proceedings, even if the exact terminology has fallen out of administrative fashion. And for the Board, the case is a reminder that the DuPont factors are supposed to work together. If one factor finds a broad functional overlap, the others cannot be analyzed as though the services live in separate worlds.

That is why *Apex Bank* matters beyond this single ASPIRE fight. It is a good snapshot of how trademark law handles an increasingly fragmented financial-services ecosystem: by letting old words do surprisingly modern work.



# Strength Before Similarity: Why Third-Party ‘Aspire’ Uses May Change the Mark-Comparison Analysis

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* is a good reminder that trademark confusion analysis is not supposed to be conducted in silos. The Board got the core “services” question right: banking and financing services are highly similar to credit card services, so Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks face a serious hurdle. But the court vacated two key parts of the Board’s analysis because the Board treated the scope of third-party “Aspire” evidence too narrowly. That matters well beyond this dispute, especially in crowded financial-services markets where many firms use aspirational-sounding brand language and where the strength of a mark often depends on how much that language has been diluted by others’ use.

## The Board was too narrow on third-party uses

The opinion’s cleanest holding is on DuPont factor six, which asks about “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” The Board looked only at third-party “Aspire” marks used for credit card services, because it had framed the relevant public that way. The Federal Circuit said that was legally too cramped. Once the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar — indeed, partially legally identical — there was no reason to slice the factor-six universe more narrowly than the factor-two universe.

That point is subtle but important. The court did not say that any third-party use of “Aspire” in the abstract is relevant. It did say that the sixth factor is about similar marks on similar services, not identical services, and that the Board had already made a broad similarity finding as to the services themselves. In other words, if the services are closely related enough to count as highly similar under factor two, the Board cannot then pretend that only a smaller, artificially narrowed subset of the marketplace counts when assessing whether “Aspire” is a weak term in the financial-services field.

That reasoning fits comfortably with *Juice Generation* and *Jack Wolfskin*, where the Federal Circuit made clear that a crowded field of similar marks can narrow the scope of protection a mark receives. It also reflects a practical trademark truth: if consumers are used to seeing “Aspire” on a range of financial products and services, they are less likely to assume that every new “Aspire” brand comes from the same source.

## Why factor one had to go back too

The more interesting move is the court’s decision to vacate the first DuPont factor as well. Factor one looks at similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. On the surface, that factor is about comparison — ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK. But the Federal Circuit recognized that commercial impression is not formed in a vacuum. It is influenced by the mark’s commercial strength, and commercial strength is precisely what factor six can illuminate.

That is why the remand on factor six necessarily spilled over into factor one. If the Board, on remand, concludes that “Aspire” is more diluted in the financial-services space than it thought, then the scope of protection for CC Serve’s mark may shrink. And if the scope of protection shrinks, the overall commercial impression of ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK may shift too. A weaker mark generally receives a narrower field of protection; that can change whether a new composite mark like ASPIRE BANK seems like a close variant or just another use of a common term in a crowded market.

The court was careful not to say that the marks are nonconfusing or that factor one necessarily favors Apex. It simply held that the Board’s factor-one analysis could not stand once factor six had to be reconsidered. That is analytically sensible. Trademark strength is not some separate afterthought; it affects how much similarity matters. A distinctive, commercially strong mark can make a modestly similar junior mark look much more dangerous. A diluted mark may not.

## The opinion’s broader doctrinal lesson

The most useful doctrinal takeaway is that the DuPont factors are analytically distinct but not hermetically sealed off from each other. The Board had effectively treated similarity as a one-way ratchet: because the services were similar, factor two mattered a lot, but when it came to factor six, the universe of relevant third-party evidence got narrowed. The Federal Circuit rejected that asymmetry. Once a tribunal has found that the services are highly similar, it should not apply a stricter similarity lens when deciding whether third-party uses show that the cited mark is weak.

That is an important signal for the TTAB and for litigants. Opponents will likely push harder to introduce third-party use evidence across a broader swath of related services, not just the exact niche occupied by the prior registrant. Applicants, in turn, will have more room to argue that a senior mark’s scope is limited by a broader crowded field, especially in industries like banking, payments, lending, and consumer finance where brand families often overlap in theme and vocabulary.

Still, the decision is not a license to flood the record with every “Aspire” registration in the federal register. The evidence still has to be similar enough to matter. The court’s point is narrower and more useful: where the Board has already found a close relationship between the parties’ services, it should not artificially shrink the marketplace when assessing third-party use and mark strength.

## What happens next

On remand, the Board will have to decide how many “Aspire” uses in the broader financial-services space belong in the factor-six analysis and what that means for the commercial strength of ASPIRE. That, in turn, may alter the first-factor comparison between ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK. But the court left intact the Board’s core finding that the services are highly similar, which means Apex still faces a meaningful obstacle.

The decision is thus less about the outcome than about method. The Federal Circuit is telling the Board to treat strength and similarity as connected questions, not compartmentalized ones. In trademark law, that is often the right instinct: what a mark means depends in part on how crowded the field is, and how crowded the field is depends in part on how broadly the relevant services are defined.



## Trademark Law for a Modular Financial Industry: *Apex Bank* and the Problem of Who the Consumer Thinks Is the Source

### Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is a useful reminder that trademark law does not ask who actually performs each back-end function in a modern financial product. It asks what consumers are likely to think about source.

That matters a lot in the credit-card and banking world, where the product on the front end is often assembled by multiple actors: a bank issues the card, a servicer handles the account, a brand owner licenses its mark, and the consumer may never know — or care — which entity sits behind the curtain. In that setting, “source confusion” can be very real even when the parties are not direct competitors in the ordinary sense.

The Federal Circuit did not rewrite trademark doctrine. But it did make an important point about how that doctrine should work in a modular financial-services market: if the Board finds the parties’ services are highly similar, it cannot then artificially narrow the marketplace when it assesses whether similar third-party marks make the senior mark weak.

### What the court held

The core of the case is straightforward. CC Serve owns the registered mark ASPIRE for credit card services. Apex Bank sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained CC Serve’s opposition under Section 2(d), finding a likelihood of confusion.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor, which concerns the similarity of the parties’ services. That part of the opinion is notable because Apex argued that it does not offer credit cards and CC Serve does not itself offer banking services. The court was not persuaded. The Board had carefully parsed the services, looked to the definitions of banking and finance, and reasonably concluded that the services are highly similar and in part legally identical.

The problem came with the sixth DuPont factor, which asks about the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. Apex had offered third-party uses of “Aspire” and “Aspire”-formative marks across financial services. But the Board confined its analysis to marks used for credit card services alone and treated marks used in other banking or financing contexts as essentially irrelevant. The Federal Circuit said that was too cramped. Because the Board had already found the services highly similar for purposes of the second factor, it should not have adopted a narrower conception of similarity for factor six. The court vacated that portion of the decision and remanded for reconsideration.

The court also vacated the Board’s analysis of the first DuPont factor, which addresses the similarity of the marks themselves in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The reason was not that the court independently found the marks dissimilar, but that a fresh look at the sixth factor could affect the assessment of the mark’s strength and, in turn, the overall commercial impression.

### The doctrinal point: consistency across DuPont factors

The most interesting part of the opinion is its insistence on consistency. The Board had effectively used one definition of similarity for the second factor and a different, narrower one for the sixth. The Federal Circuit rejected that approach, saying there was no reason to impose a “different and more stringent legal standard” when assessing similar marks in use on similar goods.

That is a sensible move. DuPont analysis is supposed to be contextual, but it is not supposed to be internally contradictory. If two services are close enough to be highly similar for purposes of confusion, then the universe of marketplace evidence relevant to weakness should not be artificially shrunk to a sliver of that market. Otherwise, the analysis risks becoming self-defeating: the Board says the services are similar enough to generate confusion, then ignores evidence of how crowded the broader field actually is.

At the same time, the court did not say that every financial-services mark using “Aspire” counts. It did not hold that the mark is weak, or that confusion is unlikely. It simply required the Board to reconsider the evidence under a more coherent view of the relevant market. That restraint is important. The decision keeps the doctrinal focus where it belongs: on consumer perception, not on formal product categories that may not map well onto how financial products are actually sold.

### Why the decision may ripple beyond this case

The downstream effect is likely to be felt most strongly in financial-services branding disputes, especially in the increasingly common world of white-label cards, bank partnerships, and embedded finance. These products are often sold under one brand but powered by another entity or a network of entities. Consumers encounter the brand, not the corporate structure.

That reality makes source confusion plausible even where the parties do not directly compete in a conventional sense. It also means that third-party-use evidence can be especially important: if consumers are already exposed to many similar marks across adjacent financial services, a new entrant may have a harder time claiming exclusive territory in a shared word like “Aspire.”

The opinion also suggests a practical lesson for TTAB litigation: when the Board defines services broadly enough to find close similarity, it should expect to consider third-party use broadly enough to match. That does not eliminate the Board’s discretion. But it does mean that service definitions cannot be expanded for one DuPont factor and contracted for another.

So *Apex Bank* is less a dramatic trademark decision than a market-sensitive one. It recognizes that the modern financial-services consumer is often seeing the label, not the plumbing. And in trademark law, that often makes all the difference.
