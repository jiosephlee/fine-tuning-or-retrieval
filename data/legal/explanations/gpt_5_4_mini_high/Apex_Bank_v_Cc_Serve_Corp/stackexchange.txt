Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How can likelihood of confusion be a 'question of law' if the DuPont factors are treated as factual findings?
Question:
I'm confused by the standard of review section. The Federal Circuit says likelihood of confusion is a question of law, but that the individual DuPont factors are questions of fact reviewed for substantial evidence. In practice, what does that distinction do on appeal from the TTAB? Does the court independently re-weigh the factors once it accepts the Board's fact findings, or is it mostly limited to checking whether the Board applied the correct legal framework?
Answer:
The short answer is: **the Federal Circuit does not re-try the facts, but it does review the Board’s ultimate confusion analysis to make sure the Board applied the right legal rules to those facts.**

That is exactly the distinction the opinion draws:

- **Underlying DuPont factor findings = fact findings**, reviewed for **substantial evidence**.
- **Likelihood of confusion overall = legal conclusion**, reviewed **de novo**, but only *after* accepting the Board’s supported factual findings.

So in practice, the court’s role is **not** to freely re-weigh the evidence like a fresh factfinder. Instead, it asks:

1. Did the Board make supported findings on the relevant facts?
2. Did the Board apply the correct legal framework to those facts?
3. Do those facts, under the correct framework, support the legal conclusion of likely confusion?

### How Apex v. CC Serve shows this

The court says:

> “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.”

And also:

> “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence.”

That means the court treated the **individual factor findings** as factual. For example, on the **second factor** (similarity of the services), the court said substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that the services were highly similar, so it **affirmed** that finding.

But on the **sixth factor**, the court did **not** just say, “we would have weighed the evidence differently.” Instead, it held the Board’s analysis was **legally flawed** because the Board used too narrow a universe of similar marks after already finding the parties’ services were highly similar under factor two. The court’s point was that the Board used the wrong legal scope for the factor, so it **vacated and remanded** for reconsideration.

### So does the court re-weigh the factors?

**Not in the sense of substituting its own factual judgment.**  
The court generally will **not** say, “we think factor six should have counted more heavily for Apex” based on its own appraisal of the evidence.

But the court **will** intervene if:

- the Board misdefines a factor,
- ignores legally relevant evidence,
- applies a stricter or looser standard than the law permits, or
- draws the overall likelihood-of-confusion conclusion from an improper framework.

That is why the opinion remands for reconsideration of factors six and one rather than simply announcing the final confusion result itself.

### Bottom line

The distinction matters because:

- **Fact findings** get deference.
- **The legal conclusion** of likelihood of confusion does not.
- But the court usually reaches that legal conclusion **through** the Board’s fact findings, not by redoing the case from scratch.

So the Federal Circuit is **more than a mere framework checker**, but **less than a de novo factfinder**. It reviews the Board’s factual findings for support, then ensures those findings were legally evaluated under the correct DuPont analysis.

### What exactly happens procedurally when an intent-to-use application is opposed after publication?
Question:
Apex filed intent-to-use applications, CC Serve filed a letter of protest during prosecution, the examining attorney still approved the marks for publication, and then CC Serve brought an opposition before the TTAB. I'm not sure I understand the procedural significance of each step. If the examining attorney already allowed publication despite the protest, why doesn't that settle the issue? Is an opposition basically a separate adversarial proceeding where the TTAB can reach a different result based on a fuller record?
Answer:
Yes — the opinion describes the ordinary sequence, and the key point is that **publication is not a final merits determination**.

### Procedural significance of each step

1. **Intent-to-use application filed**
   - Apex filed ITU applications for the ASPIRE BANK marks.
   - At this stage, Apex was seeking registration based on an intent to use the marks in commerce.

2. **Letter of protest during prosecution**
   - CC Serve submitted a letter of protest to the USPTO during examination, arguing that the marks were confusingly similar.
   - That is an **ex parte** attempt to persuade the examining attorney to refuse the application before publication.

3. **Examining attorney approved publication**
   - The examining attorney rejected the protest’s practical effect and allowed the marks to publish.
   - That means only that the application cleared examination enough to be **published for public notice**. It is **not** a final adjudication that the mark is registrable.

4. **Opposition before the TTAB**
   - After publication, CC Serve filed a formal opposition.
   - This is a **separate, adversarial proceeding** in which the opponent and applicant litigate registrability before the TTAB.

### Why the examiner’s approval doesn’t “settle” it

Because the examiner’s decision to publish is **not binding on the TTAB** and does not foreclose an opposition. The TTAB makes its own registrability determination on the record before it.

The opinion makes that clear in substance:

- the Board **sustained the opposition under § 2(d)** despite the examiner’s earlier approval;
- the Federal Circuit explains that **likelihood of confusion** is evaluated under the **DuPont factors**, based on underlying factual findings;
- and the Board independently weighed those factors in deciding whether registration should be refused.

So the answer to your last question is basically **yes**: an opposition is a separate proceeding, and the TTAB can reach a different result from the examining attorney because it is deciding the issue in an adjudicatory setting, not merely screening the application ex parte.

### Bottom line

In Apex, the sequence was:

**application → protest → publication → opposition → TTAB refusal**

The examiner’s willingness to publish only meant “the application may go forward to publication.” It did **not** mean “the mark is entitled to registration.”

### Why did the Board treat 'credit card services' and 'banking and financing services' as legally identical in part?
Question:
The applicant argued that it doesn't offer credit cards and that CC Serve itself just partners with banks, so I expected the services to be considered related at most. But the Board said the services were 'legally identical, in part' because dictionary definitions of banking and finance can include extending credit through credit cards. How does that work in trademark law? Is the comparison based only on the wording in the registration/application rather than the parties' real-world business models, and is that why Apex's current lack of credit card services did not matter?
Answer:
Because, in a § 2(d) likelihood-of-confusion analysis, the Board compares the **services as identified in the application and registration**, not the parties’ most limited real-world business models.

That is what happened here.

The Board first had to interpret what “credit card services” and “banking and financing services” meant in the abstract, because the ID Manual no longer listed “credit card services.” It then looked to dictionary definitions and concluded that **“banking” and “finance” can include extending credit or providing funds through credit cards**. So Apex’s broad identification of “banking and financing services” reached some services that overlap with CC Serve’s “credit card services.” That is why the Board said the services were **“legally identical, in part”**—not identical in every respect, but overlapping as a matter of the legal scope of the recited services.

So your instinct about “real-world business models” is partly right, but only to a point:

- **Actual business operations do not narrow a broad identification** in the application/registration.
- The Board asks what the marks cover **as written**, because § 2(d) protects against confusion from the scope of the registration/application, not just the current products the parties happen to offer.
- That is why Apex’s current lack of credit cards did not defeat the Board’s analysis. Apex applied for “banking and financing services,” and the Board was entitled to assess that description broadly.

The opinion makes this explicit: the Board found the services highly similar, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that finding under the second DuPont factor. The court also noted that services need not be directly competitive; it is enough that they are related enough that consumers could think they come from the same source.

So the short answer is: **yes, the comparison is driven primarily by the wording of the recited services, and that is why Apex’s present business model did not control.**

### Why was the Board allowed to interpret the old identification 'credit card services' so broadly after that entry was deleted from the ID Manual?
Question:
The opinion says the old 'credit card services' entry had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual, so the Board had to determine what the identified services meant. It then included things like issuing cards, processing transactions, monitoring, account management, and access to credit scores. I'm confused about the source of that interpretation. When an old identification is vague or outdated, what legal materials can the TTAB use to define its scope, and how much flexibility does it have in doing so?
Answer:
Because the deletion from the ID Manual did **not** change the trademark registration itself. The Board still had to interpret the words **“credit card services”** as they appear in the registration, and it did that by looking to the term’s **ordinary commercial meaning** and the **record evidence** showing how such services are actually described.

That is exactly what the opinion describes:

- the Board noted the ID Manual entry had been deleted;
- it then determined what “credit card services” meant;
- and it concluded that the term includes more than just handing out plastic cards — it also covers things like **issuing cards, processing payments and transactions, authorization, monitoring/alerts, account management, counseling on credit card debt, and access to credit scores**.

So the Board was not relying on the ID Manual as controlling authority. The manual is a classification aid; once the entry was gone, the Board was free to construe the **actual registration language** using ordinary meaning and the evidence in the record.

As the Federal Circuit’s opinion shows, the Board also used **dictionary definitions** and **third-party registrations** in its likelihood-of-confusion analysis. Here, those materials supported the conclusion that credit-card services and banking/financing services overlap. The court then held that substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that the services were “highly similar.”

The practical limit is this: the TTAB has flexibility to interpret a vague or outdated identification **broadly enough to fit the language used**, but it cannot rewrite the registration or ignore the words chosen. In this case, the Board’s reading was broad, but still within the ordinary scope of “credit card services,” so the Federal Circuit affirmed it.

### Why did the Federal Circuit say the TTAB used the wrong scope of comparison under the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
For the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited the relevant third-party evidence to Aspire-formative marks used for credit card services and treated Aspire marks used for broader banking or financing services as basically irrelevant. The Federal Circuit said that was legal error because the second factor had already found the parties' services highly similar and partly legally identical. I'm struggling with the doctrinal point here: does the court mean the definition of 'similar goods/services' should stay consistent across DuPont factors unless there's a specific reason to change it? Or is this only about third-party-use evidence of weakness?
Answer:
Yes — this was mainly about the **scope of third-party-use evidence under the sixth DuPont factor**, and the Federal Circuit’s point was that the Board **made the similarity test too narrow in that context**.

### What the court held

The sixth factor asks about **“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”** In this case, the Board had already found under the second factor that Apex’s banking/financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were **“highly similar”** and even **“legally identical, in part.”**

Given that finding, the Federal Circuit said the Board could not then turn around and define the relevant universe for factor six as **only** Aspire-formative marks used for credit card services, while treating Aspire marks used in broader banking/financing services as irrelevant.

That is why the court said:

> “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.”

And it added:

> “We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”

### So what doctrinal point is the court making?

Not that the definition of “similar goods/services” must always be frozen across all DuPont factors in every case. Rather:

- **The sixth factor still requires similarity**, but
- the Board **cannot apply a stricter notion of similarity than the one it already found under factor two**, absent some reason tied to the record.

In other words, the Board had already found that the relevant services overlapped substantially. That finding should have informed the sixth-factor analysis of whether third-party Aspire uses on broader financial services were part of the relevant marketplace evidence showing weakness.

### Why does this matter?

Because the sixth factor is about **strength or weakness of the mark**. Third-party use evidence can show that consumers are used to seeing a term like “Aspire” across a field, which can mean the mark is **relatively weak** and entitled to a narrower scope of protection.

So the error was not just about “what counts as similar” in the abstract. It was that the Board **excluded potentially relevant third-party evidence by defining the market too narrowly**, even though it had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar.

### Bottom line

The Federal Circuit was **not** creating a universal rule that every DuPont factor must use the exact same definition of similarity. It was saying that, **on these facts**, the Board could not use a narrower similarity standard for factor six than the one it had already adopted for factor two. The issue was specifically the **relevant scope of third-party-use evidence for mark weakness** under the sixth factor.

### What is the difference between proving a mark is commercially weak versus conceptually weak under the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
The opinion says Apex offered third-party Aspire uses to show weakness, and the Board concluded CC Serve had not shown particular commercial strength, but Apex also had not shown the mark was commercially or conceptually weak enough to deserve a narrow scope of protection. I get that third-party use can weaken a mark, but I'm not clear on the two different kinds of weakness here. What evidence goes to commercial weakness versus conceptual weakness, and why would that matter to how much protection the ASPIRE mark gets?
Answer:
They are two different kinds of “weakness,” and they matter for different reasons.

**1. Commercial weakness = weakness in the marketplace.**  
This is what the Sixth DuPont factor is mainly getting at in this opinion. The question is whether **third parties are already using similar marks on similar goods/services**, so consumers have been exposed to a crowded field and are less likely to assume a single source.

That is why the court quotes cases like **Juice Generation** and **Jack Wolfskin**: in a crowded field, consumers are “educated to distinguish between marks,” which shows the mark has **less commercial strength**.  
Here, Apex tried to do that with third-party **ASPIRE**-formative uses. The Federal Circuit said the Board looked too narrowly only at credit-card services; because the Board had already found the parties’ services highly similar, it should have considered similar ASPIRE uses in the broader banking/financing space too.

**2. Conceptual weakness = weakness in inherent distinctiveness.**  
This is about the **term itself**: how inherently distinctive or inherently weak the mark is as a source identifier. A mark is conceptually weak if the word is common, descriptive, or otherwise not very distinctive from the start. By contrast, an inherently distinctive mark gets the “normal scope of protection.”

The opinion does not give a separate body of evidence proving ASPIRE was conceptually weak; instead, the Board said Apex had not shown the mark was “commercially or conceptually weak enough to deserve a narrow scope of protection,” and therefore treated it as “entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.”

**Why the distinction matters:**  
A mark that is weak **commercially** or **conceptually** gets a **narrower scope of protection**. That means later users can come closer to it without likely confusion. If ASPIRE were shown to be heavily diluted by similar third-party use, or inherently weak as a word mark, CC Serve would get less room to block Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks.

So, in short:

- **Commercial weakness** = “How crowded is the field?”  
- **Conceptual weakness** = “How distinctive is the word itself?”  
- **Effect** = weaker marks get less protection and a narrower zone of exclusivity.

The Federal Circuit remanded because the Board may have understated the commercial weakness evidence by limiting its review to only a slice of the relevant market.

### Why did the court vacate the first DuPont factor without actually deciding whether ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK are similar marks?
Question:
The Federal Circuit affirmed the services-similarity factor, vacated the sixth factor, and then also vacated the first factor because changes to the sixth factor could affect commercial strength and therefore commercial impression. I'm having trouble seeing the connection. I thought the first factor was just a side-by-side comparison of the marks' appearance, sound, meaning, and overall impression. How does new evidence or reconsideration of third-party Aspire marks change that analysis, and could the Board still reach the same result on remand?
Answer:
Because the Federal Circuit was not saying the **wording** of the marks must change; it was saying the **context in which those marks are perceived** might change.

Under the first DuPont factor, the Board compares the marks “in their entireties” as to appearance, sound, connotation, and **commercial impression**. That last part is the key. Commercial impression is not a purely side-by-side, dictionary-style comparison of the words on the page. It is the way the relevant public is likely to perceive the marks in the marketplace.

Here, the court held that the Board’s sixth-factor analysis was too narrow because it excluded third-party “Aspire” uses in similar financial-services fields. The court then said that a revised sixth-factor analysis “may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” That is why the first factor was vacated too.

So the linkage is:

1. **Sixth factor** looks at the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods/services.
2. That evidence can show the shared term **Aspire** is commercially weak or crowded in the relevant field.
3. If the term is weak, that can change how consumers perceive the mark’s **commercial impression** under the first factor.
4. Therefore, the first factor had to be reconsidered as well.

In other words, the court was not deciding that **ASPIRE** and **ASPIRE BANK** are or are not similar. It was saying the Board’s similarity analysis cannot be trusted until the Board redoes the sixth factor, because the strength of “Aspire” may affect how similar the marks seem overall.

And yes, the Board could absolutely reach the **same result** on remand. Vacatur does not mean the opposite result is required. It just means the Board must reconsider the first factor in light of whatever it decides on the sixth factor. If the Board again finds that the third-party evidence does not show weakness, it could again conclude that the first factor favors CC Serve.

### Why were costs awarded to Apex if it only won a vacatur and remand rather than outright registration?
Question:
The disposition says 'affirmed-in-part, vacated-in-part, and remanded,' but then it awards costs to appellant. Since Apex did not fully prevail and the court even affirmed the Board on the second DuPont factor, I'm confused why costs go to Apex. Is the party that secures a vacatur/remand usually treated as the prevailing party for appellate costs, or is there a specific Federal Circuit rule for this situation?
Answer:
No. An appellant does **not** have to win outright to receive appellate costs.

The key rule is **Fed. R. App. P. 39(a)(4)**, which governs cases like this one:

> If a judgment is **affirmed in part, reversed in part, modified, or vacated**, costs are taxed **only as the court orders**.

So in a mixed disposition, costs are **not automatic** and are **not mechanically tied to who won more issues**. The court has discretion to allocate costs however it thinks appropriate.

That is exactly what happened here. The panel:

- **affirmed** the Board on the **second DuPont factor**,
- **vacated** the Board’s analysis of the **sixth** and **first** factors,
- and **remanded** for reconsideration.

Because Apex obtained a **vacatur and remand on two factors**, the court could reasonably treat Apex as the party that achieved the more significant appellate relief, even though it did not get outright registration. The final line—**“Costs to appellant.”**—is simply the court exercising its Rule 39 discretion.

So the short answer is:

- **No special Federal Circuit rule** makes a vacatur/remand appellant automatically “prevailing.”
- But under **Rule 39(a)(4)**, the Federal Circuit may award costs to the appellant in a mixed-result appeal.
- Here, the court chose to do exactly that.
