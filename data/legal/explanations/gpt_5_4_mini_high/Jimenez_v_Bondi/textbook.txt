Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Factual Narrative and Procedural Path to the Tenth Circuit

## The Petitioners and Their Life in Bogotá

The petitioners are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. All four are natives and citizens of Colombia. Before leaving Colombia, the family lived in Bogotá and owned and worked in a furniture factory there. That detail matters because the factory later became one of the places where the family says it was threatened. The family relocated to the United States in December 2022.

The record also shows that the family’s daily life in Bogotá overlapped with both public political activity and Jimenez’s legal work. Those two strands of the story run side by side throughout the opinion, and the court later treats them separately when asking why the family was targeted.

## Political Background and Early Threats

Since 2012, the family had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party. During the 2022 Colombian presidential campaign, they supported Federico Gutierrez and went door-to-door in neighborhoods to seek votes. Beginning around the end of May 2022, Jimenez started receiving threatening phone calls once or twice a week because of that political involvement. Other Liberal Party leaders and colleagues also received threats, and in early June 2022 one colleague was kidnapped and murdered after reporting the threats.

Even after those incidents, Petitioners continued to campaign. That fact later becomes important because it shows that the political threats were not the only events in the record. The case also involves a separate dispute that brought Jimenez into contact with a dangerous criminal figure.

## Jimenez’s Legal Work and the Property Dispute

Jimenez had a provisional law license and was a law student when the relevant events occurred. He also worked as an intern for the Colombian National Prosecutor’s office, helping victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. In June 2022, he was asked to assist a woman from San Bernardo in a property dispute. She said her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and wanted Jimenez to negotiate restitution and departure from the property.

Jimenez visited the property, but the tenant refused to cooperate and told him not to “look for trouble.” Jimenez then learned that the tenant was actually the client’s ex-husband and that title to the property had been transferred to the client in a divorce settlement. He also discovered that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa,” whom the record describes as a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with Colombian President Gustavo Petro. Jimenez further learned that the property had been used as a day-rental for migrants and as a base for narcotrafficking and prostitution, and that it had been linked in reports to kidnapping and murder.

## Warnings from Authorities and Escalating Threats

Jimenez observed that the third floor of the property had been sealed off by the health department. He also learned that law enforcement had tried several searches and seizures at the property, apparently without success. A local police officer told Jimenez to stay away from the property and not to look for trouble. That warning foreshadowed what came next: the family’s threats shifted from political phone calls to direct confrontation tied to Jimenez’s work around the property.

Later that month, Jimenez received a threatening brochure at his factory that said, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” The following month, while working at the factory at night, he was confronted by two men at gunpoint who told him to leave the neighborhood. One of them accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures at the property. About two months later, the same men returned to the factory, assaulted an employee, demanded to know where Petitioners were, and again called them “snitches for the police” who were involved in politics. They said Don Rafa had ordered the threat and said they would kill Petitioners if they were seen again.

## The School Incident and Temporary Relocation

The threats escalated further when Jimenez witnessed a man approach his younger son as the boy was leaving school. The man put his arm around the child’s shoulder, covered his mouth when he tried to escape, and told him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez ran to his son and pushed the man away as the boy escaped to the family car. After that incident, the family moved to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogotá countryside for three weeks. During that period, they decided to leave Colombia and go to the United States.

Before departing, Jimenez told a police patrol officer what had happened. The officer said nothing could be done and that police officers were contributing to the problem, then told Jimenez and his family to leave. They did.

## Arrival in the United States and Removal Proceedings

Three months after arriving in the United States without being admitted or paroled, the Department of Homeland Security served each family member with a Notice to Appear. DHS charged them as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act. In August 2023, Petitioners admitted the factual allegations in the notices. The immigration judge then found them inadmissible and ordered them removed to Colombia.

Petitioners applied for asylum and withholding of removal. The two minor children proceeded as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application. The IJ credited Petitioners’ testimony but still denied relief, concluding that they had not shown past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, internal relocation problems, or that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The IJ also denied protection under the Convention Against Torture.

## The BIA’s Affirmance and the Narrowed Petition for Review

Petitioners appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals, but the Board affirmed and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected their due process argument that the IJ had violated their right to counsel. That left Petitioners with a final order of removal and a petition for review in the Tenth Circuit under 8 U.S.C. § 1252.

By the time the case reached the court of appeals, the issues had narrowed considerably. Petitioners challenged only the denial of asylum and withholding of removal. They did not pursue the CAT ruling or the due process issue. That narrowing matters because the Tenth Circuit’s later analysis focuses on three factual questions drawn from this record: whether the family suffered persecution, whether the harm was because of political opinion, and whether the Colombian government could protect them or whether they could safely relocate within Colombia.

# Appellate Posture, Standard of Review, and the Governing Asylum Framework

## Jurisdiction and the Scope of Review

The Tenth Circuit began by locating its authority to hear the case in 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which gives the court jurisdiction to review a final order of removal. That matters because this is not a de novo reconsideration of the asylum applications; it is judicial review of an administrative removal order. The court’s task was therefore limited to deciding whether the immigration judge and the Board of Immigration Appeals committed reversible error on the record already made before the agency.

Just as important, the petitioners narrowed the case on appeal. They challenged only the denial of asylum and withholding of removal. They did not seek review of the denial of protection under the Convention Against Torture, and they did not pursue the BIA’s rejection of their due process/right-to-counsel argument. Those unpursued issues dropped out of the case and did not play any role in the court’s merits analysis.

## Reviewing the BIA, with the IJ in the Background

Because a single BIA member affirmed the immigration judge’s decision, the Tenth Circuit treated the BIA’s decision as the operative decision for review. At the same time, the court explained that it could consult the IJ’s more detailed reasoning when the BIA affirmed on the same grounds. This is a common feature of immigration review: the court reviews the Board’s decision, but it may look to the IJ’s explanation when the Board’s analysis is brief or adopts the same rationale.

That procedural point shaped the opinion. The court was not deciding the case afresh; it was evaluating whether the BIA, with the IJ’s supporting reasoning in view, could reasonably conclude that the petitioners had not met their burden for asylum.

## The Substantial-Evidence Standard

The court reviewed the agency’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard. That standard is highly deferential. The agency’s findings are treated as conclusive unless the record compels the opposite result—meaning that any reasonable adjudicator would have to disagree with the BIA. It is not enough for the petitioners to show that another view of the facts was possible, or even that their view was plausible. They had to show that the evidence required a contrary conclusion.

That level of deference is central to understanding the opinion. Much of the petitioners’ argument depended on recharacterizing threats, harassment, and police inaction as persecution and government incapacity. Under substantial evidence review, the court could not simply choose among competing interpretations of the record; it had to ask whether the record forced the opposite outcome.

## Asylum, Refugee Status, and the Basic Statutory Framework

Asylum is a discretionary form of relief. Even if an applicant qualifies, the Attorney General still has discretion whether to grant it. But before discretion matters, the applicant must first establish statutory eligibility by proving that he or she is a “refugee” within the meaning of the Immigration and Nationality Act.

The statutory definition, as the court recited it, covers a person outside his or her country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. The “on account of” language is crucial: harm alone is not enough. The applicant must connect the harm to a protected ground.

In this case, the petitioners tried to fit their claim within that framework by relying primarily on political opinion. They also made passing reference to a particular social group theory, but they did not meaningfully develop that theory or establish group membership. The court therefore treated political opinion as the only preserved protected-ground argument.

## Past Persecution and the Presumption of Future Fear

One path to asylum is a showing of past persecution. If the applicant proves past persecution, that showing creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. That presumption is important because it shifts the burden in the applicant’s favor unless the government can rebut it, for example by showing changed circumstances or safe internal relocation.

The court explained that a past-persecution claim has three parts: the applicant must show harm rising to the level of persecution, that the harm was on account of a protected ground, and that the persecution was inflicted by the government or by private actors the government is unable or unwilling to control. Because the petitioners’ alleged persecutors were private actors, the government-protection element was essential. Their case could not succeed unless they could show both a protected-ground motive and a lack of effective state protection.

The opinion also reflects a practical point about persecution analysis: courts assess the events cumulatively. Threats, intimidation, and other incidents are not examined in isolation if they are part of the same pattern. Even so, the Tenth Circuit emphasized that threats alone are ordinarily not enough, and emotional or psychological distress, without more, rarely suffices to establish persecution.

## Withholding of Removal and Its Higher Burden

The petitioners also sought withholding of removal, but the opinion makes clear that withholding carries a higher burden than asylum. That means a failure to satisfy asylum necessarily defeats withholding as well. Once the court concluded that the petitioners had not shown eligibility for asylum, the withholding claim failed automatically.

That relationship is important for reading the disposition. The court did not need a separate extended analysis of withholding because the asylum analysis controlled. If the record was insufficient to show a well-founded fear of persecution for asylum purposes, it necessarily could not satisfy the stricter withholding standard.

## Claims and Theories Not Carried Forward

Finally, the court’s analysis was shaped by what the petitioners did not press on appeal. They did not challenge the denial of CAT protection, so the court did not address torture-based relief. They also did not pursue the due process/right-to-counsel issue the BIA had rejected. Those omissions narrowed the appeal to the asylum and withholding questions alone.

The court also limited the protected-ground discussion to political opinion. Although the petitioners mentioned a particular social group, they did not brief that theory in a meaningful way or establish the elements needed to make it work. For appellate purposes, that meant the court treated the nexus issue as a political-opinion case, not as a broader protected-ground case. This is a good example of how briefing choices can define the scope of judicial review as much as the administrative record does.

# Why the Court Found No Past Persecution

## Persecution Is a Case-Law Term, Not a Statutory Definition

The starting point in the court’s analysis is that the Immigration and Nationality Act does not define “persecution.” That matters because the court had to rely on Tenth Circuit precedent to supply the meaning. In this circuit, persecution requires more than unpleasant or frightening treatment. It means the infliction of suffering or harm in a way the law regards as offensive, and it must involve more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.

That framing sets a relatively high threshold. The court was not asking whether the petitioners had been mistreated or reasonably frightened. It was asking whether the mistreatment, taken at its worst, rose to the level that asylum law recognizes as persecution. The opinion repeatedly emphasizes that point: not all harassment qualifies, and not every threat becomes persecution simply because it is serious or repeated.

## The Court Considered the Incidents Collectively

The petitioners did not rely on a single event. Their claim was built from a sequence of escalating incidents: threatening phone calls tied to their Liberal Party activity, a death-threat brochure left at Jimenez’s factory, an armed confrontation in which two men ordered Jimenez to leave the neighborhood, a later visit by the same men who assaulted an employee and threatened to kill the petitioners, and the school incident involving one of their children. The court did not analyze each event in isolation. Consistent with Tenth Circuit doctrine, it considered the cumulative effect of the whole course of conduct.

That cumulative approach is important for students reading asylum cases. A court does not ask whether any one event, standing alone, is enough. Multiple lesser harms can sometimes add up to persecution. But the court’s point here was that even when the incidents were stacked together, the total picture still fell short. The opinion is careful to acknowledge the seriousness of what happened, but it concludes that the accumulated mistreatment did not cross the legal line into past persecution.

## The Lack of Lasting Physical Harm Mattered

One fact carried special weight in the court’s reasoning: the petitioners conceded that they were not physically harmed, and they also said in briefing that they suffered no lasting physical injury. The court made clear that physical harm is not strictly required to prove persecution. Still, the absence of injury mattered because it left the case resting largely on threats, intimidation, and emotional distress.

That distinction is central to understanding the opinion. The court was not saying that only beatings, torture, or detention can count as persecution. But it was saying that where the record shows no physical injury, the remaining harms must be especially severe to justify asylum relief. Here, the petitioners experienced fear and stress, but the court found that those effects, without more, did not meet the persecution threshold.

## Threats and Emotional Distress Were Not Enough

The petitioners’ strongest argument was that the repeated threats themselves amounted to persecution, especially because they produced lasting psychological harm. Duarte testified that she had trouble sleeping and resting, and that one of the children suffered panic attacks and stress. The court did not dismiss those effects as unreal or unimportant. But it treated them as consequences of fear, not as independent proof of persecution.

That is where the opinion draws an important line between serious fear and legally sufficient persecution. The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that threats alone are generally not enough. The court quoted that principle directly and then applied it here. Even though the threats were alarming and repeated, they were still threats. They were not accompanied by the kind of severe physical abuse, prolonged confinement, or comparable harm that typically pushes a case over the persecution threshold.

The court also pointed out that it has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based only on threats plus emotional or psychological harm. That does not mean such harm is irrelevant. It means the harm must be severe enough, in context, to amount to persecution, and the panel did not think this record compelled that conclusion.

## Prior Cases Showed How High the Severity Bar Is

To explain why it rejected the petitioners’ claim, the court compared this case to prior Tenth Circuit and unpublished decisions. Those cases functioned as benchmarks. In one, a 10-hour arrest and detention plus threats still did not amount to persecution. In another, four phone threats and one vague in-person threat did not qualify. In yet another, threats with machetes and six robberies were still insufficient because the threats were tied to compliance with a demand.

Those comparisons mattered because they showed that the Tenth Circuit’s persecution standard is demanding. The court was not saying the petitioners’ experiences were trivial. It was saying that, measured against circuit precedent, their mistreatment was not worse than conduct that the court had already found insufficient in earlier cases. That made it easier for the panel to conclude that the Board of Immigration Appeals was not compelled to find past persecution here.

## Substantial Evidence Controlled the Outcome

The standard of review shaped everything. The court was reviewing the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard, which is highly deferential. Under that standard, the Board’s finding is conclusive unless the record would compel any reasonable adjudicator to reach the opposite result. So the question was not whether the panel might have viewed the facts sympathetically or even whether persecution was a possible reading of the record. The question was whether the evidence forced that conclusion.

The answer was no. Because the petitioners had no lasting physical harm, because the threats alone did not establish persecution, because the psychological effects did not independently carry the claim, and because precedent set a high bar that this case did not clear, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s conclusion. The bottom line is that the aggregate harm was serious and frightening, but not legally enough to count as past persecution under the INA.

# Nexus to a Protected Ground: Political Opinion Versus Retaliation for the Don Rafa Conflict

## The protected-ground theory the court actually reviewed

The nexus question in this case was narrower on appeal than it may have looked at the agency level. Petitioners originally referred to both political opinion and particular social group, but they did not develop the particular social group theory in any meaningful way on appeal. As a result, the Tenth Circuit limited its review to political opinion. That is a good reminder that asylum law is not just about proving mistreatment; the applicant also has to identify and support a legally cognizable protected ground, and appellate courts will not supply arguments that were not actually pressed.

That narrowing mattered because it shaped the court’s entire causation analysis. The issue was not whether the petitioners had been threatened or frightened, but whether those threats were “on account of” their political opinion. The court therefore asked a very specific question: were the threats driven by Petitioners’ Liberal Party activity, or were they driven by Jimenez’s later conflict with Don Rafa and the property investigation?

## The statutory rule: a protected ground must be “one central reason”

The opinion applies the asylum statute’s nexus requirement, which asks whether persecution occurred “on account of” a protected ground. Under the REAL ID Act formulation the court quotes, the protected ground must be “at least one central reason” for the harm. That does not mean it must be the only reason. Persecutors can have mixed motives, and an asylum claim is not defeated just because the attacker was also motivated by personal, economic, or criminal reasons.

But “one central reason” is still a demanding standard. The court repeated the familiar language that the protected ground cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate,” and it cannot play only a “minor role.” In other words, the applicant has to show that the protected ground was truly important to the persecutor’s decision to act. The legal question is not whether politics was somewhere in the background, but whether it was central enough to explain the threats or violence.

## Why the chronology mattered

The court’s nexus analysis turned heavily on timing. Petitioners had some politically themed threats before the property dispute, including threatening calls while they were active in the Liberal Party campaign and a brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Standing alone, that might suggest a political component. But the court emphasized that there was no evidence tying Don Rafa to those early campaign-related threats.

The more serious escalation happened only after Jimenez became involved in the property matter. He visited the property, learned about its illicit uses, investigated further, and discovered that the tenant was connected to Don Rafa and a criminal operation. After that, the threats became more direct and more specific. Men confronted him at the furniture factory, accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, and blamed him for searches and seizures at the property. Later, when those same men returned, they assaulted an employee and again demanded to know where Petitioners were, saying the order came from Don Rafa. The court viewed those statements as the clearest evidence of motive.

That sequence of events led the court to read the case as a retaliation case, not a political persecution case. The threats escalated after Jimenez interfered with Don Rafa’s property and exposed a site used for narcotrafficking, prostitution, and other criminal activity. In the court’s view, the persecutors were reacting to a perceived threat to their operation, revenue, and security, not targeting Petitioners because of their Liberal Party affiliation.

## Why the court treated politics as secondary

The key move in the opinion is the distinction between a political identity and a political motive. Petitioners were in fact politically active, and the record showed that the attackers knew that. But knowing someone is political is not the same as persecuting them because of politics. The court concluded that any political reference was subordinate to the practical reason for the threats: Jimenez had become a problem for Don Rafa.

That is why the court focused so much on words like “snitch,” “prosecutor’s office,” and “searches and seizures.” Those phrases point to retaliation against law-enforcement cooperation or law-related interference, not necessarily to punishment for political ideology. The court also noted that Petitioners had not shown Don Rafa was behind the early campaign threats, which weakened their attempt to connect the entire course of events to political opinion. On this record, the political aspect looked like context, not causation.

The opinion’s bottom line on this point is very clear: Petitioners’ political opinion was “merely tangential and subordinate” to the real reason they were threatened. Even if politics played some role in how the persecutors viewed them, it was not a central reason for the harm.

## Deference and the appellate posture

The substantial-evidence standard was crucial. The Tenth Circuit did not ask whether it would have drawn the same inference as the IJ and BIA. It asked whether the record compelled the opposite conclusion. Because the agency’s view—that the threats were mainly retaliation for Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa and his perceived role in exposing criminal activity—was reasonable, the court could not reweigh the evidence.

That is the practical lesson from the nexus discussion. When the record supports two plausible motives, the applicant must do more than show that a protected ground was one possible explanation. He must show that the protected ground was central enough that no reasonable adjudicator could see the case another way. Here, the court held that Petitioners did not meet that burden. Because they failed to establish nexus to political opinion, their asylum claim failed, and their withholding claim failed as well because withholding requires an even higher showing.

# Future Persecution, Government Protection, Internal Relocation, and Final Disposition

## Turning from Past Persecution to Future Persecution

After rejecting the petitioners’ claim of past persecution, the court still had to address the rest of the asylum analysis because asylum can also be based on a well-founded fear of future persecution. In other words, even when an applicant cannot show that prior harm was severe enough to count as persecution, the applicant may still qualify for asylum if the record establishes a reasonable fear of being persecuted if returned home. But here, the court reviewed that question under the substantial-evidence standard, which is highly deferential: the petitioners had to show that the record compelled a conclusion contrary to the BIA’s.

That posture mattered. Because the petitioners did not prove past persecution, they did not get the usual presumption of a future fear. So the burden remained on them to show that their fear of future harm was well founded and that the Colombian government could not or would not protect them. The court’s remaining analysis focused on those points.

## Government Protection and the Notice Requirement

The petitioners’ persecutors were private actors, not the Colombian state itself, so the case turned on the familiar “unable or unwilling” requirement. In private-actor cases, asylum law asks not just whether the applicant was harmed, but whether the home government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. The court emphasized that this inquiry focuses on the government’s response, or expected response, to the reported harm.

A key part of that analysis is notice. Courts generally want to know whether the applicant gave the government a chance to respond, because without notice there is usually no way to tell what the police or other authorities would have done. Reporting persecution is therefore important evidence, though not an absolute prerequisite. An applicant can still satisfy the requirement by showing that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. But if the applicant made only minimal efforts to seek protection, that can weaken the claim.

Here, the petitioners’ efforts were thin. Jimenez and Duarte testified that they spoke to two individual police officers, who told them not to seek trouble and to leave. The court treated those interactions as limited and informal. They were not formal complaints, and they did not show that the Colombian government as a whole had been asked to intervene. The court also noted that the petitioners did not go beyond those conversations to broader police, prosecutorial, or other institutional channels, even though Jimenez and his father had government or law-enforcement connections in Colombia.

## Why the Record Did Not Compel a Finding That Colombia Could Not or Would Not Protect Them

The petitioners argued that reporting would have been pointless or dangerous because their persecutors were aligned with the Colombian government. The court rejected that argument as unsupported by the record. On the evidence presented, the government already knew about the criminal activity tied to the property that Jimenez investigated, and law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures there. That fact undercut the claim that the persecutors were effectively sheltered by the state or that the state was indifferent to them.

The court also drew a line between the comments of individual officers and the position of the Colombian state. A single officer saying “don’t look for trouble” does not establish that the government as a whole is unwilling or unable to help. At most, it suggests one officer’s reaction in one situation. The court therefore held that the record did not compel a finding that Colombia could not protect the family, especially when the petitioners did not meaningfully report the threats to higher or broader authorities.

So, even accepting the petitioners’ testimony as credible, the court concluded that they had not shown enough government failure to satisfy the asylum standard. The evidence showed limited contact with police, not a demonstrated breakdown of state protection.

## Internal Relocation Within Colombia

The court next addressed internal relocation. Even if an applicant fears harm in one part of a country, asylum may still be denied if the applicant could avoid future persecution by moving to another part of the country where the threat would not follow. The relocation inquiry asks whether relocation is both possible and reasonable in light of the circumstances.

That doctrine fit the record here. The petitioners had already spent three weeks safely staying with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside before leaving for the United States. Their relatives continued to live in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father still worked in the criminal justice system. Those facts supported the view that the danger was localized rather than nationwide.

The court also found it important that the threatening men told the petitioners to leave the neighborhood, not the country. That detail suggested a local criminal dispute tied to Jimenez’s involvement with the property and with Don Rafa, rather than a countrywide campaign against the family. Although the petitioners argued that relocation would be too costly, administratively difficult, and unsafe because of their persecutors’ alleged political ties, the court viewed those assertions as only lightly developed and not enough to overcome the contrary evidence. On this record, the court concluded that the petitioners had not shown that relocation within Colombia was unreasonable.

## Final Disposition: No Asylum, Therefore No Withholding

Having rejected the petitioners’ arguments on persecution, government protection, and relocation, the court denied the petition for review. Because asylum requires a lower showing than withholding of removal, failure to satisfy the asylum standard necessarily meant failure to satisfy the withholding standard as well. The court therefore denied both forms of relief.

The opinion illustrates a recurring asylum problem: when threats come from private criminals, the applicant must do more than show fear and local danger. The record must also show that the home government cannot or will not help, and that the danger cannot reasonably be avoided elsewhere in the country. Here, the court held that the evidence did not compel those conclusions.
