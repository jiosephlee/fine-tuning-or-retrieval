Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Politics or Retaliation? Mixed Motives, Criminal Enterprise, and the Nexus Problem in *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why this case matters

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is a reminder that asylum law is not a general protection against danger, even when the danger is real, politically tinged, and terrifying. The Tenth Circuit’s decision turns on a recurring and often outcome-determinative question: when violence comes from actors who are politically connected, or who lash out at political activists, how do we tell whether the harm was “on account of” political opinion, as the INA requires, or simply retaliation for getting in the way of a criminal enterprise?

That distinction mattered here because Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family had more than one plausible story to tell. They were active in Colombia’s Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential candidate. But Jimenez also crossed paths with a powerful criminal figure, “Don Rafa,” while trying to resolve a property dispute and, in the court’s telling, became someone the group perceived as a “snitch” who threatened their operations. The Tenth Circuit held that the latter was the real story, and that the family’s political activity was at most tangential.

## What the court saw in the record

The opinion is careful in its chronology, and the chronology drives the result. The petitioners had been involved in Liberal Party politics for years, and Jimenez received threatening calls during the 2022 campaign. But the court emphasized that those early threats were not tied to Don Rafa or his associates. The escalation began later, after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute at a site linked to Don Rafa’s criminal activity. When Jimenez tried to negotiate with the tenant, he was warned not to “look for trouble.” After he learned the property was being used for narcotrafficking, prostitution, and other crimes, he and his family started receiving more direct threats.

Those threats, the court stressed, repeatedly focused on Jimenez as a “snitch” and on his role with the prosecutor’s office and the searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. The men who threatened the family did mention politics at points, including a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” but the panel treated that as incidental rather than central. In its view, the record supported the agency’s conclusion that the motive was to protect revenue, security, and criminal control—not to punish Liberal Party affiliation.

That framing is what doomed the asylum claim under the INA’s nexus requirement. Under the “one central reason” standard, a protected ground need not be the only reason for the harm, but it must be more than incidental, tangential, or subordinate. The Tenth Circuit concluded that Jimenez’s political opinion fell on the wrong side of that line. The family was targeted, in the court’s view, because Jimenez became a perceived obstacle to a criminal operation.

The court also rejected the petitioners’ past-persecution theory, noting that they had not suffered physical harm and that threats alone generally do not amount to persecution in this circuit. And it separately held that they had not shown the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, in part because they had made little effort to seek formal protection and because the record showed the government had previously attempted searches and seizures at the relevant property. Finally, even if they had established a future-fear claim, the court said the family could reasonably relocate within Colombia.

## The court’s motive analysis is orthodox, but not simple

From a doctrinal standpoint, the opinion is very much in line with the Tenth Circuit’s asylum cases. The panel did not say that political activists can never be persecuted by criminal organizations. It said something narrower: on this record, substantial evidence supported the agency’s finding that politics was not a central reason for the harm.

That is an important distinction. Asylum law often fails at the level of proof, not principle. The harder the facts look like a blend of politics, corruption, and criminal enterprise, the more tempting it is to treat all the ingredients as one political stew. But the statute demands more precision. Retaliation against someone who interferes with a racketeering operation is not automatically persecution “on account of” political opinion, even if the racketeers themselves operate in a politically charged environment.

At the same time, the case exposes the difficulty of parsing motive in modern violence. “Snitch” is a slippery label. It can mean informer, enemy, meddler, traitor, or political opponent. It can be used by criminals to describe anyone who threatens their control, regardless of whether the threat is law-enforcement, civic, or partisan in nature. The court took the label here as evidence of operational retaliation, not political persecution. That is defensible on this record, but it also shows how much turns on the surrounding evidence: who made the threats, what they said, when the violence escalated, and whether the record connects the persecutor’s animus to the applicant’s political views rather than to the applicant’s conduct.

## The downstream lesson for future cases

The biggest practical takeaway is for asylum applicants and their lawyers. If the claim sits at the intersection of political activity and anti-corruption or law-enforcement work, the record has to do more than show that the applicant was politically engaged and later threatened by dangerous people. It has to show why the persecutor cared. Was the applicant targeted because of party affiliation, campaign activity, or public opposition to a regime? Or was the applicant targeted because he became inconvenient to a criminal network?

Those are not the same thing, and *Jimenez* suggests the Tenth Circuit will not blur them lightly. That matters especially in countries where organized crime and political power overlap, because many applicants will have both kinds of exposure. The opinion suggests that courts will keep looking for a clean causal link to a protected ground, even when the underlying violence looks deeply political in the colloquial sense.

The decision also reinforces a broader trend in asylum adjudication: courts are wary of turning criminal retaliation into political persecution unless the evidence really supports that leap. That may leave some deserving applicants without protection, but it also preserves the statute’s core structure. Asylum is not a remedy for every form of danger; it is a remedy for a particular kind of danger, inflicted for a protected reason.

In that sense, *Jimenez v. Bondi* is less a dramatic doctrinal turn than a disciplined application of existing law. But it is still a useful signal. In mixed-motive cases, especially those involving politically connected organized crime, the winning argument will rarely be that politics was somewhere in the background. It will be that politics was central—and that the record proves it.
