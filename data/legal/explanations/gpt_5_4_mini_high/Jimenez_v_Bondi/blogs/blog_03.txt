Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Report It or Lose It? *Jimenez v. Bondi* and the Tenth Circuit’s Demanding View of Government “Unable or Unwilling” Protection

## Why the case matters

For asylum applicants, proving persecution is only half the fight. They also have to show that their home government either could not or would not protect them. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit used that requirement to deny relief to a Colombian family who credibly described threats, intimidation, and a frightening encounter with men tied to a local criminal figure. The court’s bottom line was not that nothing bad happened. It was that the record did not compel a conclusion that Colombia was unable or unwilling to help.

That makes the case important well beyond its facts. The panel’s analysis shows how much turns on notice: what did the applicants report, to whom, and with what documentation? And it shows how a court can treat isolated police contacts as too thin to prove systemic government failure, especially when the record also contains some evidence of state action against the relevant criminal activity.

## The court’s approach: notice first, inference second

The opinion, by Judge Tymkovich, is careful to say that failure to report persecution is not automatically fatal. That is doctrinally right. The Tenth Circuit has said before that an applicant can still win if filing a report would have been futile or dangerous. But in *Jimenez*, the panel concluded that the petitioners had not built that kind of record.

What did they do? They spoke with two individual police officers, both of whom essentially told them not to “look for trouble.” But they did not file a report, pursue a complaint beyond their immediate neighborhood, or use the law-enforcement channels Jimenez himself might have been expected to know about as someone with legal training and family ties to the justice system. The court treated that absence as significant because, under its prior precedent, the government-protection inquiry is partly about whether the state had notice and an opportunity to respond. Without notice, the court reasoned, there is little basis for concluding the state would have failed to act.

The panel also rejected the idea that the brief encounters with individual officers proved a broader government unwillingness to protect. That distinction matters. As the court noted, one unhelpful officer is not the same thing as a government policy of nonprotection. The opinion leans on that point to keep the analysis at the systemic level rather than letting a single anecdote stand in for the whole state.

## Why the searches of the property mattered

The government-protection analysis got another boost from a fact that cuts against the petitioners’ theory: the authorities had already tried to search and seize the property linked to Don Rafa’s operations. The court used that evidence to undercut the claim that the persecutors were effectively aligned with, or protected by, the Colombian state.

That is a subtle but important move. The court did not say those searches solved the problem, or that police activity elsewhere necessarily meant Jimenez was safe. Instead, it treated those efforts as evidence that the state had at least tried to address the underlying criminal conduct. In other words, the record showed some willingness to act, even if the petitioners experienced local indifference or hostility.

That framing matters doctrinally because asylum law asks whether the government is unable or unwilling “to control” the persecutors, not whether every police officer is sympathetic, competent, or brave. The panel’s approach suggests that evidence of formal law-enforcement activity can be powerful rebuttal evidence, even when applicants can point to frightening local encounters.

## The tension in the reasoning

The strongest part of the opinion is its insistence that courts should not infer statewide incapacity from a handful of informal conversations. That is sensible, especially under substantial-evidence review. But the decision also reveals the pressure that “notice” doctrine can place on asylum seekers.

In many real-world settings, people do not file reports because they fear retaliation, distrust the police, or believe the police are compromised. The Tenth Circuit acknowledges that possibility in the abstract, but in practice it demands concrete evidence that reporting would have been dangerous or futile. Without that evidence, the absence of a report becomes a major obstacle. That can look a lot like a de facto reporting requirement, even if the court is careful not to call it one.

There is a real policy question here. Should asylum law require applicants to risk approaching potentially hostile authorities before they can prove the government is unable or unwilling to protect them? *Jimenez* does not answer that question expressly, but its logic pushes in that direction: if you don’t report, you may lose; if you do report only informally, that may still not be enough; and if there is some evidence that the government elsewhere tried to intervene, the claim gets even harder.

## Downstream effects

The practical lesson for practitioners is obvious: build the record on reporting, or explain in detail why reporting was impossible. Applicants need evidence of threats to safety if they sought help, documentation of police refusals, and proof that complaints went nowhere. General assertions that the police are corrupt or aligned with persecutors will usually not carry the day on review.

The doctrinal lesson is broader. *Jimenez* fits a line of cases in which the Tenth Circuit treats the “unable or unwilling” prong as a fact-intensive, evidence-heavy inquiry that is difficult to satisfy without concrete, state-facing proof. Combined with substantial-evidence review, that approach gives immigration judges and the BIA substantial room to reject claims based on local hostility, even when the applicant’s fear is objectively understandable.

The upshot is that *Jimenez* is not just another denied asylum petition. It is a reminder that, in the Tenth Circuit at least, the path to proving government failure often runs through notice, documentation, and proof that the state had a real chance to act—and did not.
