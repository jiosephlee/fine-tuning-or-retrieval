Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Is Terror Not “Persecution”? *Jimenez v. Bondi* and the Tenth Circuit’s High Bar for Threat-Based Asylum Claims

## Why the case matters

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is a stark reminder that, in asylum law, being credible and being terrified are not the same as meeting the legal definition of persecution. The petitioners — a Colombian family that received repeated threats, faced two armed confrontations at their business, saw an employee assaulted, and endured a frightening incident outside their child’s school — asked the Tenth Circuit to recognize those events as past persecution. The panel, in an opinion by Judge Tymkovich, said the record did not compel that conclusion.

That matters because the case sits right at the fault line of modern asylum doctrine. Courts routinely say they assess harm “cumulatively,” not incident by incident. But *Jimenez* shows the limits of that principle in practice: repeated threats and deep psychological trauma, even when paired with a menacing encounter involving a child, may still be treated as something short of persecution unless there is clearer physical injury, detention, or comparable coercion.

## The court’s reasoning

The panel’s analysis is best understood as a series of narrowing moves. First, on the threshold question of past persecution, the court accepted the petitioners as credible but emphasized that the Tenth Circuit’s precedent draws a line between threats and persecution. The opinion acknowledged that the family had received recurring threatening calls, a death-filled brochure, gunpoint warnings at the factory, and a later visit in which armed men threatened to kill them if they reappeared. But the court relied on its established rule that threats alone are generally insufficient, and it noted that the petitioners admitted they had not been physically harmed.

The panel did not ignore the emotional impact. It discussed Duarte’s insomnia and the child’s panic attacks, but treated those consequences as understandable reactions to fear rather than independent proof of persecution. That is where the opinion becomes most revealing. The court recited the familiar cumulative-harm principle, yet concluded that, even in the aggregate, the incidents were not severe enough. In other words, the court did not dispute that the family had been terrorized; it held only that the terror did not cross the doctrinal threshold.

The nexus analysis did a lot of work as well. Petitioners argued that they were targeted because of their political activity with the Colombian Liberal Party and their support for a presidential candidate. But the panel found substantial evidence that the escalated threats were primarily tied to Jimenez’s involvement with a property dispute implicating “Don Rafa,” a criminal figure linked to the property and upset about searches and seizures. The men who threatened the family called Jimenez a “snitch” and accused him of working with the prosecutor’s office. That made the case look, in the court’s view, less like political repression and more like retaliation for professional interference with criminal activity. The court was careful to say that a persecutor can have mixed motives, but it held that political opinion here was, at most, “tangential and subordinate.”

The opinion also rejected the claim that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the family. That part of the reasoning is doctrinally significant because asylum claims based on private-actor violence often rise or fall on the government-protection prong. The panel stressed that the family had made only minimal efforts to obtain police assistance. They spoke to a couple of officers who discouraged them from seeking trouble, but they did not file formal reports or show that broader police protection was unavailable. The court also pointed to evidence that the government knew about the property and had attempted searches and seizures there. That undercut the claim that the persecutors were effectively beyond state reach.

Finally, the panel held that safe internal relocation was available. The family had lived with Jimenez’s mother in the countryside for three weeks before leaving Colombia, and the threats, as the court put it, were aimed at forcing them out of the neighborhood, not out of the country. That finding mattered because it gave the government another route to defeat any future-fear theory even if the past-persecution theory had been closer.

## The broader lesson

The most important takeaway from *Jimenez* is not that threats can never amount to persecution. It is that, in the Tenth Circuit, threats usually need to be accompanied by something more concrete — actual violence, detention, sustained coercion, or strong evidence of governmental failure — before they compel reversal. The opinion is fully consistent with the court’s precedent, but it also shows how demanding that precedent has become. A family can be stalked by armed men, threatened with death, and frightened by an apparent attempt to grab a child, yet still lose because the record does not show enough physical harm or because the threats are characterized as criminal retaliation rather than protected-ground persecution.

That has real downstream effects. Asylum applicants in the Tenth Circuit will likely need to build more robust records on both severity and motive: formal police reports where possible, corroboration of government inaction, and evidence tying the harm to a protected ground rather than to a business dispute, extortion scheme, or personal vendetta. For petitioners facing cartel-linked or politically connected actors, *Jimenez* is a warning that courts may view “terror” as insufficiently legal unless it is paired with more tangible injury or unmistakable state complicity.

At the same time, the opinion raises a familiar tension in asylum law. The statute protects against persecution, not generalized danger. But in the real world, the tools of intimidation are often threats, surveillance, and fear rather than bruises. *Jimenez* reflects a judiciary trying to preserve that doctrinal boundary. Whether one sees that as sound line-drawing or as an unduly narrow reading of persecution likely depends on how much weight one gives to the terror itself. The Tenth Circuit, here, gave it less than the petitioners needed.



# Politics or Retaliation? Mixed Motives, Criminal Enterprise, and the Nexus Problem in *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why this case matters

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is a reminder that asylum law is not a general protection against danger, even when the danger is real, politically tinged, and terrifying. The Tenth Circuit’s decision turns on a recurring and often outcome-determinative question: when violence comes from actors who are politically connected, or who lash out at political activists, how do we tell whether the harm was “on account of” political opinion, as the INA requires, or simply retaliation for getting in the way of a criminal enterprise?

That distinction mattered here because Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family had more than one plausible story to tell. They were active in Colombia’s Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential candidate. But Jimenez also crossed paths with a powerful criminal figure, “Don Rafa,” while trying to resolve a property dispute and, in the court’s telling, became someone the group perceived as a “snitch” who threatened their operations. The Tenth Circuit held that the latter was the real story, and that the family’s political activity was at most tangential.

## What the court saw in the record

The opinion is careful in its chronology, and the chronology drives the result. The petitioners had been involved in Liberal Party politics for years, and Jimenez received threatening calls during the 2022 campaign. But the court emphasized that those early threats were not tied to Don Rafa or his associates. The escalation began later, after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute at a site linked to Don Rafa’s criminal activity. When Jimenez tried to negotiate with the tenant, he was warned not to “look for trouble.” After he learned the property was being used for narcotrafficking, prostitution, and other crimes, he and his family started receiving more direct threats.

Those threats, the court stressed, repeatedly focused on Jimenez as a “snitch” and on his role with the prosecutor’s office and the searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. The men who threatened the family did mention politics at points, including a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” but the panel treated that as incidental rather than central. In its view, the record supported the agency’s conclusion that the motive was to protect revenue, security, and criminal control—not to punish Liberal Party affiliation.

That framing is what doomed the asylum claim under the INA’s nexus requirement. Under the “one central reason” standard, a protected ground need not be the only reason for the harm, but it must be more than incidental, tangential, or subordinate. The Tenth Circuit concluded that Jimenez’s political opinion fell on the wrong side of that line. The family was targeted, in the court’s view, because Jimenez became a perceived obstacle to a criminal operation.

The court also rejected the petitioners’ past-persecution theory, noting that they had not suffered physical harm and that threats alone generally do not amount to persecution in this circuit. And it separately held that they had not shown the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, in part because they had made little effort to seek formal protection and because the record showed the government had previously attempted searches and seizures at the relevant property. Finally, even if they had established a future-fear claim, the court said the family could reasonably relocate within Colombia.

## The court’s motive analysis is orthodox, but not simple

From a doctrinal standpoint, the opinion is very much in line with the Tenth Circuit’s asylum cases. The panel did not say that political activists can never be persecuted by criminal organizations. It said something narrower: on this record, substantial evidence supported the agency’s finding that politics was not a central reason for the harm.

That is an important distinction. Asylum law often fails at the level of proof, not principle. The harder the facts look like a blend of politics, corruption, and criminal enterprise, the more tempting it is to treat all the ingredients as one political stew. But the statute demands more precision. Retaliation against someone who interferes with a racketeering operation is not automatically persecution “on account of” political opinion, even if the racketeers themselves operate in a politically charged environment.

At the same time, the case exposes the difficulty of parsing motive in modern violence. “Snitch” is a slippery label. It can mean informer, enemy, meddler, traitor, or political opponent. It can be used by criminals to describe anyone who threatens their control, regardless of whether the threat is law-enforcement, civic, or partisan in nature. The court took the label here as evidence of operational retaliation, not political persecution. That is defensible on this record, but it also shows how much turns on the surrounding evidence: who made the threats, what they said, when the violence escalated, and whether the record connects the persecutor’s animus to the applicant’s political views rather than to the applicant’s conduct.

## The downstream lesson for future cases

The biggest practical takeaway is for asylum applicants and their lawyers. If the claim sits at the intersection of political activity and anti-corruption or law-enforcement work, the record has to do more than show that the applicant was politically engaged and later threatened by dangerous people. It has to show why the persecutor cared. Was the applicant targeted because of party affiliation, campaign activity, or public opposition to a regime? Or was the applicant targeted because he became inconvenient to a criminal network?

Those are not the same thing, and *Jimenez* suggests the Tenth Circuit will not blur them lightly. That matters especially in countries where organized crime and political power overlap, because many applicants will have both kinds of exposure. The opinion suggests that courts will keep looking for a clean causal link to a protected ground, even when the underlying violence looks deeply political in the colloquial sense.

The decision also reinforces a broader trend in asylum adjudication: courts are wary of turning criminal retaliation into political persecution unless the evidence really supports that leap. That may leave some deserving applicants without protection, but it also preserves the statute’s core structure. Asylum is not a remedy for every form of danger; it is a remedy for a particular kind of danger, inflicted for a protected reason.

In that sense, *Jimenez v. Bondi* is less a dramatic doctrinal turn than a disciplined application of existing law. But it is still a useful signal. In mixed-motive cases, especially those involving politically connected organized crime, the winning argument will rarely be that politics was somewhere in the background. It will be that politics was central—and that the record proves it.



# Report It or Lose It? *Jimenez v. Bondi* and the Tenth Circuit’s Demanding View of Government “Unable or Unwilling” Protection

## Why the case matters

For asylum applicants, proving persecution is only half the fight. They also have to show that their home government either could not or would not protect them. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit used that requirement to deny relief to a Colombian family who credibly described threats, intimidation, and a frightening encounter with men tied to a local criminal figure. The court’s bottom line was not that nothing bad happened. It was that the record did not compel a conclusion that Colombia was unable or unwilling to help.

That makes the case important well beyond its facts. The panel’s analysis shows how much turns on notice: what did the applicants report, to whom, and with what documentation? And it shows how a court can treat isolated police contacts as too thin to prove systemic government failure, especially when the record also contains some evidence of state action against the relevant criminal activity.

## The court’s approach: notice first, inference second

The opinion, by Judge Tymkovich, is careful to say that failure to report persecution is not automatically fatal. That is doctrinally right. The Tenth Circuit has said before that an applicant can still win if filing a report would have been futile or dangerous. But in *Jimenez*, the panel concluded that the petitioners had not built that kind of record.

What did they do? They spoke with two individual police officers, both of whom essentially told them not to “look for trouble.” But they did not file a report, pursue a complaint beyond their immediate neighborhood, or use the law-enforcement channels Jimenez himself might have been expected to know about as someone with legal training and family ties to the justice system. The court treated that absence as significant because, under its prior precedent, the government-protection inquiry is partly about whether the state had notice and an opportunity to respond. Without notice, the court reasoned, there is little basis for concluding the state would have failed to act.

The panel also rejected the idea that the brief encounters with individual officers proved a broader government unwillingness to protect. That distinction matters. As the court noted, one unhelpful officer is not the same thing as a government policy of nonprotection. The opinion leans on that point to keep the analysis at the systemic level rather than letting a single anecdote stand in for the whole state.

## Why the searches of the property mattered

The government-protection analysis got another boost from a fact that cuts against the petitioners’ theory: the authorities had already tried to search and seize the property linked to Don Rafa’s operations. The court used that evidence to undercut the claim that the persecutors were effectively aligned with, or protected by, the Colombian state.

That is a subtle but important move. The court did not say those searches solved the problem, or that police activity elsewhere necessarily meant Jimenez was safe. Instead, it treated those efforts as evidence that the state had at least tried to address the underlying criminal conduct. In other words, the record showed some willingness to act, even if the petitioners experienced local indifference or hostility.

That framing matters doctrinally because asylum law asks whether the government is unable or unwilling “to control” the persecutors, not whether every police officer is sympathetic, competent, or brave. The panel’s approach suggests that evidence of formal law-enforcement activity can be powerful rebuttal evidence, even when applicants can point to frightening local encounters.

## The tension in the reasoning

The strongest part of the opinion is its insistence that courts should not infer statewide incapacity from a handful of informal conversations. That is sensible, especially under substantial-evidence review. But the decision also reveals the pressure that “notice” doctrine can place on asylum seekers.

In many real-world settings, people do not file reports because they fear retaliation, distrust the police, or believe the police are compromised. The Tenth Circuit acknowledges that possibility in the abstract, but in practice it demands concrete evidence that reporting would have been dangerous or futile. Without that evidence, the absence of a report becomes a major obstacle. That can look a lot like a de facto reporting requirement, even if the court is careful not to call it one.

There is a real policy question here. Should asylum law require applicants to risk approaching potentially hostile authorities before they can prove the government is unable or unwilling to protect them? *Jimenez* does not answer that question expressly, but its logic pushes in that direction: if you don’t report, you may lose; if you do report only informally, that may still not be enough; and if there is some evidence that the government elsewhere tried to intervene, the claim gets even harder.

## Downstream effects

The practical lesson for practitioners is obvious: build the record on reporting, or explain in detail why reporting was impossible. Applicants need evidence of threats to safety if they sought help, documentation of police refusals, and proof that complaints went nowhere. General assertions that the police are corrupt or aligned with persecutors will usually not carry the day on review.

The doctrinal lesson is broader. *Jimenez* fits a line of cases in which the Tenth Circuit treats the “unable or unwilling” prong as a fact-intensive, evidence-heavy inquiry that is difficult to satisfy without concrete, state-facing proof. Combined with substantial-evidence review, that approach gives immigration judges and the BIA substantial room to reject claims based on local hostility, even when the applicant’s fear is objectively understandable.

The upshot is that *Jimenez* is not just another denied asylum petition. It is a reminder that, in the Tenth Circuit at least, the path to proving government failure often runs through notice, documentation, and proof that the state had a real chance to act—and did not.



# Safe Somewhere in Colombia? Internal Relocation After *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why the relocation holding matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is, on its face, a routine asylum denial: no past persecution, no protected-ground nexus, no proof that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to help. But the panel’s alternative holding on internal relocation is especially important because it shows how asylum law often turns on geography as much as on danger. Even where threats are serious and tied to criminal networks, an applicant can lose if the record suggests the risk is local rather than nationwide and there is a plausible place elsewhere in the country to live safely.

That matters because internal-relocation doctrine is one of the main ways courts sort localized private violence from true countrywide persecution. In cases involving gangs, narco-linked actors, or politically connected criminals, applicants often argue that the threat is effectively everywhere. *Jimenez* is a reminder that those arguments need a developed record. The panel had little trouble concluding that this family had not shown that they could not reasonably resettle elsewhere in Colombia.

## How the panel got there

The court’s relocation analysis rested on a few concrete facts. First, the men who threatened the family told them to leave the neighborhood, not the country. Second, after the final incident, the family spent three weeks in Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogotá countryside without incident before deciding to come to the United States. Third, relatives remained in Colombia unharmed, and Jimenez’s father continued to work in the criminal-justice system.

Those facts allowed the panel to treat the threats as geographically bounded. The opinion is careful not to say that a temporary stay with relatives always proves safety, and it acknowledges the family’s argument that family presence alone does not guarantee security. But the court found that the petitioners offered too little beyond speculation. Their concerns about the burden of moving, the condition of Colombia’s judicial infrastructure, and the alleged political ties of their persecutors were, in the court’s view, underdeveloped and unsupported by the record.

The relocation holding also dovetailed with the court’s treatment of government protection. The panel emphasized that the record showed Colombian authorities had attempted searches and seizures at the property connected to Don Rafa, which cut against the idea that the state was categorically unable or unwilling to respond. That does not end every asylum case involving criminal actors, but it does make it harder to say that danger necessarily follows the applicant everywhere in the country.

## The doctrinal point: asylum is not just about fear, but about where fear can be escaped

There is a broader lesson here. Internal relocation is often treated as a secondary issue, but it can become the decisive issue in cases involving private violence. The doctrine asks a pragmatic question: even assuming the applicant faces real danger in one place, is there another part of the country where the danger can be avoided and relocation would be reasonable? In *Jimenez*, the Tenth Circuit answered yes because the threats were localized, the family had already shown they could live elsewhere for a period of time, and the evidence did not show a nationwide hunt.

That approach is doctrinally orthodox, but it also has sharp edges. Criminal organizations and politically connected actors often do not announce that they have national reach; they simply have it. So the real fight in future cases will be evidentiary: applicants will need to show not only that the persecutor is dangerous, but that the danger is portable. That can mean evidence of broad organizational presence, prior efforts to track the family across regions, or facts showing that relocation would be unreasonable for reasons tied to the applicant’s profile, health, finances, or family circumstances.

## Downstream effects

For practitioners, *Jimenez* will likely be cited for a straightforward proposition: when threats are tied to a specific neighborhood, a specific dispute, or a specific property, and the record shows a period of safe residence elsewhere, the government can win on internal relocation. For applicants, the opinion is a cautionary tale about overreliance on generalized assertions that a persecutor is powerful or politically connected. Courts want evidence that the danger extends beyond the original flashpoint.

At the same time, the opinion leaves room for stronger cases. The panel did not hold that family safety in the home country automatically defeats relocation, nor that any temporary stay elsewhere is dispositive. It held only that, on this record, substantial evidence supported the agency’s conclusion that the petitioners could safely and reasonably relocate within Colombia. In asylum law, that distinction matters a great deal.
