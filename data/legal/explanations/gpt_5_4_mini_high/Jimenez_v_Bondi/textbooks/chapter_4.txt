Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # Nexus to a Protected Ground: Political Opinion Versus Retaliation for the Don Rafa Conflict

## The protected-ground theory the court actually reviewed

The nexus question in this case was narrower on appeal than it may have looked at the agency level. Petitioners originally referred to both political opinion and particular social group, but they did not develop the particular social group theory in any meaningful way on appeal. As a result, the Tenth Circuit limited its review to political opinion. That is a good reminder that asylum law is not just about proving mistreatment; the applicant also has to identify and support a legally cognizable protected ground, and appellate courts will not supply arguments that were not actually pressed.

That narrowing mattered because it shaped the court’s entire causation analysis. The issue was not whether the petitioners had been threatened or frightened, but whether those threats were “on account of” their political opinion. The court therefore asked a very specific question: were the threats driven by Petitioners’ Liberal Party activity, or were they driven by Jimenez’s later conflict with Don Rafa and the property investigation?

## The statutory rule: a protected ground must be “one central reason”

The opinion applies the asylum statute’s nexus requirement, which asks whether persecution occurred “on account of” a protected ground. Under the REAL ID Act formulation the court quotes, the protected ground must be “at least one central reason” for the harm. That does not mean it must be the only reason. Persecutors can have mixed motives, and an asylum claim is not defeated just because the attacker was also motivated by personal, economic, or criminal reasons.

But “one central reason” is still a demanding standard. The court repeated the familiar language that the protected ground cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate,” and it cannot play only a “minor role.” In other words, the applicant has to show that the protected ground was truly important to the persecutor’s decision to act. The legal question is not whether politics was somewhere in the background, but whether it was central enough to explain the threats or violence.

## Why the chronology mattered

The court’s nexus analysis turned heavily on timing. Petitioners had some politically themed threats before the property dispute, including threatening calls while they were active in the Liberal Party campaign and a brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Standing alone, that might suggest a political component. But the court emphasized that there was no evidence tying Don Rafa to those early campaign-related threats.

The more serious escalation happened only after Jimenez became involved in the property matter. He visited the property, learned about its illicit uses, investigated further, and discovered that the tenant was connected to Don Rafa and a criminal operation. After that, the threats became more direct and more specific. Men confronted him at the furniture factory, accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, and blamed him for searches and seizures at the property. Later, when those same men returned, they assaulted an employee and again demanded to know where Petitioners were, saying the order came from Don Rafa. The court viewed those statements as the clearest evidence of motive.

That sequence of events led the court to read the case as a retaliation case, not a political persecution case. The threats escalated after Jimenez interfered with Don Rafa’s property and exposed a site used for narcotrafficking, prostitution, and other criminal activity. In the court’s view, the persecutors were reacting to a perceived threat to their operation, revenue, and security, not targeting Petitioners because of their Liberal Party affiliation.

## Why the court treated politics as secondary

The key move in the opinion is the distinction between a political identity and a political motive. Petitioners were in fact politically active, and the record showed that the attackers knew that. But knowing someone is political is not the same as persecuting them because of politics. The court concluded that any political reference was subordinate to the practical reason for the threats: Jimenez had become a problem for Don Rafa.

That is why the court focused so much on words like “snitch,” “prosecutor’s office,” and “searches and seizures.” Those phrases point to retaliation against law-enforcement cooperation or law-related interference, not necessarily to punishment for political ideology. The court also noted that Petitioners had not shown Don Rafa was behind the early campaign threats, which weakened their attempt to connect the entire course of events to political opinion. On this record, the political aspect looked like context, not causation.

The opinion’s bottom line on this point is very clear: Petitioners’ political opinion was “merely tangential and subordinate” to the real reason they were threatened. Even if politics played some role in how the persecutors viewed them, it was not a central reason for the harm.

## Deference and the appellate posture

The substantial-evidence standard was crucial. The Tenth Circuit did not ask whether it would have drawn the same inference as the IJ and BIA. It asked whether the record compelled the opposite conclusion. Because the agency’s view—that the threats were mainly retaliation for Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa and his perceived role in exposing criminal activity—was reasonable, the court could not reweigh the evidence.

That is the practical lesson from the nexus discussion. When the record supports two plausible motives, the applicant must do more than show that a protected ground was one possible explanation. He must show that the protected ground was central enough that no reasonable adjudicator could see the case another way. Here, the court held that Petitioners did not meet that burden. Because they failed to establish nexus to political opinion, their asylum claim failed, and their withholding claim failed as well because withholding requires an even higher showing.
