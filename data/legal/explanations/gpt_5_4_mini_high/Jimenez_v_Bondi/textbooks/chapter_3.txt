Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Why the Court Found No Past Persecution

## Persecution Is a Case-Law Term, Not a Statutory Definition

The starting point in the court’s analysis is that the Immigration and Nationality Act does not define “persecution.” That matters because the court had to rely on Tenth Circuit precedent to supply the meaning. In this circuit, persecution requires more than unpleasant or frightening treatment. It means the infliction of suffering or harm in a way the law regards as offensive, and it must involve more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.

That framing sets a relatively high threshold. The court was not asking whether the petitioners had been mistreated or reasonably frightened. It was asking whether the mistreatment, taken at its worst, rose to the level that asylum law recognizes as persecution. The opinion repeatedly emphasizes that point: not all harassment qualifies, and not every threat becomes persecution simply because it is serious or repeated.

## The Court Considered the Incidents Collectively

The petitioners did not rely on a single event. Their claim was built from a sequence of escalating incidents: threatening phone calls tied to their Liberal Party activity, a death-threat brochure left at Jimenez’s factory, an armed confrontation in which two men ordered Jimenez to leave the neighborhood, a later visit by the same men who assaulted an employee and threatened to kill the petitioners, and the school incident involving one of their children. The court did not analyze each event in isolation. Consistent with Tenth Circuit doctrine, it considered the cumulative effect of the whole course of conduct.

That cumulative approach is important for students reading asylum cases. A court does not ask whether any one event, standing alone, is enough. Multiple lesser harms can sometimes add up to persecution. But the court’s point here was that even when the incidents were stacked together, the total picture still fell short. The opinion is careful to acknowledge the seriousness of what happened, but it concludes that the accumulated mistreatment did not cross the legal line into past persecution.

## The Lack of Lasting Physical Harm Mattered

One fact carried special weight in the court’s reasoning: the petitioners conceded that they were not physically harmed, and they also said in briefing that they suffered no lasting physical injury. The court made clear that physical harm is not strictly required to prove persecution. Still, the absence of injury mattered because it left the case resting largely on threats, intimidation, and emotional distress.

That distinction is central to understanding the opinion. The court was not saying that only beatings, torture, or detention can count as persecution. But it was saying that where the record shows no physical injury, the remaining harms must be especially severe to justify asylum relief. Here, the petitioners experienced fear and stress, but the court found that those effects, without more, did not meet the persecution threshold.

## Threats and Emotional Distress Were Not Enough

The petitioners’ strongest argument was that the repeated threats themselves amounted to persecution, especially because they produced lasting psychological harm. Duarte testified that she had trouble sleeping and resting, and that one of the children suffered panic attacks and stress. The court did not dismiss those effects as unreal or unimportant. But it treated them as consequences of fear, not as independent proof of persecution.

That is where the opinion draws an important line between serious fear and legally sufficient persecution. The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that threats alone are generally not enough. The court quoted that principle directly and then applied it here. Even though the threats were alarming and repeated, they were still threats. They were not accompanied by the kind of severe physical abuse, prolonged confinement, or comparable harm that typically pushes a case over the persecution threshold.

The court also pointed out that it has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based only on threats plus emotional or psychological harm. That does not mean such harm is irrelevant. It means the harm must be severe enough, in context, to amount to persecution, and the panel did not think this record compelled that conclusion.

## Prior Cases Showed How High the Severity Bar Is

To explain why it rejected the petitioners’ claim, the court compared this case to prior Tenth Circuit and unpublished decisions. Those cases functioned as benchmarks. In one, a 10-hour arrest and detention plus threats still did not amount to persecution. In another, four phone threats and one vague in-person threat did not qualify. In yet another, threats with machetes and six robberies were still insufficient because the threats were tied to compliance with a demand.

Those comparisons mattered because they showed that the Tenth Circuit’s persecution standard is demanding. The court was not saying the petitioners’ experiences were trivial. It was saying that, measured against circuit precedent, their mistreatment was not worse than conduct that the court had already found insufficient in earlier cases. That made it easier for the panel to conclude that the Board of Immigration Appeals was not compelled to find past persecution here.

## Substantial Evidence Controlled the Outcome

The standard of review shaped everything. The court was reviewing the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard, which is highly deferential. Under that standard, the Board’s finding is conclusive unless the record would compel any reasonable adjudicator to reach the opposite result. So the question was not whether the panel might have viewed the facts sympathetically or even whether persecution was a possible reading of the record. The question was whether the evidence forced that conclusion.

The answer was no. Because the petitioners had no lasting physical harm, because the threats alone did not establish persecution, because the psychological effects did not independently carry the claim, and because precedent set a high bar that this case did not clear, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s conclusion. The bottom line is that the aggregate harm was serious and frightening, but not legally enough to count as past persecution under the INA.
