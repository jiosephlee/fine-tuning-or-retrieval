Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What does "substantial evidence" review actually let a court of appeals do in an asylum case?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The Tenth Circuit keeps saying it reviews the BIA's factual findings for "substantial evidence" and cannot reweigh the record unless any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to reach the opposite result. In practice, how deferential is that? If the record could support either side, does the noncitizen basically lose on petition for review? I'm trying to understand why the court spends so much time saying there was "substantial evidence" for the BIA instead of deciding for itself whether these facts amount to persecution or political targeting.
Answer:
In this case, “substantial evidence” review means the court of appeals is **not deciding asylum facts from scratch**. It is asking only whether the BIA’s factual findings are **supported by enough evidence that a reasonable adjudicator could agree with them**.

The Tenth Circuit states the standard this way:

> “The BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”

That is very deferential. As the court also puts it, it is **“not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence.”** So the court does not ask, “What would we have decided?” It asks, “Could the BIA reasonably have decided this way on this record?”

### Practical effect
Yes — **if the record could reasonably support either side, the petitioner usually loses** on petition for review. To win, the noncitizen must show the evidence doesn’t just favor the other side, but **compels** it.

That is exactly how the court uses the standard in *Jimenez v. Bondi*:

- On **past persecution**, the petitioners had threats and emotional distress, but the court said those facts did **not compel** a finding of persecution.
- On **nexus**, the court accepted the BIA’s view that the threats were driven mainly by Jimenez’s dispute with Don Rafa, not his political opinion.
- On **government protection**, the court held the record did not compel the conclusion that Colombia was unable or unwilling to protect them.

So the court was not saying, “We think the BIA is obviously right in an ordinary sense.” It was saying something narrower: **the record does not force the opposite conclusion**.

### Why the court spends so much time on “substantial evidence”
Because in asylum cases, many issues are **fact questions** or **fact-heavy determinations**. Under the statutory scheme, the agency — not the court of appeals — is the primary factfinder. The court’s job is mainly to check whether the agency’s factual conclusions are supportable on the administrative record.

### Bottom line
“Substantial evidence” review is highly deferential. In practice:

- **Agency wins if its view is reasonable**, even if another view is also reasonable.
- **Petitioner wins only if the record compels the opposite result.**
- The court of appeals does **not** independently re-decide persecution, motive, or government protection on a blank slate.

That’s why the opinion repeatedly says there is “substantial evidence” supporting the BIA rather than substituting the court’s own judgment.

### Why weren't repeated death threats, being held at gunpoint, and the incident involving the child enough to count as past persecution?
Question:
The opinion says courts look at incidents cumulatively, not one by one. Here the family got repeated threatening phone calls, a brochure saying "death to snitches from Liberal Party," Jimenez was held at gunpoint, armed men later came back and assaulted an employee while looking for the family, and then a stranger put a hand over the son's mouth and told him to run to his dad. Even if there was no lasting physical injury, this seems much more serious than just verbal harassment. What is the doctrinal line between "serious threats" and persecution in asylum law, especially when the threats are repeated and accompanied by armed confrontations?
Answer:
Because, under Tenth Circuit asylum law, the line is not “serious or frightening threats,” but **harm rising to the level of persecution**—and persecution is more than “restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*; *Jimenez v. Bondi*.

The panel in *Jimenez* did exactly what you describe: it **considered the incidents cumulatively**. But it still held that, even taken together, they did not compel a finding of past persecution because:

1. **Threats alone are usually not enough.**  
   The court expressly said: “**threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution**.” It cited cases where even multiple threats, including in-person threats, did not amount to persecution. See *Karki*; *Chhetri*.

2. **There was no physical harm or lasting injury.**  
   The petitioners conceded they were **not physically harmed** and had **no lasting physical injury**. The court noted that physical harm is not required, but the overall harm still has to be severe enough to count as persecution. Here, the incidents were alarming, but they did not involve beating, detention, torture, or other sustained violence.

3. **The incidents were intimidation, not a sustained campaign of severe mistreatment.**  
   Being held at gunpoint, the factory visit by armed men, and the encounter with the child were all serious and understandably traumatic. But the court treated them as **threatening confrontations** rather than harm of the kind that crosses the persecution threshold. The child incident, in particular, was frightening, but the record shows the child was not injured or abducted.

4. **Cumulative review does not mean every cluster of threats becomes persecution.**  
   The court reiterated that it must look at the record as a whole, but that principle does not erase the baseline rule that persecution requires **more than sporadic threats or harassment**. The panel concluded that, even in combination, these events were not enough.

So the doctrinal line is this: **repeated threats plus an armed encounter may be enough if they result in or are accompanied by sufficiently severe harm, restraint, or violence; but in the Tenth Circuit, threats and intimidation without physical harm or comparable severity often remain below the persecution threshold.**

Here, the court’s bottom line was not that the events were trivial—they plainly were not—but that **substantial evidence supported the BIA’s view that they were still not “persecution” as that term is used in asylum law**. Because of that deferential standard of review, the panel would not reweigh the facts and substitute its own judgment.

### How did the court decide political opinion was only tangential when the threats mentioned the Liberal Party and said they were "in politics"?
Question:
The nexus part is where I get most lost. The court says the main motive was Jimenez's involvement with Don Rafa's property and criminal operation, not the family's political opinion. But some of the threats explicitly referenced the Liberal Party, and the attackers also called them "snitches for the police" who were "in politics." In a mixed-motive case, how do courts figure out whether political opinion is "one central reason" instead of just incidental? Does the timing of the escalation after the property dispute outweigh the explicit political language in the threats?
Answer:
The key is that **asylum nexus is about the persecutor’s motive, not just the words used in the threats**.

Under the statute, political opinion must be **“at least one central reason”** for the harm. As the Tenth Circuit put it, it cannot be **“incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate”** to another motive. And on judicial review, the court does **not** ask whether it might have weighed the evidence differently; it asks only whether the BIA’s finding was **compelled** to be the opposite.

That is why the panel could acknowledge the political references and still reject nexus. In this record, the court thought the **more specific and more direct motive** was retaliation for Jimenez’s involvement with **Don Rafa’s property and criminal operation**:

- the threats **escalated after** Jimenez got involved in the property dispute and investigated the property;
- the men said he was a **“snitch” for the prosecutor’s office** and blamed him for **searches and seizures** at the property;
- they later said the order came from **Don Rafa**;
- the references to the Liberal Party and to being “in politics” were present, but the court viewed them as **secondary** in light of that more immediate retaliation theory.

So the answer to your timing question is: **yes, timing mattered, but not by itself**. The court relied on the **sequence plus the content** of the later threats. The fact that the explicit political language came alongside accusations about police/prosecutor cooperation and the property dispute made politics look **incidental** rather than central.

In other words, the court was not saying political language is irrelevant. It was saying that **in context**, the evidence supported a finding that the real reason for the threats was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s operations, not his political opinion. That is why the court concluded political opinion was “merely tangential and subordinate” to the main reason for harm.

### Does an asylum applicant usually need to file a formal police report to prove the government was unable or unwilling to protect them?
Question:
The court seemed to put a lot of weight on the fact that Petitioners only spoke to two individual police officers and did not really pursue the matter further, despite claiming police corruption and government ties to Don Rafa. I understand that lack of reporting can undercut the claim, but how formal does the reporting have to be? Is talking to patrol officers ever enough to put the government on notice? And what kind of evidence is usually needed to show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, so the failure to report does not sink the claim?
Answer:
**Short answer: no.** An asylum applicant does **not always have to file a formal police report**, but the applicant **usually must show that the government had notice and a chance to respond** to the harm. In the Tenth Circuit’s formulation, that “unable or unwilling” inquiry looks at whether the applicant sought protection, and **a failure to report can undercut the claim**—though it is **not automatically fatal**. The applicant can still prevail by showing that reporting would have been **futile or dangerous**. *Jimenez v. Bondi* (citing *Singh* and *Aviles-Gonzalez*).

What *Jimenez* makes clear is that **talking to individual officers is not necessarily enough**. Petitioners said they spoke to two police officers, but the court held that this did not establish that the **Colombian government** was unwilling or unable to protect them. The problem was not just the lack of a formal report; it was that there was **nothing showing those officers’ reaction reflected the state’s position**, and nothing showing Petitioners pursued help through broader channels. The court quoted the idea that a “one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer” does not necessarily prove governmental unwillingness or inability.

So, in practice:

- **Formal report not required in every case**
- But some evidence of **government notice/opportunity to act** usually is
- **Casual complaints to patrol officers** may help, but often won’t be enough by themselves
- If there was **no report**, the applicant needs evidence that reporting would have been **useless or unsafe**

What kind of evidence would support futility or danger? *Jimenez* suggests evidence like:
- credible testimony that police are **complicit** or directly tied to the persecutors,
- evidence the applicant or others **tried to report and were ignored or threatened**,
- proof that officials **participated in the abuse** or actively protected the persecutors,
- other record evidence showing the government’s response would have been impossible or dangerous.

But in *Jimenez*, the record actually cut the other way: the court noted that the government had **attempted searches and seizures** at the relevant property, which undermined the claim that officials were aligned with Don Rafa or would simply refuse to act.

So the bottom line is: **no formal police report is categorically required, but some meaningful effort to obtain official protection usually is—unless the applicant can convincingly show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.**

### Who bears the burden on internal relocation when past persecution was not established, and why did three weeks with relatives matter so much?
Question:
The relocation discussion confused me because the court says Petitioners already failed to show eligibility for asylum, but then it still says they could safely relocate within Colombia. I thought a presumption of future persecution arises only after past persecution is shown, and then the government can rebut it through safe relocation. If there is no past persecution finding, who has the burden to show relocation is or is not reasonable? Also, why is the fact that they stayed three weeks at Jimenez's mother's home considered meaningful evidence of safe relocation, rather than just a temporary hiding place?
Answer:
Because Petitioners **did not establish past persecution**, there was **no presumption of future persecution for the government to rebut**. In that posture, the **asylum applicants keep the burden** of proving they are refugees by a preponderance of the evidence, which includes showing a well-founded fear of future persecution and, where relevant, that they **cannot avoid the harm by relocating within the country**.

That is why the panel’s relocation discussion comes only **as an alternative**: “Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, **but even if they had**…” The court was not shifting the burden back and forth in the same way it would after a past-persecution finding. It was saying: even assuming future fear, the record still supports internal relocation as a safe, reasonable option.

As for the **three weeks at Jimenez’s mother’s home**, the court treated that as **real evidence of actual safety**, not just a theoretical hiding place. Internal relocation is a factual question: can the applicant live elsewhere in the country and avoid the persecutors? Here, the panel pointed to three things together:

1. the family **actually stayed unharmed** in the Bogota countryside for three weeks;
2. the threats told them to leave the **neighborhood**, not the country; and
3. relatives remained in Colombia unharmed.

So the significance of the three weeks is not that any short stay automatically defeats asylum. It is that, on this record, it was **some concrete proof that moving away from the immediate area worked** and that the danger looked localized rather than nationwide. Under substantial-evidence review, that was enough for the court to say the record supports safe, reasonable internal relocation.

### Why does losing on the asylum claim automatically mean losing withholding of removal here?
Question:
At the end, the court says that because Petitioners failed to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution for asylum, they necessarily failed to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. I thought asylum and withholding are distinct forms of relief, with withholding being mandatory if the standard is met. Is the court saying this only because the same factual failures (no persecution, no nexus, no government inability/unwillingness, relocation possible) defeat both claims? Or are there situations where an applicant can lose asylum but still win withholding?
Answer:
Yes — in **this opinion**, the court is using a shorthand that is only true **because of the way Petitioners lost asylum**.

### What the court means
Asylum and withholding of removal are separate forms of relief, but they overlap a lot on the merits:

- **Asylum** requires showing a **well-founded fear of persecution** on account of a protected ground.
- **Withholding of removal** requires a **higher showing** — essentially that persecution is **more likely than not** on account of a protected ground.

So if an applicant cannot prove the **lower asylum standard on the same facts**, the applicant ordinarily cannot prove the **higher withholding standard** either. That is what the court means when it says the Petitioners “necessarily fail” to meet the withholding standard. The court cites **Carias-Mejia** and **Uanreroro** for that proposition.

### Why that applies here
The panel did **not** deny asylum for some asylum-only reason. It denied asylum because the record failed on the substantive elements of the persecution claim:

- no past persecution,
- no nexus to political opinion,
- no showing the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them,
- and safe internal relocation was available.

Those are the kinds of factual failures that defeat both asylum and withholding. Since withholding is the **more demanding** form of relief, the same evidentiary record cannot support it if it already falls short of asylum.

### Could someone lose asylum but still win withholding?
**Yes, sometimes.** That usually happens when asylum is denied for a reason that is **unique to asylum**, not because the underlying persecution claim fails. For example, asylum can be denied for reasons that do not necessarily foreclose withholding, such as certain procedural or discretionary issues.

But that is **not** what happened in *Jimenez*. Here, the court rejected the claim on the **merits**, so the withholding claim fell with it.

### Bottom line
The court is **not** saying asylum and withholding are the same thing. It is saying:

> because Petitioners failed to prove the factual predicates for asylum, they also failed to prove the even more demanding standard for withholding.

That is a merits-based shortcut, not a blanket rule that every asylum loss automatically dooms withholding in every case.

### Is the court's refusal to address the particular social group and CAT arguments a waiver issue, an exhaustion issue, or both?
Question:
The opinion says Petitioners argued nexus based on both political opinion and particular social group, but then the court says they did not brief the existence of a particular social group or their membership in it, so review was limited to political opinion. It also notes they did not appeal the CAT denial or the due process claim. I'm trying to separate the doctrines here: when does an issue get rejected because it was not exhausted before the BIA, and when is it considered waived because it was not adequately briefed in the court of appeals?
Answer:
**Short answer: the court is treating these as appellate waiver/abandonment issues, not as exhaustion issues — at least on the face of this opinion.**

### 1. The particular-social-group theory
The panel says:

> Petitioners, however, did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group. Rather, in arguing the nexus requirement, Petitioners allege that they were persecuted … because of their political opinion. Accordingly, we limit our review to whether Petitioners establish a nexus … to their political opinion.

That is a classic **waiver/forfeiture in the court of appeals** problem: the issue was mentioned, but not actually developed in the briefing, so the court declines to consider it. The court is **not** saying here that the PSG theory was unexhausted before the BIA.

### 2. The CAT and due process claims
The opinion also says:

> They do not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT or their due process claim.

That means those issues were **abandoned on petition for review**. Again, that is **appellate waiver**, not an exhaustion holding. The court is simply saying the petitioners did not ask it to review those rulings.

### 3. Where exhaustion would fit
**Exhaustion** is a different doctrine: it asks whether the petitioner raised the issue to the **BIA** before seeking judicial review. If an argument was never presented to the agency, the court may refuse to review it for failure to exhaust.

But this opinion does **not** invoke exhaustion in the quoted passages. Instead, it disposes of the PSG issue because it was **not briefed**, and the CAT/due process issues because they were **not appealed**.

### Bottom line
- **PSG**: waiver/forfeiture in the Tenth Circuit for inadequate briefing  
- **CAT and due process**: waiver/abandonment because not appealed  
- **Exhaustion**: a separate possible issue, but not the basis the court states here
