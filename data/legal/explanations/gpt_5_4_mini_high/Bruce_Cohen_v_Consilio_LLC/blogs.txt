Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Who Gets the Penalty? The Eighth Circuit Reads Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act as a Commissioner-Only Remedy

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* matters because it draws a sharp line between recovering unpaid wages and recovering statutory penalties. That distinction is often the difference between a routine wage case and a high-stakes one. Here, Consilio had already paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen claimed were due. What remained was the penalty layer — and the court held that, under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act, that penalty is not privately recoverable by an employee under § 181.101. It is a remedy reserved to the Commissioner.

That holding narrows the leverage private plaintiffs can bring in Minnesota wage litigation. It also offers a clean example of textualist statutory interpretation: when the legislature knows how to give employees a penalty remedy and does so expressly in some provisions, courts are reluctant to infer the same remedy from a different provision that channels penalty enforcement through an agency.

## The court’s reading of the statute

The key move in Judge Kelly’s opinion is structural. Cohen sued under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, and § 181.171 does authorize a civil action for violations of certain provisions, including § 181.101. But § 181.171 does not itself create every remedy in the abstract. Instead, it says an employer is liable for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” and separately for “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”

That second clause did the heavy lifting. The court looked to § 181.101, the provision Cohen said Consilio violated, and asked whether that section itself provides a private penalty remedy. The answer, in the panel’s view, was no. Section 181.101(a) speaks in terms of the Commissioner serving a demand for payment, then collecting wages and a penalty if payment is not made. The statute also says that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee. But for the employee, the statute only preserves the ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.”

That is a meaningful choice of words. The opinion treats it as a deliberate allocation of enforcement authority: employees may sue for wages, but the penalty mechanism belongs to the Commissioner. The court reinforced that reading by pointing to neighboring provisions, §§ 181.13 and 181.14, where the legislature expressly authorized discharged or resigning employees to “charge and collect a penalty.” The contrast is the point. Minnesota knew how to confer a private penalty remedy when it wanted to; the omission in § 181.101 was therefore treated as intentional.

## The practical effect: a narrower private remedy

As a matter of doctrine, the opinion is straightforward and hard to quarrel with. The court did not say employees lack a cause of action under the Payment of Wages Act. It said something narrower but important: the statute gives them a right to sue for wages, not a private right to collect the average-daily-wage penalty that the Commissioner may pursue in the statutory demand process.

That distinction will matter in real cases. Wage plaintiffs often file suit after an employer has already corrected the pay problem, or after the employer tenders the unpaid wages once litigation begins. In that setting, penalties become the main remaining source of exposure. By reading § 181.101’s penalty language as commissioner-only, the Eighth Circuit makes it harder for employees to convert a wage dispute into a private penalty claim unless the statute clearly authorizes that result.

The opinion also reflects a broader pattern in wage-and-hour litigation: courts are increasingly attentive to whether a statute creates a private enforcement right, or merely a public enforcement scheme with some benefits flowing to the employee. Here, the panel treated the Commissioner as the gatekeeper for penalties under § 181.101, not as a redundant participant in a private lawsuit. That may promote statutory coherence, but it also means enforcement depends more heavily on agency action.

## What this means going forward

For Minnesota litigants, the lesson is to read the remedy provisions closely. A general private cause of action does not automatically carry every remedy associated with a wage statute. If the legislature has placed penalties in the hands of the Commissioner, courts are likely to respect that allocation.

That has at least two downstream effects. First, plaintiffs’ lawyers will need to be more careful about which claims they plead and what remedies they seek. A claim for unpaid wages under § 181.171 remains viable; a private claim for the § 181.101 penalty does not. Second, employers may have more room to limit exposure by paying back wages promptly, though that obviously does not eliminate all litigation risk.

The panel did not resolve everything in the case. It vacated and remanded the separate MFLSA penalty issue because the live-jurisdiction question was unresolved after Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages, and the special concurrence flagged standing concerns as well. But on the Payment of Wages Act issue, the court’s answer was clear: the average-daily-wage penalty belongs to the Commissioner, not to the employee.

For Minnesota wage litigation, that is a significant narrowing of the private remedial arsenal. And for statutory interpreters, it is a textbook reminder that when the legislature speaks with precision in one section and withholds the same language in another, courts are likely to take the distinction seriously.



# Laidlaw in the Wage-and-Hour Context: Standing and Mootness After the Employer Pays Everything Owed

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* is about more than one lawyer-reviewer’s overtime claim. It asks a recurring federal-courts question with real practical bite in wage-and-hour litigation: once an employer has paid all back wages and liquidated damages, can the plaintiff still keep the case alive by seeking statutory penalties that are payable to the state?

That issue matters because modern wage cases often turn on exactly this sequence. An employer discovers exposure, makes the named plaintiff whole, and then argues the federal case is over. Plaintiffs respond that penalties, fees, or injunctive relief still supply a live controversy. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion does not give employers a clean escape hatch, but it does insist that Article III still matters. Whether the case survives now depends on whether the challenged overtime policy is still in place, and whether any alleged violation is truly capable of recurring.

## What the Eighth Circuit actually did

The panel, in an opinion by Judge Kelly, treated the case statute by statute. It had little trouble affirming the dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim, holding that the statute’s penalty provision is enforced by the Commissioner, not the employee. It likewise rejected the Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim, largely because Cohen had already been paid and did not marshal a statutory basis for penalties. And it refused to entertain his request for injunctive relief, because that request was not properly framed in the district court.

The most interesting part is the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act claim. There, Cohen had stipulated that Consilio paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. So the only thing left was statutory penalties, which the parties agreed would go to the Commissioner rather than Cohen personally. That setup forced the court into Article III territory. A private plaintiff who has been made whole on his own wages has a harder time showing a continuing personal stake in penalties paid to the government. But under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, civil penalties can sometimes redress an injury by deterring future violations. So the real question became whether Consilio’s challenged overtime policy was still operating.

The panel’s answer was careful, not categorical. It said that if the policy remains, Cohen’s penalty claim may still be viable. If the policy has been abandoned and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot. Because the record did not resolve that question, the court vacated the judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded so the district court can decide whether it has jurisdiction at all.

## Why *Laidlaw* helps Cohen — and why it may not save him

The court’s reliance on *Laidlaw* is the key doctrinal move. In *Laidlaw*, the Supreme Court held that civil penalties payable to the government can still redress a plaintiff’s injury, because they may deter the defendant from continuing the unlawful conduct. That principle has long prevented defendants from mooting cases simply by pointing out that the money would not go to the named plaintiff.

But *Laidlaw* is not a blank check. The deterrence rationale works best when there is an ongoing violation or a credible threat of recurrence. That is why the Eighth Circuit framed the issue through the voluntary-cessation doctrine: a defendant cannot unilaterally erase federal jurisdiction by stopping the challenged conduct unless it is absolutely clear the conduct will not recur. In other words, paying back wages does not automatically end the case if the employer’s policy is still alive.

That distinction is important in wage-and-hour litigation. If the challenged pay practice remains in effect, penalties can still serve a forward-looking function. If the employer has genuinely changed course, though, the plaintiff’s claim for government-paid penalties starts to look much less like redress for a live injury and more like a request for an advisory opinion about past conduct.

## Judge Kobes’s concurrence points to a deeper problem

Judge Kobes concurred in the remand, but he flagged a sharper issue: perhaps the real problem is not mootness but standing. He was not convinced that *Laidlaw* squarely answers whether Cohen has Article III standing to seek MFLSA penalties at all, especially given differences between the penalties in *Laidlaw* and the penalties authorized here. The parties had not briefed that question, so he was content to vacate and remand on mootness grounds first.

That separate writing is a useful reminder that standing and mootness are related but not identical. Standing asks whether the plaintiff had a sufficient personal stake when suit began. Mootness asks whether that stake still exists now. In a case like this, where the plaintiff has already received all individualized monetary relief, courts may disagree about which doctrine does the heavy lifting. Judge Kelly’s opinion treats the issue as one of continuing controversy; Judge Kobes suggests the redressability problem may run deeper.

## The downstream effect

The practical effect of the decision is modest but meaningful. Plaintiffs in wage cases cannot assume that a statutory penalty claim will automatically keep a federal case alive after the employer has paid the wages and liquidated damages. They still need a live dispute, and they may need to show that the challenged policy continues or could realistically recur. Employers, meanwhile, should not assume that cutting a check moots everything. If the allegedly unlawful practice remains on the books, penalty exposure may continue.

More broadly, the case is another sign that federal courts are still wrestling with the limits of *Laidlaw* outside classic environmental litigation. The deterrence logic is familiar, but it becomes less intuitive when the plaintiff has been fully compensated and the only remaining relief runs to the state. The Eighth Circuit did not resolve that tension. It just pushed the jurisdictional question back to the district court, where the facts about Consilio’s overtime policy will now do the work Article III requires.



## When “Wage Theft” Is Not a Penalty Claim: The Narrow Private Reach of Minnesota’s Notice Provisions

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio* is a useful reminder that not every wage-law violation comes with a privately enforceable penalty attached. That matters because modern wage-and-hour litigation often turns on a familiar move: once the employer pays the underlying wages, plaintiffs try to keep the case alive by recasting a notice, timing, or recordkeeping violation as a standalone penalty claim. In this case, the court declined to take that step for Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act.

The practical consequence is significant. If the statute does not clearly authorize private penalties for a notice violation, then curing the wage deficiency may sharply reduce exposure. And more broadly, the decision signals that courts in the Eighth Circuit are unlikely to infer high-value penalty remedies from wage statutes that speak in terms of notice obligations unless the legislature has said so expressly.

## What the court did

Cohen’s MWTA theory was based on Consilio’s alleged failure to give notice of a change in overtime policy. But by the time the case reached summary judgment, the parties had stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages he claimed and all liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. So the remaining issue was not unpaid wages. It was penalties.

That framing made Cohen’s MWTA claim hard to sustain. The panel noted that Cohen pointed to no statutory authority permitting civil penalties under the particular MWTA notice provisions he invoked, Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4). With no meaningful argument and no textual hook for penalties, the court simply affirmed the dismissal.

The opinion is short on extended statutory exegesis here, but that brevity is itself telling. The court did not treat the Wage Theft Act’s notice requirements as a free-standing private enforcement device. Instead, it treated them as obligations whose violation does not automatically generate a private penalty claim, especially once the employer has paid the wages at issue.

That approach is consistent with the court’s broader statutory method elsewhere in the opinion. On the MPWA claim, the panel emphasized that the Minnesota legislature knows how to authorize penalties when it wants to do so, pointing to other provisions in the wage code that expressly allow an aggrieved employee to “charge and collect a penalty.” The absence of comparable language in the MWTA notice provisions mattered.

## The doctrinal takeaway

The real doctrinal point is not that the MWTA is toothless. It is that courts are reluctant to convert a statutory notice requirement into a privately recoverable penalty regime absent clear legislative authorization. That distinction matters because notice provisions serve a different function from wage-payment provisions. They are meant to improve transparency and compliance. They are not necessarily meant to create a separate damages engine once the underlying wages have been satisfied.

This matters even more in cases like Cohen’s, where the employer’s alleged violation is technical or historical and the employee has already been made whole on pay. In that setting, plaintiffs often press for penalties as the only remaining remedy. The Eighth Circuit’s response was essentially: not unless the statute says so. That is a sensible limit. Without it, every payroll-notice misstep could become a potentially large penalty case, untethered from actual wage loss.

At the same time, the decision leaves some room for future litigation. The panel did not purport to announce a categorical rule that the MWTA never authorizes penalties. It resolved the case on the record before it: Cohen had been paid, he did not identify a statutory basis for penalties, and he offered no developed appellate argument for reversal. So the ruling is best read as a narrow refusal to infer a remedy, not a broad construction of every possible MWTA enforcement path.

## The downstream effects

For employers, the decision is a useful defense-side data point. Fixing wage underpayments may not eliminate all exposure, but it may eliminate a lot of it if the remaining claim depends on penalties that the statute does not expressly authorize. For employees and plaintiffs’ lawyers, the lesson is equally clear: if the theory is a penalty claim based on a notice violation, the complaint and briefing need a specific statutory foundation, not just the rhetorical force of “wage theft.”

And for lawmakers, the opinion is a nudge. If Minnesota intends the Wage Theft Act’s notice provisions to support private penalty actions, that intent needs to be written down with far more clarity. Courts will not do that work for them.



# You Can’t Amend a Wage Case in a Summary Judgment Brief

## Why this case matters

*Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* is nominally a wage-and-hour case. But the Eighth Circuit’s most durable lesson is procedural: when your theory of relief changes, you do not get to swap it in at summary judgment and hope the court treats that as good enough.

That point may sound technical, but it matters a lot in employment cases. Employers often cure the core back-pay dispute while leaving only penalties, statutory add-ons, or prospective relief in play. Once that happens, the litigation can become a contest over remedies more than wages. The Eighth Circuit’s decision is a reminder that even in that posture, plaintiffs still have to plead, preserve, and litigate their remedial theories in an orderly way.

## The litigation narrowed fast

Cohen, a Minnesota-based hourly document-review attorney for Consilio, sued over unpaid overtime under Minnesota wage statutes. Before the merits were fully litigated, Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages Cohen said he was owed. The parties then stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA, and that if the court granted summary judgment for Consilio, the case would be fully resolved.

That left a much narrower dispute: statutory penalties and injunctive relief. The district court rejected all of Cohen’s remaining claims. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the rejection of his Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim, remanded the MFLSA penalties issue for a jurisdictional look, and disposed of the MWTA claim because Cohen offered no meaningful statutory basis for penalties.

The most interesting part, though, is what happened to the injunction.

## Why the injunction request failed

Cohen argued on appeal that he was entitled to an injunction requiring Consilio to rescind its illegal overtime policy. But the Eighth Circuit said that request was not properly before it.

The problem was not that injunctive relief is categorically unavailable in wage cases. The problem was procedural. In the complaint, Cohen had sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief. Later, in summary judgment briefing, he shifted and asked for a different injunction: one aimed at withdrawing the 2019 policy. The district court did not consider that new request, and the Eighth Circuit would not either.

The panel’s explanation was blunt and sensible: new claims belong in amended complaints, not in opposition briefs. That is a straightforward application of Rule 15 and ordinary notice principles. A summary judgment brief is supposed to test the claims already in the case, not quietly rewrite them.

## The deeper procedural lesson

This is the kind of ruling that can seem minor in the abstract and be extremely important in practice. Requests for injunctive relief are not fungible. An injunction aimed at one policy may require a different factual record, different proof of ongoing harm, and different Article III analysis than an injunction aimed at a different policy or a different version of the same policy.

That matters especially here, because the rest of the case had already narrowed so sharply. Once Cohen had been paid his individual overtime and liquidated damages, the case was no longer about making him whole in the ordinary sense. It was about whether anything remained live — penalties, prospective relief, or both. In that posture, a plaintiff cannot simply keep adding remedial theories as the case evolves.

The Eighth Circuit’s approach also fits the basic fairness concerns that underlie pleading rules. A defendant is entitled to know what it must defend against. If a plaintiff could change the requested injunction in a summary judgment brief, the defendant would have little reason to take discovery, develop evidence, or make strategic decisions around the new theory. The litigation would become a moving target.

## The court’s broader signal on wage litigation

The opinion has downstream significance beyond Minnesota wage law. Wage cases often travel the same path: a plaintiff sues for unpaid wages, the employer pays the principal amount, and the remaining fight becomes about statutory penalties, liquidated damages, or an injunction to stop the challenged practice. Courts are increasingly likely to police the line between those remedies and the pleadings that launch the case.

That is not anti-plaintiff; it is pro-orderly litigation. If a plaintiff wants prospective relief, the complaint should say so clearly and the theory should be preserved consistently. If the facts change, counsel should seek leave to amend. What counsel cannot do is wait until summary judgment, when the record has been built around a different case, and then ask the court to treat a new injunction as if it had always been there.

The panel’s handling of the MFLSA penalties claim points in the same direction. There, the court was attentive to mootness and standing because the parties’ stipulations had already resolved the wage and liquidated-damages claims. Whether penalties could keep the case alive depended on whether the challenged conduct was ongoing or likely to recur. That is a reminder that in a narrowed wage case, the precise remedy really matters. So does the precise pleading.

## The bottom line

*Cohen v. Consilio* is not just a ruling about Minnesota overtime statutes. It is a reminder that remedies have to be litigated with the same discipline as liability. If you want an injunction, ask for it clearly, early, and consistently. If the theory changes, amend the complaint. Summary judgment briefing is not a do-over.
