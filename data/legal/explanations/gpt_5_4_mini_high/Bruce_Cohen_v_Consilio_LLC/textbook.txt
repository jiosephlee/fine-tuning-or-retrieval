Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# 1. Factual Background, Claims, and Procedural Setup

## Consilio’s business model and Cohen’s work in Minnesota

Consilio, LLC is a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Bruce Cohen began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer in Minnesota. That detail matters because the lawsuit is about wage-and-hour rights for hourly employees, and the Minnesota statutory claims Cohen later asserted all arise out of his work relationship with Consilio.

The dispute began when Consilio changed how it would pay overtime. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers to announce a new premium overtime policy that would take effect in August 2019. The email explained that the company was eliminating overtime premium pay and that employees would instead be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. In practical terms, Cohen’s claim is that this change was unlawful because Minnesota wage statutes required more than base-rate pay for overtime work.

## Cohen’s lawsuit and the statutory claims he asserted

In August 2020, Cohen sued Consilio on behalf of himself and a putative class. A putative class action is a case filed on behalf of a proposed group of similarly situated employees, even though the court has not yet certified the class. Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to pay overtime wages owed to him and other employees. He brought claims under three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act, and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act.

Those claims were aimed at different aspects of the alleged wage violation, but they all stemmed from the same overtime-policy change. Cohen’s complaint sought several forms of relief: the unpaid overtime wages themselves, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. That mix of remedies is important because, as the case developed, some forms of relief were satisfied by payment, while others remained disputed.

## Consilio’s post-suit payments and the narrowing of the dispute

After the lawsuit was filed, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties then stipulated that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he did not dispute being paid any liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA.

Those stipulations dramatically narrowed the case. What remained disputed was not whether Cohen had been paid the wages and liquidated damages he claimed, but whether he could still recover statutory penalties and injunctive relief. The parties also disagreed about the amount and availability of penalties under the Minnesota statutes. Cohen claimed large penalties under each statute; Consilio argued the statutes did not authorize him to recover them.

The parties further agreed that the case should proceed in an unusual way. They stipulated that the “most expedient and efficient path forward” was for the district court to decide Consilio’s summary-judgment motion on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before any further discovery or litigation. They also agreed that if the court granted summary judgment to Consilio in full, no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved. That procedural agreement explains why the district court decided the case on summary judgment with a relatively thin factual record and why the appeal focuses so heavily on legal questions and justiciability.

## The summary-judgment posture and appellate review

The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all three claims, and Cohen appealed. Summary judgment is proper when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Here, that standard mattered because many facts were already stipulated, so the key questions became legal ones: what the Minnesota statutes authorize, whether the penalties Cohen sought were available, and whether the MFLSA claim remained live for federal jurisdiction purposes.

The Eighth Circuit reviewed the summary-judgment ruling de novo. De novo review means the appellate court considers the issue anew, without deferring to the district court’s legal conclusions, while viewing the evidence and reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. In a case like this one, where the parties have largely agreed on the relevant facts, de novo review makes the appellate court’s role especially focused on statutory interpretation and jurisdiction rather than factfinding.

# 2. The MPWA Issue: Who May Seek Average Daily Wage Penalties?

## Cohen’s MPWA Theory and the Posture of the Issue

The MPWA question was a pure statutory-interpretation issue raised at summary judgment. By the time the case reached that stage, Consilio had already paid Cohen his claimed overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA, so the only live disagreement on the MPWA claim concerned penalties. Cohen argued that Consilio’s failure to pay overtime correctly entitled him to “average daily wage penalties” under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act. The Eighth Circuit reviewed the summary judgment ruling de novo and asked a narrow question: does the MPWA let a private employee, like Cohen, seek those penalties himself?

## Section 181.171 Is the Enforcement Gateway

The court began with § 181.171, the MPWA’s civil-action provision. That statute allows a “person” to bring a civil action for violations of certain MPWA provisions, including § 181.101. It also says that an employer found to have violated one of those provisions is liable for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” But the statute then adds an important limitation: the employer is liable for “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” In other words, § 181.171 does not itself create a free-standing penalty remedy. It sends the court back to the particular subsection allegedly violated and asks what remedy that subsection actually authorizes.

That structure mattered here because Cohen’s theory depended on § 181.101. So the court’s task was to read § 181.101 carefully and determine whether it grants employees a personal right to collect penalties.

## What § 181.101(a) Actually Says About Penalties

The court concluded that § 181.101(a) assigns penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, not to the employee. The statutory sequence is specific. First, the Commissioner may “serve a demand for payment” on behalf of the employee. If the employer does not pay within ten days, then the Commissioner “may charge and collect” the wages earned and “a penalty.” The statute also says that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee.

Cohen relied on the fact that the collected funds ultimately go to the employee, but the court rejected the idea that this payment flow creates a private right to seek the penalty in the first place. The statute distinguishes between the person who enforces the penalty and the person who receives the money. Under the text, the Commissioner enforces the penalty; the employee receives the proceeds.

The court also emphasized what the employee is expressly allowed to do. Section 181.101 says that nothing in the section prevents an employee from “prosecuting a claim for wages.” That language preserves a private action for wages, but it does not mention a private action for penalties. The court read that omission as deliberate. In statutory terms, the employee has a right to pursue wages, while the Commissioner has the authority to pursue the penalty mechanism.

## Plain Language and the Legislature’s Choice to Say So Elsewhere

The court’s reading rested on Minnesota’s plain-language approach to statutes. Citing Allan, the court reiterated that when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, it must be given effect. Here, the text of § 181.101(a) is structured around Commissioner enforcement, and the court saw no ambiguity that would justify reading a private penalty right into the statute.

The court then reinforced that reading with a comparative point. In other parts of the Minnesota wage statutes, the legislature expressly gave employees the power to seek penalties. Sections 181.13 and 181.14 provide examples: when certain employees are discharged or resign, they may recover unpaid wages and also “charge and collect a penalty.” The court invoked the “legislature knows how” canon, relying on Lagasse, to reason that if the legislature had wanted private employees to recover penalties under § 181.101 as well, it knew how to say so. Its choice not to do so was significant.

## Holding on the MPWA Claim

Putting those pieces together, the court held that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. Because Cohen was a private plaintiff, not the Commissioner, he could not recover the penalties he requested under the MPWA. That is why the Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Consilio on the MPWA claim.

# 3. The MFLSA Issue: Paid Wages, Remaining Penalties, and Article III Jurisdiction

## The MFLSA claim after Consilio paid the wages

The MFLSA portion of the case changed character after Consilio paid Cohen and the other Minnesota document reviewers the overtime wages and liquidated damages they had demanded. Cohen stipulated that he had been paid “for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he did not dispute receiving the liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. In other words, the ordinary compensatory part of the case was gone. What remained was Cohen’s request for statutory penalties.

That fact mattered because the penalties were not payable to Cohen personally. The parties agreed that any MFLSA penalties would be paid to the Commissioner. So the appellate court was not looking at a typical wage claim in which the plaintiff still sought money for himself. It was looking at a claim for a government-paid penalty, with the plaintiff’s interest resting, if at all, on the idea that the penalty would deter continued violations.

## Why the court treated jurisdiction as the first question

The Eighth Circuit paused before reaching the merits of the MFLSA penalty dispute because federal courts can decide only live Article III cases or controversies. The opinion reminds the reader that standing is not a one-time inquiry. A plaintiff must have standing for each claim and for each form of relief he seeks, and that interest must continue throughout the litigation. The court cited Town of Chester for the proposition that a plaintiff needs standing for each requested remedy, and Sisney for the related point that the required personal interest must survive from filing to final judgment.

That framing is important here. Once Consilio paid the wages and liquidated damages, Cohen no longer had a direct compensation claim under the MFLSA. The only remaining question was whether the penalties still gave him a sufficient stake in the case. Because that question goes to federal jurisdiction, the court treated it as threshold. If there is no live controversy, the court cannot properly decide the merits of the penalty issue at all.

## Civil penalties, redressability, and Laidlaw

Cohen relied on the idea that a civil penalty can still redress a plaintiff’s injury even when the money goes to the government. The court accepted the general principle from Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: penalties may indirectly help an injured plaintiff because they can discourage ongoing violations and deter future ones. Redressability does not always require a direct payment to the plaintiff.

But the opinion also makes clear that this principle is not automatic. A penalty claim only helps if it still does something for the plaintiff’s legal interest. If the defendant’s unlawful conduct is ongoing, or threatens to recur, a penalty may still have deterrent value and therefore keep the case live. If the challenged conduct is truly over and will not come back, then the plaintiff may no longer have a personal stake sufficient to support federal jurisdiction.

That is why the court did not resolve the MFLSA issue on the merits. The key jurisdictional question was not whether penalties can ever be available under the statute, but whether this plaintiff still had a live Article III interest in them on this record.

## Mootness, voluntary cessation, and the unresolved policy question

The court’s main concern was mootness. Even if a plaintiff had standing when the suit began, later events can eliminate the controversy. Here, the unresolved factual question was whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy was still in effect. The parties had agreed to move straight to summary judgment before further discovery, so the record did not clearly answer that point.

That uncertainty mattered because of the voluntary-cessation doctrine. A defendant cannot defeat federal jurisdiction merely by stopping the challenged conduct after being sued. Under Friends of the Earth, the case remains live unless it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur. So if Consilio’s old overtime policy was still in place, or if it could reasonably return, Cohen’s penalty claim might still be viable. If Consilio had definitively abandoned the policy and recurrence was unlikely, the claim might be moot.

The Eighth Circuit therefore refused to assume jurisdiction away or jurisdiction into existence. Instead, it concluded that the present record was not enough to decide whether Cohen still had a live personal stake in the MFLSA penalties issue.

## Why the court vacated and remanded

Because jurisdiction was unresolved, the court did not affirm the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA claim. It also did not reverse on the merits. Instead, it vacated the MFLSA portion of the judgment and remanded for the district court to determine whether Article III jurisdiction exists.

That disposition is significant. Vacatur signals that the merits ruling cannot stand until the court first confirms that it had power to decide the case. The appellate court did not say Cohen wins the penalty claim; it said the federal courts need to know whether there is still a live controversy before anyone decides who wins.

## The special concurrence’s caution

Judge Kobes agreed that remand was appropriate, but he wrote separately to express doubt that Cohen had standing to seek MFLSA penalties under Laidlaw in the first place. He thought there may be important differences between the penalties in Laidlaw and the penalties available under the MFLSA, and he noted that the parties had not fully briefed that issue.

The concurrence also suggested that related issues such as attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest should be revisited on remand. In other words, the concurrence underscored that the case may still have important consequences depending on what the district court decides about mootness and standing, but those consequences should be addressed only after jurisdiction is sorted out.

# 4. The MWTA Notice Claim and the Failed Request for Injunctive Relief

## The MWTA notice theory and why Cohen’s appeal failed

Cohen’s remaining statutory claim under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act was not a direct claim for unpaid wages. Instead, he argued that Consilio failed to give the notice required when it changed its overtime policy, pointing to Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4). On appeal, Cohen complained that the district court gave this claim little attention. The Eighth Circuit was not persuaded.

The key problem was that Cohen had already stipulated that Consilio had paid him everything he was owed in wages. That left only the question of penalties. But Cohen did not point the court to any meaningful statutory authority showing that the specific MWTA provisions he invoked authorize the civil penalties he sought. The court therefore treated the argument as undeveloped and waived. That is an important appellate lesson: a party cannot simply name a claim and ask for reversal. The appellant must actually explain why the statute supports the requested remedy, using argument and authority. If the brief does not do that, the court may decline to consider the issue at all.

So, even though Cohen objected that the district court had not said much about the MWTA theory, the Eighth Circuit affirmed. It did not need to decide the broader merits of whether Consilio’s notice was inadequate, because Cohen failed to present a developed appellate argument showing a legal basis for the penalties he wanted.

## Why the court treated the injunction request as improperly raised

Cohen also asked for injunctive relief, but his appellate request did not match what he had pleaded in the district court. In the complaint, he sought one set of injunction-related remedies. Later, in summary-judgment briefing, he shifted course and asked for a different injunction: an order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy.

The Eighth Circuit would not consider that new request. The court relied on the ordinary pleading rule that new claims, or materially new requests for relief, must be added through an amended complaint, not introduced for the first time in an opposition brief. That is what Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 is for. Summary-judgment briefing can clarify or support an existing claim, but it cannot substitute for a proper pleading when a party wants to change the case’s theory or expand the relief sought.

Because Cohen’s request to rescind the overtime policy was not properly presented in the operative complaint, the district court did not err by ignoring it, and the Eighth Circuit likewise refused to treat it as a live issue on appeal. The court therefore affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA claim and declined to reach the merits of the newly framed injunction request.

# 5. Disposition, Doctrinal Takeaways, and What Remains on Remand

## The mixed disposition

This appeal ends with a split result. The Eighth Circuit affirms the district court in part, vacates in part, and remands. The court leaves in place the summary judgment ruling against Cohen on his Minnesota Payment of Wages Act claim and his Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim, and it also rejects his effort to obtain injunctive relief on the theory he advanced on appeal. But the court vacates the judgment on the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act claim and sends that claim back so the district court can first decide whether it still has jurisdiction at all.

That disposition reflects the procedural posture of the case. Consilio had already paid Cohen and the other Minnesota reviewers the overtime wages and liquidated damages they claimed under the wage law. That meant the case had narrowed to penalties and other nonwage remedies. The parties even stipulated that the court should decide summary judgment first, before any further litigation. But the stipulation did not answer the separate question whether a live federal case or controversy still existed.

## The settled point: no private recovery of § 181.101 average daily wage penalties on this theory

The clearest merits holding in the opinion is the one rejecting Cohen’s attempt to recover “average daily wage penalties” under § 181.101 of the MPWA. The court reads the statute as giving the Commissioner, not the employee, the power to demand payment and collect the penalty. Section 181.171 allows a person to sue for violations of certain MPWA provisions and to obtain “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” but any civil penalties or damages must be the ones “provided for in the section violated.” For § 181.101, the court concludes, the penalty mechanism belongs to the Commissioner.

The court’s reasoning is mostly textual. Section 181.101(a) speaks in terms of the Commissioner serving a demand for payment, and if the employer still does not pay, the Commissioner may “charge and collect” the wages and a penalty. The statute then says the money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee. By contrast, the employee is described as having the right to prosecute a claim for wages. That structure matters: the statute gives the employee a right to wages, but not an express private right to collect the penalty.

The court reinforces that reading by pointing to nearby statutes where the legislature did expressly authorize an employee to “charge and collect a penalty,” such as §§ 181.13 and 181.14. The point is a familiar one in statutory interpretation: when the legislature uses penalty language in one place but not another, courts presume it did so intentionally. So, on this record, Cohen cannot obtain the wage-penalty remedy he sought under § 181.101.

## The unresolved point: the MFLSA penalty claim and federal jurisdiction

The MFLSA claim is different, not because the court resolves the merits, but because it refuses to assume jurisdiction is secure once the wage and liquidated-damages claims have been paid. Cohen stipulated that Consilio had already paid everything he was owed in overtime wages and liquidated damages. What remained was his demand for statutory penalties, which the parties agreed would be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself.

That setup triggers the court’s standing and mootness analysis. A plaintiff must have standing for each claim and each form of relief, and that interest must continue throughout the litigation. Civil penalties can sometimes satisfy redressability even when they are not paid to the plaintiff, because they can deter future violations and encourage the defendant to stop ongoing unlawful conduct. The court relies on Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for that principle.

But there is a catch. If Consilio’s challenged overtime policy is no longer in place, and if it is “absolutely clear” that the conduct will not recur, then the penalty claim may be moot. The opinion therefore does not decide whether Cohen wins or loses on the MFLSA penalty issue as a merits matter. Instead, it vacates the district court’s summary judgment ruling and remands for the threshold jurisdictional question: is there still a live controversy?

## Stipulations can narrow the case without eliminating jurisdictional uncertainty

This case is a good example of how stipulations can simplify the merits while leaving Article III issues unresolved. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he alleged were owed. That eliminated the main compensatory dispute. But payment of the underlying wages does not automatically erase every possible claim, especially when a plaintiff seeks penalties for an allegedly ongoing policy.

So the stipulation narrowed the case, but it did not necessarily end it. If Consilio’s policy remained in effect, the penalties might still redress an ongoing injury by deterring continued violations. If the policy had been rescinded and could not reasonably recur, there may be nothing left for a federal court to redress. The district court must sort that out on remand before reaching any further merits questions.

## Waiver, pleading, and the importance of developed briefing

The court’s treatment of the MWTA claim and the injunction request shows two recurring appellate lessons: arguments must be developed, and requested relief must be properly pleaded.

On the MWTA claim, Cohen did not offer meaningful statutory authority showing that the notice provisions he invoked authorize civil penalties. He also stipulated that he had already been paid all wages owed. With no developed argument for reversal, the court treats the issue as waived and leaves the dismissal in place.

The injunction claim fails for a different reason. Cohen tried to shift his requested relief in summary judgment briefing, asking for an order requiring Consilio to rescind the 2019 overtime policy. But that was not the relief pleaded in the complaint, and new claims or theories are not properly introduced in a briefing paper. If a party wants different injunctive relief, the complaint must be amended under Rule 15(a). The court therefore declines to consider the new request.

## What the case means for remand and for future Minnesota wage litigation

Practically, the case teaches that Minnesota wage claims in federal court are highly statute-specific. A plaintiff cannot assume that every wage statute carries a private penalty remedy, and the identity of the recipient matters. If the statute sends penalties to the Commissioner, the plaintiff must still show a live federal controversy, especially once wages and liquidated damages have been paid.

On remand, the district court will likely need to determine whether Consilio’s overtime policy is still operating. If it is, the MFLSA claim for penalties may remain justiciable. If it is not, the court will need to decide whether voluntary cessation or mootness defeats jurisdiction. The special concurrence underscores that ancillary issues such as attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest may also need attention depending on how the jurisdictional question is resolved.

In short, the opinion leaves Cohen with a partial victory in the sense that his MFLSA claim was not finally decided against him. But it also gives him only a narrow path forward: he must show that the case still presents a live controversy before the district court can reach any remaining penalty issues.
