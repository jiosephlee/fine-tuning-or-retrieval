Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## When “Wage Theft” Is Not a Penalty Claim: The Narrow Private Reach of Minnesota’s Notice Provisions

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio* is a useful reminder that not every wage-law violation comes with a privately enforceable penalty attached. That matters because modern wage-and-hour litigation often turns on a familiar move: once the employer pays the underlying wages, plaintiffs try to keep the case alive by recasting a notice, timing, or recordkeeping violation as a standalone penalty claim. In this case, the court declined to take that step for Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act.

The practical consequence is significant. If the statute does not clearly authorize private penalties for a notice violation, then curing the wage deficiency may sharply reduce exposure. And more broadly, the decision signals that courts in the Eighth Circuit are unlikely to infer high-value penalty remedies from wage statutes that speak in terms of notice obligations unless the legislature has said so expressly.

## What the court did

Cohen’s MWTA theory was based on Consilio’s alleged failure to give notice of a change in overtime policy. But by the time the case reached summary judgment, the parties had stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages he claimed and all liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. So the remaining issue was not unpaid wages. It was penalties.

That framing made Cohen’s MWTA claim hard to sustain. The panel noted that Cohen pointed to no statutory authority permitting civil penalties under the particular MWTA notice provisions he invoked, Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4). With no meaningful argument and no textual hook for penalties, the court simply affirmed the dismissal.

The opinion is short on extended statutory exegesis here, but that brevity is itself telling. The court did not treat the Wage Theft Act’s notice requirements as a free-standing private enforcement device. Instead, it treated them as obligations whose violation does not automatically generate a private penalty claim, especially once the employer has paid the wages at issue.

That approach is consistent with the court’s broader statutory method elsewhere in the opinion. On the MPWA claim, the panel emphasized that the Minnesota legislature knows how to authorize penalties when it wants to do so, pointing to other provisions in the wage code that expressly allow an aggrieved employee to “charge and collect a penalty.” The absence of comparable language in the MWTA notice provisions mattered.

## The doctrinal takeaway

The real doctrinal point is not that the MWTA is toothless. It is that courts are reluctant to convert a statutory notice requirement into a privately recoverable penalty regime absent clear legislative authorization. That distinction matters because notice provisions serve a different function from wage-payment provisions. They are meant to improve transparency and compliance. They are not necessarily meant to create a separate damages engine once the underlying wages have been satisfied.

This matters even more in cases like Cohen’s, where the employer’s alleged violation is technical or historical and the employee has already been made whole on pay. In that setting, plaintiffs often press for penalties as the only remaining remedy. The Eighth Circuit’s response was essentially: not unless the statute says so. That is a sensible limit. Without it, every payroll-notice misstep could become a potentially large penalty case, untethered from actual wage loss.

At the same time, the decision leaves some room for future litigation. The panel did not purport to announce a categorical rule that the MWTA never authorizes penalties. It resolved the case on the record before it: Cohen had been paid, he did not identify a statutory basis for penalties, and he offered no developed appellate argument for reversal. So the ruling is best read as a narrow refusal to infer a remedy, not a broad construction of every possible MWTA enforcement path.

## The downstream effects

For employers, the decision is a useful defense-side data point. Fixing wage underpayments may not eliminate all exposure, but it may eliminate a lot of it if the remaining claim depends on penalties that the statute does not expressly authorize. For employees and plaintiffs’ lawyers, the lesson is equally clear: if the theory is a penalty claim based on a notice violation, the complaint and briefing need a specific statutory foundation, not just the rhetorical force of “wage theft.”

And for lawmakers, the opinion is a nudge. If Minnesota intends the Wage Theft Act’s notice provisions to support private penalty actions, that intent needs to be written down with far more clarity. Courts will not do that work for them.
