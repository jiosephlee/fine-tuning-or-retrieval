Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What effect did the parties' stipulation have on the case after Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages?
Question:
The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he alleged were due, and they also agreed that the “most expedient and efficient path forward” was for the district court to decide summary judgment before further discovery or litigation. Since Cohen originally sued on behalf of himself and a putative class, I'm confused about what this stipulation did procedurally. Did it effectively narrow the case to a pure legal dispute about penalties and other remedies for Cohen individually? And does a stipulation like this risk waiving factual development that might have mattered later, especially on things like whether the challenged overtime policy was still ongoing?
Answer:
The stipulation did **not** formally amend the complaint or automatically dismiss the case, but it had a very practical procedural effect: it **conceded away the back-pay side of the case** and left only the remaining remedies/issues the parties were still fighting about.

### What the stipulation did
According to the opinion, the parties agreed that Cohen had:

- “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed,” and
- “been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].”

That meant there was no longer a live dispute over **unpaid overtime** or **liquidated damages** for Cohen individually. The remaining disagreement was about:

- statutory penalties under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA, and
- injunctive relief.

So, yes, the case was effectively narrowed from a wage-collection suit into a more limited dispute over **whether any additional statutory remedies remained available** after the payments.

### Why the parties also stipulated to summary judgment first
The second stipulation—agreeing that “the most expedient and efficient path forward” was to have the court decide summary judgment “before any further discovery or litigation”—was a **case-management agreement**. It told the district court: decide the legal issues on the current record first, and if defendants win entirely, the case is over.

The Eighth Circuit quoted the parties’ acknowledgment that if the court granted summary judgment in full, “**no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved.**”

### Did that waive factual development?
Not in the sense of waiving every factual issue in the case, but it **did** limit further factual development by agreement.

The opinion shows the key point: the stipulation did **not** resolve everything. For the MFLSA penalty claim, the court said there was still a factual/jurisdictional problem because it was **disputed whether Consilio’s challenged overtime policy was still in place**. The court explained:

- if the policy was still ongoing, penalties might still provide redress;
- if the policy had ended and recurrence was “absolutely clear,” the claim might be moot.

Because that factual issue remained unresolved, the court **vacated and remanded** for the district court to determine jurisdiction.

So the stipulation did **not** eliminate the relevance of whether the policy was ongoing. The court expressly recognized that this fact still mattered.

### Bottom line
The stipulation:

1. **conceded payment of all wages and liquidated damages** Cohen claimed;
2. **narrowed the case** to penalties and injunctive relief;
3. **allowed the court to decide summary judgment before further discovery**; but
4. **did not eliminate all factual disputes**, especially whether the overtime policy was still in effect, which the Eighth Circuit treated as potentially relevant to mootness/jurisdiction.
