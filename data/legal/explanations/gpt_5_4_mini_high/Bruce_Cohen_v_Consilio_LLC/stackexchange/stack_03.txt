Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the difference between standing and mootness in the court's discussion of the MFLSA penalties issue?
Question:
The opinion says the court has a “serious concern regarding our jurisdiction” over Cohen's request for MFLSA penalties. It then discusses both standing and mootness, saying standing must exist at the start of the case and mootness concerns whether that personal interest continues throughout the litigation. I'm struggling to separate those concepts in this case. Is the idea that Cohen may have had standing when Consilio was still using the allegedly unlawful overtime policy, but the case could become moot if the policy was later abandoned? Or is there also a threshold question whether he ever had standing at all to pursue penalties payable only to the government?
Answer:
The court is treating **standing** and **mootness** as different jurisdictional questions on a **time axis**:

- **Standing** asks whether Cohen had a sufficient personal stake **when the case began**.
- **Mootness** asks whether that personal stake **still exists as the case goes on**.

That is exactly how the court frames it: “the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness).” So they are related, but not the same.

## How that plays out here

On the MFLSA penalties issue, Cohen had already been paid his **overtime wages and liquidated damages**. So the only thing left was **statutory penalties**, and the parties agreed those penalties would be paid to the **Commissioner**, not to Cohen.

That creates a jurisdictional question because Article III requires a concrete, redressable injury. But the court also notes that **civil penalties paid to the government can sometimes still support standing** if they redress ongoing unlawful conduct by deterring it, citing **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw**.

So the key distinction is:

### 1. Standing question
Could Cohen sue for penalties at all, even though the penalties go to the Commissioner?
- The majority does **not** definitively decide this.
- The concurrence expressly says it is “not convinced” Cohen has standing.
- That is the threshold Article III question: whether penalties payable to the government can redress Cohen’s injury under these facts.

### 2. Mootness question
Even if Cohen had standing when he filed suit, does the claim remain live?
- The majority focuses here.
- It says if Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy **remains in place**, the penalties claim may still be viable.
- But if the policy has been abandoned and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur, the claim may be **moot**.

## Bottom line

Your first instinct is basically right: **mootness concerns whether the claim became stale because the challenged policy ended.** But the opinion also leaves open a separate and more fundamental issue: **whether Cohen ever had Article III standing to seek penalties payable only to the government in the first place.**

So the court is not saying, “standing existed and then the claim went moot” with certainty. It is saying:

- there may be a **standing problem** because Cohen seeks only government-paid penalties, and
- there may also be a **mootness problem** if Consilio has already stopped the policy and there is no realistic chance of recurrence.

That is why the court vacated and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction.
