Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # 3. The MFLSA Issue: Paid Wages, Remaining Penalties, and Article III Jurisdiction

## The MFLSA claim after Consilio paid the wages

The MFLSA portion of the case changed character after Consilio paid Cohen and the other Minnesota document reviewers the overtime wages and liquidated damages they had demanded. Cohen stipulated that he had been paid “for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he did not dispute receiving the liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. In other words, the ordinary compensatory part of the case was gone. What remained was Cohen’s request for statutory penalties.

That fact mattered because the penalties were not payable to Cohen personally. The parties agreed that any MFLSA penalties would be paid to the Commissioner. So the appellate court was not looking at a typical wage claim in which the plaintiff still sought money for himself. It was looking at a claim for a government-paid penalty, with the plaintiff’s interest resting, if at all, on the idea that the penalty would deter continued violations.

## Why the court treated jurisdiction as the first question

The Eighth Circuit paused before reaching the merits of the MFLSA penalty dispute because federal courts can decide only live Article III cases or controversies. The opinion reminds the reader that standing is not a one-time inquiry. A plaintiff must have standing for each claim and for each form of relief he seeks, and that interest must continue throughout the litigation. The court cited Town of Chester for the proposition that a plaintiff needs standing for each requested remedy, and Sisney for the related point that the required personal interest must survive from filing to final judgment.

That framing is important here. Once Consilio paid the wages and liquidated damages, Cohen no longer had a direct compensation claim under the MFLSA. The only remaining question was whether the penalties still gave him a sufficient stake in the case. Because that question goes to federal jurisdiction, the court treated it as threshold. If there is no live controversy, the court cannot properly decide the merits of the penalty issue at all.

## Civil penalties, redressability, and Laidlaw

Cohen relied on the idea that a civil penalty can still redress a plaintiff’s injury even when the money goes to the government. The court accepted the general principle from Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: penalties may indirectly help an injured plaintiff because they can discourage ongoing violations and deter future ones. Redressability does not always require a direct payment to the plaintiff.

But the opinion also makes clear that this principle is not automatic. A penalty claim only helps if it still does something for the plaintiff’s legal interest. If the defendant’s unlawful conduct is ongoing, or threatens to recur, a penalty may still have deterrent value and therefore keep the case live. If the challenged conduct is truly over and will not come back, then the plaintiff may no longer have a personal stake sufficient to support federal jurisdiction.

That is why the court did not resolve the MFLSA issue on the merits. The key jurisdictional question was not whether penalties can ever be available under the statute, but whether this plaintiff still had a live Article III interest in them on this record.

## Mootness, voluntary cessation, and the unresolved policy question

The court’s main concern was mootness. Even if a plaintiff had standing when the suit began, later events can eliminate the controversy. Here, the unresolved factual question was whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy was still in effect. The parties had agreed to move straight to summary judgment before further discovery, so the record did not clearly answer that point.

That uncertainty mattered because of the voluntary-cessation doctrine. A defendant cannot defeat federal jurisdiction merely by stopping the challenged conduct after being sued. Under Friends of the Earth, the case remains live unless it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur. So if Consilio’s old overtime policy was still in place, or if it could reasonably return, Cohen’s penalty claim might still be viable. If Consilio had definitively abandoned the policy and recurrence was unlikely, the claim might be moot.

The Eighth Circuit therefore refused to assume jurisdiction away or jurisdiction into existence. Instead, it concluded that the present record was not enough to decide whether Cohen still had a live personal stake in the MFLSA penalties issue.

## Why the court vacated and remanded

Because jurisdiction was unresolved, the court did not affirm the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA claim. It also did not reverse on the merits. Instead, it vacated the MFLSA portion of the judgment and remanded for the district court to determine whether Article III jurisdiction exists.

That disposition is significant. Vacatur signals that the merits ruling cannot stand until the court first confirms that it had power to decide the case. The appellate court did not say Cohen wins the penalty claim; it said the federal courts need to know whether there is still a live controversy before anyone decides who wins.

## The special concurrence’s caution

Judge Kobes agreed that remand was appropriate, but he wrote separately to express doubt that Cohen had standing to seek MFLSA penalties under Laidlaw in the first place. He thought there may be important differences between the penalties in Laidlaw and the penalties available under the MFLSA, and he noted that the parties had not fully briefed that issue.

The concurrence also suggested that related issues such as attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest should be revisited on remand. In other words, the concurrence underscored that the case may still have important consequences depending on what the district court decides about mootness and standing, but those consequences should be addressed only after jurisdiction is sorted out.
