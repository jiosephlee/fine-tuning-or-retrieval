Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 4: # 4. The MWTA Notice Claim and the Failed Request for Injunctive Relief

## The MWTA notice theory and why Cohen’s appeal failed

Cohen’s remaining statutory claim under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act was not a direct claim for unpaid wages. Instead, he argued that Consilio failed to give the notice required when it changed its overtime policy, pointing to Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4). On appeal, Cohen complained that the district court gave this claim little attention. The Eighth Circuit was not persuaded.

The key problem was that Cohen had already stipulated that Consilio had paid him everything he was owed in wages. That left only the question of penalties. But Cohen did not point the court to any meaningful statutory authority showing that the specific MWTA provisions he invoked authorize the civil penalties he sought. The court therefore treated the argument as undeveloped and waived. That is an important appellate lesson: a party cannot simply name a claim and ask for reversal. The appellant must actually explain why the statute supports the requested remedy, using argument and authority. If the brief does not do that, the court may decline to consider the issue at all.

So, even though Cohen objected that the district court had not said much about the MWTA theory, the Eighth Circuit affirmed. It did not need to decide the broader merits of whether Consilio’s notice was inadequate, because Cohen failed to present a developed appellate argument showing a legal basis for the penalties he wanted.

## Why the court treated the injunction request as improperly raised

Cohen also asked for injunctive relief, but his appellate request did not match what he had pleaded in the district court. In the complaint, he sought one set of injunction-related remedies. Later, in summary-judgment briefing, he shifted course and asked for a different injunction: an order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy.

The Eighth Circuit would not consider that new request. The court relied on the ordinary pleading rule that new claims, or materially new requests for relief, must be added through an amended complaint, not introduced for the first time in an opposition brief. That is what Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 is for. Summary-judgment briefing can clarify or support an existing claim, but it cannot substitute for a proper pleading when a party wants to change the case’s theory or expand the relief sought.

Because Cohen’s request to rescind the overtime policy was not properly presented in the operative complaint, the district court did not err by ignoring it, and the Eighth Circuit likewise refused to treat it as a live issue on appeal. The court therefore affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA claim and declined to reach the merits of the newly framed injunction request.
