Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The Dispute’s Setting: Refugee Admissions, Funding, and the Statutory Framework

## The order’s procedural posture

This Ninth Circuit order is not the court’s final merits decision. It is a stay pending appeal, which means the panel is deciding whether the district court’s preliminary injunctions should remain suspended while the appeal is litigated. That posture matters because the court is not yet resolving every legal question finally. Instead, it is asking which side is more likely to win, and what the consequences are of letting the district court’s orders operate in the meantime. The background section of the order is therefore designed to explain the dispute the court is managing: a challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions and to the government’s later suspension of funding for refugee resettlement services.

## The executive orders and the two-stage refugee program

The first executive action was Executive Order No. 14163, issued on January 20, 2025, and titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” It directed that entry into the United States of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, or USRAP, be suspended pending further findings. The order was issued under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a). In the order, the President made the finding that refugee entry would be detrimental to the national interest, which is the statutory predicate for invoking § 1182(f).

A few days later, on January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended all funding of USRAP under a separate executive order, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” That funding suspension was broader than a simple pause on admissions. It also reached domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted into the United States. The case therefore involves two different government actions: one that stops refugees from entering, and another that affects what happens after entry, including the services refugees receive once they are here.

## How USRAP works: admission and resettlement are different stages

The order treats USRAP as having two distinct components. The first is overseas processing and admission: refugees are screened, approved, and then admitted into the United States. The second is domestic resettlement: once refugees arrive, the government provides reception and placement services, such as job training, English-language training, cash assistance, and related support.

That distinction is central to understanding the case. A rule that suspends entry addresses who may come into the country. A rule that suspends funding for resettlement services addresses what assistance refugees receive after they are admitted. The plaintiffs’ claims track that division. Some plaintiffs are refugees already admitted to the United States. Others are refugees who have been approved for resettlement but remain outside the country. The organizational plaintiffs are nonprofits that perform the government’s refugee-processing and resettlement work through cooperative agreements. Their interests are affected by both the admissions suspension and the funding suspension, but in different ways.

## The plaintiffs and the litigation they filed

The plaintiffs filed suit in February 2025 in the Western District of Washington. They argued that the suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of USRAP violated the Administrative Procedure Act. The Refugee Act is cited here because it supplies the statutory framework for the federal government’s ongoing treatment of refugees, not just the initial decision whether to admit them.

That is why the order quotes Negusie v. Holder, which described the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” The citation signals that refugee law is not limited to the moment of entry. It also governs the government’s responsibilities after admission, which is the foundation for the plaintiffs’ challenge to the funding suspension and the termination of resettlement agreements.

The district court first issued an oral preliminary injunction on February 25, and then a written order on February 28 clarifying that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court entered a second preliminary injunction requiring the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the first ruling. The government appealed both orders.

## The President’s authority under § 1182(f) and the role of Hawaii

For the admissions side of the case, the key statute is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That provision says that whenever the President finds that the entry of aliens or a class of aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may suspend their entry for as long as he deems necessary. The order also notes § 1185(a), but the stay analysis focuses primarily on § 1182(f) because that is the provision that expressly authorizes a presidential suspension of entry.

The panel relies on Trump v. Hawaii to explain why the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to the admissions suspension. Hawaii emphasized that § 1182(f) gives the President broad discretion and that courts should not engage in a searching inquiry into the President’s reasons. In that framework, the central question is whether the President made the required finding of detriment. The Ninth Circuit says that prerequisite was met here. That is why, at least on the admissions question, the court sees the government as likely to prevail.

## Domestic resettlement under § 1522 and the dispute over whether it is mandatory

The harder question is the government’s duty to support refugees after they arrive. That question turns on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the domestic resettlement provision of the Refugee Act. The panel points to several parts of that statute.

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make resources available for employment training and placement, English-language training, cash assistance, and equal access for women to training and instruction. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) deals with unaccompanied refugee minors and says that, during the interim period before placement, the Director shall assume legal and financial responsibility if necessary and may make decisions to provide immediate care. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) says the Secretary of Health and Human Services shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, providing interim support, medical services, support services, and case management as needed.

The majority reads these provisions as imposing real statutory obligations, at least once appropriations exist. Judge Lee’s partial dissent reads the same statute differently. He emphasizes that § 1522 is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees,” and that many of its subsections say the Director is “authorized” to act. On his view, the statute permits refugee-service programs but does not require the executive branch to keep them operating. That interpretive disagreement is the core of the APA dispute over the funding suspension.

## Cooperative agreements, appropriations, and congressional oversight

The statute does not require the government to provide these services directly. Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) allows the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. That is how USRAP has historically worked. The State Department has long used cooperative agreements with nonprofit resettlement organizations to perform overseas processing support and domestic reception and placement services.

Those agreements matter because the government ended them. On February 26, it terminated every cooperative agreement for refugee reception and placement services in the United States and most of the agreements for processing support abroad. The government later told the district court it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency, but at oral argument it could not say whether that effort had produced anything concrete. The panel treated that uncertainty as important because it suggested the government might not be able to deliver the services that § 1522 contemplates without reinstating the existing agreements.

Congressional appropriations also frame the issue. The order notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, later reduced by $800 million through a rescissions act. The statute’s “to the extent of available appropriations” language and the existence of a substantial appropriation both matter to the question whether the executive branch can simply shut the program down. Section 1523 adds another sign of congressional oversight by requiring the Secretary to report annually to the Judiciary Committees of the House and Senate on activities under the subchapter.

## Why this setting matters to the stay order

Taken together, these facts explain why the Ninth Circuit split the case into two very different analytical paths. The admissions suspension sits on the broad foundation of § 1182(f) and the deference recognized in Hawaii. The resettlement-services dispute, by contrast, turns on the more detailed and arguably more mandatory structure of § 1522, the availability of appropriated funds, and the government’s practical ability to keep refugee services running through nonprofit agreements. The stay order reflects that difference: the panel was willing to treat the admissions challenge as one the government was likely to win, but it was not prepared to say the government was likely to prevail on every challenge to the suspension of refugee services.

# From District Court Injunctions to an Interim Appellate Stay

## The February 2025 suit in the Western District of Washington

The case began in February 2025, when the plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington. The plaintiffs included refugees who had already been admitted to the United States, refugees who had been approved for resettlement but were still abroad, and three resettlement organizations that had agreements with the State Department to help process refugees overseas and provide domestic resettlement services. Their lawsuit attacked two separate government actions: first, President Trump’s executive order suspending refugee admissions under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, or USRAP; and second, the State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding, including funding for domestic resettlement services.

That procedural split matters. One part of the case concerns who may enter the country as a refugee. Another concerns what services the government must provide after refugees have been admitted. The district court responded to both issues with preliminary injunctions, and the Ninth Circuit’s later stay orders tracked that same division.

## The district court’s February 25 oral preliminary injunction

On February 25, the district court orally entered a preliminary injunction barring enforcement or implementation of several sections of Executive Order 14163. A preliminary injunction is an early merits-based order that preserves the status quo while the case is litigated; it is not a final judgment, but it can immediately stop the government from carrying out challenged conduct. Here, the district court concluded at least preliminarily that the executive order could not be enforced as written.

The next day, February 26, the government responded by terminating every cooperative agreement for refugee reception and placement services in the United States, and nearly all agreements for overseas processing support. That action intensified the dispute, because it meant the government was not just defending the refugee-entry suspension; it was also dismantling the network of contractors and grantees that made refugee resettlement work in practice.

## The February 28 written order and the March 24 injunction

On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the scope of its oral ruling. That written order specified that the injunction also prohibited suspension of USRAP funding. In other words, the court treated the funding freeze as part of the same controversy and made clear that the earlier oral injunction reached it too.

A month later, on March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction. This one ordered the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements it had terminated after the February 25 ruling. So by that point, the district court had entered two related but distinct injunctions: one aimed at the legality of the refugee admissions suspension, and another aimed at the government’s termination of resettlement contracts and funding.

For appellate purposes, those two injunctions created two separate fronts. The government appealed both orders, filing notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25.

## The government’s appeals and the Ninth Circuit’s March 25 partial stay

Once the appeals were filed, the government asked the Ninth Circuit for a stay pending appeal. A stay pending appeal is different from the district court’s preliminary injunction: it temporarily pauses the injunction while the appellate court considers whether the government is likely to win, whether it will suffer irreparable harm without relief, and how the balance of harms and the public interest cut. In this case, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted only a partial stay on March 25.

The motions panel relied heavily on Trump v. Hawaii, emphasizing the deferential language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which gives the President authority to suspend the entry of classes of aliens when he finds their entry would be detrimental to the national interest. On that basis, the panel concluded that the government was likely to succeed in challenging the injunction as it applied to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions. But the panel did not give the government everything it wanted. It preserved a narrow carveout for individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025, the date the executive order went into effect.

That carveout is important because it shows the panel was not simply choosing between all-or-nothing relief. Instead, it left a small subset of cases outside the stay, creating an exception that later became the focus of further litigation and implementation disputes. The panel then issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9 to clarify that limited carveout.

## The July 14 enforcement framework and the July 18 administrative stay

After briefing but before the scheduled merits argument, the district court on July 14 issued another order designed to implement the carveout. It established a framework for enforcing the limited exception and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. In practical terms, the district court was trying to operationalize the earlier appellate carveout: identify who was covered, how they would be processed, and what compliance would look like.

The government responded on July 17 with an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the Ninth Circuit’s stay order. The following day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is usually a short-term, emergency measure that holds things in place while the court decides what more permanent interim relief to grant. It is not the same thing as a full stay pending appeal, but it serves the same practical purpose in the immediate moment: freezing the disputed order long enough for appellate review.

The court also accelerated the case, scheduling oral argument on an expedited basis. That choice signals that the panel viewed the dispute as urgent and wanted to address the interim relief quickly rather than wait for the ordinary calendar.

## The September expedited argument and the new stay pending appeal

The panel heard oral argument on September 3, 2025, and then issued the order reproduced here. The court made clear that an opinion on the merits would come later, but that in the meantime the order itself would govern as the operative stay pending appeal. It therefore vacated both its July 18 administrative stay and its earlier March 25 stay, as later clarified in April and May. In other words, the panel replaced its prior stopgap orders with a new interim arrangement.

This is a key procedural point. The panel was not finally deciding the appeal. It was managing the case while the appeal remained pending. That is why the order repeatedly says that a merits opinion will issue “in due course” and that the present order is only a stay pending final resolution of the appeal.

The panel also stated that the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions were stayed in their entirety, except as specified in the order. At the same time, the order explains that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the refugee admissions suspension, but not likely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act challenge relating to the government’s duty to provide reception and placement services after refugees are admitted. On that basis, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

So the interim order did two things at once: it lifted the district court’s injunctions broadly, but it also preserved relief tied to the statutory resettlement-services issue. That mixed disposition explains why the order is not a final merits opinion and why Judge Lee wrote separately in partial concurrence and partial dissent. The panel had reached enough agreement to issue interim relief, but not full agreement on how far the federal government’s obligations under the refugee-resettlement statute extended.

## Why this procedural history matters

For a law student, the main lesson is that the procedural posture here is doing a lot of work. The district court entered preliminary injunctions against both the refugee admissions suspension and the funding/contract terminations. The Ninth Circuit then intervened with partial stays, a limited carveout, and later an administrative stay when the district court tried to enforce that carveout. The present order is another interim step: it supersedes the earlier stay orders and sets the rules while the appeal awaits a full opinion.

When you read the order this way, the sequence becomes easier to follow. First comes the district court’s injunction. Then comes the government’s appeal and request for a stay. Then comes the motions panel’s partial stay, its carveout, and the later enforcement dispute. Finally, after expedited argument, the panel issues a new interim stay order and signals that the merits decision is still to come.

# The Stay Standard and the Panel’s Treatment of the Refugee-Admissions Suspension

## Procedural posture: an expedited interim order, not the final merits decision

This order came at a very early and fast-moving stage of the appeal. After the district court entered preliminary injunctions blocking the refugee-admissions suspension and related funding and contracting changes, the Ninth Circuit issued a series of temporary stay orders and then heard expedited oral argument. The panel’s September 12 order is therefore not the court’s final merits opinion. It is an interim order that keeps the district court’s injunctions from operating while the appeal is pending, and the panel expressly says that a full opinion will issue later.

That procedural posture matters. A stay pending appeal does not decide who ultimately wins the case. It asks only whether the challenged injunction should remain in force while the appellate court considers the appeal. Here, the panel vacated its earlier administrative and partial stays and replaced them with a new stay order pending final resolution of the appeal. As a practical matter, that means the refugee-admissions suspension remained operative during appellate review, even though the court reserved fuller analysis for later.

## The four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder

The panel begins with the familiar stay framework from *Nken v. Holder*. A stay applicant must show four things: a strong likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury if a stay is denied, lack of substantial injury to the other side, and that the public interest favors a stay. The court quotes *Nken* for the proposition that the first two factors are the “most critical.”

That framing is important for reading the order. A stay is an equitable, discretionary remedy, so the court does not treat the four factors as a mechanical checklist. Instead, it assesses whether the stay applicant has shown enough to justify temporarily suspending the lower court’s order. In this case, the government needed to show that it had a serious chance of prevailing on appeal and that letting the injunction remain in place would cause harm that could not easily be undone.

## Why likelihood of success and irreparable injury are the “most critical” factors

The panel’s discussion shows how much weight these first two factors carry. If the government is likely to win on the merits of the refugee-admissions issue, that heavily favors a stay. If, in addition, the government faces harm from being compelled to implement a nationwide injunction against a presidential entry restriction, the case for a stay becomes stronger still.

The order does not spell out irreparable harm in detail, but it ties the stay inquiry to the President’s broad immigration and foreign-affairs authority. That context matters because harms involving admission decisions, foreign relations, and the execution of executive branch policy are often hard to reverse later. Once refugees enter or are admitted under a particular regime, the status quo cannot simply be restored after the fact. For the panel, those considerations, together with the merits showing, made a stay appropriate.

## The panel’s conclusion that the government is likely to prevail on the refugee-admissions challenge

On the core refugee-admissions question, the panel says the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. The key point is that the President acted under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which authorizes him to suspend entry when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.

The panel’s merits preview is concise but clear. It relies on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* and its statement that § 1182(f) gives the President broad power to suspend entry of a class of aliens. The panel concludes that the refugee suspension falls within that statutory authority and that the executive order does not exceed any textual limit in the statute. In stay posture, that was enough to support a conclusion that the government was likely to win on this aspect of the appeal.

## *Trump v. Hawaii*’s central role: deference under § 1182(f)

*Trump v. Hawaii* is the doctrinal anchor for the panel’s analysis. The order quotes *Hawaii* for the idea that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and gives the President “ample power” to impose entry restrictions beyond those elsewhere found in the Immigration and Nationality Act. That language does a lot of work here.

The panel treats *Hawaii* as controlling on the level of judicial scrutiny. It says courts may not engage in a “searching inquiry” into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. In other words, once the President has made the statutory finding required by § 1182(f)—that the entry of the covered aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests—the court does not second-guess the policy reasoning behind that finding. The question is not whether the court would have reached the same policy judgment, but whether the President acted within the authority Congress gave him.

## The statutory prerequisite under § 1182(f): a presidential finding of detriment

The panel reads § 1182(f) to contain one key prerequisite: the President must find that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The order says that requirement was “undoubtedly” satisfied here. That is the statute’s threshold, and the court treats it as the only textual condition that matters for the stay analysis.

This is a classic example of how courts distinguish between statutory authority and policy wisdom. The panel is not saying the refugee suspension is a good idea or that the plaintiffs’ concerns are trivial. It is saying that, for purposes of the stay, the statute gives the President enough room to suspend entry of a covered class of aliens so long as he makes the required detriment finding. Because the executive order did that, the government showed a strong likelihood of success on this issue.

## The text allowing suspension of “any class of aliens”

The panel also emphasizes the breadth of the statutory text. Section 1182(f) allows the President to suspend the entry of “all aliens or any class of aliens” as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or to impose appropriate restrictions. The court reasons that the refugee-admissions suspension fits comfortably within that language because refugees are a class of aliens.

This textual point is what lets the court say the order does not exceed any “textual limit” on presidential authority. The opinion does not suggest that § 1182(f) is boundless in every respect; rather, it says that the refugee suspension falls inside the authority Congress expressly gave the President. That is why the court concludes the government is likely to prevail on this challenge, at least at the stay stage.

## How foreign-affairs and public-interest concerns support the stay

The panel’s discussion of the remaining stay factors is brief, but the message is clear. The public-interest inquiry and the balance of harms are informed by the President’s broad discretion over immigration and foreign affairs. The order says the remaining factors, “touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States,” favor the government.

That is an important analytical move. In cases involving entry restrictions, the public interest often overlaps with the government’s own institutional interests and its responsibility to conduct foreign relations and protect national interests. The panel treats those interests as weighing against judicial interruption of the President’s suspension while the appeal is pending. So even though the order does not dwell on separate irreparable-harm findings, it clearly understands those harms in light of the national and foreign-policy stakes of the refugee-admissions program.

## What the stay accomplishes, and why the court reserved fuller merits analysis

The immediate effect of the order is to keep the district court from enforcing its injunctions against the refugee-admissions suspension while the appeal proceeds. The government therefore retains the benefit of the presidential suspension under Executive Order 14163 during the appellate process. At the same time, the panel is careful not to overstate what it has decided. It says expressly that a full opinion on the merits will issue later.

That reservation is significant for two reasons. First, it reminds the reader that a stay decision is provisional. Second, it shows that the panel was deciding issue by issue. The court could conclude that the government was likely to prevail on the refugee-admissions issue under § 1182(f) while still signaling possible problems with other parts of the plaintiffs’ APA claims. For this chapter, the key takeaway is that the panel found the government sufficiently likely to win on the challenge to the refugee-entry suspension that the district court’s injunctions had to be stayed pending appeal.

# The APA and Resettlement-Services Issue: Why the Panel Preserves Limited Relief

## Entry restrictions and post-admission services are treated as different legal questions

The interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* separates two questions that can be easy to blur together. One concerns the President’s suspension of refugee entry into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other concerns what the government must do for refugees who have already been admitted, or for refugees whose domestic resettlement services were already underway. The panel is willing to stay the district court’s injunctions almost entirely, but it does not think the government has shown a strong likelihood of success on every challenge relating to the second issue.

That distinction matters because the court’s stay analysis is not a final merits decision. Under *Nken v. Holder*, the court asks whether the government is likely to succeed on the merits, whether it will suffer irreparable harm without a stay, and how the balance of harms and the public interest cut. The panel says the government likely can show its immigration-entry authority is broad enough to suspend refugee admissions. But the panel is not persuaded that the government is likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge to the termination of domestic refugee-services funding.

## The panel’s APA point is narrow but important

The court does not finally decide the APA claim in this order. It says only that, for stay purposes, the government has not shown a strong enough case to justify wiping out all of the district court’s relief. The reason is statutory: the panel reads 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as requiring the government to provide certain resettlement services once refugees have been admitted to the United States.

That is why the order draws a sharp line between stopping entry and stopping services. The first is governed by the President’s § 1182(f) authority. The second is governed by the Refugee Act’s domestic resettlement scheme. In the panel’s view, the government may have broad discretion over who enters, but that does not automatically mean it may stop the post-arrival services Congress has directed it to provide.

## Section 1522 is read as an operating statutory scheme, not a dead letter

The panel points first to § 1522(a)(1)(A), which instructs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, English-language training, cash assistance, and equal access for women. In the panel’s reading, those are not merely aspirational goals. They are statutory duties tied to the government’s provision of assistance to refugees after admission.

The order then relies on other parts of § 1522 that use similarly directive language. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) says the Director “shall” assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minor refugees during interim periods when necessary and is authorized to make decisions for their immediate care. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) says the Secretary “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, providing interim support, medical services, support services, and case management as needed. Taken together, these provisions make the domestic resettlement system look, in the panel’s view, like an ongoing statutory program rather than a merely optional grant scheme.

The panel also invokes Congress’s appropriations and reporting provisions. It notes that Congress continued to appropriate substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance, even if later reduced by rescission, and it points to § 1523’s annual reporting requirement. Those features support the inference that Congress expected the resettlement program to keep operating under legal and fiscal accountability. Judge Lee’s separate opinion reads the same statute very differently, emphasizing the title “Authorization for programs” and the statute’s repeated “authorized” language. The majority’s interim order, however, treats the mandatory provisions as enough to create a serious likelihood that the government cannot lawfully shut off post-admission services wholesale.

## The cooperative-agreement record makes the statutory problem concrete

The statutory reading matters because the government had already terminated the agreements that actually deliver the services. Section 1522 allows reception and placement services to be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. The organizational plaintiffs were just such providers. They had cooperative agreements with the State Department to process refugees overseas and to provide resettlement services after arrival in the United States.

The panel emphasizes what happened next. The State Department terminated all domestic reception-and-placement agreements and most of the overseas processing agreements. The government then told the district court it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency. But when the case reached the panel, the government could not say whether any replacement providers had actually been secured. That uncertainty mattered a great deal. If the existing agreements are gone and no replacement is in place, then the government may be unable to satisfy the statutory duties the panel sees in § 1522.

That is the practical reason the panel preserves limited relief. The court is not simply vindicating abstract statutory language. It is trying to prevent a gap in services for refugees who have already been admitted and who, under the statute, are supposed to receive reception and placement assistance.

## The remedy is a narrow carveout from the stay

The order therefore does something unusual but very specific: it stays the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety, except for the part dealing with post-admission resettlement services. On that issue, the panel directs the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide” the § 1522 reception-and-placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

The scope of that relief is also carefully limited. The services to be restored are the “usual and customary services” previously provided under the agreements. The panel is not creating a new refugee program or expanding relief beyond the prior contractual baseline. It is preserving the existing service structure so that admitted refugees continue to receive the standard package of assistance Congress appears to have required while the appeal is pending.

For a law student, the key is to see how the stay order functions. Most of the district court’s injunctions are paused because the government has a strong showing on the refugee-entry issue. But the panel treats the APA challenge to the termination of post-admission services differently. Because the government has not shown that it is likely to prevail on that issue, and because the record suggests it may not be able to meet its statutory obligations without the existing agreements, a narrow slice of relief survives the stay. That is the core move in this part of the opinion.

# Judge Lee’s Partial Dissent and the Opinion’s Broader Significance

## The Interim Posture and What the Order Actually Does

This Ninth Circuit order is not the final merits opinion; it is a stay pending appeal issued after expedited oral argument. That procedural posture matters. Under *Nken v. Holder*, the court asks whether the stay applicant is likely to succeed on the merits, whether it will suffer irreparable harm without a stay, whether a stay will harm the other side, and where the public interest lies. A stay therefore reflects a predictive judgment, not a final ruling. Here, the panel vacated its earlier administrative stay and prior partial stay, then stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety, except as the order specifically provides. At the same time, the panel signaled that an opinion on the merits would come later.

That makes the order a useful study in emergency appellate management. The court first issued a limited stay, then clarified it, then entered an administrative stay when the district court later created a framework to enforce the carveout, and finally heard the case on an expedited schedule. The order shows how appellate courts can manage fast-moving litigation in stages while preserving space for a later merits decision.

## Judge Lee’s Agreement on the Moratorium

Judge Lee begins from the same premise as the majority on the entry issue: the President has broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend entry of a class of aliens when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. In the order’s terms, that authority “exudes deference,” and the court may not conduct a searching inquiry into the President’s justifications. On that point, Lee agrees that the district court was wrong to enjoin the executive branch from implementing the President’s decision to suspend refugee admissions.

That agreement is important because it separates two distinct questions that can be easy to blur together. One question is whether the government may stop new refugees from entering the country. Another is whether the government may stop providing domestic services to refugees who have already been admitted, or who have been conditionally approved before the suspension. Lee accepts the first proposition. His disagreement is about the second.

## The Statutory Fight Over § 1522

The heart of Lee’s partial dissent is his reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the statute governing domestic resettlement and assistance for refugees. The majority treats at least some parts of § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations on the government to provide reception and placement services, which matters because the government had terminated cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations. Lee, by contrast, reads § 1522 as an authorization statute rather than a mandate.

He starts with the title: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” For Lee, “authorization” is permissive, not compulsory. That reading is reinforced, in his view, by subsections (b) through (e), which repeatedly say the Director “is authorized” to provide assistance. Those verbs suggest discretion. They do not, in his view, create a freestanding duty to operate the programs at all.

Lee also reads subsection (a) differently from the majority. That subsection uses the word “shall” and speaks in mandatory-sounding terms, but it begins with the qualifier, “In providing assistance under this section.” Lee understands that phrase to mean that subsection (a) supplies conditions and considerations that apply if the government chooses to fund programs under the discretionary provisions of § 1522. In other words, the “shall” language tells the agency how to proceed if it provides assistance; it does not itself require the agency to provide that assistance in the first place.

His narrower reading of the more specific provisions follows the same logic. Section 1522(d)(2)(B), dealing with unaccompanied minor refugees, says the Director “shall assume legal responsibility” only “if necessary,” and authorizes decisions to provide immediate care. Lee sees that as a limited, necessity-based provision, not a broad funding mandate. Section 1522(e)(7)(A), addressing cash and medical support for recently resettled refugees, says the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects” for refugees less than 36 months in the country, but only “as needed.” Again, Lee treats that as leaving the scope of implementation to executive judgment.

So the core methodological divide is simple but decisive: the majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties that the court can enforce; Lee reads it as a permissive framework that authorizes programs if appropriations are available, but does not require the executive branch to keep them operating.

## Why the Disagreement Matters

That interpretive difference drives the APA question. If § 1522 imposes a legal duty to provide reception and placement services, then the government’s decision to terminate the cooperative agreements that carry out those services may be unlawful and subject to judicial correction. If, however, § 1522 merely authorizes those programs, then the court has far less power to compel the executive branch to reinstate them. Lee’s bottom line is that courts cannot order the government to fund services Congress did not require it to provide.

That is why he would stay the district court’s injunctions in their entirety. He does not dispute the President’s authority to halt refugee admissions, but he rejects the majority’s tentative conclusion that the government likely violated § 1522 by defunding the domestic resettlement apparatus. His dissent is therefore not just about one statute. It is about the boundary between judicial review and executive discretion when Congress has spoken in mixed terms—using mandatory language in some places and authorization language in others.

The case’s broader significance lies in that boundary. It illustrates the separation between border-entry authority and domestic support obligations: one statute can give the President wide latitude to bar entry, while another may still require the government to support refugees already admitted. It also shows how emergency appellate procedure can preserve, suspend, and partially carve back relief before the merits are fully decided. And it is a good example of statutory interpretation in action, where the court must decide whether words like “shall,” “authorized,” and “as needed” create a binding duty or merely regulate the terms of a discretionary program.

## What the Order Leaves Open

Even after this stay order, several issues remain unresolved. The court has not yet issued its promised merits opinion. It has not finally decided the validity of Executive Order No. 14163, the legality of the funding suspension, or the full scope of any duty to maintain refugee services. The order does, however, reveal the panel’s provisional view: the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to the admissions moratorium, but not likely to defeat at least one APA challenge concerning domestic resettlement services. Judge Lee’s dissent shows how much turns on whether § 1522 is read as a command or a grant of authority.
