Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # The APA and Resettlement-Services Issue: Why the Panel Preserves Limited Relief

## Entry restrictions and post-admission services are treated as different legal questions

The interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* separates two questions that can be easy to blur together. One concerns the President’s suspension of refugee entry into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other concerns what the government must do for refugees who have already been admitted, or for refugees whose domestic resettlement services were already underway. The panel is willing to stay the district court’s injunctions almost entirely, but it does not think the government has shown a strong likelihood of success on every challenge relating to the second issue.

That distinction matters because the court’s stay analysis is not a final merits decision. Under *Nken v. Holder*, the court asks whether the government is likely to succeed on the merits, whether it will suffer irreparable harm without a stay, and how the balance of harms and the public interest cut. The panel says the government likely can show its immigration-entry authority is broad enough to suspend refugee admissions. But the panel is not persuaded that the government is likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge to the termination of domestic refugee-services funding.

## The panel’s APA point is narrow but important

The court does not finally decide the APA claim in this order. It says only that, for stay purposes, the government has not shown a strong enough case to justify wiping out all of the district court’s relief. The reason is statutory: the panel reads 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as requiring the government to provide certain resettlement services once refugees have been admitted to the United States.

That is why the order draws a sharp line between stopping entry and stopping services. The first is governed by the President’s § 1182(f) authority. The second is governed by the Refugee Act’s domestic resettlement scheme. In the panel’s view, the government may have broad discretion over who enters, but that does not automatically mean it may stop the post-arrival services Congress has directed it to provide.

## Section 1522 is read as an operating statutory scheme, not a dead letter

The panel points first to § 1522(a)(1)(A), which instructs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, English-language training, cash assistance, and equal access for women. In the panel’s reading, those are not merely aspirational goals. They are statutory duties tied to the government’s provision of assistance to refugees after admission.

The order then relies on other parts of § 1522 that use similarly directive language. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) says the Director “shall” assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minor refugees during interim periods when necessary and is authorized to make decisions for their immediate care. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) says the Secretary “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, providing interim support, medical services, support services, and case management as needed. Taken together, these provisions make the domestic resettlement system look, in the panel’s view, like an ongoing statutory program rather than a merely optional grant scheme.

The panel also invokes Congress’s appropriations and reporting provisions. It notes that Congress continued to appropriate substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance, even if later reduced by rescission, and it points to § 1523’s annual reporting requirement. Those features support the inference that Congress expected the resettlement program to keep operating under legal and fiscal accountability. Judge Lee’s separate opinion reads the same statute very differently, emphasizing the title “Authorization for programs” and the statute’s repeated “authorized” language. The majority’s interim order, however, treats the mandatory provisions as enough to create a serious likelihood that the government cannot lawfully shut off post-admission services wholesale.

## The cooperative-agreement record makes the statutory problem concrete

The statutory reading matters because the government had already terminated the agreements that actually deliver the services. Section 1522 allows reception and placement services to be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. The organizational plaintiffs were just such providers. They had cooperative agreements with the State Department to process refugees overseas and to provide resettlement services after arrival in the United States.

The panel emphasizes what happened next. The State Department terminated all domestic reception-and-placement agreements and most of the overseas processing agreements. The government then told the district court it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency. But when the case reached the panel, the government could not say whether any replacement providers had actually been secured. That uncertainty mattered a great deal. If the existing agreements are gone and no replacement is in place, then the government may be unable to satisfy the statutory duties the panel sees in § 1522.

That is the practical reason the panel preserves limited relief. The court is not simply vindicating abstract statutory language. It is trying to prevent a gap in services for refugees who have already been admitted and who, under the statute, are supposed to receive reception and placement assistance.

## The remedy is a narrow carveout from the stay

The order therefore does something unusual but very specific: it stays the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety, except for the part dealing with post-admission resettlement services. On that issue, the panel directs the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide” the § 1522 reception-and-placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

The scope of that relief is also carefully limited. The services to be restored are the “usual and customary services” previously provided under the agreements. The panel is not creating a new refugee program or expanding relief beyond the prior contractual baseline. It is preserving the existing service structure so that admitted refugees continue to receive the standard package of assistance Congress appears to have required while the appeal is pending.

For a law student, the key is to see how the stay order functions. Most of the district court’s injunctions are paused because the government has a strong showing on the refugee-entry issue. But the panel treats the APA challenge to the termination of post-admission services differently. Because the government has not shown that it is likely to prevail on that issue, and because the record suggests it may not be able to meet its statutory obligations without the existing agreements, a narrow slice of relief survives the stay. That is the core move in this part of the opinion.
