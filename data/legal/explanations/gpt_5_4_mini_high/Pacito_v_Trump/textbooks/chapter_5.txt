Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 5: # Judge Lee’s Partial Dissent and the Opinion’s Broader Significance

## The Interim Posture and What the Order Actually Does

This Ninth Circuit order is not the final merits opinion; it is a stay pending appeal issued after expedited oral argument. That procedural posture matters. Under *Nken v. Holder*, the court asks whether the stay applicant is likely to succeed on the merits, whether it will suffer irreparable harm without a stay, whether a stay will harm the other side, and where the public interest lies. A stay therefore reflects a predictive judgment, not a final ruling. Here, the panel vacated its earlier administrative stay and prior partial stay, then stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety, except as the order specifically provides. At the same time, the panel signaled that an opinion on the merits would come later.

That makes the order a useful study in emergency appellate management. The court first issued a limited stay, then clarified it, then entered an administrative stay when the district court later created a framework to enforce the carveout, and finally heard the case on an expedited schedule. The order shows how appellate courts can manage fast-moving litigation in stages while preserving space for a later merits decision.

## Judge Lee’s Agreement on the Moratorium

Judge Lee begins from the same premise as the majority on the entry issue: the President has broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend entry of a class of aliens when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. In the order’s terms, that authority “exudes deference,” and the court may not conduct a searching inquiry into the President’s justifications. On that point, Lee agrees that the district court was wrong to enjoin the executive branch from implementing the President’s decision to suspend refugee admissions.

That agreement is important because it separates two distinct questions that can be easy to blur together. One question is whether the government may stop new refugees from entering the country. Another is whether the government may stop providing domestic services to refugees who have already been admitted, or who have been conditionally approved before the suspension. Lee accepts the first proposition. His disagreement is about the second.

## The Statutory Fight Over § 1522

The heart of Lee’s partial dissent is his reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the statute governing domestic resettlement and assistance for refugees. The majority treats at least some parts of § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations on the government to provide reception and placement services, which matters because the government had terminated cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations. Lee, by contrast, reads § 1522 as an authorization statute rather than a mandate.

He starts with the title: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” For Lee, “authorization” is permissive, not compulsory. That reading is reinforced, in his view, by subsections (b) through (e), which repeatedly say the Director “is authorized” to provide assistance. Those verbs suggest discretion. They do not, in his view, create a freestanding duty to operate the programs at all.

Lee also reads subsection (a) differently from the majority. That subsection uses the word “shall” and speaks in mandatory-sounding terms, but it begins with the qualifier, “In providing assistance under this section.” Lee understands that phrase to mean that subsection (a) supplies conditions and considerations that apply if the government chooses to fund programs under the discretionary provisions of § 1522. In other words, the “shall” language tells the agency how to proceed if it provides assistance; it does not itself require the agency to provide that assistance in the first place.

His narrower reading of the more specific provisions follows the same logic. Section 1522(d)(2)(B), dealing with unaccompanied minor refugees, says the Director “shall assume legal responsibility” only “if necessary,” and authorizes decisions to provide immediate care. Lee sees that as a limited, necessity-based provision, not a broad funding mandate. Section 1522(e)(7)(A), addressing cash and medical support for recently resettled refugees, says the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects” for refugees less than 36 months in the country, but only “as needed.” Again, Lee treats that as leaving the scope of implementation to executive judgment.

So the core methodological divide is simple but decisive: the majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties that the court can enforce; Lee reads it as a permissive framework that authorizes programs if appropriations are available, but does not require the executive branch to keep them operating.

## Why the Disagreement Matters

That interpretive difference drives the APA question. If § 1522 imposes a legal duty to provide reception and placement services, then the government’s decision to terminate the cooperative agreements that carry out those services may be unlawful and subject to judicial correction. If, however, § 1522 merely authorizes those programs, then the court has far less power to compel the executive branch to reinstate them. Lee’s bottom line is that courts cannot order the government to fund services Congress did not require it to provide.

That is why he would stay the district court’s injunctions in their entirety. He does not dispute the President’s authority to halt refugee admissions, but he rejects the majority’s tentative conclusion that the government likely violated § 1522 by defunding the domestic resettlement apparatus. His dissent is therefore not just about one statute. It is about the boundary between judicial review and executive discretion when Congress has spoken in mixed terms—using mandatory language in some places and authorization language in others.

The case’s broader significance lies in that boundary. It illustrates the separation between border-entry authority and domestic support obligations: one statute can give the President wide latitude to bar entry, while another may still require the government to support refugees already admitted. It also shows how emergency appellate procedure can preserve, suspend, and partially carve back relief before the merits are fully decided. And it is a good example of statutory interpretation in action, where the court must decide whether words like “shall,” “authorized,” and “as needed” create a binding duty or merely regulate the terms of a discretionary program.

## What the Order Leaves Open

Even after this stay order, several issues remain unresolved. The court has not yet issued its promised merits opinion. It has not finally decided the validity of Executive Order No. 14163, the legality of the funding suspension, or the full scope of any duty to maintain refugee services. The order does, however, reveal the panel’s provisional view: the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to the admissions moratorium, but not likely to defeat at least one APA challenge concerning domestic resettlement services. Judge Lee’s dissent shows how much turns on whether § 1522 is read as a command or a grant of authority.
