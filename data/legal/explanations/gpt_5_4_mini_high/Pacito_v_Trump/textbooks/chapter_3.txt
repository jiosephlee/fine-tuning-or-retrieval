Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # The Stay Standard and the Panel’s Treatment of the Refugee-Admissions Suspension

## Procedural posture: an expedited interim order, not the final merits decision

This order came at a very early and fast-moving stage of the appeal. After the district court entered preliminary injunctions blocking the refugee-admissions suspension and related funding and contracting changes, the Ninth Circuit issued a series of temporary stay orders and then heard expedited oral argument. The panel’s September 12 order is therefore not the court’s final merits opinion. It is an interim order that keeps the district court’s injunctions from operating while the appeal is pending, and the panel expressly says that a full opinion will issue later.

That procedural posture matters. A stay pending appeal does not decide who ultimately wins the case. It asks only whether the challenged injunction should remain in force while the appellate court considers the appeal. Here, the panel vacated its earlier administrative and partial stays and replaced them with a new stay order pending final resolution of the appeal. As a practical matter, that means the refugee-admissions suspension remained operative during appellate review, even though the court reserved fuller analysis for later.

## The four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder

The panel begins with the familiar stay framework from *Nken v. Holder*. A stay applicant must show four things: a strong likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury if a stay is denied, lack of substantial injury to the other side, and that the public interest favors a stay. The court quotes *Nken* for the proposition that the first two factors are the “most critical.”

That framing is important for reading the order. A stay is an equitable, discretionary remedy, so the court does not treat the four factors as a mechanical checklist. Instead, it assesses whether the stay applicant has shown enough to justify temporarily suspending the lower court’s order. In this case, the government needed to show that it had a serious chance of prevailing on appeal and that letting the injunction remain in place would cause harm that could not easily be undone.

## Why likelihood of success and irreparable injury are the “most critical” factors

The panel’s discussion shows how much weight these first two factors carry. If the government is likely to win on the merits of the refugee-admissions issue, that heavily favors a stay. If, in addition, the government faces harm from being compelled to implement a nationwide injunction against a presidential entry restriction, the case for a stay becomes stronger still.

The order does not spell out irreparable harm in detail, but it ties the stay inquiry to the President’s broad immigration and foreign-affairs authority. That context matters because harms involving admission decisions, foreign relations, and the execution of executive branch policy are often hard to reverse later. Once refugees enter or are admitted under a particular regime, the status quo cannot simply be restored after the fact. For the panel, those considerations, together with the merits showing, made a stay appropriate.

## The panel’s conclusion that the government is likely to prevail on the refugee-admissions challenge

On the core refugee-admissions question, the panel says the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. The key point is that the President acted under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which authorizes him to suspend entry when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.

The panel’s merits preview is concise but clear. It relies on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* and its statement that § 1182(f) gives the President broad power to suspend entry of a class of aliens. The panel concludes that the refugee suspension falls within that statutory authority and that the executive order does not exceed any textual limit in the statute. In stay posture, that was enough to support a conclusion that the government was likely to win on this aspect of the appeal.

## *Trump v. Hawaii*’s central role: deference under § 1182(f)

*Trump v. Hawaii* is the doctrinal anchor for the panel’s analysis. The order quotes *Hawaii* for the idea that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and gives the President “ample power” to impose entry restrictions beyond those elsewhere found in the Immigration and Nationality Act. That language does a lot of work here.

The panel treats *Hawaii* as controlling on the level of judicial scrutiny. It says courts may not engage in a “searching inquiry” into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. In other words, once the President has made the statutory finding required by § 1182(f)—that the entry of the covered aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests—the court does not second-guess the policy reasoning behind that finding. The question is not whether the court would have reached the same policy judgment, but whether the President acted within the authority Congress gave him.

## The statutory prerequisite under § 1182(f): a presidential finding of detriment

The panel reads § 1182(f) to contain one key prerequisite: the President must find that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The order says that requirement was “undoubtedly” satisfied here. That is the statute’s threshold, and the court treats it as the only textual condition that matters for the stay analysis.

This is a classic example of how courts distinguish between statutory authority and policy wisdom. The panel is not saying the refugee suspension is a good idea or that the plaintiffs’ concerns are trivial. It is saying that, for purposes of the stay, the statute gives the President enough room to suspend entry of a covered class of aliens so long as he makes the required detriment finding. Because the executive order did that, the government showed a strong likelihood of success on this issue.

## The text allowing suspension of “any class of aliens”

The panel also emphasizes the breadth of the statutory text. Section 1182(f) allows the President to suspend the entry of “all aliens or any class of aliens” as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or to impose appropriate restrictions. The court reasons that the refugee-admissions suspension fits comfortably within that language because refugees are a class of aliens.

This textual point is what lets the court say the order does not exceed any “textual limit” on presidential authority. The opinion does not suggest that § 1182(f) is boundless in every respect; rather, it says that the refugee suspension falls inside the authority Congress expressly gave the President. That is why the court concludes the government is likely to prevail on this challenge, at least at the stay stage.

## How foreign-affairs and public-interest concerns support the stay

The panel’s discussion of the remaining stay factors is brief, but the message is clear. The public-interest inquiry and the balance of harms are informed by the President’s broad discretion over immigration and foreign affairs. The order says the remaining factors, “touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States,” favor the government.

That is an important analytical move. In cases involving entry restrictions, the public interest often overlaps with the government’s own institutional interests and its responsibility to conduct foreign relations and protect national interests. The panel treats those interests as weighing against judicial interruption of the President’s suspension while the appeal is pending. So even though the order does not dwell on separate irreparable-harm findings, it clearly understands those harms in light of the national and foreign-policy stakes of the refugee-admissions program.

## What the stay accomplishes, and why the court reserved fuller merits analysis

The immediate effect of the order is to keep the district court from enforcing its injunctions against the refugee-admissions suspension while the appeal proceeds. The government therefore retains the benefit of the presidential suspension under Executive Order 14163 during the appellate process. At the same time, the panel is careful not to overstate what it has decided. It says expressly that a full opinion on the merits will issue later.

That reservation is significant for two reasons. First, it reminds the reader that a stay decision is provisional. Second, it shows that the panel was deciding issue by issue. The court could conclude that the government was likely to prevail on the refugee-admissions issue under § 1182(f) while still signaling possible problems with other parts of the plaintiffs’ APA claims. For this chapter, the key takeaway is that the panel found the government sufficiently likely to win on the challenge to the refugee-entry suspension that the district court’s injunctions had to be stayed pending appeal.
