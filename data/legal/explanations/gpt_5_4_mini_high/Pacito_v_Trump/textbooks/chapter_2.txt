Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # From District Court Injunctions to an Interim Appellate Stay

## The February 2025 suit in the Western District of Washington

The case began in February 2025, when the plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington. The plaintiffs included refugees who had already been admitted to the United States, refugees who had been approved for resettlement but were still abroad, and three resettlement organizations that had agreements with the State Department to help process refugees overseas and provide domestic resettlement services. Their lawsuit attacked two separate government actions: first, President Trump’s executive order suspending refugee admissions under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, or USRAP; and second, the State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding, including funding for domestic resettlement services.

That procedural split matters. One part of the case concerns who may enter the country as a refugee. Another concerns what services the government must provide after refugees have been admitted. The district court responded to both issues with preliminary injunctions, and the Ninth Circuit’s later stay orders tracked that same division.

## The district court’s February 25 oral preliminary injunction

On February 25, the district court orally entered a preliminary injunction barring enforcement or implementation of several sections of Executive Order 14163. A preliminary injunction is an early merits-based order that preserves the status quo while the case is litigated; it is not a final judgment, but it can immediately stop the government from carrying out challenged conduct. Here, the district court concluded at least preliminarily that the executive order could not be enforced as written.

The next day, February 26, the government responded by terminating every cooperative agreement for refugee reception and placement services in the United States, and nearly all agreements for overseas processing support. That action intensified the dispute, because it meant the government was not just defending the refugee-entry suspension; it was also dismantling the network of contractors and grantees that made refugee resettlement work in practice.

## The February 28 written order and the March 24 injunction

On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the scope of its oral ruling. That written order specified that the injunction also prohibited suspension of USRAP funding. In other words, the court treated the funding freeze as part of the same controversy and made clear that the earlier oral injunction reached it too.

A month later, on March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction. This one ordered the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements it had terminated after the February 25 ruling. So by that point, the district court had entered two related but distinct injunctions: one aimed at the legality of the refugee admissions suspension, and another aimed at the government’s termination of resettlement contracts and funding.

For appellate purposes, those two injunctions created two separate fronts. The government appealed both orders, filing notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25.

## The government’s appeals and the Ninth Circuit’s March 25 partial stay

Once the appeals were filed, the government asked the Ninth Circuit for a stay pending appeal. A stay pending appeal is different from the district court’s preliminary injunction: it temporarily pauses the injunction while the appellate court considers whether the government is likely to win, whether it will suffer irreparable harm without relief, and how the balance of harms and the public interest cut. In this case, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted only a partial stay on March 25.

The motions panel relied heavily on Trump v. Hawaii, emphasizing the deferential language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which gives the President authority to suspend the entry of classes of aliens when he finds their entry would be detrimental to the national interest. On that basis, the panel concluded that the government was likely to succeed in challenging the injunction as it applied to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions. But the panel did not give the government everything it wanted. It preserved a narrow carveout for individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025, the date the executive order went into effect.

That carveout is important because it shows the panel was not simply choosing between all-or-nothing relief. Instead, it left a small subset of cases outside the stay, creating an exception that later became the focus of further litigation and implementation disputes. The panel then issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9 to clarify that limited carveout.

## The July 14 enforcement framework and the July 18 administrative stay

After briefing but before the scheduled merits argument, the district court on July 14 issued another order designed to implement the carveout. It established a framework for enforcing the limited exception and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. In practical terms, the district court was trying to operationalize the earlier appellate carveout: identify who was covered, how they would be processed, and what compliance would look like.

The government responded on July 17 with an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the Ninth Circuit’s stay order. The following day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is usually a short-term, emergency measure that holds things in place while the court decides what more permanent interim relief to grant. It is not the same thing as a full stay pending appeal, but it serves the same practical purpose in the immediate moment: freezing the disputed order long enough for appellate review.

The court also accelerated the case, scheduling oral argument on an expedited basis. That choice signals that the panel viewed the dispute as urgent and wanted to address the interim relief quickly rather than wait for the ordinary calendar.

## The September expedited argument and the new stay pending appeal

The panel heard oral argument on September 3, 2025, and then issued the order reproduced here. The court made clear that an opinion on the merits would come later, but that in the meantime the order itself would govern as the operative stay pending appeal. It therefore vacated both its July 18 administrative stay and its earlier March 25 stay, as later clarified in April and May. In other words, the panel replaced its prior stopgap orders with a new interim arrangement.

This is a key procedural point. The panel was not finally deciding the appeal. It was managing the case while the appeal remained pending. That is why the order repeatedly says that a merits opinion will issue “in due course” and that the present order is only a stay pending final resolution of the appeal.

The panel also stated that the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions were stayed in their entirety, except as specified in the order. At the same time, the order explains that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the refugee admissions suspension, but not likely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act challenge relating to the government’s duty to provide reception and placement services after refugees are admitted. On that basis, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

So the interim order did two things at once: it lifted the district court’s injunctions broadly, but it also preserved relief tied to the statutory resettlement-services issue. That mixed disposition explains why the order is not a final merits opinion and why Judge Lee wrote separately in partial concurrence and partial dissent. The panel had reached enough agreement to issue interim relief, but not full agreement on how far the federal government’s obligations under the refugee-resettlement statute extended.

## Why this procedural history matters

For a law student, the main lesson is that the procedural posture here is doing a lot of work. The district court entered preliminary injunctions against both the refugee admissions suspension and the funding/contract terminations. The Ninth Circuit then intervened with partial stays, a limited carveout, and later an administrative stay when the district court tried to enforce that carveout. The present order is another interim step: it supersedes the earlier stay orders and sets the rules while the appeal awaits a full opinion.

When you read the order this way, the sequence becomes easier to follow. First comes the district court’s injunction. Then comes the government’s appeal and request for a stay. Then comes the motions panel’s partial stay, its carveout, and the later enforcement dispute. Finally, after expedited argument, the panel issues a new interim stay order and signals that the merits decision is still to come.
