Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# From Travel Ban to Refugee Freeze: How *Pacito* Extends *Trump v. Hawaii*’s Deference Under § 1182(f)

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* matters because it takes the logic of *Trump v. Hawaii* and applies it to something even more consequential than a travel ban: a wholesale suspension of refugee admissions. At the stay stage, the panel concluded that the government is likely to prevail on the claim that President Trump could halt refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and it did so with remarkably little hesitation. If that reasoning holds, § 1182(f) may function less like a limited exclusion authority and more like a broad presidential override of immigration statutes, including the Refugee Act, unless Congress has drawn a very clear textual boundary.

At the same time, the panel refused to give the government the same protection on the domestic resettlement side. It concluded that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge concerning the statutory duty to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted. That split — broad executive power to stop entry, but still-enforceable statutory obligations after entry — may end up defining the next phase of refugee litigation.

## The panel’s reliance on *Hawaii* is the real story

This is a stay order, not the final merits decision, but its reasoning is already telling. Under *Nken v. Holder*, the government had to show likely success on the merits, irreparable harm, and favorable equities and public interest. The panel said those factors favored a stay as to the refugee-admissions challenge, and its merits analysis was almost entirely a straight-line application of *Trump v. Hawaii*.

The key passage is the court’s statement that it could not engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Quoting *Hawaii*, the panel emphasized that the “sole prerequisite” in § 1182(f) is that the President “find[]” entry would be detrimental to the national interest. On the panel’s reading, that finding was made, and that was enough. The court also repeated *Hawaii*’s language that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and that the statute contains no textual limit the Executive Order transgressed.

That is an expansive reading of *Hawaii*. *Hawaii* involved a presidential proclamation restricting entry from several countries; *Pacito* involves a suspension of the refugee admissions program itself. The difference matters. Refugee admissions are not just another immigration category in the abstract; they are the product of a detailed statutory scheme. But the panel’s order treats § 1182(f) as the operative source of authority and sees no need to reconcile it with the Refugee Act at the stay stage. If that approach survives, then the Refugee Act may be far less of a constraint on presidential exclusion decisions than litigants have long hoped.

## A sharp line between admission and resettlement

The more interesting doctrinal move may be the panel’s refusal to let the administration’s § 1182(f) victory spill over into the post-admission context. The order says the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge because 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes statutory duties to provide certain services to refugees who have already been admitted. The panel pointed to language requiring resources for employment training and placement, English-language instruction, cash assistance, and support for unaccompanied refugee children, all framed in mandatory terms.

That part of the order is important for two reasons. First, it suggests the court is willing to read the Refugee Act as creating enforceable obligations once refugees are here. Second, it shows the limits of the administration’s argument that broad immigration discretion can justify shutting down the entire refugee system from end to end. The panel appears to be saying: the President may be able to suspend entry under § 1182(f), but the government still has to obey Congress’s post-entry mandates unless it can identify a lawful basis not to.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent pushes back hard on that point. He reads § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandate, emphasizing the statute’s title and permissive language — “authorized,” “as needed,” and “to the extent of available appropriations.” That disagreement may be where the merits fight ultimately lives on the services question. But even the dissent underscores the larger point: the panel sees a meaningful doctrinal divide between the power to keep refugees out and the obligation to care for those already admitted.

## What *Pacito* means going forward

If *Pacito* becomes the template, § 1182(f) will look even more like a broad presidential emergency valve. The practical lesson for future litigants is that attacking the underlying suspension itself will be very hard unless Congress has imposed a clear, specific limit that courts are willing to enforce against the President. General statutory schemes, even detailed ones like the Refugee Act, may not be enough if a court thinks § 1182(f) speaks directly to the entry question.

That shifts the center of gravity in refugee litigation. The strongest challenges may no longer be aimed at stopping the suspension outright, but at forcing the government to honor statutory duties after admission, preserve funding, and keep the resettlement apparatus functioning. In other words, *Pacito* suggests that the more promising legal terrain may be implementation rather than prohibition.

The broader implication is unsettling for anyone who thought *Hawaii* was confined to the facts of the travel ban. The Ninth Circuit’s stay order suggests the case may instead stand for a more general proposition: unless Congress has clearly said otherwise, § 1182(f) lets the President suspend the entry of a whole class of aliens — including refugees — on the basis of his own national-interest finding. If that is where the law is headed, the real check on refugee freezes may be legislative, not judicial.



# Stopping Admissions Is One Thing, Abandoning Admitted Refugees Is Another

## Why this case matters

*Pacito v. Trump* may end up being remembered less for whether the President can pause new refugee admissions than for what happens to refugees already here. In an expedited stay order, the Ninth Circuit signaled that those are not the same question. On the admissions side, the panel said the government is likely to prevail under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and *Trump v. Hawaii*. On the services side, though, the panel concluded the government is not likely to win at least one APA challenge because Congress appears to have imposed real duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted.

That distinction is doctrinally important. It separates the President’s broad power over entry from the government’s post-entry statutory obligations. And if that line holds, it could become a recurring feature of immigration litigation: the executive may be able to stop new arrivals, but not necessarily dismantle the domestic infrastructure Congress created for people who have already been admitted.

## The panel’s split ruling

This is only a stay order, not the full merits opinion the court says will come later. But it is still a strong signal. The panel vacated its earlier stays and stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions in large part, but it also directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees admitted to the United States.

On the admissions question, the court leans heavily on *Hawaii*. Section 1182(f), the panel says, “exudes deference” and gives the President broad discretion to suspend entry when he finds that entry would be detrimental to the national interest. The court explicitly says it cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the President’s justifications. On that logic, the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the refugee-entry suspension itself.

But the panel stops there. It says the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim because § 1522 does more than merely authorize refugee services; it appears to require them. The court points to language telling the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make resources available for employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and equal participation for women. It also cites provisions requiring responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children and directing the Secretary to develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have recently arrived. In the panel’s view, those provisions matter because Congress has already told the executive branch to run a domestic resettlement system, not merely to permit one if officials feel like it.

The practical consequence is significant. The court did not just say the government may not shut off a program in the abstract. It ordered the reinstatement of cooperative agreements that actually make the resettlement system function.

## The statutory divide the court draws

The key move in the order is to treat refugee admissions and refugee resettlement as separate statutory domains. That is not self-evident as a matter of policy, but it is plausible as a matter of law. Section 1182(f) is about entry. Section 1522 is about what happens after admission. The panel’s reasoning suggests that a broad power to suspend entry does not automatically carry with it a power to suspend every downstream statutory obligation tied to refugees already in the country.

That matters because executive-branch arguments in immigration cases often blur the line between admission control and program administration. If the President can stop new refugees from coming in, can the executive also terminate the contractor network that provides initial housing, job placement, language training, and case management for refugees who were admitted before the cutoff? The Ninth Circuit’s answer, at least provisionally, is no—at least not without confronting the language of § 1522.

The “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier will likely be the center of the later merits fight. The panel cites the fact that Congress appropriated billions for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, even after a rescission, as part of the backdrop for concluding that the statutory program still has funding. That does not mean the government must spend without limit. But it does suggest the court sees a funded statutory program that cannot simply be switched off by executive preference.

## Judge Lee’s narrower reading

Judge Lee’s partial dissent sharpens the interpretive dispute. He agrees that the President can impose a moratorium on refugee admissions, but he reads § 1522 as authorization rather than command. His argument is textually straightforward: the section is titled “Authorization for programs,” repeatedly uses “is authorized,” and, in his view, the mandatory-sounding language in subsection (a) merely sets conditions for when the government chooses to fund a program.

He also resists the majority’s reliance on the more specific provisions in subsections (d) and (e), reading them as limited, qualified, and still deferential to executive discretion. Under that reading, the statute gives the government room to create, scale back, or terminate refugee services so long as appropriations exist. If that view prevails, the court would have little basis to force the executive branch to keep operating a program it has chosen to wind down.

The dissent’s logic is not weak. In many spending statutes, “authorized” really does signal discretion, and program titles are often poor evidence of a command. But the majority’s response appears to be that the statute’s structure, taken as a whole, goes beyond mere permission. Congress did not just describe refugee services in aspirational terms; it assigned responsibilities, including for children and for recently arrived refugees, and did so in a statutory scheme that presupposes continuing federal administration.

## What to watch next

The immediate takeaway is that the Ninth Circuit is treating the refugee system as two things at once: an entry regime with enormous presidential discretion, and a domestic resettlement regime with enforceable statutory obligations. That is a potentially important template for future cases. It invites litigants to challenge not just who is allowed in, but whether the government can legally disable the administrative machinery that Congress built for those already admitted.

If the later merits opinion follows this order, the case could become a leading example of how far *Hawaii* goes—and how far it does not. The President may have wide latitude to stop new refugee admissions. But under the Ninth Circuit’s apparent view, that does not necessarily license abandoning refugees already in the United States to a system Congress has told the government to maintain.



# Authorization or Obligation? The Real Statutory Fight in *Pacito v. Trump*

## Why this order matters

The most consequential part of the Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* is not the part about refugee admissions. On that front, the panel largely treats the case as a familiar *Trump v. Hawaii* question: when the President invokes 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), courts are supposed to show substantial deference, and the government is likely to win a stay on the challenge to the suspension of refugee entry.

The harder issue is what happens after a refugee has already been admitted. There, the panel refuses to give the government the same kind of latitude. It concludes that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge because the statute governing refugee resettlement, 8 U.S.C. § 1522, appears to impose actual duties to provide reception and placement services. Judge Lee’s partial dissent, by contrast, reads the same statute as largely permissive: Congress authorized refugee-assistance programs, but did not require the executive branch to keep them running.

That is the real fight in the case, and it is likely to matter well beyond refugee policy. If the panel’s view prevails, it will reinforce the idea that broad executive control over admission does not carry with it a power to defund or disable congressionally structured domestic programs. If Judge Lee’s view prevails, it would give the executive much more room to reshape statutory programs by ending cooperative agreements and freezing funding, so long as the underlying statute contains enough language of authorization and discretion.

## The statute: where “authorized” meets “shall”

The panel’s reasoning turns on the structure of § 1522. Judge Lee emphasizes the statute’s title — “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” — and the repeated use of “authorized” in subsections (b) through (e). That is not a trivial point. Congress often uses “authorized” to signal that an agency may create a program, rather than must.

But the panel points to a different set of words: in § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director “shall,” “to the extent of available appropriations,” make available sufficient resources for employment training, English-language instruction, cash assistance, and equal participation by women. In § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director “shall assume legal responsibility” for unaccompanied refugee children when necessary. And in § 1522(e)(7)(A), the Secretary “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than 36 months.

That combination matters. A statute can be partly discretionary and partly mandatory. Judge Lee is surely right that § 1522 does not read like a model of crisp drafting; it is a blend of program authorization, funding mechanics, and operational commands. But the panel’s key move is to treat the mandatory verbs as doing real work. If Congress said the agency “shall” provide certain services, and also specified that those services may be delivered through cooperative agreements, then the executive cannot simply terminate all the agreements and declare the program dormant without confronting the statute’s plain language.

The panel also points to the government’s own uncertainty about whether it had a replacement provider lined up. That uncertainty cuts against a stay because it suggests the administration was not merely changing contractors; it was risking a failure to provide services the statute appears to require. In stay terms, that is enough to make the government’s position look weaker on the APA side, even while it remains strong on the admissions side.

## Judge Lee’s dissent: authorization is not obligation

Judge Lee’s dissent is strongest where it highlights the statute’s overall permissive design. The title says “authorization.” The operative subsections repeatedly say the Director “is authorized” to provide assistance. And phrases like “to the extent of available appropriations” and “as needed” are genuine limiting language. Read that way, § 1522 looks less like a mandatory spending command and more like a framework within which the executive may choose how much to do.

Her reading of § 1522(a) is especially important. She treats subsection (a) as a set of “conditions and considerations” that apply if the government chooses to fund refugee programs under the more discretionary subsections that follow. That is a plausible structural argument. It avoids reading every “shall” as an absolute command detached from the broader statutory scheme.

Still, the dissent has to work hard to explain away the mandatory verbs in subsections (d) and (e). Saying that those provisions are qualified by “necessary” or “as needed” helps, but it does not eliminate the force of “shall.” Judge Lee is right that those phrases leave room for administrative judgment, yet they do not obviously convert a duty into a permission. The panel’s interim order seems to read those limits as setting the scope of the duty, not negating it.

## The deeper doctrinal point

What makes *Pacito* interesting is that it splits immigration law into two very different zones. On the front end, the President’s authority to suspend entry under § 1182(f) remains broad and deferential. On the back end, once the government has admitted refugees or approved them for resettlement, Congress may have imposed statutory obligations that the executive cannot sidestep by defunding the program.

That distinction has obvious implications for future fights over executive branch “realignment” efforts. A president may have substantial authority to slow, suspend, or condition entry. But if Congress has separately created a domestic assistance regime, with mandatory language and appropriated funds, the executive may not be able to eliminate the program through contract terminations alone. In that sense, this case sits near the line between ordinary administrative discretion and impoundment-like disputes over whether the executive can effectively refuse to carry out a statutory program.

There is also a remedial issue lurking in the background. The panel does not say the court can run the refugee program itself. It says the government must reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary” to provide the services required by § 1522, and that those services should include the “usual and customary” services under prior agreements. That is a narrower, more administrable remedy than a wholesale judicial takeover of refugee resettlement. But it still reflects a meaningful proposition: when Congress creates a statutory program, courts can require the executive to keep the machinery operating.

## Bottom line

The interim order in *Pacito* gives the government a major win on refugee admissions, but it also signals that domestic refugee-resettlement services may be a different matter altogether. Judge Lee’s dissent captures the executive’s best argument: § 1522 is written in the language of authorization, not compulsion. The panel’s response is that Congress used mandatory terms where it meant to, and those terms cannot be erased by an executive decision to freeze the program.

That is likely the decisive question when the merits opinion arrives. And whatever the Ninth Circuit ultimately says, the case is a reminder that in immigration law, the power to keep people out is not the same as the power to stop providing for those already inside.



# The Emergency Docket Comes to the Court of Appeals: *Pacito*’s Stay Order as a Shadow Merits Decision

## Why this case matters

*Pacito v. Trump* is not just another immigration stay order. It is a reminder that, in high-stakes public-law cases, the “stay pending appeal” can function less like a pause button and more like an early merits ruling. Here, the Ninth Circuit did two unusual things at once: it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions against the refugee-admissions freeze, and it also directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements needed to keep statutory refugee services operating for refugees already admitted to the United States.

That combination matters. Stays are supposed to preserve the status quo while an appeal is pending. This order does more than preserve; it reallocates legal and practical authority in a way that will shape the refugee program before the court’s full merits opinion arrives. In that sense, *Pacito* is a useful case study in how appellate courts are managing urgent, program-wide disputes under compressed timelines.

## A stay order that does real merits work

The panel’s analysis follows the familiar *Nken v. Holder* framework: likelihood of success, irreparable harm, injury to other parties, and the public interest. But the order quickly shows why stay litigation often collapses into merits litigation. On the refugee-admissions issue, the court relies almost entirely on *Trump v. Hawaii* and § 1182(f), emphasizing that the President need only “find” that entry would be detrimental to the national interest and that courts may not conduct a “searching inquiry” into the President’s reasons. On that logic, the government is “likely to prevail” on the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions.

That part of the order will surprise few readers who have followed the post-*Hawaii* landscape. The more interesting move comes on the APA side. The panel says the government is not likely to prevail on at least one challenge because § 1522 appears to impose statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services for refugees who have already been admitted. It points to provisions using mandatory language—“shall” make resources available, “shall” assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied children when necessary, “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for certain refugees—and notes that Congress has appropriated substantial funding for migration and refugee assistance. On the record before it, and in light of the government’s uncertainty about whether any replacement provider existed, the court ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services.

That is a striking form of interim relief. Courts usually grant stays by withholding enforcement of lower-court orders. They do not often use a stay posture to compel the executive branch to restore contractual infrastructure. The practical effect here is to keep the refugee-resettlement system from collapsing while the appeal proceeds, even as the panel signals that the government is likely to win on the entry restriction itself.

## The real fight is over § 1522

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent makes clear where the fault line lies. He agrees that the President has authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions, but he rejects the majority’s reading of § 1522 as imposing a mandatory duty to fund resettlement services. In his view, the section is framed as an authorization, not a command: its title says “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement,” and the operative subsections repeatedly say the Director “is authorized” to provide assistance. He reads the “shall” language in § 1522(a) as conditional—an instruction about how to proceed if the government chooses to fund programs under the section, not a freestanding command to operate those programs.

That textual dispute is not just academic. It determines whether the government can suspend services to admitted refugees while a refugee-admissions freeze is in place. If § 1522 is merely permissive, then the district court’s injunction on the funding side overreaches. If § 1522 contains affirmative duties, then the government’s own uncertainty about replacement providers makes continued judicial supervision much easier to justify.

My sense is that the majority’s approach is more plausible than the dissent suggests, at least for purposes of interim relief. The statute is not written as a blank check. It contains multiple mandatory-sounding provisions and ties the obligations to appropriated funds. The panel was also confronting an administrative vacuum: the government told the district court it was preparing a new request for proposals, but at argument it could not say what progress had been made. That kind of record matters when the question is whether to leave refugees without services during the appeal. The order reflects a pragmatic judgment that, whatever the ultimate merits, the government had not shown it could lawfully or practically shoulder out the existing service network right away.

## What this says about the appellate emergency docket

The broader lesson is about procedure. The panel heard expedited argument, said an opinion would follow, and in the meantime issued an order that does substantial merits work. That is not quite the Supreme Court’s “shadow docket,” but it is closer to that style of adjudication than the traditional appellate model. In a case like this, the emergency posture is not incidental; it is the mechanism through which the court decides what the law will look like while the merits are still pending.

That may be necessary in sprawling public-law cases with immediate consequences. But it also means the distinction between interim relief and final resolution is getting blurrier. The more often appellate courts use stay orders to parse statutory text, weigh programmatic consequences, and direct affirmative executive action, the more those orders begin to function as quasi-merits decisions. *Pacito* is a good example of that trend: a stay order that not only pauses a district court injunction, but also effectively redraws the contours of refugee policy pending appeal.

The merits opinion will matter, of course. But for now, the Ninth Circuit has already told us a great deal about where it thinks this case is headed—and about how much substantive law can be made in the emergency interval before the full opinion arrives.
