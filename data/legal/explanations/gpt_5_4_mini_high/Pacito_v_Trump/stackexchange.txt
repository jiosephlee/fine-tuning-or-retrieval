Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How can the Ninth Circuit issue a stay pending appeal before releasing its full merits opinion?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural posture here. The panel says the case is "submitted for decision" and that "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course," but in the meantime it vacates its prior stays and enters a new stay pending appeal. Is this normal appellate practice? What exactly is the legal effect of an interim order like this compared to the eventual merits opinion? I'm also not sure why the court says the government's July 17 emergency motion is "denied as moot" after changing the stay structure anyway.
Answer:
Yes. This is normal appellate practice in an emergency case.

What the Ninth Circuit did here was **not** decide the merits in the final sense; it issued an **interim stay order** while the merits opinion is still being written.

### 1) A stay pending appeal is a provisional order, not the merits judgment
Under **Nken v. Holder**, a court can grant a stay pending appeal based on the familiar four factors, especially likelihood of success and irreparable harm. That kind of order simply **suspends enforcement of the district court’s injunctions** while the appeal is being decided. It does **not** itself resolve the appeal.

That is exactly the posture the panel described:

> “An opinion will issue in due course. In the interim, this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court.”

So the panel is saying: we’ve heard argument, and while the full opinion is being finalized, we are entering a binding order that controls the parties immediately.

### 2) The court can issue the stay before the written merits opinion
Appellate courts often do this in expedited or emergency cases. The panel can determine, on the basis of briefing and argument, that a stay should enter now, while the full explanatory opinion comes later.

Here, the order itself shows that sequence:

- oral argument was already held,
- the case was “submitted for decision,”
- the panel then entered a new stay order,
- and said the merits opinion will follow.

That is procedurally ordinary. The later opinion will explain the court’s reasoning and resolve the appeal; the interim order just governs the case in the meantime.

### 3) Why vacate the old stays?
Because the new order replaces them.

The panel explicitly said it was vacating:

- its **administrative stay** of July 18, and
- its **prior stay** of March 25, as amended.

Then it issued a fresh stay “pending final resolution of this appeal.” That avoids multiple overlapping stay orders and makes clear what rule applies going forward.

### 4) Why was the government’s July 17 motion denied as moot?
Because the court’s own new order effectively overtook the relief the government had sought.

A motion is **moot** when the court’s subsequent action makes separate relief unnecessary. Here, once the panel vacated the earlier stay structure and entered a new stay order, there was no longer a live need to decide the government’s July 17 emergency motion to “enforce, clarify or amend” the old stay order. The new order addressed the operative posture directly.

### Bottom line
The interim stay order is a **procedural, immediately effective command** that preserves the status quo pending the appellate court’s full opinion. The later merits opinion will be the court’s **substantive resolution** of the appeal. The two are related, but they are not the same thing.

### Why does 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) seem to let the President suspend refugee entry despite the Refugee Act's detailed statutory scheme?
Question:
The panel says the government is likely to win on the challenge to the executive order because under Trump v. Hawaii the only prerequisite in § 1182(f) is that the President find entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. But the Refugee Act of 1980 seems to create an ongoing framework for refugee admissions. How do courts decide whether the President's broad § 1182(f) power overrides, supplements, or is limited by the more specific refugee statutes? I feel like there should be some argument that a later, more specific statute cabins the general suspension power.
Answer:
Short answer: **in this order, the Ninth Circuit is not treating the Refugee Act as a limit on § 1182(f) because the provisions it relies on regulate a different subject — domestic resettlement after admission — while § 1182(f) regulates entry itself.** And on the entry question, the panel says it is bound by *Trump v. Hawaii*.

A few points from the order make that clear:

1. **The panel treats § 1182(f) as a broad entry-suspension power.**  
   It quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that the only statutory prerequisite is that the President “find[]” entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The order says:
   > “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’”
   
   And it adds that § 1182(f)’s text is “clear” and authorizes suspension of “any class of aliens” with no textual limit in the order itself.

2. **The Refugee Act provisions discussed in the order are mostly post-entry provisions.**  
   The panel’s APA discussion focuses on **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which governs reception and placement services, employment training, English training, cash assistance, and similar services for **refugees who have been admitted to the United States**. The order repeatedly frames these as services for refugees “after their admission into the United States.”

   That means the panel is not reading § 1522 as a statute about who gets to enter. It is reading it as a statute about what happens **once refugees are already here**.

3. **So the court is harmonizing the statutes, not saying one repeals the other.**  
   The easiest way to read the order is:

   - **§ 1182(f)** lets the President suspend **entry** of a class of aliens, including refugees, if he makes the requisite detrimental-interest finding; and
   - **§ 1522** still governs the government’s duties toward refugees **already admitted**.

   On that reading, there is no direct conflict to resolve.

4. **The opinion does not do a full “specific statute beats general statute” analysis.**  
   That canon only matters if the statutes are actually in conflict or Congress has clearly limited the earlier statute. The order does not find such a limit in the Refugee Act. Instead, it relies on *Hawaii* and says the government is “likely to prevail” on the challenge to the entry suspension.

So the key is that the panel sees **entry** and **resettlement** as separate statutory domains. The Refugee Act’s detailed scheme does not, in this order, cabin the President’s § 1182(f) authority to suspend entry; it just continues to govern the treatment of refugees once admitted.

### What is the legal difference between suspending refugee 'entry' and cutting off services for refugees already admitted?
Question:
One thing I can't reconcile is that the panel seems very deferential to the President on stopping future refugee admissions, but at the same time says the government is probably not going to win on at least one APA claim involving services for refugees already in the U.S. Is the key distinction that § 1182(f) only concerns entry of aliens from abroad, while domestic resettlement obligations arise from a different statute altogether? If so, why did the district court initially treat the funding suspension as tied closely enough to the executive order to enjoin it broadly?
Answer:
Yes. The key distinction in *Pacito* is that the two actions rest on different legal authority and regulate different things:

- **Suspending “entry”** is a border/immigration power. The panel treats that as governed by **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)**, which lets the President suspend the entry of aliens or a class of aliens if he finds their entry would be detrimental. The panel relies on *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that this authority “exudes deference” and that courts should not conduct a searching review of the President’s justification.

- **Cutting off services for refugees already admitted** is a **domestic resettlement** issue, not an entry issue. The panel points to **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which governs reception and placement services after refugees are in the United States, including employment training, English-language assistance, cash assistance, and related support. That is why the court says the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim: the legality of defunding those services turns on § 1522 and the APA, not on § 1182(f).

So your instinct is right: **§ 1182(f) is about who may enter; § 1522 is about what the government must do for refugees once admitted.** The deference that applies to the former does not automatically carry over to the latter.

As for the district court’s broad injunction, the order shows why it did that. The court first orally enjoined implementation of the executive order, then issued a written order “to define the scope” of that injunction and stated that it also prohibited the **suspension of USRAP funding**. That makes sense in preliminary-injunction terms because the funding cutoff was treated as part of the same overall program shutdown: it came immediately after the oral ruling, affected the same USRAP system, and would have undercut relief for refugees and resettlement agencies while the case was pending.

The appellate panel, however, is separating the two issues for stay purposes: it is staying the injunction as to the **entry suspension**, but not as to the **domestic service obligations** tied to § 1522.

### Did the earlier carveout for 'conditionally approved' refugees create any rights, and why was it effectively withdrawn here?
Question:
Earlier in the case, the motions panel had denied a stay as to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025, and later clarified that carveout. In this new order, though, the court stays the district court's injunctions almost entirely and only preserves relief related to reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. Why wasn't the earlier carveout preserved? Does a prior stay ruling on likelihood of success have any law-of-the-case effect, or can the merits panel revisit it freely?
Answer:
No. The earlier carveout did **not** create a substantive right to admission; it was only a **temporary stay determination**.

Here’s the key distinction:

- A **stay pending appeal** is not a merits judgment. Under *Nken v. Holder*, the court is making a **provisional prediction** about likely success, irreparable harm, equities, and the public interest.
- So when the motions panel said the stay was denied “to the extent” the injunction applied to people who were **conditionally approved** before January 20, 2025, that was just a limited interim carveout. It preserved the district court’s injunction for that subset **for the time being**; it did not adjudicate a final legal entitlement.

Why was it withdrawn? Because in this order the panel says, after full briefing and oral argument, that it is **vacating** the prior stay order and staying the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety except as specified.” The panel’s current view is that:

1. the government is **likely to prevail** on the challenge to the refugee-admissions suspension under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), citing *Trump v. Hawaii*; and
2. the only relief it is not staying is the part tied to **reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States**, because the panel says the government is **not likely to prevail** on that APA issue under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

So the earlier carveout disappeared because the panel **reassessed the stay factors on the fuller record** and decided not to keep that exception.

As for law of the case: a prior **stay ruling on likelihood of success is not the same as a final merits holding**. It may have some persuasive force, but it is generally **provisional** and can be revisited when the court later hears the case on full briefing and argument. That is exactly what happened here: the panel expressly **vacated** the earlier stay orders and replaced them with a new one.

In short: the earlier carveout was a temporary procedural accommodation, not a vested right, and the later panel was free to revise it in light of the full appeal.

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 impose a mandatory duty to provide refugee resettlement services, or merely authorize the Executive to do so?
Question:
The majority and Judge Lee seem to read § 1522 in very different ways. The panel points to language like "shall" and specific subsections requiring the government to provide certain services, especially for unaccompanied minors and recent refugees. Judge Lee says the section's title and the repeated use of "authorized" in subsections (b)-(e) show that the statute is basically permissive, with subsection (a) just supplying conditions if the Executive chooses to fund programs. How would a court ordinarily resolve this kind of textual conflict between 'authorized' and 'shall'? Is the majority reading § 1522 as a mandatory spending statute, or only as a mandatory program-administration statute once appropriated funds exist?
Answer:
**Short answer:** In this order, the **majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties on the Executive to provide specified refugee resettlement services, but only within the limits of available appropriations**. It is **not** reading the statute as a freestanding command that Congress must appropriate money. So the better description is **mandatory program administration once funding exists**, not a mandatory spending statute in the sense of requiring new appropriations.

### How the panel is reading the statute

The majority focuses on the operative text, especially:

- **§ 1522(a)(1)(A)**: the Director must act **“to the extent of available appropriations”** and is told that, in providing assistance, he **“shall”** make resources available, provide English training opportunities, ensure cash assistance is made available in a non-discouraging way, and ensure equal opportunities for women.
- **§ 1522(d)(2)(B)**: during the interim period for an unaccompanied refugee child, the Director **“shall assume legal responsibility”** if necessary.
- **§ 1522(e)(7)(A)**: the Secretary **“shall develop and implement”** alternative projects for refugees under 36 months in the U.S.

On that reading, Congress did more than merely authorize a program; it **directed that certain services be provided**. The order then says the government is “not likely to prevail” because the statute makes these services **“statutorily mandated”** and directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements needed to provide them.

### How Judge Lee reads it

Judge Lee reads the same statute as **permissive overall**:

- The title says **“Authorization for programs...”**
- Subsections **(b)–(e)** repeatedly say the Director/Secretary is **“authorized”** to provide assistance.
- Section **(a)**, in her view, supplies only **conditions and considerations** if the Executive chooses to operate those authorized programs.

So, for Judge Lee, the statute authorizes refugee resettlement services but does not require the Executive to keep the programs running.

### How courts ordinarily resolve “authorized” vs. “shall”

Ordinarily, a court tries to **harmonize** the provisions rather than let one word erase the other:

1. **“Shall” is ordinarily mandatory** unless context shows otherwise.
2. **“Authorized” is ordinarily permissive**, but it does not necessarily negate other mandatory language in the same statute.
3. **A title cannot override operative text**; it can help resolve ambiguity, but it is not controlling.
4. If there is tension, courts usually read the statute so that **the authorization to create a program and the duty to administer it in a certain way can coexist**.

That is essentially what the majority appears to be doing here: reading **“authorized”** in subsections **(b)–(e)** as granting program authority, while reading **“shall”** in **(a), (d), and (e)** as imposing binding obligations on how the authorized program must operate.

### Bottom line

So, based on this opinion:

- **Not** a pure mandatory spending statute.
- **Yes** to a mandatory duty to provide the specified resettlement services **once appropriations are available**.
- Judge Lee would call the whole scheme authorization-only, but the panel majority is treating at least some of § 1522 as **mandatory program requirements** for admitted refugees, limited by the phrase **“to the extent of available appropriations.”**

Since this is still a **stay order pending a later merits opinion**, it reflects the panel’s interim reading rather than a final merits holding—but that is the reading the majority is currently applying.

### What does 'to the extent of available appropriations' actually mean for enforceability under the APA?
Question:
The panel notes that Congress appropriated billions for migration and refugee assistance, though some amount was later rescinded. It then relies on § 1522's requirement that services be provided "to the extent of available appropriations." Does that phrase mean courts can review whether the agency is unlawfully withholding action once Congress has appropriated enough money, or does it still leave the Executive broad discretion about whether and how to spend those funds? I'm trying to understand whether this is really an APA 'unlawfully withheld' issue, an appropriations issue, or both.
Answer:
In the panel’s reading, **“to the extent of available appropriations” is a limit on the duty, not a grant of unfettered discretion**.

Here’s the basic idea:

- **Section 1522 supplies the substantive obligation.** The order says the government “must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States” and quotes § 1522(a)(1)(A), which uses “shall” and then qualifies that duty with “to the extent of available appropriations.”
- **That phrase means the obligation exists only so far as Congress has actually made money available.** So the Executive cannot be ordered to spend money Congress has not appropriated or has withdrawn by rescission.
- **But once appropriations exist, the agency cannot simply refuse to do what the statute requires.** That is why the panel says the government is “not likely to prevail” on the APA claim and directs reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide” the § 1522 services.

So this is **both** an appropriations issue and an APA issue, but they do different work:

- **Appropriations law** defines the outer boundary: no spending beyond available appropriations.
- **The APA** supplies the enforcement mechanism if the agency is allegedly **withholding action Congress required** within that appropriated amount.

That said, the order still leaves room for some **implementation discretion**—for example, how the services are administered and through which contracts or agreements—because § 1522 expressly allows the use of “contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements.” What the panel is not treating as discretionary is the decision to suspend the services wholesale while appropriations remain available.

Judge Lee’s separate writing takes the opposite view, reading § 1522 as authorization rather than a mandate. But the panel’s order itself points the other way: **the phrase limits the duty; it does not erase it.**

### Can a court order the government to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements with specific nonprofits, rather than just requiring statutory compliance?
Question:
The panel directs the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary" to provide the required services, including the usual services under the prior agreements. That seems like a very specific remedial order. Under the APA, I thought courts usually set aside agency action or compel action required by law, but not necessarily force the government to contract with particular private parties. What is the source of authority for this kind of remedy? Could the government comply by creating a different mechanism for delivering the services, or does the order really lock it into the old cooperative-agreement model?
Answer:
The short answer is: the source of authority is the court’s equitable remedial power in an APA case, exercised to enforce a statutory duty — not a free-standing power to “make” the government contract with these nonprofits forever.

The panel grounds the remedy in **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which it reads as imposing a duty to provide reception and placement services for admitted refugees. It also notes that Congress expressly allows those services to be delivered through **“contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements”** with nonprofit agencies. So when the court says the government must “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary” to provide those services, it is not inventing a new procurement regime; it is ordering the agency to use one of the statutory mechanisms Congress already authorized in order to avoid continued noncompliance.

That is why the panel frames the order this way:

> “In light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 …”

So the remedy is **service-focused, not nonprofit-focused**. The nonprofits are not being given a standalone entitlement to contracts; rather, the court is requiring the government to restore the agreements **to the extent necessary** to ensure the statutorily required services continue.

Could the government comply by using a different mechanism? **In theory, perhaps — but only if that alternative actually provides the § 1522 services.** The order does not say the old cooperative-agreement model is the only lawful model in all circumstances. But as written, it does require reinstatement of the agreements that are necessary right now, and it specifically says those services must include the “usual and customary services” previously provided. So the government could not simply replace the agreements with some different arrangement that fails to restore equivalent services.

In other words, the court is not ordering a particular contract for its own sake; it is ordering the agency to take the steps needed to comply with the statute, and reinstating the terminated cooperative agreements is the mechanism it chose because the government had not shown another viable way to do that.
