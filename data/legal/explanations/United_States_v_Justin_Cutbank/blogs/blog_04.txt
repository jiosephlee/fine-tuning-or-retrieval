Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

# Harmless Error as Endgame: Why Evidentiary Appeals Keep Failing in the Eighth Circuit

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is the latest iteration of a familiar story in the Eighth Circuit: a defendant mounts a multi-front challenge to trial rulings—evidentiary decisions, confrontation and hearsay questions, limits on impeachment material—and the court affirms the conviction not because the rulings were unimpeachable, but because any error was harmless in light of the “substantial” body of testimonial and physical proof. The panel’s opinion, drawing on DeMarce and Keys, reiterates the circuit’s high bar for reversal based on evidentiary error. For defense counsel, the practical takeaway is blunt: absent a preserved, compelling record showing a realistic risk that the jury was “substantially swayed,” appellate review is unlikely to undo a conviction supported by DNA, eyewitness testimony, and other corroboration.

Cutbank’s case is illustrative because it presents the typical mix that courts find hard to overturn: contested witness testimony about prior observation of the weapon, police statements and reports, disputed impeachment avenues, and emotionally charged collateral material (the victim’s sister’s death by gun). Yet the physical evidence—DNA on the rifle, the victim’s phone along the route Cutbank likely took, and corroborating witness identifications—anchored the verdict. The panel’s analysis shows how the harmless-error inquiry operates as an endgame—the last, often decisive step in preserving convictions following arguably contestable trial rulings.

## The panel’s reasoning — a close reading

The opinion resolutely proceeds in two parts: first, a rapid dismissal of several evidentiary challenges by characterizing the contested testimony as intrinsic or properly admissible; second, a conventional harmless-error analysis accepting that, even if any marginal mistakes occurred, they could not have meaningfully affected the jury’s verdict.

On admissibility, the court leans on long-standing principles. Prior sightings of the same sawed-off Marlin rifle were treated as intrinsic evidence—tending to show possession, control, or continuity of possession—which the court tied to Battle. The court likewise treated police recounting of the victim’s statements as essentially non-prejudicial where the declarant (D.F.) later testified in person, thereby satisfying Confrontation Clause concerns (Charboneau). Cross-examination and impeachment disputes—such as limits on reading segments of an investigator’s report into the record—were resolved under authentication and foundational rules (Cumbie), rather than as constitutional or per se reversible errors.

When it moves to harmlessness, the opinion relies on DeMarce and Keys for the pivotal formulation: reversal requires a showing that the jury "may have been substantially swayed" by the alleged error. The panel applied that standard with deference to the district court’s factual findings and the PSR’s adopted statements at sentencing. The cumulative-error doctrine received a brief, familiar dispatch: only substantial prejudice arising from multiple errors would justify reversal, and here the physical and testimonial record was too robust for such a showing.

## What the opinion shows about the “substantially swayed” standard in practice

Cutbank demonstrates three features of the Eighth Circuit’s harmless-error approach that trial lawyers must internalize.

First, the standard is outcome-sensitive and fact-intensive. Courts repeatedly point to DNA on the rifle, cell phone found on the likely route, victim and housemate identifications, and the defendant’s resistance and biting of an officer. When physical evidence and corroborating testimony are present, the appellate court is predisposed to treat most evidentiary disputes as unlikely to have moved the jury’s view.

Second, the opinion emphasizes preservation and evidentiary foundation at trial. The court enforced authentication requirements for impeachment with extrinsic documents and stressed that the district court had made pretrial rulings treating certain testimony as intrinsic evidence. In practice, this means appellate courts will scrutinize whether defense counsel (1) lodged precise contemporaneous objections; (2) tried to create a record by proffering excluded evidence or asking for limiting instructions; and (3) took available steps to authenticate material that the defense intended to use for impeachment. Absent those efforts, the appellate record is thin and harmless-error review will be correspondingly deferential.

Third, cumulative-error reversal is rare unless errors interact to create a realistic, aggregate likelihood of prejudice. The panel refused to aggregate the alleged mistakes into something that could have “substantially sway[ed]” the jury. Courts will not assume that separate, modest errors suddenly become prejudicial in the face of a strong evidentiary tableau.

## Doctrinal implications and the downstream effect on trial strategy

Doctrinally, Cutbank reinforces two settled but consequential rules. One is that evidence of prior possession or knowledge about an instrumentality (here, the rifle) is often intrinsic and therefore admissible under Rule 404(b) concerns, at least where it tends directly to show control or continuity. The second is that appellate harmless-error analysis will be heavily informed by the existence of independent physical proof—DNA, recovered items, forensic linkage—which functions as a kind of appellate insurance.

The practical downstream effects for defense attorneys are significant. If you are defending a client in a case with strong physical corroboration, the realistic goal at trial shifts from hoping for a clean, unassailable record for appellate reversal to being aggressive about preservation and record-building:

- Make contemporaneous, specific objections and obtain rulings on the record. Vague or belated objections are easy to fold into harmlessness rationales.
- When the court excludes or limits impeachment materials (police reports, notes), move to proffer the excluded content outside the jury’s presence and obtain a ruling that precisely identifies the basis for exclusion. That makes clear whether the exclusion was foundational (authentication) or substantive (hearsay), and it gives an appellate court something concrete to evaluate.
- Seek limiting instructions promptly when evidence carries potential for prejudice, and, if the court refuses, ask for a running record indicating how the evidence was offered and for what purpose.
- For incidents involving emotionally fraught collateral facts (e.g., a relative being killed by a gun), be mindful that spontaneous, responsive testimony elicited on cross-examination is frequently allowed; if you want exclusion, get a pretrial ruling or a motion in limine with full briefing.
- If a forensic or DNA link exists, anticipate that appellate harmless-error review will be skeptical; allocate trial resources accordingly (e.g., focus on undermining the reliability or chain of custody of forensics rather than litigating ancillary evidentiary minutiae that the court may deem non-prejudicial).

## An analytical perspective: is this healthy for appellate review?

From an institutional perspective, the Eighth Circuit’s approach is defensible. Harmless-error review exists to avoid undermining verdicts where the totality of evidence establishes guilt beyond other reasonable dispute. The appellate court’s role is not to retry the facts and overturn convictions merely because defendants can point to one or two debatable trial rulings. Cutbank follows that logic.

But there is a tension. When appellate courts dispose of claims primarily on harmlessness rather than on the correctness of legal rulings, two problems arise. First, the law’s precedential value is blunted. A ruling that “any error was harmless” does not develop fine-grained standards about how trial courts should handle close evidentiary questions. Second, the standard can, at times, shift the burden toward defendants to produce a counterfactual showing of how the jury might have been persuaded otherwise—a hard task when the trial record is silent because objections were not fully pressed or proffers not made.

Cutbank therefore does double duty: it reminds trial counsel that diligent preservation matters and it signals to district courts that evidentiary discretion will be upheld if the verdict is tethered to corroborative physical or eyewitness evidence. For appellate courts, the case is an invitation to remain pragmatic. But for those concerned about consistency and doctrinal development, there is a countervailing need for appellate opinions that squarely address and, where appropriate, correct troubling trial law errors rather than dismissing them as harmless in cases with strong factual records.

## Conclusion — what defense counsel should take away

Cutbank is not novel. It reiterates established Eighth Circuit lines: prior possession can be intrinsic; an in-court recantation or later testimony cures Confrontation Clause problems; extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated; and absent clear prejudice, evidentiary rulings rarely require reversal when corroborative proof exists. The guiding lesson is procedural and pragmatic: to preserve appellate avenues, defense counsel must build a meticulous trial record—timely, specific objections; detailed proffers of excluded evidence; requests for limiting instructions; and explicit preservation of cumulative-error arguments.

If your client’s case rests on contesting forensic linkage or other central proofs, litigate those core issues vigorously at trial. If, instead, you are forced to wage a war on peripheral evidentiary skirmishes, recognize that appellate courts will likely classify many such skirmishes as harmless in the face of DNA, eyewitnesses, and a coherent narrative. In short: at the Eighth Circuit, harmless error is often the endgame—defense counsel should plan accordingly.



## Stacking Enhancements on Top of ACCA: The Reach of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) After Kisor

Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank is an important, if understated, contribution to two recurring sentencing fights: (1) how readily a district court may apply a four‑level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession “in connection with another felony,” even when the defendant is also an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) defendant; and (2) the inference courts will draw from unobjected‑to facts in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) — and from commentary to the Guidelines — when calculating the advisory range. The opinion affirms a within‑range 292‑month sentence reached by layering § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) on top of the ACCA result and other firearm adjustments, relying in significant part on the PSR and the Guidelines’ commentary note 14(C). That posture crystallizes several doctrinal tensions: potential double‑counting of conduct across enhancements, the force of Guidelines commentary in the post‑Kisor era, and the practical necessity of preserving “in connection with” objections for further review.

What the court held and why

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and found to be an armed career criminal under § 924(e). The PSR recommended several enhancements under USSG § 2K2.1, including a four‑level enhancement under subsection (b)(6)(B) because the firearm was “possessed in connection with another felony offense.” The district court adopted the PSR’s factual recitation without objection. On appeal Cutbank argued that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement in addition to ACCA (and the other enhancements) produced an excessive sentence and that the enhancement should not be applied in these circumstances.

The Eighth Circuit rejected those contentions. Two features of the court’s analysis are notable. First, the court emphasized that the district court adopted the PSR’s statements of fact and that Cutbank failed to preserve a meaningful objection to the PSR findings at sentencing. Where factual findings are adopted without objection, the appellate standard is highly deferential and reversal requires clear error. Second, the court pointed to the Guidelines commentary — specifically the note that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” — as supporting the enhancement’s applicability even when other sentencing regimes (like ACCA) are also in play. Finally, the court framed the issue as largely one of procedural preservation and sentencing discretion under § 3553(a): the district court calculated the Guidelines range and said it considered the § 3553(a) factors, and a within‑range sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal.

How the court’s reasoning fits into the larger doctrinal landscape

Two doctrinal axes intersect here.

First, the double‑counting or “overlap” concern. Defendants have long argued that applying multiple enhancements or separate sentencing regimes based on the same underlying conduct—stacking a Guidelines enhancement that punishes possession in connection with another felony on top of ACCA’s severe penalty that rests on prior violent felonies—can amount to impermissible double punishment or produce an unreasonable sentence. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank treats this primarily as a discretionary problem for the district court to weigh within the § 3553(a) calculus (and as one a defendant must preserve). The court does not treat the potential overlap as a per se legal bar to applying both regimes, especially when the Guidelines commentary and the PSR support the factual predicate for the enhancement.

Second, the interpretive force of Guidelines commentary after Kisor v. Wilkie. For decades, courts often treated commentary to the United States Sentencing Guidelines as highly persuasive (or even controlling) when reasonably construed. Kisor limited the deference owed to administrative interpretations in the Auer context, insisting on careful steps before any deference is granted and pushing courts to apply traditional tools of statutory (or text) construction first. While Kisor addressed agency interpretive deference in the administrative law arena, its doctrinal ripples affect how courts approach the Sentencing Commission’s commentary: courts are less likely to treat commentary as commanding unless the commentary is a reasonable construction of an ambiguous Guideline and the usual Kisor predicates are satisfied.

Cutbank, however, does not wade into a full Kisor‑style interpretive battle. The panel leans on the PSR and the plain language of the commentary’s note (n.14(C)) to justify the enhancement — and the defendant’s failure to object at sentencing meant the court did not need to decide hard questions about the permissible scope of the commentary under Kisor. That posture makes the decision an important example of how procedural default and adoption of PSR facts can blunt or avoid broader interpretive fights.

Analytical perspective: strengths, weaknesses, and preservation strategy

The Eighth Circuit’s result is defensible on conventional sentencing and procedural grounds. If a defendant does not contest the PSR findings and the district court properly calculates the Guidelines range, appellate courts are rightly reluctant to upset within‑range sentences. The court also correctly notes that § 2K2.1’s commentary expressly contemplates application of the enhancement even without a companion conviction for the “other felony,” which forecloses granular arguments that the government must have separately charged or proved contemporaneous other‑felony convictions beyond a preponderance of the evidence.

But the decision leaves open important questions that litigants — and higher courts — may want to resolve. First, there is a real doctrinal tension between ACCA and Guidelines enhancements tailored to conduct that also constitutes the predicate for ACCA: when does layering a Guidelines enhancement meaningfully double count? The Eighth Circuit treats this as a matter for the sentencing court to consider in exercising discretion under § 3553(a), but a persuasive legal argument can be mounted that some enhancements should be categorically inapplicable when they directly replicate conduct for which a statutory enhanced penalty has already been imposed. Absent such a rule, district courts must be vigilant in explaining how multiple regimes serve distinct deterrent or incapacitative goals and are not merely cumulative punishment for the same conduct.

Second, plaintiffs who want to challenge the interpretive force of commentary like note 14(C) post‑Kisor must preserve that fight at sentencing. Because Kisor calls for a textured analysis before affording deference to an agency’s (or Commission’s) interpretation, appellate review will be easier if the district court has a record on whether the Guideline text is genuinely ambiguous, whether the commentary is a reasonable construction of that text, and whether any special circumstances weigh against deference. Cutbank’s failure to make those arguments below effectively insulated the enhancement from appellate scrutiny.

Circuit splits and the road ahead

Circuits differ in their willingness to apply Guidelines commentary to support enhancements and in their treatment of “in connection with” enhancements. Some courts focus on the objective causal relationship between the firearm and the other felony conduct (looking for a temporal or functional nexus), while others accept broader linkages. Similarly, courts have varied in how much weight to give PSR statements adopted without objection. The practical upshot is predictable: a defendant seeking to overturn a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement should (1) object at sentencing to the PSR’s factual recitations and demand live proof of the “in connection with” predicate, (2) press the district court to articulate reasons why applying the enhancement does not improperly double count conduct punished by ACCA (or other statutes), and (3) preserve a Kisor‑style challenge to the Guidelines commentary by asking the court to analyze textual ambiguity and the reasonableness (or not) of the Commission’s construction.

For appellate advocates eyeing en banc review or certiorari, that preserved record is essential. A fully developed record would allow a court to confront squarely whether the Sentencing Commission’s commentary — when it says the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought” — should carry dispositive interpretive weight in cases where other, overlapping sentencing regimes are in play. It would also let higher courts clarify whether application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in ACCA cases presents a structural problem of double counting, or merely a discretionary judgment that district courts must address under § 3553(a).

Downstream effects

The practical lesson from Cutbank is immediate and procedural: defendants facing possible § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) exposure must litigate the factual and legal predicates at sentencing, not save them for appeal. More substantively, unless and until a circuit or the Supreme Court embraces a categorical limit on stacking Guidelines enhancements with ACCA, districts will continue to apply both regimes, provided they make at least a minimal record showing they considered § 3553(a) factors and that the PSR or evidence supports the enhancement. That reality increases the importance of rigorous PSR challenges and of obtaining explicit district‑court findings on how multiple enhancements operate without producing impermissible double punishment.

Conclusion

Cutbank underscores a simple but powerful dynamic in modern federal sentencing: procedural choices at sentencing — adoption of the PSR, failure to object to Guidelines treatment, and the absence of a Kisor‑style interpretive record — often determine the outcome as much as the substantive merits of an enhancement claim. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion does not resolve the harder doctrinal questions about double counting and the interpretive status of Guidelines commentary; it merely shows how those questions can be avoided when defendants do not preserve them. For practitioners, the takeaway is plain: litigate these issues early, make the court decide them expressly, and build a record that will allow an appellate court (or the Supreme Court) to confront the larger tensions left open by decisions like Cutbank.
