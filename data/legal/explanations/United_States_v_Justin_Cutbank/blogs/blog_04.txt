Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

# ACCA After Erlinger: Jury Findings and the Eighth Circuit’s Streamlined Review in United States v. Cutbank

## Why Cutbank matters

United States v. Cutbank — an October 7, 2025 decision by an Eighth Circuit panel — is not a flashy doctrinal pivot, but it is an instructive snapshot of how lower courts are managing the interplay between jury factfinding for ACCA enhancements and judge-driven guideline calculations in the wake of recent developments (what practitioners here are calling “post‑Erlinger” practice). The case shows two linked realities: first, trial juries increasingly are being asked to resolve the ACCA-triggering questions that matter to statutory punishment (here, the jury found Cutbank had three or more prior violent felonies, which brought the ACCA into play); and second, once a sentencing court properly calculates the Guidelines range — often relying on an adopted Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) — appellate courts will give substantial deference, making it difficult to overturn guideline-driven within-range sentences.

Cutbank therefore highlights the tactical fault lines defense counsel must guard: which ACCA facts to put to the jury, what to preserve (and how) at sentencing for objections to PSR narratives, and how appellate review will treat sentencing challenges when the district court appears to have followed the mechanics the courts demand.

## What the Eighth Circuit did

The facts underlying Cutbank’s conviction are stark: a violent domestic incident, flight and resistance to police, recovery of a sawed-off Marlin .22 with Cutbank’s DNA on it, and eyewitness identification placing the weapon with him before the night in question. The jury convicted him of being a felon in possession under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), and the jury specifically found he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering ACCA enhancements.

On appeal Cutbank raised a mélange of evidentiary objections and attacked a four‑level guidelines enhancement under USSG §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (possession “in connection with another felony offense”). The Eighth Circuit rejected the evidentiary claims as either unsupported or harmless in light of the strong record. As to sentencing, the court emphasized two linked principles. First, the record shows the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements “without objection,” and those adopted facts were sufficient to support the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. Second, because the district court correctly calculated the applicable Guidelines and imposed a within-range sentence while stating it considered the §3553(a) factors, appellate courts will ordinarily presume the sentence reasonable and grant only deferential review.

The opinion reiterates the settled procedural point from Gall and Rosales‑Mireles: a correct Guidelines calculation is a prerequisite to substantive reasonableness review, and the Eighth Circuit will not demand a “mechanical recitation” of §3553(a) factors so long as the record shows they were considered.

## Where Erlinger and Cutbank intersect — and where questions remain

The opinion itself does not redraw ACCA doctrine, but it reflects a practical posture increasingly visible after Erlinger (and the subsequent district and circuit practice that followed): when ACCA is at issue, courts often submit the predicate‑conviction questions — particularly the “different occasions” or multiplicity questions that determine whether prior convictions count separately — to juries. Cutbank’s posture, with the jury having found three or more prior violent felonies, fits that trajectory. That matters because, where jury findings are made, the government has satisfied the Apprendi/Blakely/Alleyne line of concerns about judicial factfinding increasing statutory penalties.

But Cutbank also underscores an open set of questions that practitioners should watch. Erlinger (and the post‑Erlinger practice noted by courts) has not settled every boundary of what must be found by a jury versus what a judge may decide at sentencing. Is the jury required to resolve the character of each prior conviction (for example, categorical versus conduct‑based inquiries), or may some predicate‑related factual nuances be resolved by the judge so long as the jury determines the ultimate ACCA threshold? Lower courts continue to differ on the contours of that division, and Cutbank shows appellate courts will be disposed to accept a district court’s handling of those questions when the procedural boxes are checked — jury findings where parties press for them, and an adopted PSR to supply the factual substrate otherwise.

## PSRs, §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and the practical import of unobjected facts

Cutbank’s unsuccessful attack on the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is a useful cautionary tale about PSRs. The guideline commentary expressly allows the enhancement “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The PSR in Cutbank’s case recommended multiple enhancements and was adopted by the district court without objection. On appeal, that adoption supplied the factual basis for the four‑level enhancement; the Eighth Circuit applied ordinary (and deferential) review and found no clear error.

Two practical lessons flow from that posture. First, defense counsel must actively contest PSR statements they dispute — timely, specific objections preserve issues for later review. Second, when the government seeks enhancements tied to “connection with another felony,” it will often rely on PSR narratives and non‑conviction conduct; judges commonly accept those narratives absent timely, authenticated dispute. Cutbank reiterates the point that failure to contest PSR facts is frequently fatal to appellate challenges.

The opinion also touches on authentication and impeachment rules in trial evidence disputes (for example, the court permitted defense cross-examination but required foundation for reading portions of a police report into the record). These evidentiary skirmishes matter because the same factual threads that support trial convictions often feed PSR narratives and sentencing enhancements; a robust, preserved record at trial improves the defendant’s posture at sentencing and on appeal.

## Appellate posture: constrained review when the Guidelines are properly calculated

Cutbank demonstrates the Eighth Circuit’s streamlined approach on appeal to sentences within correctly calculated guideline ranges. The court treats a within-Guidelines sentence as presumptively reasonable and will not upset the district court absent clear procedural error or substantive unreasonableness demonstrated in the record. Importantly, the circuit requires only that the record show the court considered §3553(a) factors; it does not demand an itemized or formulaic recitation.

For defense counsel, that means two things: pressing for a lower Guidelines calculation (e.g., by litigating enhancements and base‑level components) is often the most effective route to a more favorable sentence, and preserving objections to PSR facts and to the admission of the underlying evidence becomes doubly important because appellate courts will otherwise decline to second‑guess a within‑range sentence grounded in an adopted PSR.

## Practice pointers and unresolved issues for practitioners

Cutbank’s practical lessons are straightforward. At trial and in the jury charge phase, defense counsel should insist on clear jury findings on any fact that could elevate statutory exposure under ACCA — and, when appropriate, press for specific interrogatories so the record plainly shows what the jury decided. At sentencing, scrutinize the PSR line by line: lodge timely, specific objections, request evidentiary hearings when facts are disputed, and ensure the district court does not adopt contested assertions without addressing objections. Where a guideline enhancement rests on conduct for which there was no separate charge or conviction, demand the government produce admissible, authenticated proof sufficient for the judge to find the necessary facts; do not rely on the PSR as a fallback.

On the doctrinal front, Cutbank highlights that we remain in a transitional posture: courts are accommodating jury resolution of ACCA-triggering issues in many cases, but the exact demarcation between what juries must decide and what judges may find for guideline purposes is still being worked out in lower courts. That uncertainty will likely drive continued litigation over jury interrogatories, the admissibility and sufficiency of PSR materials, and the interplay between Apprendi‑line jury requirements and guideline factfinding.

## Bottom line

Cutbank is not a doctrinal watershed, but it is a practical road map. When a jury finds the ACCA threshold, and a sentencing court adopts a PSR without objection, appellate courts will routinely affirm tough, within‑range sentences. For defense lawyers that means litigating FACTS early and often — in pleadings, at trial, in jury instructions, and at the PSR stage — because once the PSR is adopted and the Guidelines are correctly computed, the path to relief on appeal becomes narrow.
