Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Incident overview (Nov. 19–20, 2020)
The events giving rise to this prosecution occurred overnight on November 19–20, 2020. According to the government’s proof at trial, Justin Cutbank—after drinking and using methamphetamine—awoke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a gun at her face, forced her into a closet, and made paranoid accusations about “listening devices” and people outside waiting to harm him. D.F. testified Cutbank kept her in the closet at gunpoint for what felt like hours, threatened to kill her, struck her across the face with the gun, and then ordered her to wake others in the house (including her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F.). Cutbank then fled the home with D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. called the police.

## Police response at nearby garage
Separately, police received a report from a house about a man refusing to leave a garage about a half mile from D.F.’s residence. When officers arrived, Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, made erratic statements, and told officers he had a weapon. During hours of contact, Cutbank resisted attempts to detain him, bit an officer in the face and neck, and grabbed at an officer’s gun. Those events prompted a search of the route Cutbank may have taken between D.F.’s home and the garage.

## Recovered evidence along route
Officers searching the likely path recovered several items: D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA testing matched Cutbank to the rifle. Those physical items linked the garage encounter to the assault at D.F.’s home and provided key forensic evidence of Cutbank’s connection to the weapon.

## Witness identification
At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the same sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle as a weapon they had observed Cutbank possess on prior occasions. D.F. testified she had seen the rifle a “handful of times” before the night in question; C.F. testified she had seen the gun used “once or twice” before. Those witness identifications were central to the government’s theory that Cutbank had possessed and exercised control over the firearm before and during the charged incident.

## Trial results
A jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The jury also found that Cutbank had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies, a factual finding that triggers the enhanced penalty regime of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) (the Armed Career Criminal Act, or ACCA). Multiple evidentiary rulings at trial—about prior possession testimony, hearsay and limiting instructions, impeachment materials, and related matters—were later raised on appeal.

## Sentencing: Guidelines calculations and sentence imposed
The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) computed the applicable advisory Guidelines range as follows. The PSR used a base offense level of 26 based on the offense involving a sawed-off firearm (as described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)), added a three-level increase for an altered/obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police, and a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm “in connection with” another felony offense. Those adjustments produced a total offense level of 35. Combined with a Criminal History Category VI, the PSR’s calculation yielded an advisory Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection and imposed a within-Guidelines sentence of 292 months.

## Issues preserved for appeal
On appeal to the Eighth Circuit, Cutbank challenged several trial rulings as evidentiary and procedural errors that he said deprived him of a fair trial—claims that the brief presented with minimal legal citation or analysis. He also challenged the district court’s application of the four-level USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (possession “in connection with” another felony) and argued that the resulting within-Guidelines sentence conflicted with the sentencing statute’s reasonableness requirement under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit reviewed those contentions, considered the preserved record (including the district court’s adoption of the PSR), and affirmed the conviction and sentence.

# Evidentiary Framework and Standards of Review

This chapter explains the principal evidentiary doctrines and appellate standards the Eighth Circuit relied on when evaluating Justin Cutbank’s challenges to the district court’s trial rulings. Each rule is stated briefly, then tied to how the court applied it in this case so you can see why the court affirmed.

## Standard of review: abuse of discretion for evidentiary rulings and limiting instructions

Most evidentiary rulings and decisions about jury instructions are reviewed for abuse of discretion on appeal. That standard means the appellate court gives the district court deference and will reverse only if the district court made a clear error of judgment or applied the wrong legal standard. In Cutbank the Eighth Circuit repeatedly invoked that standard when reviewing the district court’s rulings on (1) whether testimony constituted Rule 404(b) propensity evidence or intrinsic evidence, (2) whether a limiting instruction was required and when, and (3) whether the court properly curtailed the reading of portions of a police report for impeachment. Because trial courts are in the best position to assess evidentiary issues, the appellate court declined to substitute its judgment unless the district court abused its broad discretion.

## Intrinsic evidence vs. Rule 404(b) propensity evidence: prior possession of the same firearm

Rule 404(b) restricts evidence of other acts offered to prove a defendant’s character to show propensity to commit the charged offense. But it does not bar “intrinsic” evidence—other acts that are part of the same transaction, background, or necessary to complete the story of the crime. The Eighth Circuit has held that prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic because it helps prove later possession by establishing ownership or control (United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014)). 

Applied here: the district court admitted testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off Marlin rifle before the night of the offenses. The court and the panel treated that prior-observation testimony as intrinsic evidence—probative of continuous possession and control of the very weapon found near the scene—so Rule 404(b) did not bar it. Battle was the controlling authority the court cited for that proposition.

## Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause: declarant testimony cures the Clause problem

Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered for its truth and is generally inadmissible unless an exception applies. The Confrontation Clause, however, presents a separate constitutional limit on testimonial hearsay: testimonial statements by an unavailable declarant are inadmissible at a criminal trial unless the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine that declarant. When the declarant actually testifies at trial, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because the defendant can cross-examine the declarant (United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)).

Applied here: Cutbank had asked for a limiting instruction to prevent jurors from treating D.F.’s pretrial statements to police as substantive proof until she testified. The court found it unnecessary to exclude hearsay, and D.F. did testify at trial, so any Confrontation Clause concern was resolved—her courtroom testimony meant the jury could consider her statements and the government’s use of them did not run afoul of Charboneau.

## Limiting instructions: no need for contemporaneous instruction if a proper final instruction is given

A district court does not commit reversible error simply because it declines to give a limiting instruction at the precise moment a piece of evidence is admitted. The court may give an appropriate instruction at the close of trial (or otherwise) and is not required to instruct the jury about each evidentiary item as it comes in (United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014)). The question on appeal is whether the court abused its discretion in deciding when and whether to give a limiting instruction and whether the instruction it did give (if any) was sufficient.

Applied here: Cutbank sought an admonition to avoid hearsay/Confrontation problems before D.F.’s testimony. The district court declined to issue the requested contemporaneous instruction and instead relied on routine practices and the availability of a later bench instruction if needed. Because the testimony was not improperly admitted and D.F. ultimately testified, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion in the timing or omission of the requested limiting instruction.

## Rule 403 balancing: excluding evidence only when unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value

Rule 403 allows exclusion of relevant evidence only when its probative value is substantially outweighed by risks such as unfair prejudice, confusion, misleading the jury, or undue delay. Trial courts perform the balancing test and enjoy wide latitude in weighing probative value against prejudicial effect; appellate courts disturb that balance only for abuse of discretion.

Applied here: On cross-examination D.F. explained she disliked guns and mentioned that her sister had been killed by a gun. Cutbank moved to strike that testimony. The district court denied the motion, treating the statement as responsive to impeachment questioning about inconsistent statements. On appeal Cutbank argued prejudice, but pointed to no showing that the testimony’s probative value was substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice under Rule 403. The appellate court therefore upheld the district court’s discretionary balancing.

## Impeachment by prior inconsistent statements: extrinsic evidence must be authenticated

A party may impeach a witness with prior inconsistent statements, but extrinsic evidence (documents, reports, etc.) used for that impeachment must be properly authenticated and meet evidentiary requirements. The Eighth Circuit has reiterated that authentication is a prerequisite before extrinsic materials can be read into evidence for impeachment (United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)).

Applied here: Defense counsel sought to read portions of a police report to impeach C.F.’s testimony about prior observations of the gun. The court allowed questions about what C.F. had told police but sustained government objections to counsel reading parts of the police report without establishing its foundation because the report was not authored by C.F. The appellate court approved that ruling as within the district court’s discretion to require authentication before admitting extrinsic impeachment material, citing Cumbie.

## Harmless error and cumulative-error principles

Even if a court makes an erroneous evidentiary ruling, reversal is not automatic. Under the harmless-error doctrine the conviction will be reversed only if the error may have substantially swayed the jury (United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009)). And an appellate court will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless the aggregate errors produced substantial prejudice (United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019)).

Applied here: The panel found that, even assuming any of Cutbank’s evidentiary objections had merit, the government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence (D.F./C.F. identifications, DNA on the weapon, items recovered on the likely path). Because the record showed the district court’s rulings did not substantially influence the verdict, any asserted errors were harmless. The court also rejected a cumulative-prejudice argument because Cutbank failed to show substantial prejudice from the combined rulings.

Conclusion

Taken together, these doctrinal and standard-of-review principles explain why the Eighth Circuit gave the district court wide latitude in admitting testimony about prior observations of the weapon, in managing impeachment materials, and in handling limiting instructions, and why the appellate court treated any asserted errors as non‑reversible given the weight of the government’s case. Understanding these rules—intrinsic evidence v. 404(b), the Confrontation Clause posture where declarants testify, Rule 403 balancing, authentication requirements for extrinsic impeachment, and the abuse-of-discretion/harmless‑error framework—shows why the panel affirmed Cutbank’s conviction.

# Applying the Law to the Five Trial Rulings (A–E)

This chapter explains how the Eighth Circuit applied controlling evidentiary rules and standards of review to each of Cutbank’s five trial challenges. For each ruling (A–E) the court followed a familiar sequence: identify the governing legal principle (e.g., what counts as intrinsic evidence, when a limiting instruction is required, the scope of permissible impeachment), state the standard of review (usually abuse of discretion), apply the principle to the record facts, and decide whether any error was harmless. Understanding that sequence will help you read appellate opinions that review trial-court evidentiary decisions.

## A. Prior sightings of the sawed‑off rifle by D.F. and C.F.: intrinsic evidence, not Rule 404(b) propensity

The key legal question in A was whether testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 on prior occasions was forbidden propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) or admissible intrinsic evidence relevant to the charged possession. The court relied on the well‑established distinction between propensity evidence (extrinsic acts offered only to show a defendant’s character to prove action in conformity therewith) and intrinsic evidence (events that are part of the same transaction or are directly probative of an essential element, such as possession, identity, or motive).

The opinion cites United States v. Battle for the proposition that prior possession of a firearm is “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” In practical terms, if a witness has repeatedly seen the defendant with the same gun before the charged incident, those sightings tend to prove continuous control or ownership and therefore are not being offered simply to show a criminal propensity. The district court made that factual-legal determination at the pretrial stage, and the Eighth Circuit treated it as a discretionary evidentiary ruling. Because the testimony bore directly on an element of the offense (possession/control of the weapon) and fit within the intrinsic evidence doctrine, the appellate court concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the testimony.

When you read this section of the opinion, note the two-step move typical in 404(b) disputes: identify the proffered purpose for the prior act (here, to show continuous possession/control), and evaluate whether the prior act is actually intrinsic to the charged conduct rather than extrinsic propensity evidence. The appellate court accepted the district court’s judgment that the prior‑sighting testimony was intrinsic, which is a deferential outcome in this type of evidentiary dispute.

## B. Denial of a pretrial limiting instruction about D.F.’s statements to police: hearsay and Confrontation Clause considerations

Cutbank asked the court to instruct the jury that D.F.’s statements to police could not be used as substantive evidence until D.F. testified, aiming to avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems. The court applied two controlling ideas.

First, a trial judge need not repeatedly give limiting instructions at the precise moment evidence is introduced so long as an appropriate limiting instruction is provided at the close of trial; courts review refusals to give such piecemeal instructions for abuse of discretion (citing United States v. Bennett). Second, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant appears and testifies in person, so statements D.F. made to police lose their Confrontation‑Clause bite once she testified at trial (citing United States v. Charboneau).

Applying those principles, the Eighth Circuit observed that (1) the record did not show the district court admitted hearsay that required immediate limiting instructions, (2) D.F. later testified at trial, and (3) the Confrontation Clause problem therefore did not exist. The appellate court thus found no abuse of discretion in the district court’s handling of the requested pretrial instruction. Read this as an application of the principle that procedural protections (like limiting instructions or confrontation rights) must be considered in context: a pretrial request for prophylactic language is judged against what actually transpired at trial and whether the defendant was prejudiced by any admitted statements.

## C. Renewed objections to prior‑sighting testimony at trial: consistency with the pretrial ruling

At trial Cutbank renewed his objections to D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony that they had previously seen Cutbank with the rifle. The district court overruled these renewed objections consistent with its pretrial ruling that the evidence was intrinsic. On appeal, Cutbank offered no new legal authority to undermine that pretrial ruling.

The appellate court’s decision here is an example of deference to a district court’s evidentiary rulings when the trial court’s reasons are consistent and grounded in the record. Because the court had already decided that prior sightings were directly relevant to possession and continuous control (Battle), sustaining that ruling at trial did not constitute an abuse of discretion. When a defendant reasserts an argument already rejected pretrial, the appellate inquiry focuses on whether the trial record or the asserted legal framework shows that the trial court’s decision was unreasonable; here it did not.

## D. Refusal to strike D.F.’s statement that her sister was killed by a gun: explanation for inconsistent statements and Rule 403 balance

During cross‑examination about prior inconsistent statements, D.F. explained some inconsistencies by saying she was “not familiar with guns” and that she did not like guns because her sister had been killed by a gun. Cutbank moved to strike the latter comment. The district court denied the motion.

The court applied two connected evidentiary ideas. First, testimony that tends to explain why a witness made inconsistent prior statements is generally permissible impeachment‑rehabilitation material: it is responsive to cross‑examination and helps the jury evaluate credibility. Second, even if such testimony has some prejudicial tendency, Rule 403 requires excluding only when the probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. The appellate court saw no substantial risk of unfair prejudice here: the statement was a brief explanation offered in response to cross‑examination, and C.F. later made a similar comment without objection, reducing the likelihood that the jury was unfairly swayed by an isolated emotional statement.

Thus the Eighth Circuit concluded the district court did not abuse its broad discretion in admitting the explanation. For students, the lesson is how explanation-for-inconsistency functions: when a witness offers a reason for an earlier inconsistent statement, courts generally allow it unless the statement’s prejudicial effect greatly outweighs its probative value. The appellate court will defer to the trial judge’s resolution of that balancing unless the ruling is unreasonable.

## E. Limits on reading from a police report to impeach C.F.: authentication and permissible impeachment

Cutbank’s defense sought to impeach C.F. by reading portions of a police report into the record that memorialized things C.F. said to an investigator. The district court allowed the defense to question C.F. about what she told the officer, but sustained government objections to counsel’s attempt to read the officer‑authored report into evidence without foundation. On appeal the court treated the rule governing extrinsic impeachment (and document use) as central.

The governing principle is that extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach a witness’s prior inconsistent statement, but such extrinsic evidence must be authenticated and otherwise admissible. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Cumbie for the authentication requirement. A report authored by an officer is not automatically the witness’s own statement; to admit the report (or read it into evidence) the proponent must establish that it is authentic and permissible as a prior recorded recollection or a statement of the witness under an applicable hearsay exception—or else obtain the witness’s admission of the report’s contents. The district court’s approach—permitting direct cross‑examination about what C.F. told police while requiring a foundation before admitting the officer’s report—reflects ordinary evidentiary safeguards and was a valid exercise of discretion.

The opinion also notes that Cutbank did not develop this argument on appeal, and that the government’s brief tried to expand the issues; the panel declined to entertain any problem not properly focused by the appellant. This illustrates two practical points for litigators: (1) you must preserve arguments and develop them on appeal, and (2) appellate courts will not overturn reasonable trial‑court document‑admission rulings where proper authentication was lacking.

## Harmlessness and cumulative‑error resolution

Even assuming any of the district court’s evidentiary rulings were erroneous, the Eighth Circuit applied the harmless‑error framework and found no reversible error. The opinion invokes the standard that an appellate court will reverse only if the erroneously admitted evidence may have substantially swayed the jury (citing DeMarce). Here, the record contained substantial independent evidence tying Cutbank to the rifle and the charged conduct: DNA on the gun matched Cutbank, the phone linked to the victim was recovered along the path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself, eyewitness identifications placed the weapon with Cutbank, and Cutbank physically resisted arrest and injured an officer. Given that robust mix of physical and testimonial proof, the appellate court held any evidentiary error either had only slight influence or none at all on the verdict.

The court also rejected a cumulative‑error claim. Even when multiple errors are alleged, reversal is appropriate only if the errors cumulatively result in substantial prejudice. Because the individual rulings were proper or harmless and the government’s evidence was strong, the panel concluded there was no substantial cumulative prejudice.

Conclusion

Across rulings A–E the Eighth Circuit applied familiar, deferential standards: admission of intrinsic evidence and reasonable Rule 403 balances are reviewed for abuse of discretion; limiting‑instruction refusals are reviewed for abuse of discretion and judged in context of what actually happened at trial and whether the declarant testified; extrinsic impeachment requires authentication; and any errors must be shown to have substantially influenced the jury to warrant reversal. In each instance the court concluded the district court acted within its broad trial discretion and, in any event, any error was harmless in light of the strong direct and physical evidence. This sequence—identify the rule, apply deferential review, and assess prejudice—is the template to watch for when reading appellate treatment of trial evidentiary rulings.

# Sentencing Framework: ACCA, Guidelines, and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

This chapter explains how the Eighth Circuit resolved the sentencing questions in United States v. Justin Cutbank, focusing on the mechanics of the Sentencing Guidelines in the presence of an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) finding and the application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The court’s reasoning illustrates three linked tasks a district court must perform at sentencing: (1) make and (when contested) support the factual findings that underlie guideline adjustments, (2) select and compare the applicable guideline offense levels (including those generated by the ACCA guideline), and (3) consider the § 3553(a) factors before imposing a sentence. Appellate review is deferential when the district court follows those steps.

## How the ACCA finding interacts with the Guidelines (USSG § 4B1.4)

Cutbank’s jury found he had three or more prior violent-felony convictions, bringing 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) (the ACCA) into play. The Guidelines implement that statutory status through the Career Offender/Armed Career Criminal provisions. When an ACCA predicate is found, the Sentencing Guidelines provide a cross-reference rule: the court calculates the offense level that results from applying the ACCA-oriented guideline (USSG § 4B1.4) and compares that level to the offense level otherwise calculated under the ordinary guideline for the offense. If the ACCA-derived offense level is less than the ordinary calculation, the Guidelines direct the court to apply the greater offense level. In Cutbank the PSR’s ordinary calculation produced an offense level of 35, while the ACCA guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34; the Guidelines therefore required applying the higher level (35). The court relied on USSG § 4B1.4(b) and the PSR’s computations to reach that result.

## The PSR calculations in this case — how a base level becomes an advisory range

The PSR started with a base offense level of 26 under USSG § 2K2.1(a) because the firearm was a sawed-off rifle (26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) characterization). The PSR then added specific-offense-characteristic increases: +3 because the firearm’s serial number had been altered/obliterated, +2 for recklessly creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and +4 under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm “in connection with” another felony offense. These adjustments produced a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category VI (based on Cutbank’s extensive record), the Guidelines produced an advisory range of 292–365 months’ imprisonment. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection and imposed a sentence at the bottom of that range (292 months).

The mechanics illustrated here are straightforward: the PSR proposes a base level, applies applicable specific-offense-characteristic adjustments, totals the offense level, pairs that with the defendant’s criminal-history category, and derives the advisory Guidelines range. Any dispute about the facts the PSR relies on (for example, whether the firearm was used “in connection with” another felony) is typically resolved by the district court; on appeal factual findings are reviewed for clear error.

## § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and Application Note 14(C): what “in connection with” means for the four-level enhancement

Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) imposes a four-level enhancement when the defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense.” The Commentary to § 2K2.1 clarifies in Application Note 14(C) that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” In Cutbank the government relied on the PSR’s narrative (adopted by the district court) connecting Cutbank’s possession of the sawed-off rifle to violent conduct in D.F.’s home and to other felonious conduct that night. Because the PSR facts (adopted without objection) supported the application of the enhancement under the plain language of the guideline and its note, the court found no error in applying the +4 increase.

The opinion does not attempt to elaborate a standalone rule for every factual scenario where “in connection with” applies; instead it rests on the settled principle that the enhancement can be based on uncharged conduct reflected in the PSR, so long as the factual basis is adopted or otherwise supported at sentencing.

## Which offense level governs when ACCA and other guideline calculations differ

When guideline calculations yield two different offense levels — one produced by ordinary application of § 2K2.1 and another produced by the ACCA provision (USSG § 4B1.4) — the Guidelines direct the district court to use the greater offense level. That rule resolved Cutbank’s dispute: the non-ACCA calculation (level 35) exceeded the ACCA-derived level (34), so level 35 governed. Because the district court correctly compared and selected the higher level in accordance with USSG § 4B1.4(b), its Guidelines calculation was procedurally proper.

## Standards of review and procedural prerequisites for appellate consideration

Two overarching procedural principles guided the court’s review. First, a district court must begin sentencing by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range; only then may a reviewing court address substantive reasonableness. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Second, before reaching substantive-reasonableness review, an appellate court must ensure no significant procedural error occurred, such as an incorrect Guidelines calculation. See Rosales‑Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143–44 (2018).

In practice this review structure means appellate courts examine: whether the district court’s factual findings that support guideline adjustments are clearly erroneous (the standard applied to PSR facts in this circuit, see United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929–30 (8th Cir. 2013)); whether the district court applied the Guidelines’ rules correctly; and whether the record shows meaningful consideration of the § 3553(a) factors. A within-Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal (United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)), and the appellate court will affirm where the record shows the district court adopted the PSR, considered the defendant’s arguments, correctly calculated the range, and imposed a within-range sentence — as the Eighth Circuit found in Cutbank.

## Bottom line from Cutbank

The Eighth Circuit affirmed because the district court adopted the PSR facts without objection, correctly computed the § 2K2.1 offense level including the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (consistent with Application Note 14(C)), compared that offense level to the ACCA-derived level under USSG § 4B1.4(b), applied the greater level (35), and imposed a within-Guidelines sentence after considering § 3553(a) arguments. Under the settled standards of review (clear-error for factual findings; procedural-correctness prerequisite to substantive review; presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences), the appellate court found no reversible error.

# Sentencing Analysis and Disposition

This chapter explains how the Eighth Circuit reviewed and affirmed the district court’s sentencing choices in United States v. Justin Cutbank. The court upheld both the district court’s application of a four‑level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (possession of a firearm “in connection with” another felony) and the within‑Guidelines 292‑month sentence. To understand the decision you need to track three things the court did: (1) treat the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) as the factual foundation for the enhancement, (2) resolve how the various guideline provisions interact, and (3) apply the procedural and substantive reasonableness framework that governs appellate review of sentences.

## Enhancement application: adoption of PSR facts and standard of review

The PSR recommended a four‑level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) on the ground the firearm was used in connection with another felony. At sentencing the district court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements” about Cutbank’s prior convictions and the conduct surrounding the offense. The Eighth Circuit relied on that adoption in affirming the enhancement. Two doctrinal points matter for students.

First, the Sentencing Guidelines and Eighth Circuit practice permit a sentencing court to rely on undisputed PSR facts as the factual basis for guideline enhancements. When a defendant does not object to PSR facts, the district court’s adoption of those facts provides a permissible factual predicate for enhancements. Second, the standard of review for disputed factual findings made at sentencing is clear error; the court cited Dunn for that principle. Because Cutbank neither objected below nor showed clear error in the factual findings the PSR contained, the appellate court found no reversible error in applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. The opinion also reminds readers that the Guidelines commentary expressly allows application of this enhancement “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (comment n.14(C)), so the fact that no separate felony charge accompanied the firearm possession does not preclude the enhancement.

## Guideline interaction: ACCA and USSG § 4B1.4(b)

Cutbank also argued, in effect, that the enhancement produced an excessive offense level because the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) already triggered enhanced penalties. The PSR’s arithmetic produced a total offense level of 35; the ACCA‑based guideline calculations would have produced level 34. The Guidelines themselves resolve this sort of overlap: USSG § 4B1.4(b) directs that when multiple provisions could apply, the greater offense level governs. The panel applied that rule and accepted the higher level (35) as controlling. This is a straight application of the Guidelines’ conflict‑resolution rule rather than a discretionary choice by the court; once the correct calculations are made, the higher offense level governs the Guidelines range that the district court must consider.

## Procedural reasonableness: correct calculation and consideration of § 3553(a)

Before considering whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, an appellate court first asks whether the district court committed any significant procedural error, principally whether it correctly calculated the Guidelines range. The opinion quotes Gall for this framework and Rosales‑Mireles for the proposition that correct calculation is a necessary predicate to appellate review of substantive reasonableness. Here the district court did calculate the Guidelines range (offense level 35; Criminal History Category VI; advisory range 292–365 months) and adopted the PSR facts supporting the enhancements. The court also reviewed the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and it stated that it had considered the § 3553(a) factors. Because the Guidelines calculation was made correctly and the record shows the court engaged with the pertinent materials, the Eighth Circuit concluded there was no procedural error that would defeat appellate review of the sentence’s substantive reasonableness.

## Substantive reasonableness: presumption for within‑Guidelines sentences and sufficiency of the record

On substantive review the panel applied the familiar Eighth Circuit approach: a within‑Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal (the opinion cites Goodhouse for that presumption). The court emphasized that a district court need not deliver a “mechanical recitation” of every § 3553(a) factor; it only must make clear from the record that it actually considered them. The opinion relied on Maluoth for that point and on Masood to explain that when the record shows the court reviewed the materials and heard argument, the appellate court will presume the district court considered and rejected the defendant’s arguments. Thus the combination of (1) a correct Guidelines calculation, (2) the district court’s stated review of the PSR and memoranda, and (3) the fact the sentence fell within the advisory range produced by those calculations led the panel to treat the 292‑month sentence as presumptively reasonable.

It is important to note what this standard does and does not do. The presumption is rebuttable, but a defendant must point to particular facts or reasoning showing that the sentence is substantively unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors. Cutbank did not (and the panel found did not) identify such a defect. The appellate court will not substitute its own judgment for the district court’s where the record shows the court considered the appropriate factors and the sentence is within the calculated Guidelines range.

## Sentencing justifications the court relied on

The opinion frames why the district court’s within‑range sentence was supportable by describing Cutbank’s criminal history and the conduct underlying the conviction. The record established that Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms. The offense here involved domestic violence—waking and restraining his girlfriend at gunpoint, threats, assault in the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and fleeing in a manner that caused injury to an officer during his arrest. Those facts, together with the extensive criminal record, provided a concrete basis for the district court’s decision not to vary downward under § 3553(a). The appellate court viewed these considerations as legitimate sentencing explanations that fit within the statutory purposes of punishment, deterrence, protection of the public, and incapacitation.

## Outcome: affirmation

Bringing these strands together, the Eighth Circuit concluded that: the district court permissibly relied on unobjected PSR facts to apply the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (and the Guidelines commentary allows that enhancement without a separate conviction), the Guidelines conflict rule (USSG § 4B1.4(b)) properly produced offense level 35 rather than 34, the court correctly calculated the advisory range and considered § 3553(a) factors (satisfying the procedural step required by Gall and Rosales‑Mireles), and the within‑Guidelines 292‑month sentence was presumptively reasonable and not shown to be substantively unreasonable on the record. The judgment—conviction and sentence of 292 months—was affirmed.

For students, the key takeaways are: when a defendant does not object to PSR facts at sentencing those facts are a sturdy basis for guideline enhancements; the Guidelines include rules for resolving overlaps between enhancement calculations; correct calculation is a necessary precursor to substantive review; and a within‑Guidelines sentence supported by the defendant’s criminal history and offense conduct is difficult to overturn on appeal.
