A Casebook Chapter on: United_States_v_Justin_Cutbank

# Orientation to Federal Firearms Prosecutions and Sentencing

This chapter introduces the statutory framework, proof problems, evidentiary disputes, sentencing architecture, and appellate review patterns that typically arise in federal felon‑in‑possession prosecutions. The recent Eighth Circuit decision in United States v. Cutbank provides a compact illustration of many of these recurring themes: the elements and proof of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) crimes, the interplay between guideline enhancements and the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), common evidentiary rulings (propensity, hearsay, Confrontation Clause, Rule 403), the manner in which sentencing ranges are calculated and explained after Booker, and how appellate courts review both evidentiary rulings and sentences. Throughout this chapter, the Cutbank opinion will be used as the case vehicle to explain doctrine and to show how courts apply tests and standards to the facts of a typical firearms prosecution.

## Statutory core: 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (felon‑in‑possession) and § 924(e) (ACCA)

Two federal statutes form the backbone of most federal firearms prosecutions against persons with criminal histories. First, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) makes it unlawful for a person “who has been convicted in any court of, a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” to “possess in or affecting commerce, any firearm or ammunition.” Section 922(g)(1) thus has three basic elements the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt: (1) the defendant has the prohibited status (a qualifying felony conviction); (2) the defendant possessed a firearm or ammunition; and (3) the possession affected interstate commerce (a jurisdictional element usually satisfied by proof the firearm moved in commerce at some point or was manufactured outside the state).

Proof of each element raises recurring practical problems. Status is established through certified records of conviction, and courts usually treat that as straightforward. The more demanding and fact‑intensive disputes concern possession and the interstate commerce element (the latter often satisfies by affidavit in the case of commercially manufactured firearms). Cutbank illustrates the possession and identity problems courts routinely face: police located a sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle, DNA on the gun matched Cutbank, and two witnesses identified him as someone who had the gun before the incident. The jury convicted on the basis of that testimonial and physical evidence.

Second, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) — the Armed Career Criminal Act — is a statutory sentencing enhancement that applies when a defendant convicted under § 922(g) has three or more prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. ACCA triggers a mandatory minimum sentence and raises the statutory maximums applicable to the § 922(g) conviction. In modern practice the prosecutor frequently charges § 922(g) as a stand‑alone crime, and the government either proves the predicate convictions to a jury or asks the court to find them at sentencing. Cutbank’s jury found he had at least three prior violent felonies, thereby triggering the ACCA enhancement and its more severe sentencing exposure.

Understanding ACCA requires attention to how “violent felony” is defined and interpreted. The statute contains an elements (or “force”) clause, an enumerated‑offenses clause, and, historically, a residual clause invalidated by the Supreme Court. Courts evaluate whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony by comparing the statutory elements of the prior offense to the language of ACCA and to controlling Supreme Court precedent (e.g., Johnson, Mathis, and related cases). In Cutbank, the ACCA issue was not litigated at length because the jury found the requisite predicates and the district court adopted the PSR’s factual recital of prior convictions without objection — a procedural posture that often forecloses appellate challenge to predicate status.

## Typical proof problems in possession cases (actual vs. constructive possession; identification of the firearm)

Possession is the element that supplies most litigation in § 922(g)(1) prosecutions. Courts distinguish between actual and constructive possession. Actual possession exists when the firearm is on the defendant’s person or within his immediate physical control. Constructive possession exists when a defendant has the power and intent to exercise dominion or control over the firearm even if it is not on his person — for example, a weapon stored in a bedroom or a vehicle the defendant controls. Joint constructive possession is often charged where multiple people had access to the location of the firearm; the government must show each defendant exercised sufficient dominion and control.

Two inquiries dominate: (1) was the defendant aware of the weapon’s presence (knowledge), and (2) did the defendant have the ability and intent to control the weapon (dominion or control)? Both inquiries are fact questions that the jury resolves from direct and circumstantial evidence: presence near the weapon, access to its storage area, statements admitting possession, fingerprint or DNA evidence, witness testimony identifying the defendant with the weapon, and the overall contextual picture including flight, threats, or gestures. In Cutbank, the government relied on DNA on the rifle, witness testimony that they had seen him with that same sawed‑off rifle before the incident, the discovery of the victim’s cell phone and a knife along the likely path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself, and the events during the police encounter (resistance, grabbing an officer’s gun). Those facts collectively supported a jury verdict that Cutbank possessed the sawed‑off rifle.

Identification of the firearm is important for two reasons. First, the government must prove the complained‑of firearm is the one the defendant possessed. Witness identification of the same weapon at prior times, forensic identification (DNA, fingerprints), and chain‑of‑custody evidence for physical items found by police form the typical proofs. Second, classification of the firearm (e.g., sawed‑off shotgun, short‑barreled rifle) can affect the statutory and guideline consequences. Alterations such as a sawed‑off barrel can change the base offense level under the Sentencing Guidelines and, in some cases, implicate federal statutes governing National Firearms Act weapons. Cutbank’s PSR treated the Marlin .22 rifle with a sawed‑off barrel and stock as a weapon that increased the base offense level under USSG § 2K2.1.

Because possession is often proved circumstantially, defense counsel will contest witness identifications, challenge forensic links (chain of custody, lab protocols, contamination), and argue that joint possession or third‑party access negates the required dominion and control. Prosecutors respond with corroborating physical evidence and testimony that establishes continuity of possession and ownership. Courts routinely instruct juries on the difference between actual and constructive possession and on the need to find knowledge and control beyond mere proximity.

## The Sentencing Guidelines’ role post‑Booker: advisory ranges and district court discretion

The federal Sentencing Guidelines remain the central starting point for most federal sentences, even after Booker transformed the Guidelines from mandatory rules into advisory ones. The proper sentencing process has two principal steps: first, the district court must correctly calculate the applicable advisory Guidelines range; second, the court must consider the § 3553(a) factors and determine whether a within‑Guidelines sentence, a variance above or below the range, or a departure is appropriate. Gall v. United States and later cases establish that procedural accuracy in calculating the Guidelines range is a prerequisite to meaningful appellate review of substantive reasonableness.

Guideline calculations begin with the base offense level specified in the applicable guideline (for firearms cases, typically USSG § 2K2.1), then add or subtract enhancements and adjustments (e.g., for possession of a sawed‑off weapon, altered serial number, reckless endangerment during flight, or possession in connection with another felony). The total offense level, combined with the defendant’s Criminal History Category, yields the advisory range. If a statutory mandatory minimum applies (as with ACCA), the court must respect that minimum.

In Cutbank, the PSR set out a base offense level of 26 because the weapon was a sawed‑off firearm, then recommended multiple enhancements: a three‑level increase for an altered serial number, a two‑level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing (behavior at the scene of arrest), and a four‑level increase under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm “in connection with another felony offense.” Those adjustments produced a total offense level of 35 and, together with a Criminal History Category of VI, an advisory range of 292–365 months. The district court adopted the PSR, considered § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within‑range sentence of 292 months. On appeal the court affirmed, applying the usual presumption of reasonableness for within‑Guidelines sentences and noting that the district court had appropriately calculated the Guidelines and considered the statutory sentencing factors.

Two other points are essential. First, the standard of proof for guideline factfinding at sentencing is typically a preponderance of the evidence, and appellate courts review such factual findings for clear error. Thus, a sentencing enhancement that depends on disputed facts will survive appellate review unless the district court’s finding lacks evidentiary support or is clearly erroneous. Second, the Guidelines themselves sometimes interact with statutory enhancements (e.g., ACCA and the career offender guideline USSG § 4B1.4). When multiple guideline provisions could apply, the Guidelines manual specifies which offense level controls; courts follow those rules unless they conflict with statute.

## Recurring evidentiary disputes in firearms trials: propensity, hearsay, confrontation, and Rule 403

Evidentiary issues recur in firearms prosecutions because trials often hinge on witness credibility, prior acts, hearsay statements to police, and balancing probative value against unfair prejudice.

Rule 404(b) and intrinsic evidence. Prosecutors frequently seek to introduce testimony that a defendant had possessed or been seen with the same firearm before the charged incident. The defense often objects, arguing such testimony is impermissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). Courts apply a familiar distinction: evidence of other acts that is intrinsic to the charged offense (because it explains the context, forms part of the same transaction or series of transactions, or is necessary to make the charged act understandable) is not governed by Rule 404(b) and is generally admissible. Extrinsic acts intended solely to show propensity must satisfy the Rule 404(b) test (notice, relevance to a permissible nonpropensity purpose, and a Rule 403 balancing). The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Battle, invoked by the Cutbank panel, encapsulates the common rule: prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to later possession because it tends to establish ownership or control and thus is often intrinsic and admissible. Cutbank demonstrates the application: both D.F. and C.F. testified they had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle on prior occasions; the district court rejected the 404(b) objection and admitted the testimony as intrinsic — an evidentiary ruling the appellate court affirmed.

Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause. Statements made to police by victims or third parties commonly surface in firearms prosecutions: 911 calls, statements to officers at the scene, or prior out‑of‑court descriptions of the events. Such statements may be admissible for nonhearsay purposes — for example, to show the context of the officers’ conduct, to explain why officers took particular investigatory steps, or to show state of mind — without violating the hearsay rule. Additionally, the Confrontation Clause is not implicated when the declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross‑examination; a live witness’s prior out‑of‑court statements may then be used, and the jury can evaluate them in light of cross‑examination. In Cutbank the court allowed testimony and did not give the special limiting instruction the defense requested; the appellate court found no abuse of discretion because the proffered testimony was not hearsay in its admitted form and because D.F. ultimately testified at trial, satisfying the Confrontation Clause under the authority cited in the opinion.

Impeachment, extrinsic evidence, and authentication. Defendants often seek to impeach witnesses with prior inconsistent statements recorded by police or in incident reports. Federal Rule of Evidence 613 governs prior inconsistent statements and impeachment generally; extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny the statement, and in some circumstances the foundational requirements of authentication remain. Police reports that memorialize witness statements are hearsay if offered for the truth of the matter asserted and are not automatically admissible for impeachment without foundation or authentication (or an applicable exception). Cutbank demonstrates that courts may require proper foundation before allowing counsel to read portions of a police report into the record; the Eighth Circuit declined to fault the district court’s insistence on authenticating extrinsic impeachment evidence.

Rule 403 balancing. Even admissible evidence may be excluded under Rule 403 if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or undue delay. Firearms cases often involve visceral, prejudicial material — graphic descriptions, family tragedies, prior violent acts — and defendants will frequently move under Rule 403 to exclude references that might inflame the jury. The trial judge has broad discretion here. Cutbank’s record included testimony that the victim’s sister had been killed by a gun; the court allowed that testimony as it explained the victim’s hesitancy and unfamiliarity with guns after prior inconsistent statements. The appellate court found no abuse of discretion because no substantial unfair prejudice was shown and because the testimony fairly responded to cross‑examination about prior statements.

Limiting instructions. District courts have discretion whether to give or when to give limiting instructions. They need not provide a running cautionary instruction each time a potentially problematic item of evidence is admitted; a timely and clear instruction at the close of evidence frequently suffices. Appellate courts review denial of a requested limiting instruction for abuse of discretion, mindful that the judge’s decision should be viewed in the setting of the entire trial and the jury charge.

Cutbank illustrates how these evidentiary doctrines are applied in practice: the court admitted prior observations of the same firearm (intrinsic evidence), permitted testimony about out‑of‑court statements when the declarant testified at trial (minimizing Confrontation Clause concerns), required authentication before allowing reading from a police report (protecting against unreliable extrinsic impeachment), and declined to strike testimony that provided context for inconsistent statements (Rule 403 analysis).

## The Sentencing enhancement “in connection with another felony” and related guideline concepts

One frequent source of appellate argument in firearms cases is the application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the four‑level enhancement for possessing a firearm “in connection with another felony offense.” The guideline’s comment (n.14(C)) clarifies that the enhancement applies regardless of whether a charge was brought or a conviction obtained for the other felony. The enhancement’s application requires a finding that the firearm facilitated, promoted, or enabled the commission of another felony — a functional relationship and a temporal/spatial nexus are usually required. Courts interpret “in connection with” by looking for evidence that the weapon had a purpose closely tied to the other criminal conduct (for instance, use to intimidate victims during a robbery, to facilitate a shooting, to protect contraband in a drug offense, or to enable escape or assault).

At sentencing, a district court evaluates the totality of events surrounding the firearm: where it was found relative to other criminal acts, the defendant’s conduct (e.g., threats, using the gun while committing another offense), and other provenance evidence. The standard of proof is the preponderance of the evidence, and appellate review is for clear error on factual findings. In Cutbank the PSR recommended the four‑level enhancement on the basis that the firearm was connected with other felonious conduct — assault, kidnapping, and the threats to the victim — and the district court adopted the PSR. On appeal the Eighth Circuit upheld the court’s application of the enhancement: the PSR’s factual recitation had been adopted, the requisite nexus and functional relationship were supported by the record, and the proper standard of review (clear error) yielded no reversible error.

A related doctrinal issue is how guideline enhancements interact with statutory sentencing regimes such as ACCA. The Guidelines contain a career offender or armed career criminal guideline (USSG § 4B1.4) designed to harmonize with statutory authorizations; the manual contains rules to determine which offense level applies when overlapping provisions could produce different ranges. Where a defendant’s predicate convictions would trigger ACCA or other career enhancements, the Guidelines instruct the sentencing court to apply the greater offense level if appropriate. Cutbank’s sentencing posture illustrates that courts will apply the greater applicable offense level and adopt the PSR’s treatment when the facts and prior convictions are uncontested or unobjected to at sentencing.

## Appellate review overview: evidentiary abuse of discretion, harmless error, procedural vs. substantive reasonableness

A final theme concerns how appellate courts review the sorts of rulings that arise in firearms prosecutions and sentencing.

Evidentiary rulings. An appellate court generally reviews a district court’s evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion. That standard gives trial judges wide latitude: a ruling that falls within the zone of reasonable choices will be affirmed. But where a defendant preserves objections and the ruling is erroneous, reversal still requires a showing of prejudice under the harmless‑error doctrine. The Eighth Circuit has stated that an erroneous evidentiary ruling warrants reversal only if “the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence” (DeMarce). Where overwhelming independent evidence of guilt exists, appellate courts frequently find any error harmless. The Cutbank court applied these principles: the defendant’s insistence that certain testimony was improper was rejected as unpersuasive, and even if any error occurred the government’s other strong evidence rendered the error harmless.

Sentence review: procedural and substantive reasonableness. Sentencing appeals operate in two distinct phases. First, appellate courts ask whether the district court committed any significant procedural error — failure to calculate the Guidelines range correctly, failing to consider the statutory factors in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), treating the Guidelines as mandatory, failing to explain a sentence adequately, or selecting a sentence based on clearly erroneous facts. If procedural error exists, remand is usually required. If the procedural aspects pass muster, the court turns to substantive reasonableness — whether the sentence is reasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors. Since Gall and subsequent cases, a within‑Guidelines sentence is accorded a rebuttable presumption of reasonableness on appeal, but the presumption can be displaced by persuasive evidence that the sentence is substantively unreasonable given the totality of circumstances.

The standard of review reflects these distinctions. Procedural errors are reviewed de novo where statutory interpretation is involved and for abuse of discretion when the district court exercises sentencing discretion; factual findings supporting a sentence are reviewed for clear error. Substantive reasonableness is reviewed for abuse of discretion. The Cutbank opinion underscores these points: the appellate court first ensured the district court had correctly calculated the Guidelines and considered the § 3553(a) factors (satisfying the procedural step), then affirmed the within‑range sentence under the presumption of reasonableness, concluding the district court did not abuse its broad sentencing discretion.

Special appellate concerns arise when the defendant failed to object at sentencing. A defendant’s failure to object ordinarily subjects the claim to forfeiture, and the defendant must show plain error on appeal. In Cutbank, because the defendant did not object to the PSR’s factual recitations about prior convictions, the district court’s adoption of those facts at sentencing made it unlikely the defendant could prevail on appeal.

Conclusion

Federal felon‑in‑possession cases combine garden‑variety criminal proof questions with distinctive firearms‑related forensic and sentencing issues. The Cutbank decision illustrates how courts apply well‑established doctrines: prior possession of the same weapon is generally admissible as intrinsic evidence; hearsay to which the declarant testifies at trial does not offend the Confrontation Clause; extrinsic impeachment evidence requires authentication; Rule 403 balances are factspecific and deferential to trial judges; the Sentencing Guidelines remain the starting point for sentencing, and district courts must correctly calculate the advisory range and consider § 3553(a) factors; sentencing enhancements such as the “in connection with another felony” provision require a preponderance finding of a functional nexus and are reviewed for clear error; and appellate courts will reverse only for prejudicial evidentiary error or procedural or substantive sentencing error. Students should view Cutbank as a prototype: the case packages common evidentiary skirmishes, proofs of possession and identity, guideline enhancements, and the familiar appellate posture that governs review of such prosecutions. These are the skills and rules you will apply to novel fact patterns in exams and in practice.

# Elements of § 922(g)(1): Status, Possession, Knowledge, and Commerce

This chapter explains what the government must prove to obtain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (a felon-in-possession prosecution), how those elements have been reshaped by Rehaif and its progeny, and how courts treat common evidentiary disputes that arise at trial. The recent Eighth Circuit decision in United States v. Cutbank (No. 24-1225) provides a useful vehicle for discussing these issues in practice: Cutbank’s prosecution and appeal illustrate disputes over proof of possession, the admissibility of prior-possession testimony, the application of Rehaif’s knowledge requirement in the trial context, and the minimal interstate-commerce nexus required under federal law. This chapter organizes doctrine and practice around the four elemental questions a jury must resolve in a § 922(g)(1) prosecution: (1) was the defendant a person barred by § 922(g)(1) at the time of the charged conduct; (2) did the defendant know that he had that prohibited status (the Rehaif requirement); (3) did the defendant knowingly possess the firearm (actual, constructive, or joint possession); and (4) was there a sufficient interstate-commerce nexus.

## The Elements of a § 922(g)(1) Offense: An Overview

A § 922(g)(1) charge requires proof of four basic facts beyond a reasonable doubt:

- That the defendant has the disqualifying status—i.e., was previously convicted of a felony (or otherwise a person subject to a statutory prohibition) under § 922(g)(1);
- That the defendant knew that he had that status at the time of the possession (the mental-state element mandated by Rehaif v. United States);
- That the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm—possession in fact or constructive/joint possession plus knowledge of the presence of the firearm; and
- That the firearm was in or affected interstate commerce.

Each of these elements has particular legal tests and evidentiary rules that govern trial presentation, jury instructions, and appellate review. The remainder of this chapter treats each element in turn, with attention to practical proof issues and the interaction of these elements in evidentiary rulings like those in Cutbank.

## Prior-Felon Status and Proof After Rehaif

Historically, courts treated the status element of § 922(g)(1) as a relatively straightforward matter of record: the government proved a past felony conviction by introducing certified records, and that was enough to establish that the defendant belonged to the class of persons barred from possessing firearms. Rehaif v. United States, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), changed the mental-state calculus: Rehaif held that, for prosecutions under § 922(g) and § 924(a)(2), the government must prove not only that the defendant belonged to a prohibited class (e.g., had a prior felony conviction), but also that the defendant knew at the time of the firearm possession that he belonged to that class.

The immediate practical consequence of Rehaif is that the government must furnish proof—direct or circumstantial—that the defendant knew of his disqualifying status when he possessed the firearm. Proof can take many forms:

- Direct admissions by the defendant (e.g., statements acknowledging a prior conviction, admissions that he knew he could not possess firearms);
- Documentary evidence demonstrating that the defendant was informed of his conviction and its consequences (e.g., plea colloquies, written judgments, probation or parole notices);
- Circumstantial evidence from the defendant’s criminal history (e.g., a recent and numerous pattern of felony convictions, ongoing supervision, period of incarceration, or easily available public record informing the defendant of the conviction);
- Evidence of steps taken to conceal the firearm or other consciousness-of-guilt evidence probative of the defendant’s awareness of his status in context.

Rehaif did not prescribe a specific formula for proof; the Supreme Court required proof of knowledge of status as an element the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. In a practical trial setting prosecutors will therefore offer certified conviction records to establish the status element and support that evidence with witness testimony or documentary proof that the defendant knew about the conviction—plea colloquy transcripts, parole files, or the defendant’s own statements.

When a defendant fails to preserve a Rehaif objection at trial (for example, by failing to request a jury instruction that includes knowledge of status or failing to object to the omission of that element), appellate courts apply plain-error review. The Supreme Court’s treatment of Rehaif-related appellate claims (and the courts of appeals’ follow-up decisions) make clear that a defendant who attempts to raise a Rehaif claim for the first time on appeal must satisfy the demanding plain-error standard. Under that standard the defendant must show (1) there was an error, (2) the error is clear or obvious, (3) the error affected substantial rights—usually meaning there is a reasonable probability the error affected the outcome—and (4) the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Lower courts assessing prejudice look to the record evidence relating to knowledge of status and determine whether the evidence is sufficient to remove any reasonable doubt a jury could have had on that element. See, e.g., Greer v. United States, 141 S. Ct. 2090 (2021) (applying plain-error review to the omission of a Rehaif instruction and evaluating prejudice by reference to the record).

The Cutbank prosecution illustrates how courts treat proof of status at sentencing and at trial. At sentencing the district court adopted, without objection, the Presentence Investigation Report’s recitation of Cutbank’s prior convictions; that adoption supplied a firm factual basis for ACCA enhancement. For the guilt phase, the government relied on witnesses and documentary evidence of prior convictions and on testimony that connected Cutbank to the sawed-off firearm. Students should note the distinction: at trial the government must prove status and the defendant’s knowledge of that status beyond a reasonable doubt; at sentencing the district court may adopt facts in an uncontested PSR under a preponderance standard for guideline calculation, but any sentencing enhancement tied to status can have profound consequences (as in Cutbank, where ACCA applied). Defense counsel should therefore object and seek a Rehaif instruction whenever there is any colorable argument that the defendant lacked awareness of his felony status.

## The Rehaif Knowledge Requirement and Appellate Posture

Rehaif’s knowledge-of-status requirement created numerous litigation points about preservation and appellate review. If a defendant affirmatively objects at trial to the omission of a Rehaif element or requests an instruction tracking Rehaif’s requirement, the trial court must address that request; reversal is possible if the omission constitutes structural or preserved error. If the defendant fails to preserve the error, appellate review is for plain error, and the defendant shoulders the burden of demonstrating prejudice and the other plain-error factors.

In assessing prejudice under plain-error review, appellate courts examine the entire trial record to determine whether the omission of the knowledge-of-status instruction likely affected the verdict. Courts will examine, among other things, whether the defendant (1) admitted or otherwise conceded the felony conviction at trial, (2) had recent or numerous felony convictions or ongoing supervision suggesting he know of his status, (3) made statements or took actions indicating an awareness of his criminal history, or (4) had other circumstances that would have put the defendant on notice of his disqualifying status (e.g., pending parole, supervised release, or a recent plea colloquy). Where the record contains strong, uncontradicted evidence that the defendant knew about his status, appellate courts have generally concluded Rehaif omissions were not prejudicial under plain-error review. By contrast, a thin record on knowledge—e.g., limited or ambiguous evidence of a distant conviction without proof the defendant knew it had collateral consequences—may support a successful Rehaif claim when preserved and may satisfy plain error when the record shows reasonable probability of a different verdict.

Because appellate review turns on the record in each case, trial counsel should preserve Rehaif claims through request for a Rehaif instruction or through contemporaneous objection to avoid the heavy burden of plain-error review on appeal.

## Possession: Actual, Constructive, and Joint Possession; Proof and Evidentiary Indicia

A separate set of questions involves what it means to “possess” a firearm and how the government proves possession beyond a reasonable doubt. Possession in § 922(g)(1) cases is typically established by proof that the defendant had dominion and control over the weapon and knew of its presence. Courts recognize three paradigms of possession:

- Actual possession: the firearm is in the defendant’s immediate physical custody or control (e.g., found on the defendant’s person or in a container the defendant was carrying). Actual possession is typically the easiest form for the government to prove because the weapon’s physical presence with the defendant is direct evidence of control.

- Constructive possession: the firearm is not on the person but is in a place under the defendant’s dominion and control (e.g., a firearm found in the defendant’s home, vehicle, or an area the defendant exclusively controls). Constructive possession requires proof that the defendant had the ability and intent to exercise dominion and control over the firearm, even if someone else handled it at a particular moment.

- Joint possession: two or more people share dominion and control over the weapon (e.g., a firearm in a common area of a residence used by multiple occupants). The government may convict a defendant who has joint constructive possession if it proves that the defendant knowingly had the power and intent to exercise control over the firearm.

To sustain a possession conviction the government must prove not only the defendant’s relationship to the weapon (actual, constructive, or joint possession) but also that the defendant knew of the firearm’s existence. Evidence used to establish possession and knowledge can be direct or circumstantial and commonly includes:

- Eyewitness identification and testimony placing the firearm in the defendant’s hands or in places he controlled. In Cutbank, for example, the victim and a household member identified the sawed-off rifle as one they had seen Cutbank with before the night in question; the district court allowed this testimony as intrinsic evidence of continuous possession rather than extrinsic evidence of propensity.

- Physical evidence connecting the defendant to the weapon: DNA on the gun, fingerprints, possession of ammunition, or clothing fibers. In Cutbank, DNA on the sawed-off Marlin matched the defendant, a fact the jury could properly consider in assessing control and possession.

- Proximity and opportunity: testimony that the defendant was alone in a garage with the weapon, or that the weapon was found along a path the defendant likely used, are circumstantial indicators that the defendant possessed the weapon.

- Statements by the defendant: admissions, threats while holding the firearm, or statements acknowledging ownership are powerful evidence of possession and knowledge.

- Behavior indicative of control or concealment: fleeing with the firearm, changing the weapon, or placing it in a location to avoid detection are conductive of the required inference of dominion and knowledge.

Courts routinely instruct juries that possession may be actual, constructive, or joint and require the jury to find the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm. When multiple forms of possession are possible, the jury may be instructed in the disjunctive (the government may prove any of these modes), but the instruction should require specific findings—i.e., that the jury find beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant had dominion and control and knew of the firearm.

Practical evidence-law consequences follow. The government often seeks to admit testimony that a witness had seen a defendant with the same weapon on prior occasions to show ownership or continuity of control. Defense counsel frequently objects, arguing such evidence is improper propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). But courts often hold that testimony about prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic when it tends to prove an element of the charged offense—ownership, dominion, or continuous possession—and therefore is admissible without Rule 404(b) analysis. In the Eighth Circuit, United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), illustrates this principle: prior possession of the firearm is “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon.” Cutbank applied that principle in admitting testimony from the victim and a housemate that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. The court treated those statements as intrinsic to proving possession rather than as prohibited character propensity evidence.

Defense counsel should be attentive, however, to the risk that prior-possession testimony will stray into inflammatory or collateral matters (e.g., other criminal acts); courts will exclude testimony that functions primarily to show bad character rather than to establish ownership or continuity. In cross-examination, the defense may probe witnesses’ inconsistent statements to the police, but extrinsic impeachment requires proper authentication; courts commonly prevent reading police reports into the record without foundation that the witness actually made the statements in the report. See United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), cited in Cutbank.

Evidence of actual threats, domestic violence, or a victim’s family trauma may arise in cross-examination— such as the victim’s testimony in Cutbank that she did not like guns because her sister had been killed. District courts have broad discretion to admit or exclude such testimony under Rules 401 and 403; absent an objection or a clear showing of prejudice, appellate courts defer to that discretion. Cutbank illustrates the practical limits of such challenges: the court denied motions to strike testimony about the victim’s sister’s death and found no unfair prejudice, a ruling the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

## Interstate Commerce Nexus: Minimal but Necessary

Section 922(g) is a federal statute; Congress’s power to regulate possession of firearms rests on the Commerce Clause. As a result, the government must prove that the firearm "was in or affecting commerce"—a requirement satisfied by showing a minimal interstate-commerce nexus. The Supreme Court and the courts of appeals have uniformly recognized that the nexus requirement is modest: proof that the weapon previously traveled in interstate commerce, that it was manufactured outside the state, or that it crossed state lines at some point in its history is typically sufficient.

The common ways the government proves the interstate-commerce element are: introducing testimony from the manufacturer or an expert that the firearm was produced out of state, introducing records showing interstate shipment, or establishing that the firearm’s make and model is known to be manufactured in another state or imported. Serial-number records, manufacturer catalogs, or Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) trace evidence often suffice. The government need not show the defendant personally transported the firearm across state lines or that the interstate transport occurred immediately before the offense; a prior passage in interstate commerce at any time in the firearm’s history is sufficient.

Defendants sometimes challenge interstate-commerce proof on the ground that the government has provided only conclusory testimony without documentary corroboration. Courts generally allow a minimal amount of proof at trial; if the government’s proof is weak but not nonexistent, the issue usually goes to the weight of the evidence rather than admissibility. Rarely will a court find the interstate-commerce element lacking when the government introduces basic evidence of manufacture or ownership outside the state.

In Cutbank, the court’s opinion does not dwell on the interstate-commerce evidence in detail, but the conviction record and the PSR treated the sawed-off rifle as within the statutory framework—an approach consistent with the modest showing the Commerce Clause requires.

## Indictments and Jury Instructions in the Post-Rehaif Era

The doctrine discussed above has practical consequences for the drafting of indictments and jury instructions and for trial preservation strategies.

Indictment. A § 922(g)(1) indictment typically pleads the statute’s elements: the defendant’s status as a convicted felon, the possession of the firearm, the interstate-commerce nexus, and the necessary mens rea (“knowingly” possessed). After Rehaif, prosecutors must ensure their charging documents and proposed instructions track the knowledge-of-status element, although a mere omission in the indictment is not invariably fatal if the government proves the element beyond a reasonable doubt at trial. To avoid appellate defense claims, prosecutors commonly allege both that the defendant “knowingly” possessed the firearm and that the defendant was knowingly a person who had previously been convicted of a felony.

Jury instructions. Trial judges must instruct juries on each element the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt, and after Rehaif, that obligation includes the knowledge-of-status element unless the defendant expressly waives the right or concedes status. A model § 922(g)(1) instruction in the post-Rehaif era will include language similar to this (simplified for illustration):

- The defendant has previously been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year;
- At the time of the possession alleged, the defendant knew he had that status (knew he had previously been convicted of a felony); and
- The defendant knowingly possessed the firearm, and the firearm had traveled in or affected interstate commerce.

The sequence and precise wording matter: courts should ensure the jury understands that the government must prove knowledge of status in addition to the objective fact of the prior conviction. Defense counsel should request a Rehaif instruction and object if the court declines, to preserve the claim for appeal.

Preservation and strategic considerations. Defense counsel should consider the following strategic points:

- Preservation: Object to any instruction that omits knowledge of status and request a Rehaif instruction. Failing to do so subjects the claim to plain-error review on appeal, which is a heavy burden.

- Stipulations: Sometimes the defense will stipulate to the prior conviction to avoid the admission of prejudicial records and testimony. A stipulation waives argument about the existence of the conviction but leaves open a Rehaif challenge only in the narrow circumstances where the defense asserts lack of knowledge of the conviction despite agreeing the record shows it. Careful colloquy is necessary so that the record reflects what the defendant concedes and what remains disputed.

- Cross-examination and impeachment: When the government offers victim and bystander testimony that the defendant had possessed the same firearm before the charged incident, defense counsel should evaluate whether the testimony is intrinsic (admissible to show ownership/continuous possession) or improper evidence of bad character. Cutbank demonstrates the courts’ inclination to treat prior use of the same weapon as probative of ownership and control rather than solely propensity.

- Authentication: To impeach government witnesses with prior inconsistent statements, the defense must authenticate extrinsic evidence before reading police reports or the like into evidence. Cutbank shows the district court refusing to allow the reading of portions of a police report without foundation; the point is fundamental to impeachment practice.

## Standards of Review: Evidentiary Rulings and Rehaif Errors

Courts review district courts’ evidentiary decisions under a deferential abuse-of-discretion standard. In Cutbank the defendant challenged multiple trial rulings—admission of prior-possession testimony, refusal to instruct the jury about hearsay limits at the moment of admission, and rulings on cross-examination and impeachment—but the Eighth Circuit affirmed under the abuse-of-discretion standard. Those rulings illustrate two important practical points: (1) judges have substantial latitude in admitting intrinsic evidence that bears on possession and ownership; and (2) when the record contains strong, independently persuasive evidence of possession and of status, even an error in admitting marginally relevant evidence will likely be deemed harmless under the harmless-error test.

When a defendant fails to object to a Rehaif omission at trial, appellate courts apply plain-error review (that same demanding deferential standard noted earlier). When the omission is preserved, the reviewing court examines whether the omission constituted legal error and whether that legal error warrants reversal under the usual standards for preserved error. Because Rehaif goes to the elements the government must prove, courts will reverse a preserved Rehaif omission unless the record shows the government proved the knowledge-of-status element beyond a reasonable doubt.

## Practical Takeaways and Trial Tips

- Preserve Rehaif challenges. Request a jury instruction that expressly requires the government to prove the defendant knew of his disqualifying status at the time of possession.

- Consider stipulation tradeoffs carefully. A stipulation to a prior conviction can reduce the risk of prejudice, but it may concede the status element; if the defense’s best Rehaif argument is lack of knowledge, do not stipulate away that issue without an on-the-record discussion with the client.

- Use admission and documentary proof. Prosecutors should introduce certified conviction records and any other materials (plea colloquies, parole/probation records, statements to police) that show the defendant knew of his conviction and its consequences.

- Prove possession across the spectrum. When actual possession is unavailable, marshal constructive-possession evidence: proximity, accessibility, physical evidence like DNA or fingerprints, eyewitness testimony linking the defendant to the weapon, and conduct (flight, concealment) showing conscious control.

- Anticipate 404(b) and intrinsic-evidence disputes. Prior sightings of the same firearm are commonly admissible to show ownership or continuity of possession; defense counsel should develop reasons to limit or contextualize such testimony when it threatens to spill into character evidence.

- Authenticate impeachment evidence. When impeaching witnesses with prior statements, be prepared to lay the foundation and authenticate police reports or other documents properly before attempting to read them into the record.

- Remember the minimal interstate-commerce element. The government’s burden on commerce is modest but real; prosecutors should be prepared to introduce readily-available evidence of the firearm’s out-of-state manufacture or shipment.

## Conclusion

A successful § 922(g)(1) prosecution requires the government to assemble proof on four elements: status, knowledge of status (Rehaif), knowing possession, and interstate commerce. Rehaif heightened the government’s burden by adding the knowledge-of-status element to the list of facts the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. Trial counsel—prosecution and defense alike—must plan accordingly: prosecutors must present direct or circumstantial evidence of knowledge of status; defense counsel must preserve Rehaif objections or consider strategic stipulations; and both sides must be prepared to litigate possession issues (actual, constructive, joint) with an eye to admissibility and error preservation. The Cutbank decision illustrates how these issues play out in practice: prior possession evidence was admissible as intrinsic proof of control; Confrontation concerns were satisfied where witnesses testified at trial; impeachment evidence required proper authentication; and Rehaif-related objections not raised at trial are subject to the demanding plain-error standard on appeal. Understanding these doctrinal contours will enable counsel to make informed strategic choices at every stage of a § 922(g)(1) prosecution.

# Second Amendment Challenges to Felon Disarmament After Bruen

This chapter situates prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) — the federal felon‑in‑possession offense — in the post‑Bruen constitutional landscape. It explains Bruen’s history‑and‑tradition test and how courts have applied that test to status‑based disarmament, surveys the Eighth Circuit’s approach to § 922(g)(1) challenges (with attention to pre‑ and post‑Bruen developments), describes the emerging law and circuit splits concerning as‑applied challenges by non‑violent felons, and offers practical guidance for litigators on preserving and presenting Second Amendment objections in § 922(g)(1) cases. The chapter uses an Eighth Circuit felon‑in‑possession appeal as a teaching vehicle: the opinion illustrates the routine posture of § 922(g)(1) cases, the kinds of factual records that matter for constitutional claims, and the strategic risks of failing to press a Second Amendment defense.

## Bruen’s history‑and‑tradition test and its application to status‑based prohibitions

New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass’n v. Bruen fundamentally changed how courts evaluate Second Amendment challenges. Bruen rejected the familiar two‑step approach that had required lower courts to evaluate both whether the regulated activity was protected and whether the regulation was justified under means‑end scrutiny. Instead, Bruen established a textual and historical framework. The threshold question is textual: does the Second Amendment’s plain text cover the defendant’s conduct — that is, does the regulation burden activity falling within the amendment’s protections (for example, possession of a handgun for self‑defense)? If the answer is yes, the government must justify the regulation by demonstrating that it is consistent with the Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation. That demonstration is not a formalistic catalog of identical antecedents; rather, courts engage in analogical reasoning: they look for historical regulations that are relevantly similar in purpose and burden to the modern regulation.

Two features of the Bruen framework are particularly important when courts consider status‑based prohibitions (laws that disarm people because of a class or status, such as felons, the mentally ill, or those subject to protective orders). First, Bruen places the initial burden on the challenger to show that the challenged regulation burdens conduct the Amendment protects; courts routinely treat private possession of a firearm for self‑defense as conduct within the scope of the Amendment’s plain text. Once that showing is made, the burden shifts to the government to identify a historical tradition of regulation that is analogous. Second, Bruen emphasized that historical analogues must be genuinely comparable in both purpose and burden: categorical, status‑based disarmaments face particular difficulty because many historical regulations focused on dangerous conduct (e.g., felonious violence) or on particular groups viewed as untrustworthy in a context that may differ from modern federal felony conviction categories.

After Bruen, the Supreme Court applied the same mode of analysis in United States v. Rahimi (2024) when it reviewed the federal prohibition on firearm possession by persons subject to domestic violence protective orders. The Court held that the government failed to point to a historical analogue for a categorical, status‑based prohibition of the sort at issue and thus invalidated the federal rule as applied to the petitioner. Rahimi stands for the proposition that categorical status‑based bans cannot survive Bruen unless the government can show longstanding historical precedent for disarming persons in that status or can otherwise tie the modern rule to historical practices that are meaningfully analogous.

The practical lessons for § 922(g)(1) are thus clear. A facial challenge that treats § 922(g)(1) as categorically exempted from Bruen’s framework will generally fail because a statute can be “longstanding” and thus presumptively lawful if it is comparable to historical prohibitions. But an as‑applied challenge mounted by a non‑violent felon who can show that his possession of a firearm for lawful purposes is within the Second Amendment’s textual coverage forces the government to do the historical work — and Rahimi demonstrates that in some contexts the government’s historical showing may be inadequate.

## The Eighth Circuit approach upholding § 922(g)(1) against facial challenges (e.g., United States v. Jackson)

The Eighth Circuit has long treated § 922(g)(1) as a valid exercise of Congress’s power to disarm persons with felony convictions. Before Bruen, Eighth Circuit precedent upheld felon‑disarmament statutes against facial Second Amendment attacks by applying the two‑step test and often sustaining the prohibition as reasonable in light of public‑safety rationales and the government’s interest in keeping firearms from persons with demonstrated criminal histories.

After Bruen, the Eighth Circuit has been cautious but generally has not accepted broad facial invalidation of § 922(g)(1). Under Bruen, a facial challenge succeeds only if the government cannot point to a historical tradition justifying the regulation for all persons and in all its applications covered by the statute. Courts in the Eighth Circuit that have confronted facial challenges have required challengers to clear Bruen’s textual threshold — typically easy because personal possession for self‑defense is plainly protected — and then reviewed the historical record. Many Eighth Circuit opinions have concluded that there exist sufficient historical analogues (statutes disarming certain classes of people — those convicted of serious crimes, persons of ill repute, or persons deemed dangerous) to sustain a broad statutory prohibition like § 922(g)(1). For example, in cases like United States v. Jackson, the court rejected a facial attack by reasoning that the historical record reflects longstanding traditions of disarming people because of criminality and dangerousness, and that § 922(g)(1) fits within that tradition.

Two points bear emphasis for doctrinal analysis. First, “longstanding” does not mean precisely coterminous in text. Bruen allows analogical reasoning; courts may uphold a modern regulation if historical prohibitions are similar in their scope and function, even if the historical law used different labels. Second, the Eighth Circuit has given considerable deference to Congress’s classification of felons as a group that may be disarmed where the prohibition is not narrowed to exclude non‑violent, rehabilitated persons. This deference historically reflected a policy judgment about balancing public safety against the individual right to arms.

But Bruen and Rahimi complicate the terrain. After Rahimi, the Eighth Circuit — like other courts — must take seriously the requirement that the government show historical analogues adequate to support status‑based disarmament in particular cases. An Eighth Circuit opinion that upheld § 922(g)(1) against a facial challenge before Rahimi may be distinguishable from a post‑Rahimi as‑applied challenge brought by a non‑violent felon who can argue that the historical record supports disarming violent or dangerous felons but not a category as broad as “all felons.”

## As‑applied challenges by non‑violent felons: circuit splits and trends

The most significant doctrinal battleground after Bruen and Rahimi is the as‑applied challenge mounted by a person with felony convictions that do not involve violence, weapon use, or recent dangerous conduct. Such a litigant typically admits the defendant’s felony status but argues that the specific application of § 922(g)(1) to him violates the Second Amendment because he was not at the time engaging in the sort of dangerous conduct that historical regulations targeted, and because the government cannot point to historical laws disarming persons with convictions like his.

Two conceptual questions arise in these as‑applied claims. The first is the textual threshold: does the defendant’s possession fall within the Second Amendment’s scope? Courts generally answer yes when the defendant possessed a usable firearm for lawful purposes such as self‑defense, even when the possession occurred in the course of other wrongdoing, although possession tied to criminal activity may be more difficult to defend under the textually grounded account (possession in furtherance of violent crime may fall outside the core because it is not “lawful” in purpose). The second question — and the more difficult one for governments — is whether there is a historical tradition of disarming an analogous class of persons. After Rahimi, courts have demanded a more granular historical showing: a tradition of disarming persons based on conviction categories analogous in seriousness and function to the modern felon label.

As a result, lower courts are now split. Several circuits and district courts have allowed as‑applied challenges to proceed or have held that § 922(g)(1) may be unconstitutional as applied to non‑violent felons when the government cannot identify historical analogues tailored to that subclass of persons. Other courts have rejected as‑applied challenges, concluding that historical prohibitions against persons of ill repute, people convicted of crimes, or people considered dangerous provide a sufficient analogue for disarming modern felons categorically.

The split centers on two analyses: (1) whether “felon” maps to any historically recognized class that was disarmed (e.g., “those convicted of infamous crimes,” “felo de se,” or people of “bad character”), and (2) whether historical practice distinguished between violent and non‑violent crimes the way modern law does. Courts tending to uphold § 922(g)(1) emphasize broad historical categories and Congress’s authority to regulate dangerousness; courts more receptive to as‑applied challenges demand evidence that historical disarmament targeted persons with convictions comparable in seriousness and nature to the contemporary non‑violent offender.

Practically, the trend is that post‑Rahimi decisions have given more traction to as‑applied claims by non‑violent felons than was common pre‑Bruen. But the outcome is fact‑specific and historical‑evidence dependent. A non‑violent felon who possessed a firearm for self‑defense and who can show his prior convictions were neither violent nor recent, and who lacks a history of recidivism or dangerousness, may obtain relief in some jurisdictions; in others, courts will find historical analogues sufficient to sustain the prohibition.

## Strategic considerations: preserving Second Amendment objections in § 922(g)(1) cases

The Eighth Circuit opinion at the head of this chapter encapsulates an everyday risk for defense counsel: failure to raise a Second Amendment objection and to develop the factual and historical record necessary for an as‑applied challenge can foreclose later relief. The opinion shows typical procedural and evidentiary disputes, sentencing issues, and the presence of aggravating facts (prior violent convictions, the sawed‑off rifle, flight and assault on officers) that made a constitutional challenge unlikely to succeed. Even when the record exposes potentially arguable constitutional claims, a defendant who does not press those claims loses strategic leverage and appellate options.

Below is a practical checklist and litigation strategy for attorneys who represent clients charged under § 922(g)(1), drawn from Bruen, Rahimi, and the lower‑court practice that has developed in their wake.

- Early assessment and preservation. Evaluate Second Amendment arguments at the earliest stages. If the client is a non‑violent felon or has convictions long in the past or for offenses that did not involve violence or weapons, consider moving to dismiss on Second Amendment grounds or seeking a pretrial ruling that would preserve an as‑applied challenge. If counsel does not press the constitutional claim pretrial, the argument may be forfeited for appeal except for plain‑error review, which is difficult to win.

- Develop the factual record on status and conduct. As‑applied challenges succeed or fail on facts. Assemble a detailed record on the nature of prior convictions: statutory citations, elements of the offenses, factual circumstances underlying the convictions, dates, sentences, and evidence of rehabilitation (steady employment, family ties, sobriety, expungement/sealing where applicable). Similarly document the circumstances of the charged possession: was the firearm present for self‑defense in the home, or was it possessed in furtherance of criminal activity? Concrete facts that demonstrate lawful, defensive possession strengthen the textual‑coverage showing; facts suggesting dangerous conduct provide the government with countervailing evidence.

- Seek tailored jury instructions and bench findings. If the case goes to trial and an as‑applied challenge is preserved, ask for jury instructions or a trial record that establishes the facts relevant to the constitutional issue: whether the defendant exercised the firearm, whether it was accessible for lawful self‑defense, and whether the possession was connected to other felonious conduct. If the defendant is convicted, request that the district court make specific factual findings at sentencing that address whether the prior convictions were violent and whether the present possession was in furtherance of dangerous conduct — findings useful on appeal.

- Historical evidence and expert assistance. Bruen requires historical analogues when the modern burden is within the Second Amendment’s textual scope. Prepare, in advance, historical materials that might support an as‑applied claim: evidence that early American or English law disarmed persons convicted of certain classes of offenses but not others, historical practice distinguishing violent from non‑violent wrongdoing, and statutes or practices that show selective disarmament of dangerous persons rather than categorical bans. Conversely, anticipate and be prepared to rebut the government’s historical evidence. Consulting or retaining an expert in legal history can be important; lower courts have relied on detailed historical briefs.

- Procedural posture and remedies. If the court rejects an as‑applied challenge, counsel should be prepared to pursue appellate review promptly. Where the defendant is sentenced under an enhanced regime (e.g., ACCA) or otherwise faces a high sentence, weigh the tactical benefits of preserving constitutional issues for appeal versus plea disposition. Note that prevailing on an as‑applied challenge can lead to vacatur of the § 922(g)(1) conviction or to dismissal of the indictment, but courts may also react defensively by charging alternative offenses or relying on other statutes.

- Interplay with other doctrines. Consider whether alternative arguments are available — for example, attacking the indictment’s failure to prove a requisite interstate commerce element is largely foreclosed by modern doctrines, but other statutory or evidentiary arguments (chain of custody, possession elements, proof of knowing status as felon) may be easier to prove. In sentencing contexts, attacks on enhancements (see USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in the opinion that served as this chapter’s vehicle) should be preserved because a sentence within the Guidelines is presumed reasonable on appeal absent significant procedural error.

- Preservation language and appeal record. When preserving a Bruen/Rahimi challenge, make explicit constitutional objections in motions, pleadings, and on the record at hearings. Ask the court to certify specific findings of fact and law necessary for appeal, and make sure the appellate record contains the full evidentiary basis (PSR exhibits, conviction transcripts, plea colloquies) that will be needed to argue both the textual threshold and the absence of historical analogues.

## Conclusion and practical recommendations

Bruen replaced the familiar two‑step approach to Second Amendment litigation with a history‑and‑tradition framework that makes historical evidence dispositive once a challenger shows the conduct is protected by the Amendment’s text. Rahimi clarified that categorical, status‑based prohibitions are vulnerable when the government cannot identify a tradition that plausibly maps onto the modern regulation. For § 922(g)(1) prosecutions, the Eighth Circuit has historically been deferential to Congress’s decision to disarm felons, and courts have generally rejected facial attacks. But the post‑Bruen/Rahimi landscape is more hospitable to focused as‑applied challenges by non‑violent felons, provided counsel preserves the issue, develops the factual record about the nature of prior convictions and the circumstances of possession, and marshals historical evidence (or shows its absence).

The practical advice for defense counsel is straightforward: litigate the Second Amendment question early; develop a careful factual record showing whether the defendant’s prior offenses involved violence, weapons, or dangerousness; preserve evidentiary rulings and factual findings that bear on the textual and historical inquiries; and plan to marshal historical materials or expert testimony if the case proceeds to briefing and appeal. Failure to do so risks leaving a constitutional argument underdeveloped or forfeited — a recurring feature of routine felon‑in‑possession appeals, including the illustrative Eighth Circuit opinion that opened this chapter, where the defendant did not press a Second Amendment claim and faced a heavy factual record of violent past convictions and dangerous conduct at the offense.

This is a rapidly evolving area of law. Bruen and Rahimi supply the analytical framework, but the resolution of many § 922(g)(1) claims will turn on the facts of prior convictions and the availability (or absence) of historical analogues. Savvy practitioners will prepare both the doctrinal and factual scaffolding necessary to make those showings.

# Proving Possession: Intrinsic Evidence vs. Rule 404(b) Propensity

Possession cases frequently turn on circumstantial proof: the government must show the defendant exercised dominion and control over a weapon. Because possession is often proved by weaving together a sequence of events and observations, courts must decide whether evidence of prior acts—earlier sightings of a weapon, prior statements about the weapon, prior possession of similar guns—is intrinsic to the charged offense (and therefore admissible to tell the story of the offense) or whether it is extrinsic propensity evidence governed by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank (No. 24-1225 (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025)) provides a concise example of how the intrinsic-evidence doctrine operates in a felon-in-possession prosecution involving a sawed-off rifle and illustrates the interplay among Rules 404(b), 403, and 105, and the Huddleston standard for admissibility.

This chapter explains the doctrinal lines courts draw, the policy reasons behind those lines, and practical rules students and practitioners can apply when litigating possession cases. We begin with the intrinsic-evidence doctrine—its forms and rationales—then review Rule 404(b) and the Huddleston framework. We then examine Eighth Circuit precedent that treats prior possession of the same firearm as intrinsic (United States v. Battle) and analyze how the Cutbank court applied these principles. We conclude with discussion of continuous-possession theories, the continuing role of Rule 403 balancing even for intrinsic evidence, and the use of Rule 105 limiting instructions.

## Intrinsic evidence doctrine: completing the story, same transaction/series, continuous possession

The intrinsic-evidence doctrine rests on a simple premise: not all prior acts are offered to show a defendant’s criminal character or propensity. Some evidence of other acts is admitted because it is part of the same factual narrative that explains the charged offense. Courts describe intrinsic evidence in a variety of ways—evidence “inextricably intertwined” with the charged offense, evidence that “completes the story,” or evidence that is part of the “same transaction or series of transactions.” When the evidence is intrinsic, it is not governed by Rule 404(b) and ordinarily is admissible if relevant and not barred by other rules (e.g., Rule 403).

Possession cases illustrate each of these rationales. Suppose an eyewitness testifies that she saw the defendant carrying a particular rifle “a handful of times” at the house in question before the night of the charged offense. That testimony helps establish an uninterrupted course of possession and tends to prove ownership or control of the weapon when it is found later. Such testimony is part of the narrative that connects the earlier sightings to the charged possession and is therefore intrinsic. Courts will similarly treat as intrinsic earlier conduct that establishes the context in which the charged offense occurred (for example, the defendant taking a gun from a closet earlier in the evening and returning later), because excluding that evidence would leave an incomplete and misleading account of the charged events.

Cutbank typifies this approach. The defendant moved to exclude testimony that two witnesses had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 on prior occasions, arguing the testimony was propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The government and the district court treated this testimony as intrinsic—evidence relevant to proving possession of the same weapon by demonstrating ownership or control and continuous possession. On appeal the Eighth Circuit agreed, citing United States v. Battle for the proposition that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” Cutbank’s witnesses testified that they had seen the same sawed-off rifle with the defendant before the incident; admitting that testimony completed the story of how the defendant came to possess the weapon on the date charged.

Two points follow for practice. First, timing and identity often determine whether prior acts are intrinsic. Evidence of conduct that is temporally and factually connected to the charged offense is more likely to be intrinsic. Second, evidence is more persuasive as intrinsic when the earlier act involves the very instrumentality of the charged offense—a uniquely identifiable firearm, a distinctive piece of clothing, or a singular modus operandi—because such evidence helps establish identity, ownership, or continuity of conduct rather than merely a general propensity to commit wrongdoing.

## Rule 404(b) basics: permitted non-propensity uses; Huddleston admissibility framework

Rule 404(b) prohibits using evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a defendant’s character or propensity to act in conformity with that character. But Rule 404(b) is not an exclusionary rule that bars all other-act evidence. The rule explicitly contemplates admissibility for other purposes—intent, knowledge, absence of mistake, identity, motive, opportunity, preparation, plan, or when the act is part of a common scheme or plan. The key inquiry under Rule 404(b) is the purpose for which the evidence is offered. If the proponent offers the evidence for a permissible, non-propensity purpose and satisfies other admissibility requirements, Rule 404(b) does not bar it.

Huddleston v. United States (485 U.S. 681 (1988)) supplies the admissibility framework courts apply when an item of prior-act evidence is extrinsic and thus potentially within Rule 404(b). Huddleston articulated two principal requirements before such evidence may be admitted: (1) the government must produce sufficient evidence from which a reasonable juror could conclude that the defendant committed the prior act; and (2) the evidence must be relevant to a material issue other than the defendant’s character, and its probative value must not be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice under Rule 403. The Huddleston standard is deferential to the trial judge’s role in assessing the factual foundation for the jury’s consideration of the prior act; the judge need not make explicit findings that the prior act occurred beyond a reasonable doubt or by clear and convincing evidence—the question is whether a reasonable jury could so find.

Huddleston also implicates the court’s gatekeeping role: when the prior act is sufficiently probative of a permissible issue and sufficiently supported by evidence, the jury may be allowed to consider it, with appropriate limiting instructions if necessary. The Eighth Circuit applies the Huddleston test (and its progeny) when a district court treats evidence as extrinsic, but courts often avoid the Huddleston analysis entirely by treating the evidence as intrinsic and therefore outside Rule 404(b)’s reach.

In Cutbank, the district court and the Eighth Circuit avoided Huddleston because the disputed testimony was intrinsic: prior eyewitness sightings of the same sawed-off rifle were directly relevant to establishing possession and control of the firearm at the time of the charged offense. Had the prior sightings involved a different gun or unrelated bad acts, Huddleston would have required the government to introduce sufficient evidence that the defendant actually committed the prior act and to show a valid non-propensity purpose for that evidence.

## Eighth Circuit precedent: prior possession of the same firearm as intrinsic evidence (United States v. Battle)

The Eighth Circuit has long recognized that prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic in prosecutions for possession. In United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), the court observed that prior possession of a specific firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control. Battle thus stands for the proposition that testimony that a witness saw the defendant with the same gun on prior occasions is not the sort of character evidence Rule 404(b) seeks to exclude; rather, it is part of the evidentiary thread that connects the defendant to the gun.

The Battle rule is not limitless. It matters that the prior possession the government offers points to the very instrumentality at issue and not merely to a defendant’s general familiarity with weapons. The Eighth Circuit’s focus on identity and continuity explains why Battle is frequently cited to uphold the admission of prior sightings, DNA or fingerprint matches to the same weapon, unique serial number alterations, or other proof that links the earlier act to the instrumentality seized in the charged offense.

Cutbank relied heavily on Battle. The witnesses testified they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off Marlin “a handful of times” before the night in question; furthermore, the rifle later recovered had DNA matching Cutbank and distinctive physical alterations (sawed-off barrel and stock). These facts squarely fit Battle’s rationale: the prior sightings were relevant to establishing ownership/control of the very weapon at issue and thus were intrinsic evidence probative of the possession charge.

Students should note how the Battle line compares to other circuits and contexts. When the prior act consists of possession of a different firearm (or other criminal conduct unrelated in time, place, or method), courts are more likely to treat it as extrinsic and require the Huddleston showing and a clear non-propensity purpose. Conversely, when the prior act is tied to the same weapon with identifying features or corroborating physical evidence, Battle and its progeny permit admission as part of the story.

## Continuous possession as a theory to link earlier sightings to the charged offense

Continuous-possession is a concept courts invoke when earlier observations of a weapon are not isolated incidents but part of an uninterrupted course of possession that culminates in the later, charged possession. Demonstrating continuous possession allows the government to bridge temporal gaps between earlier sightings and the moment the government seized the firearm. Courts consider several factors in assessing whether earlier possession supports an inference of continuity: the exclusivity of access or control (did the defendant have exclusive or primary control over the premises or the object?), temporal proximity (how long between the earlier sighting and the seizure?), the circumstances of the earlier sightings (were they occasional glimpses or consistent custody?), corroborating physical evidence (DNA, fingerprints, serial-number alterations that uniquely identify the gun), and witness credibility.

Cutbank exemplified a strong continuous-possession case. Witnesses recounted seeing Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle multiple times at the residence. The rifle later recovered on a path between two relevant locations had DNA matching Cutbank and physical alterations consistent with the witnesses’ descriptions. Those facts permitted the jury (and the district court, in ruling on admissibility) to infer that the earlier sightings were not random or innocuous but were evidence of a continuing custody or control relationship with the firearm culminating in the charged possession. The Eighth Circuit accepted that continuity theory without requiring the government to prove each intervening moment of control; jury inferences from the cumulative evidence were sufficient.

But not every case of earlier sighting supports a continuous-possession inference. Suppose a witness says she saw the defendant with a rifle once years ago at a public shooting range and the defendant later is arrested with a different rifle in another state. The temporal distance, lack of exclusive control, and absence of identifying marks rebut the continuity inference; in such circumstances courts are likely to treat the earlier sighting as extrinsic evidence requiring Rule 404(b) analysis or may exclude it under Rule 403 if its probative value is slight and unfair prejudice high.

Practitioners should therefore calibrate proof of continuity: present facts that make a continuous-possession inference reasonable—repeated sightings in a particular location, exclusive access to the storage area, unique weapon characteristics, contemporaneous statements by the defendant indicating ongoing control—and be prepared to argue why the evidence is intrinsic rather than 404(b) evidence.

## Rule 403 balancing overlay even for intrinsic evidence; limiting instructions under Rule 105

Even when evidence is intrinsic, admissibility is not automatic. Federal Rule of Evidence 403 authorizes exclusion of relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. Judges are accorded broad discretion in applying Rule 403, and appellate courts will reverse only for abuse of that discretion. The Eighth Circuit reiterated this point in Cutbank: although the district court correctly treated prior sightings of the gun as intrinsic, the court still has the power under Rule 403 to exclude evidence if unfair prejudice would substantially outweigh probative value. Cutbank failed to show such prejudice.

Two lessons emerge. First, intrinsic evidence is often highly probative—evidence that completes the story of possession typically helps the jury evaluate identity, control, and intent—and so Rule 403 exclusion is less common. But circumstances may reduce probative value or increase unfair prejudice: for example, the prior act involved violence toward a different victim or a long-ago offense that would shift the jury’s focus to character. In those cases, the court should engage in a Rule 403 balancing on the record.

Second, Rule 105 provides a remedial measure when the danger is not exclusion but potential misuse by the jury. Rule 105 directs that when evidence is admissible for one purpose but not another, the court, upon request, must restrict the evidence to its proper scope and instruct the jury accordingly. Limiting instructions help the jury understand that they may consider the evidence only for a non-propensity purpose (e.g., ownership, identity, intent) and not as proof of general bad character. In Cutbank, the defense requested preemptive limiting instructions regarding hearsay and use of the witness’s statements; the district court declined to give an instruction at the time of admission but later provided the customary instruction at the close of trial, and the Eighth Circuit found no abuse of discretion. That decision underscores that courts need not provide a contemporaneous limiting instruction every time evidence is admitted; a clear instruction at the close of evidence can suffice, so long as the jury is adequately informed and the record demonstrates the court considered whether a limiting instruction was necessary.

A practical point for litigators: if you fear jury misuse, request a limiting instruction in writing and put the need for it on the record when the evidence is offered. If you want contemporaneous limiting language to prevent an immediate prejudicial impression, make that clear and explain why a later instruction would be insufficient. Judges have discretion, but advancing the argument effectively increases the chance of receiving a timely instruction.

## Authentication, impeachment, and trial mechanics: Cumbie, confrontation, and harmless error

Two additional evidentiary themes arise from Cutbank and related precedent. First, authentication matters. When a party seeks to impeach with extrinsic evidence (for example, reading a police report to impeach a witness), the extrinsic evidence must be authenticated; the rule requires an adequate foundation to admit a document as a true representation of what it purports to be. In Cutbank the district court sustained government objections to defense counsel reading portions of a police report into the record absent authentication because the report was not authored by the witness. The Eighth Circuit approved that ruling, citing United States v. Cumbie for the proposition that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated. Practitioners should therefore prepare authentication witnesses or other indicia of reliability (e.g., business-records foundation, stipulation) if impeachment will rely on reports or documents not authored by the witness.

Second, Confrontation Clause and hearsay issues are often intertwined with admissibility rulings. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied if the declarant appears at trial and testifies in person, because the defendant has the opportunity to cross-examine (see United States v. Charboneau). In Cutbank, the government’s admission of statements to police did not present a Confrontation Clause problem because the declarant (D.F.) testified. Still, defense counsel may assert both hearsay and confrontation objections to preempt potential confusion; if the declarant later testifies, the constitutional objection will generally be mooted, though hearsay form and limiting instruction issues may remain.

Finally, harmless-error review is the appellate backdrop to many evidentiary challenges. Even if a district court errs in admitting or excluding evidence, appellate reversal requires a showing that the error was not harmless—specifically, that the improperly admitted evidence may have substantially swayed the jury. The Eighth Circuit applied this standard in Cutbank and related cases (e.g., DeMarce), finding that the substantial physical and testimonial evidence—DNA on the rifle, witnesses’ identifications, the defendant’s conduct—rendered any potential error harmless. Thus, when you litigate evidentiary motions, think not only about admissibility but about the record that will support a harmless-error argument on appeal or rebut the government’s harmless-error claim.

## Practical summary and application

The Cutbank decision furnishes a compact blueprint for resolving disputes over prior-act evidence in possession cases:

- Identify whether the prior act concerns the identical instrumentality or is part of the same factual narrative. If so, argue intrinsic evidence and avoid Huddleston and 404(b) burdens; cite Battle and Cutbank for the proposition that prior possession of the same firearm tends to establish ownership and control.

- If the prior act is not the same instrumentality or is remote in time/place, prepare to defend the evidence under Rule 404(b) and Huddleston: establish a non-propensity purpose (e.g., identity, intent, absence of mistake), proffer sufficient evidence that the prior act occurred, and be ready for a Rule 403 balancing.

- Even with intrinsic evidence, ask the court to make a Rule 403 on-the-record assessment when unfair prejudice is plausibly high, and request a limiting instruction under Rule 105 when there is a risk the jury will treat the evidence as showing propensity.

- For impeachment or extrinsic-document use, ensure authentication and foundation; courts will exclude unauthenticated reports or admit them only for limited purposes.

- Preserve objections properly, but also be mindful that evidentiary errors that do not materially affect the verdict are unlikely to warrant reversal if the remaining evidence is substantial.

By distinguishing intrinsic evidence that “completes the story” from extrinsic, propensity evidence, and by applying Huddleston and Rule 403 thoughtfully, advocates and judges can better resolve close questions about what the jury should hear in possession prosecutions. Cutbank demonstrates that when the government’s narrative links repeated sightings of the same, identifiable weapon to the defendant—corroborated by physical evidence—courts will admit prior sightings as intrinsic evidence essential to proving possession.

# Hearsay, Non-Hearsay Uses, and the Confrontation Clause in Police-Response Testimony

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank furnishes a compact springboard into recurring trial problems: when may officers recount what a complaining witness or a 911 caller said without running afoul of the hearsay rule, and when does the Confrontation Clause require that out‑of‑court declarants be subject to cross‑examination before the jury may consider their prior statements for substantive truth? Cutbank also illustrates the trial‑management question of sequencing testimony and the district court’s latitude to delay a limiting instruction until the close of trial. This chapter explains the governing rules and tests, shows how they operate in practice, and highlights preservation and appellate‑review considerations that lawyers should not overlook.

## Defining hearsay and non‑hearsay purposes (effect on listener, background/context)

The basic starting point is Rule 801 of the Federal Rules of Evidence: hearsay is an out‑of‑court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. If an out‑of‑court utterance is offered for some other purpose — for example, to show why a listener acted as he did, to explain the circumstances confronting a witness or officer, or to show the declarant’s state of mind — it is not hearsay and is not excluded by Rule 802. This distinction is simple in principle but often difficult in practice: the same words can be offered either for their truth or for their effect on the listener.

Two common non‑hearsay uses arise frequently in police‑response testimony. First, officers may recount statements to explain the officers’ subsequent conduct: a 911 caller’s report that “he has a gun” explains why officers approached with weapons drawn. The purpose of this testimony is not to prove that the accused in fact possessed a gun, but to show the basis for the officers’ perception and decision‑making. Courts recognize this as a classic non‑hearsay purpose — the statement’s effect on the listener — and therefore admissible for that limited purpose. Second, out‑of‑court statements may be offered to provide background or context for a sequence of events so that the jury can understand testimony that otherwise would be a bare series of actions without explanation. Background/context testimony is treated similarly: admissible when used to explain why parties or officers did what they did, not to establish the truth of the assertions in those statements.

Both categories remain subject to the general constraints on admissibility. If the government intends to rely on the contents of the statement for its truth, it must either fit the statement within a hearsay exception or be prepared to show that the statement is non‑testimonial for Confrontation Clause purposes (discussed below). A trial court also retains its Rule 403 power to exclude otherwise admissible non‑hearsay statements if their probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or undue consumption of time. In practice, however, courts are generally tolerant of police testimony recounting statements for the explanatory purposes described above, provided the judge and counsel take steps — e.g., limiting instructions or redact-and-proffer approaches — to avoid juror confusion about the permissible uses of those statements.

Cutbank illustrates these principles. The defendant urged before trial that the witnesses’ testimony that they had seen him with the sawed‑off Marlin rifle before the charged night was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court (and the Eighth Circuit on appeal) treated prior possession testimony as intrinsic: prior observations that a defendant possessed the same weapon are directly relevant to proving contemporaneous possession or control of that weapon. Likewise, when officers testified about what they were told or found in responding to D.F.’s call, the court viewed that evidence as explaining officer actions and knowledge at the time, not as improper hearsay offered to prove the truth of contested facts.

## Crawford and Davis: testimonial versus non‑testimonial statements; the primary‑purpose test

While the hearsay doctrine governs admissibility in general, the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause imposes a constitutional limit when the government seeks to introduce an out‑of‑court statement that is “testimonial” and the declarant does not appear for cross‑examination. Crawford v. Washington established that testimonial hearsay is categorically barred unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination. But Crawford left open the definition of “testimonial.” The Supreme Court in Davis v. Washington and related cases (often grouped with Hammon and subsequent clarifications) developed the critical “primary‑purpose” test. Under Davis, statements are non‑testimonial when made in the course of police interrogation under circumstances objectively indicating that the primary purpose was to enable police assistance to meet an ongoing emergency. By contrast, statements are more likely testimonial when the circumstances indicate that the primary purpose was to produce evidence for prosecution — for example, formal statements to identify or blame a perpetrator after the emergency has ended.

Applying the Davis primary‑purpose test requires a practical, context‑sensitive inquiry — a fact‑driven assessment of whether the declarant’s statements and the questioner’s conduct were aimed principally at resolving an ongoing threat or principally at investigating past wrongdoing. Questions about timing (ongoing emergency vs. past event), formality (casual exclamation vs. structured interview), and audience (911 operator or police arriving at a scene vs. detective conducting a crime‑scene interview hours later) are central to the analysis.

Several consequences follow. First, if a statement is non‑testimonial under Davis, Crawford’s confrontation rule does not apply and the hearsay issue is controlled solely by the evidentiary rules. Thus, an officer may recount a 911 call that reports an active shooting to explain why officers acted as they did without offending Crawford if the call was made to meet an ongoing emergency. Second, where the primary purpose was investigative — a call made after the danger passes, or a sit‑down interview designed to collect evidence — the statement is testimonial; the Confrontation Clause bars its admission unless the declarant testifies or a Crawford exception applies.

Cutbank did not require a full Davis analysis because D.F., the declarant of the initial complaint, testified at trial. But the opinion highlights the usual pathway: the government argued the officers’ testimony was admissible to explain their actions and knowledge, and the district court admitted such testimony without contemporaneous limiting instructions. Because D.F. later testified, the Confrontation issue was not implicated in the Crawford sense.

## Confrontation satisfied when the declarant appears for cross‑examination (e.g., Charboneau)

The Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant “actually appear[s] in court and testify[s] in person,” so that the defendant has the opportunity to confront and cross‑examine the declarant. This is the practical implication of Crawford: the requirement of cross‑examination is met by trial testimony. The Eighth Circuit’s application of this principle in United States v. Charboneau (cited in Cutbank) reiterates the straightforward proposition that if the government calls the declarant and subjects her to cross‑examination, any prior out‑of‑court statements she made may be admitted subject to the ordinary hearsay rules and any applicable exceptions, and the constitutional objection evaporates.

That is why many prosecutions proceed by calling the complainant or 911 caller to testify. When the declarant testifies, the defense gets the constitutionally mandated opportunity to cross‑examine, and the risk that the jury will treat an out‑of‑court statement as the substituted evidence against the defendant is reduced. Courts, however, remain attentive to whether the government attempts to use the declarant’s prior statements as bootstrapping evidence before the declarant has been placed on the stand. If the declarant never testifies, the Davis primary‑purpose test and Crawford control the admissibility of the out‑of‑court statement.

In Cutbank the district court admitted officers’ testimony about the initial complaints and investigatory steps; the court later received D.F.’s testimony. On appeal the Eighth Circuit pointed to Charboneau for the proposition that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when a hearsay declarant later testifies in court, and found no abuse of discretion by the district court in admitting the officers’ testimony without giving the defendant’s requested contemporaneous limiting instruction.

## Managing trial flow: whether to preclude early reliance on declarant statements before the declarant testifies

Defense counsel often seek to prevent the jury from hearing a declarant’s out‑of‑court statements until after the declarant has testified, because jurors are naturally inclined to treat dramatic statements as evidence of the defendant’s guilt even when the court later instructs them on limiting purposes. On these grounds a defendant may move for an order that (1) limits the prosecution’s use of the declarant’s out‑of‑court statements until the declarant has testified, or (2) requires contemporaneous limiting instructions each time such a statement is admitted.

Trial judges generally have wide discretion in managing evidence and jury instructions. The Eighth Circuit in United States v. Bennett (quoted in Cutbank) held that a district court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time it is admitted if it provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial. That means a court may admit police testimony recounting out‑of‑court statements without an immediate limiting instruction, and give a single, comprehensive limiting instruction at the close of evidence, so long as that instruction adequately explains the limited purpose for which the statements were admitted.

But district courts must also be sensitive to practical realities. Judges balancing the concerns will consider the nature of the testimony, its potential for prejudice if heard before the declarant testifies, and the chance of confusion if jurors are later told to disregard the earlier evidence for substantive purposes. If the government intends to use the prior statement substantively before the declarant has been called, a court may be more likely to require a limiting instruction or even to restrict the timing of admission. Conversely, if the testimonial risk has been eliminated by the declarant’s appearance on the stand, the court’s discretion to manage sequencing is broad.

Cutbank demonstrates the common compromise. The district court admitted police testimony explaining their response to the complaint and denied the defendant’s pre‑trial request for a rigid instruction that the jury not consider D.F.’s statements as substantive until D.F. testified. The court instead allowed the evidence in context and provided — or could provide — the customary end‑of‑trial limiting instructions. Because D.F. did testify and because the court did not admit hearsay as such, the Circuit found no abuse of discretion in the trial court’s management of the trial flow.

Practical advice to defense counsel: To preserve any appellate claim, contemporaneously object and request the relief you want (a contemporaneous limiting instruction or exclusion pending the declarant’s testimony). If the court declines, make sure the record reflects the nature of the anticipated prejudice and the alternative remedy sought; a general book‑and‑claim on appeal will be less persuasive.

## Authentication, impeachment, and the use of police reports

When cross‑examining a witness about prior statements to police, trial counsel will sometimes attempt to read portions of a police report into the record or to admit the report as extrinsic impeachment evidence. This may be improper absent foundation and authentication: extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible to impeach, but it must be properly authenticated as the witness’s own statement or as accurately reporting her words. Rule 613 governs prior inconsistent statements and the procedures for introducing such impeachment evidence; Rule 901 requires authentication of extrinsic documents.

The Eighth Circuit’s recent decisions, cited in Cutbank, reinforce that district courts may require the proponent to lay a foundation before allowing portions of a police report to be read to the jury. Where the report is not authored by the witness, the defense must either call the report’s author or otherwise show that the portion accurately records the witness’s statements. United States v. Cumbie is a helpful modern example: the court upheld a district court’s requirement that prior statements used for impeachment be authenticated. Failure to do so enables the judge to bar the exhibit or the reading of the report as a proper exercise of discretion.

In Cutbank the trial court allowed the defense to question C.F. about her statements to police but sustained objections to defense counsel reading parts of the police report into evidence without foundation. The Circuit held that ruling was not an abuse of discretion.

## Appellate review of limiting‑instruction decisions (abuse of discretion; adequacy of end‑of‑trial instructions)

When a defendant challenges a district court’s refusal to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction or its decision to give only a later instruction, appellate courts apply an abuse‑of‑discretion standard to the district court’s evidentiary and instructional choices. The Eighth Circuit’s decisions — including Bennett and the Cutbank opinion — emphasize that a court need not provide an immediate instruction for each admission of evidence; it is sufficient that the court’s overall instructions adequately inform the jury of the permissible uses of the testimony.

If the defendant fails to preserve the issue, appellate courts may review for plain error. But when the issue was preserved, a defendant still faces the perennial appellate hurdle: reversal requires more than a mere error of judgment. An erroneous evidentiary ruling will lead to reversal only when the appellant shows that the error was not harmless — that is, that the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence. As the Eighth Circuit explained in DeMarce and reiterated in Cutbank, the inquiry focuses on whether the admitted evidence “influenced or had more than a slight influence on the verdict.” The cumulative‑error doctrine adds that reversal is required only when the combined effect of multiple errors produced substantial prejudice. United States v. Keys, cited in Cutbank, articulates that approach.

In practical terms, the defendant’s best appellate argument is to show how the timing or content of the police‑response testimony made later limiting instructions ineffective. Examples that courts have found persuasive include: (1) when the testimony was particularly inflammatory and admitted early so that jurors could not reasonably be expected to forget it; (2) when the government repeatedly returned to the inadmissible content in closing; or (3) when the declarant did not testify and a testimonial statement was admitted without satisfying Crawford/Davis. Merely saying “I would have preferred an earlier instruction” rarely suffices.

Cutbank’s outcome reflects these standards. The Circuit found no abuse of discretion because (1) the record did not show that hearsay was improperly admitted during trial, (2) D.F., the declarant, testified in person (thus satisfying the Confrontation Clause), and (3) the government’s overall case was strong enough that any arguable instructional timing issue was harmless.

## Practical litigation and preservation strategies

The law described above presents both opportunities and pitfalls for advocating counsel:

- If you represent the defense and you anticipate police testimony recounting out‑of‑court statements, make a pretrial proffer explaining the context and explain to the judge why an immediate limiting instruction is necessary to avoid prejudice. Request contemporaneous limiting instructions, and if the court declines, make a timely objection on hearsay and Confrontation Clause grounds so the record is clear for appeal.

- If you represent the government, be prepared to articulate the non‑hearsay purpose of the testimony at the time it is offered: explain whether it is being offered to show the effect on the listener, background/context for officer conduct, or as a party‑opponent admission. Where a statement might be testimonial under Davis, consider calling the declarant to the stand to obviate Crawford concerns.

- Authentication issues: when you intend to impeach with a police report or other extrinsic written record, be ready to lay the foundation by showing the report accurately recorded the witness’s statement or by producing the author. The absence of authentication can prevent the use of impeachment documents and may disrupt defense cross‑examination plans.

- Closing and limiting instructions: request a specific jury instruction that explains both the limited purpose for which the jury may consider the declarant’s statements and that the jury cannot treat those statements as independent proof of the defendant’s guilt unless the declarant testifies and is subject to cross‑examination. If the court declines a contemporaneous instruction, make sure the jury receives a clear end‑of‑trial limiting instruction.

- Preservation: contemporaneous objections and clear alternative requests (e.g., exclusion of the statement, redaction, instruction, or sequestration until the declarant testifies) are essential to preserve issues for appeal.

## Conclusion

Police testimony about what victims or callers told them sits at the intersection of the hearsay doctrine and the Confrontation Clause. The modern framework requires judges to identify the purpose for which such testimony is offered, to analyze whether the statements are testimonial under the Crawford/Davis primary‑purpose test, and to manage the timing and instructions so the jury understands permissible uses. Cutbank typifies the routine outcome: officers may describe callers’ statements to explain their actions; if the declarant later testifies at trial, the Confrontation concern is generally resolved; and a district court’s decision to withhold a contemporaneous limiting instruction and instead give customary end‑of‑trial instructions will not be reversed absent abuse of discretion and a showing of prejudice. The practical lesson for advocates is straightforward — think ahead about sequencing, preserve objections, and be prepared to make the record about testimonial character and the necessity of limiting instructions.

# Rule 403 and the ‘Opening the Door’: Context, Emotion, and Prejudice

The interplay between Rule 403 and the doctrinal principle commonly described as “opening the door” is among the most fact‑sensitive and frequently litigated matters that arise at trial. Rule 403 requires exclusion of relevant evidence only when its probative value is substantially outweighed by dangers such as unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury. “Opening the door” is not a free‑standing evidentiary rule; it is a fairness principle that permits a party to introduce otherwise marginal or inadmissible evidence to correct a misleading impression created by the opposing party’s conduct. Together, these doctrines govern how courts manage evidence that is emotionally charged or that tends to evoke sympathy, outrage, or other reactions that may distract a jury from its factfinding function.

United States v. Cutbank (the opinion excerpted above) provides a useful case study. There, during cross‑examination about inconsistent statements, the victim D.F. testified that she did not like guns because her sister “was killed by a gun.” The defense moved to strike that testimony under Rule 403; the district court denied the motion and allowed the testimony to remain. On appeal the Eighth Circuit upheld the ruling, citing the responsive nature of the testimony and observing no unfair prejudice or confusion. The opinion illustrates how Rule 403 balancing, the doctrine of responsive or rehabilitative testimony, and the opening‑the‑door principle operate in practice. The remainder of this chapter explains the governing legal framework, shows how courts apply it to emotionally charged testimony, and describes the practical tools trial courts and litigants may use to protect the trial’s fairness.

## Rule 403: unfair prejudice, confusion, waste of time vs. probative value

Federal Rule of Evidence 403 embodies a commonsense gatekeeping function: even relevant evidence may be excluded when its probative value is substantially outweighed by the risk that it will unfairly prejudice the jury, confuse the issues, cause undue delay, waste time, or needlessly present cumulative evidence. “Unfair prejudice” does not mean merely that the evidence hurts the opponent’s case; litigation routinely involves evidence harmful to one side. Rather, Rule 403 targets evidence that invites additional considerations or responses—emotional reaction, moral condemnation, or an irresistible but irrelevant inference—that are likely to materially distort jury decisionmaking.

The balancing under Rule 403 is context specific. The court first assesses probative value: how directly the evidence bears on a disputed fact or the credibility of a witness; whether it is the best or only means of presenting a legitimately contested point; and whether it is cumulative of other admissible proof. Next, the court evaluates the specific prejudicial dangers: will the evidence evoke irrational emotional responses (e.g., gruesome photographs or accounts of family tragedy) that are not tied to the legal elements; will it cause confusion by introducing collateral issues the jury must resolve; will it lead to undue consumption of time or multiplicity of trials on collateral topics? The judge must then decide whether the probative value is “substantially outweighed” by those dangers. That standard requires a qualitative judgment: if the risk of distortion is high and the probative value modest, exclusion is appropriate; but if the evidence is highly probative on a matter central to the prosecution or defense, Rule 403’s bar will rarely be met.

Applying these principles to emotional testimony requires particular care. Statements describing a loved one’s violent death can be powerfully evocative, and courts must guard against letting sentiment supplant reasoned factfinding. But context matters. When the emotional fact is directly relevant to an issue properly before the jury—credibility, motive, state of mind, or the sequence of events—its probative value can be high. In Cutbank, the victim’s explanation that she disliked guns because her sister had been shot was offered as a reason for earlier inconsistent statements about whether she had seen the gun that night. That explanation went to a classic credibility issue—why the witness had earlier said she did not recall seeing the gun—so its probative value was substantial. The Eighth Circuit concluded that the district court reasonably exercised its Rule 403 discretion in admitting the evidence, because the probative value of a responsiveness explanation outweighed any speculative risk of unfair prejudice.

Several practical points follow. First, courts should scrutinize whether the emotional fact is truly necessary to explain or rehabilitate a witness and whether a less inflammatory explanation suffices. Second, courts must focus on the precise role of the evidence: is it being used to prove an element of the offense, to impeach or rehabilitate a witness, or simply to inflame the jury? Third, Rule 403 is forward looking: trial judges should consider not only the immediate impact of the evidence but also the additional proof or collateral mini‑litigations it may invite.

## Opening the door: fairness after impeachment and contextual rehabilitation

The phrase “opening the door” describes the equitable rationale that a party who invites a misleading impression cannot complain if the opponent corrects that impression with responsive evidence. The doctrine is rooted in the general principle of fairness: a litigant cannot use a slice of the truth to convince the factfinder while foreclosing the other side from providing the missing context that would prevent mischief or misunderstanding.

A common scenario arises in cross‑examination. When defense counsel questions a witness at length about a prior inconsistent statement, or elicits testimony that suggests the witness was confused, reluctant, or biased, the witness may respond on redirect or re‑directing questioning that the apparent inconsistency was attributable to trauma, fear, or some emotionally salient explanation. Such testimony is often admissible for rehabilitation. Likewise, when one side makes a pointed denial or assertion that would mislead a jury absent context, the other side may properly introduce evidence that fills out the narrative.

The Cutbank opinion exemplifies this dynamic. Defense counsel repeatedly questioned D.F. about prior inconsistent statements concerning the gun—eliciting that she at times said she did not remember seeing the gun. D.F.’s explanation that she was unfamiliar with guns and that her sister had been killed by a gun was directly responsive. Under the fairness principle, it was admissible as rehabilitation—providing the jury with the circumstance that plausibly explained prior evasive or inconsistent remarks. The district court therefore did not abuse its discretion in allowing the testimony to stand.

Important limits apply. Opening the door does not license wholesale admission of otherwise inadmissible evidence. The response must be reasonably necessary to correct a demonstrably misleading impression or directly responsive to the matters opened. Courts must be attentive to scope: the corrective evidence should not expand into collateral matters that create new prejudice or trial by emotion. Moreover, the responding party must ordinarily prove what is necessary to authenticate the corrective evidence; the doctrine does not render unimpeachably true every assertion offered in redirect.

In practice, then, trial counsel must think strategically. If a party wants to avoid “opening the door” to a highly prejudicial explanation, it should avoid asking questions that elicit partial truths or that invite the witness to offer an emotionally laden explanation on redirect. Conversely, a party that recognizes the opponent has created a misleading impression should be prepared to seek admission of corrective evidence and to argue that exclusion would itself be unfair.

## Managing emotional or victim‑impact‑adjacent facts during the guilt phase

Not all emotionally charged evidence is inadmissible, but its management during the guilt phase requires extra vigilance because the jury must decide guilt from facts untethered to sentencing considerations. Courts must walk a narrow path: protecting the defendant’s right to a fair trial while ensuring the jury has the facts necessary to assess credibility and motive.

First, the court must identify the precise purpose for which the evidence is offered. Evidence that directly explains a witness’s state of mind or a failure to recall an event often has legitimate probative value for credibility. In Cutbank, the victim’s statement about her sister’s death explained why she “did not like guns,” which in turn explained apparent prior inconsistent statements about the gun. That explanation was relevant and not a mere expression of sympathy.

Second, the trial judge should consider limiting the manner of presentation. If a witness’s statement is likely to produce a strong emotional reaction, the court can permit a concise, neutral statement rather than a graphic or narrative account. Courts can also limit testimony to the fact of the event (e.g., “my sister was killed by a gun”) without allowing extensive storytelling about the scene, which risks sympathy rather than elucidation.

Third, judges should use contemporaneous safeguards: clear limiting instructions that define the permissible use of the testimony, careful control of re‑examination to prevent cumulative or repetitive emotional appeals, and, where appropriate, exclusion under Rule 403 of inflammatory detail that is not necessary to the legitimate evidentiary purpose. The court should explain to the jury, in a curative or limiting instruction, that the testimony bears only on the witness’s credibility or state of mind and is not to be used as evidence of the defendant’s propensity for violence.

Fourth, counsel can address emotional facts with pretrial motions in limine. When a defense anticipates that a witness may mention family tragedy in explaining inconsistency or bias, the defense may seek a pretrial ruling that confines the witness’s testimony to narrowly framed rehabilitative statements or, alternately, that the government must notify the court before presenting such testimony to allow an immediate ruling and a curative instruction.

Finally, witness preparation matters. The party calling a potentially emotional witness should prepare the witness to give concise, non‑narrative responses on sensitive topics and to avoid gratuitous detail. Defense counsel should train witnesses about the availability and purpose of limiting instructions and how to respond to cross‑examination without needlessly invoking emotional circumstances.

## Remedies and safeguards: striking, curative instructions, scope limitations

Even with careful pretrial planning, emotionally charged testimony may arise unexpectedly. Several remedial tools are available to trial courts to neutralize unfair prejudice without discarding probative evidence.

One immediate remedy is striking the testimony from the record and instructing the jury to disregard it. This remedy is effective when the objection is timely and the testimony is not central to the jury’s deliberations. The efficacy of a strike and instruction depends on the nature of the testimony and the jury’s likely ability to follow directions: jurors are presumed to follow limiting instructions, but when testimony is particularly inflammatory, a judge may deem the curative instruction insufficient.

A milder but frequently used remedy is a limiting instruction that tells the jury the specific purpose for which the evidence may be considered—e.g., for assessing the witness’s credibility or explaining inconsistency—and that it must not be used as proof of the defendant’s character or propensity. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank noted that the district court’s decision to let the brief testimony stand and to rely on cross‑examination and the record was reasonable; no striking or special curative instruction was necessary because the probative purpose was clear and the evidence was not demonstrably prejudicial.

Courts may also narrow the permitted scope of testimony. A court can allow a witness to say, in neutral terms, “I am not comfortable with guns because of a family death,” without allowing graphic or detailed descriptions. Another tactic is to permit only written, redacted statements to be read into the record, thereby limiting theatrics or elaboration. When testimony invites collateral mini‑litigation, the judge can exclude extraneous detail and focus only on the point necessary to explain the prior inconsistency.

If a litigant believes that the district court’s balancing under Rule 403 was erroneous, the appellate standard is highly deferential: reversal requires a showing of abuse of discretion and that the error was not harmless. The appellate court asks whether the challenged ruling “substantially swayed” the jury. Where, as in Cutbank, the government’s proof is substantial and the contested testimony is only one among many items of proof, courts frequently find any error harmless.

Finally, courts should be alert to cumulative prejudice. A single brief matter may be harmless, but repeated inflammatory incursions might so taint the record as to require reversal. Therefore, trial judges must consider both the discrete incident and its cumulative effect on the fairness of the proceedings.

## Standard of review: broad trial court discretion (e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn)

The law accords trial courts broad discretion in evidentiary matters for sound reasons. Trial judges are uniquely situated to observe the witness, the reaction of the jury, and the dynamics of the courtroom; they can manage testimony in real time to achieve the best balance between probative value and potential for prejudice. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized deference to such judgment: an evidentiary ruling will be overturned only for abuse of discretion.

Sprint/United Management Co. v. Mendelsohn (2008), among other cases, stresses the breadth of trial court discretion in managing trial evidence and the difficulty of mounting a successful appellate challenge. The Eighth Circuit repeatedly applies the abuse‑of‑discretion standard in reviewing Rule 403 rulings and “opening the door” determinations, as seen in the Cutbank opinion. Moreover, appellate courts will not reverse unless the evidentiary error substantially influenced the verdict—i.e., the defendant must show prejudice in the sense that the jury may have been “substantially swayed” by the challenged testimony.

This deferential standard shapes trial strategy. Because appellate reversal is difficult to obtain, litigants should seek immediate and explicit rulings at trial: contemporaneous objections, motions to strike, requests for limiting instructions, and articulations on the record of why the testimony is prejudicial or collateral. Such recordkeeping preserves issues and allows appellate review to focus on whether the trial court exceeded its wide discretion.

In short, Rule 403 is a powerful but carefully circumscribed tool for trial courts; opening‑the‑door principles promote fairness by allowing corrective evidence, but both doctrines are applied with deference to the trial judge’s on‑the‑spot assessments. Cutbank illustrates these principles: a brief, responsive explanation by a witness about why she disliked guns was admitted as probative rehabilitation; the district court’s judgment call survived appellate scrutiny because the evidence was relevant to credibility, not merely evocative, and because no substantial prejudice was shown.

Conclusion: balancing probative value and emotional effect

Managing emotionally charged testimony in the guilt phase of a criminal trial requires a disciplined balancing of competing values. Rule 403 asks whether the evidence will “substantially” distort the jury’s factfinding process, while the fairness principle behind “opening the door” recognizes that incomplete or misleading testimony must sometimes be corrected. Trial judges should make careful, context‑specific assessments of probative value, narrow the scope of permitted testimony, and employ curative measures when necessary. Counsel should avoid questions that invite partial truths that could open the door to prejudicial explanations, and should preserve objections and seek contemporaneous rulings to protect appellate review. When courts follow these practical and doctrinal guideposts—as the district court did in Cutbank—reversible error is unlikely.

# Impeachment with Prior Inconsistent Statements: Rule 613, Authentication, and Police Reports

This chapter explains how a litigant may use a witness’s prior inconsistent statement to impeach testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 613, the often-overlooked but critical authentication requirement under Rule 901, and the particular problems that arise when the impeaching “statement” is embedded in a police report. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank provides a compact illustration of these issues: the defendant sought to read portions of a police report into the record to impeach a government witness, the district court required authentication and sustained objections, and the Court of Appeals affirmed, relying on settled Eighth Circuit law—including United States v. Cumbie—that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated before admission. That factual posture is the vehicle for a doctrinal roadmap lawyers and judges will use repeatedly: Rule 613’s two-step foundation for extrinsic impeachment, the multiple ways to authenticate a written statement, and the alternatives to reading a police report into evidence when authentication or hearsay concerns bar that route.

## Rule 613(a)-(b): foundation requirements and extrinsic proof

Rule 613 governs impeachment with prior inconsistent statements. Its operative structure is straightforward in theory but consequential in practice.

- Rule 613(a) requires that a party confronting a witness may use a prior statement to show inconsistency. It calls for a procedure: the examiner may, but need not, show the witness the writing or make them hear the statement while they are being examined. The point is to put the witness on notice and to give the jury a clear basis for assessing credibility.

- Rule 613(b) imposes an important limitation on the use of extrinsic evidence to prove a prior inconsistent statement: extrinsic evidence is not admissible to impeach a witness with a prior inconsistent statement unless (1) the witness is given the opportunity to explain or deny the statement and (2) the opposing party is afforded an opportunity to examine the witness about it. In short, before the trier of fact may be shown a document or other item that purports to contain a prior inconsistent statement, the witness must be given a chance to address the apparent inconsistency on the stand (or previously have had that opportunity), and the opposing party must be able to cross-examine about the inconsistency.

These rules are designed to balance the truth-seeking function of impeachment with fairness to the witness and the opponent. If the witness can be presented with the statement and either adopts it or disavows it, the jury has the benefit of seeing the statement while also hearing the witness explain. If the witness is not on the stand or the examiner cannot get the witness to address the statement, courts are rightly suspicious of allowing juries to see out-of-court materials that may be unreliable.

In practice, the Rule 613(b) requirement is often satisfied by first asking the witness, on cross-examination, whether they made the prior statement or whether the document accurately records their prior words. Only if the witness disclaims or fails to recollect does the proponent offer extrinsic proof. The rule thus encourages the direct route—asking the witness about the prior statement—before resorting to document admission.

Applying these principles to the Cutbank facts: defense counsel sought to read portions of a police report that recorded a witness’s earlier statements to an investigator. The district court allowed questioning about what the witness had told police but sustained objections to reading the police report into the record because the report was not authored by the witness and lacked foundation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, emphasizing that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated—i.e., Rule 613(b)’s requirements were not satisfied simply by counsel’s attempt to read the report aloud.

## Authentication under Rule 901: who can authenticate; adoption by the witness

Admission of any writing or recorded statement as extrinsic evidence requires authentication under Rule 901(a): the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it to be. Authentication is a preliminary question for the court, and the methods to authenticate are several.

Common methods courts accept include:

- Testimony of a witness with knowledge: A witness who personally wrote the document, made the notation, or observed the making of the document may testify that it is what it purports to be. In the impeachment context, the simplest authentication is the impeached witness’s own testimony that the document correctly records a prior statement they made (often called adoption). If the witness testifies, “Yes, that is what I told the officer,” the report or the specific portions can be authenticated through the witness.

- Adoption by conduct or declaration: A witness may authenticate by adopting or affirming the substance of a writing or recording. Physical or verbal adoption—“I remember saying that” or signing a statement—provides a sufficient basis for finding authenticity.

- Testimony of the author or custodian: If an investigator or officer wrote the report, that author can authenticate the report by testifying that it was prepared contemporaneously and accurately recorded the witness’s words. A custodian of records can likewise authenticate business records (see discussion below) by laying the business-record foundation.

- Distinctive characteristics or circumstantial evidence: When a writing has distinctive markings, dates, or other characteristics that uniquely identify it, a court may accept such circumstantial proof as authentication (e.g., a recorded phone call with a known caller ID pattern, a photograph that plainly depicts the scene). Rule 901(b) lists these non-exclusive methods.

Eighth Circuit law, as applied in Cumbie and cited in Cutbank, frames these principles with a practical rule: extrinsic evidence offered to impeach a witness with a prior inconsistent statement must be authenticated. Where the report is not authored by the witness and no other witness establishes that the writing accurately records the witness’s statement, a district court may require additional foundation—often the investigator’s testimony—before allowing the document into evidence.

Some practical illustrations:

- Best practice when seeking to use a police report to impeach a witness is to ask the witness on the stand, “Did you tell Officer Smith on January 3 that X?” If the witness answers yes or adopts a writing, the report is authenticated as to those portions the witness admits.

- If the witness disclaims or has poor recollection, counsel may call the officer who prepared the report and ask whether the notes accurately reflect what the witness said. The officer’s testimony can authenticate the report and bring it within the scope of Rule 613(b)’s allowance for extrinsic proof (if the other requirements are met).

- If the report cannot be authenticated in those ways, the proponent should not attempt to read it aloud to the jury; the court is likely to sustain hearsay and authentication objections, as in Cutbank.

## Police reports: hearsay problems and authorship; limits of reading from reports

Police reports create a particular tangle of concerns: they are hearsay-heavy (containing out-of-court statements by victims, witnesses, or third parties), they are often authored by someone other than the person whose earlier statements they record, and they combine narrative, opinions, and raw statements in a single document.

Two distinct problems arise when counsel attempts to use a police report for impeachment:

1. Hearsay within hearsay. A police report typically contains statements by witnesses (e.g., “Victim said X”) memorialized by an officer. Those witness utterances are out-of-court statements and thus hearsay if offered for their truth. If the report is offered only to show prior inconsistent statements for impeachment, the offering party may claim the piece is not being admitted for its truth but solely to show inconsistency. When the witness adopts the specific portion, the statement becomes admissible for impeachment under Rule 613 and may also be admissible substantively under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) if the prior inconsistent statement was given under oath at a prior proceeding. But if the report is offered when the witness has not adopted or is unavailable, the inclusion of third-party statements creates hearsay within hearsay that must satisfy an exception for each layer.

2. Authorship and foundation. Many police reports are drafted by investigators who paraphrase or summarize a witness’s statement. Unless the witness testifies that the report accurately reflects their words, or the officer who prepared the report testifies to that effect, the court is likely to treat the report as an unauthenticated document authored by a third party. Reading such a report into the record is usually impermissible: it would be an extrinsic writing containing hearsay and lacking sufficient authentication.

The Cutbank court’s practical approach is emblematic: the district court permitted defense counsel to question the witness about prior police interviews (eliciting the substance of what she had told police), but it sustained objections when defense counsel attempted to read parts of the investigator’s report into evidence without establishing that the report was the witness’s own statement or that the investigator/writer would authenticate it. The Court of Appeals approved this limitation—authentication was required before extrinsic impeachment could be accomplished by reading the report to the jury.

What are the common judicial responses to this problem?

- If the witness adopts the writing, courts admit it (subject to other rules). Reading it then serves both impeachment and, in some cases, substantive purposes.

- If the witness does not adopt the writing but the officer who prepared the report is present, the proponent may call that officer to authenticate the report and testify that the report accurately records the witness’s words. That authentication may admit the report as extrinsic impeachment evidence under Rule 613(b).

- If the officer is not present, counsel can attempt to admit the report under a hearsay exception (business or public records) if the proponent can lay the appropriate foundation. That route raises its own complications because internal witness statements within a police report are often not admissible under the public-record exception if they contain investigatory opinions or accusatory narrative (see Rule 803(8) limitations and authorities construing it).

- If none of the above works, recorded recollection (Rule 803(5)) may be a fallback: have the witness read and adopt their prior written or recorded statement as a recorded recollection, but the rule’s foundation is strict.

A final point on police reports: trial judges have wide discretion to prevent juries from being exposed to hearsay-filled documents that may confuse the issues or unfairly prejudice a party. That discretion is particularly robust where the party proffering the report has not met authentication or hearsay-exception foundations.

## Alternative routes: recorded recollection (Rule 803(5)), prior sworn testimony (Rule 801(d)(1)(A)), public/business records (Rules 803(6), 803(8)) and their constraints

When a proponent cannot authenticate a police report as containing the witness’s own words, several alternative doctrines may permit use of prior statements—each with particular prerequisites and limits.

1. Recorded recollection—Rule 803(5).
   - Elements: the witness once had personal knowledge of the matter; the witness made or adopted a record when the matter was fresh in memory; the record accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge; and the witness now has insufficient recollection to testify fully and accurately.
   - Effect: the writing may be read into evidence (but generally is not received as an exhibit unless offered by an adverse party).
   - Practical points: counsel should establish on the record that the witness cannot now recall specifics even after examination and that the record was made contemporaneously. Recorded recollection is an evidentiary lifeline but is narrow: if the witness can recall now, the rule does not apply.

2. Prior sworn testimony—Rule 801(d)(1)(A) (nonhearsay).
   - If a witness’s prior inconsistent statement was made under oath at a prior trial, hearing, or other proceeding and the witness currently testifies and is subject to cross-examination about the earlier statement, the prior statement is not hearsay and can be admitted both for impeachment and substantive use. The crucial elements are (a) prior oath, (b) a prior proceeding (not merely a police interview), and (c) opportunity for cross-examination in the earlier proceeding (or being subject to the prior proceeding’s cross and the current opportunity for cross).
   - This exception is often unavailable where the prior statement is a police interview or an investigator’s report.

3. Business records—Rule 803(6).
   - A police report might be admitted as a business record if it was made in the regular course of the reporting entity’s business, by a person with knowledge, at or near the time of the event, and if the proponent lays the proper foundation by calling a custodian or other qualified witness.
   - Trap: a police report may qualify as a business record of a law enforcement agency, but statements within the report recounting third-party declarations are hearsay within hearsay and require their own hearsay exception. If the report contains the witness’s own prior statement and the report is otherwise admissible as a business record and properly authenticated, courts may admit it as impeachment. However, courts are often skeptical about admitting accusatory or investigative conclusions under business-record theory.

4. Public records—Rule 803(8).
   - Matters observed pursuant to a duty imposed by law and recorded by public officials may be admissible. But the exception excludes “matters observed by law-enforcement personnel in criminal cases” if admitted against a defendant in a criminal case, and courts are generally cautious with police investigatory reports, particularly as to complaints, conclusions, and opinions. Many courts exclude the narrative portions of police reports as contrary to the spirit of the exception.

Each of these alternatives has its own strategic implications. For instance, seeking a recorded recollection admission requires careful on-the-record proof that the witness cannot now recall despite being shown the writing; attempting to admit a police report as a business or public record requires calling a custodian or author to establish foundation and to address hearsay-within-hearsay. The Eighth Circuit’s jurisprudence, as seen in Cutbank and Cumbie, encourages lawyers to choose a path that will permit the necessary foundation to be established at trial rather than attempting to short-circuit the authentication step.

## Eighth Circuit’s insistence on authentication before admitting extrinsic impeachment (e.g., United States v. Cumbie)

The Eighth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized the authentication requirement for extrinsic impeachment evidence. United States v. Cumbie, cited by the Cutbank court, is a leading example: the court held that while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence still must be authenticated. Cumbie and its progeny require proffers sufficient to permit a reasonable juror to find that the document accurately represents the witness’s prior statement.

The Cutbank decision applied that rule to a common fact pattern: defense counsel sought to read portions of an investigator’s report into the record to impeach a government witness who had testified inconsistently. The district court allowed cross-examination about what the witness had told police (eliciting inconsistent statements orally from the witness), but disallowed introduction of the investigator’s report absent authentication. The Eighth Circuit approved: the proponent had not shown that the report was authored by the witness, adopted by the witness, or accurately recorded the witness’s words; thus the court did not err in rejecting the attempt to admit the report in lieu of live foundational testimony.

Key lessons from the Eighth Circuit line:

- Authentication is not a mere formality; courts will require the proponent to affirmatively connect the document to the witness or to the maker of the document.

- Cross-examination about a prior statement is often the simpler route. If the witness can be confronted with the prior statement and either adopts it or explains it, impeachment is accomplished without introducing the underlying document.

- Where the witness disavows a prior statement and the proponent wants the jury to see the contemporaneous record, the proponent must furnish authentication—either the witness’s adoption, the investigator’s testimony, or a proper hearsay exception/foundation for a business/public record. Absent that, trial courts should exclude the report as unauthenticated.

- Appellate courts reviewing such rulings apply an abuse-of-discretion standard; judges have substantial latitude to protect juries from unauthenticated, hearsay-laden materials.

## Practical trial strategies and common pitfalls

1. Always try direct confrontation first. Put the prior statement to the witness on the stand. If they admit, you are done on authentication; the writing is effectively adopted for impeachment purposes (and sometimes substantive use). Rule 613 favors this approach.

2. If the witness disavows and you want the jury to see the exact language, prepare to authenticate the writing. Call the author (investigator/officer) or a custodian to lay the business-record foundation. Anticipate and address hearsay-within-hearsay issues.

3. Consider recorded recollection if the witness genuinely cannot recall. Lay the careful foundation required by Rule 803(5). Be mindful that recorded recollection allows the writing to be read into evidence but not necessarily admitted as an exhibit.

4. Don’t assume police reports are freely admissible. Many jurisdictions scrutinize narrative reports and exclude opinions or summaries that invade the jury’s province or are hearsay.

5. If you lack a foundation witness, think about alternative evidence: body-worn camera footage, contemporaneous text messages, call logs, photos. These items typically present fewer hearsay/authentication headaches than narrative reports.

6. Preserve the record. If the court excludes a report you intended to use, make a proffer outside the presence of the jury (offer of proof) that establishes what you would have shown and by whom—this is essential should you later challenge the ruling on appeal.

7. Beware of Confrontation Clause issues in criminal cases. A prior testimonial statement by a declarant is subject to the Sixth Amendment unless the declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination. A police report that contains testimonial statements from a witness who does not testify may therefore be constitutionally inadmissible for substantive purposes; impeachment under Rule 613, however, raises distinct Confrontation Clause questions that hinge on whether the declarant testifies and is available for cross-examination.

## Conclusion: rules-of-thumb for the courtroom

The Eighth Circuit’s treatment in Cutbank—endorsing the district court’s insistence on authentication before allowing a police report to be read into evidence for impeachment—illustrates stable doctrine:

- Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement only after the witness has been afforded an opportunity to explain or deny the statement and the opposing party has an opportunity to examine the witness about it.

- Rule 901 requires authentication of any extrinsic document offered to prove a prior inconsistent statement; absent adoption by the witness or testimony from the document’s author/custodian, a court may exclude the document.

- Police reports often contain hearsay and third-party statements; they are not a shortcut for impeachment unless properly authenticated or otherwise admissible under a recognized exception (recorded recollection, business or public records, prior sworn testimony).

For trial practice: confront the witness directly first; if the witness disavows, be ready to authenticate through the author or a custodian, or to resort to recorded recollection or other admissible evidence. Failure to do so invites exclusion, and appellate courts will generally defer to the trial court’s exercise of evidentiary gatekeeping power.

# Limiting Instructions: Timing, Content, and Strategy

Limiting instructions are a routine but important tool a trial judge uses to channel the jury’s use of evidence. Federal Rule of Evidence 105 requires a court to “restrict the evidence to its proper scope and instruct the jury accordingly” when the court admits evidence for one purpose but not another, but it leaves the timing, content, and even the necessity of such instructions to the court’s discretion. The federal courts of appeals regularly confront disputes about whether a district court abused that discretion by declining to give a requested limiting instruction at the time evidence was admitted, by failing to give any instruction at all, or by giving an instruction that was too broad or too confusing. United States v. Cutbank, decided by the Eighth Circuit, provides a useful vehicle to explore these issues. In Cutbank the defendant requested a contemporaneous limiting instruction about statements a witness made to police; the district court declined to give a contemporaneous instruction and provided no special limiting language at that time. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed, explaining that a court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial,” and that declining to give a requested contemporaneous instruction is an abuse-of-discretion question. United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cited in Cutbank, is the precedent the court followed (and Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861, was cited for the Confrontation Clause point). That holding—that courts may defer some limiting instructions until the close of evidence—illuminates the balancing judges must perform, and it frames the strategic choices counsel should make about when and how to press for limiting language.

## Rule 105 and the court’s discretion to defer limiting instructions

Rule 105 is brief but capacious: “When evidence which is admissible as to one party or for one purpose but not admissible as to another party or for another purpose is admitted, the court, upon request, shall restrict the evidence to its proper scope and instruct the jury accordingly.” The rule imposes an affirmative duty to instruct “if requested,” but it does not dictate timing, require a contemporaneous explanation of every evidentiary admission, or prescribe the exact wording the judge must use. The discretion given to the trial court flows from pragmatic concerns: judges manage complex trials and must decide when an instruction will aid, rather than bewilder, the jury.

Cutbank illustrates the common exercise of that discretion. Defense counsel sought a pre-admission, front‑loaded jury instruction that the jury could not consider the victim’s out‑of‑court statements to police as substantive evidence until the witness testified at trial. The government opposed, arguing the statements were relevant to explain police conduct and the officers’ knowledge and that a traditional instruction at the close of trial would suffice. The district court agreed with the government and denied the request. On appeal the Eighth Circuit rejected the claim of error, explaining that district courts need not interrupt trial to give a limiting instruction for every admitted item of evidence and may provide an appropriate instruction at the close of the trial. See Bennett, supra.

That does not mean Rule 105 is toothless. Courts must still meaningfully “restrict the evidence to its proper scope” when evidence has a limited admissible purpose. If the record shows the evidence was admitted only for a specified, non‑truth purpose (for example, to explain a police officer’s conduct), the court should give—either contemporaneously or at the close—an instruction that explains the narrow purpose. The refusal to give any limiting instruction where there is a real risk of prejudice or misuse can be reversible error if the appellate court concludes the district court abused its discretion and the error was not harmless. The question is fact‑intensive: the court considers the nature of the evidence, the likelihood a jury will misuse it, the availability of corrective instructions later in trial, and the overall context.

Practical rule: Rule 105 obliges the judge to provide limiting instructions when requested, but it does not require the instruction to be given at the instant the evidence is admitted. The timing is committed to the trial court’s discretion. Counsel who want a contemporaneous ruling should preserve their request on the record and explain why a later instruction would be inadequate.

## When contemporaneous instructions are advisable to prevent misuse

Although courts can defer limiting instructions, there are clear circumstances in which a contemporaneous instruction is strongly advisable, if not necessary. Contemporaneous instructions are most often warranted when the evidence admitted is particularly inflammatory or highly prejudicial, when the jury is likely to misunderstand the evidence’s purpose, or when the evidentiary use is time‑sensitive (for example, when the evidence is introduced before the declarant testifies and the defendant will not have the benefit of immediate confrontation).

Consider three common scenarios:

1) Hearsay admitted for a non‑truth purpose that is highly prejudicial. When a statement containing shocking or emotionally charging content is admitted solely to explain why officers took certain actions, jurors who hear the statement may be tempted to rely on the statement’s substantive content. If the out‑of‑court statement alleges violent conduct or moral turpitude, the prejudice risk is acute. A contemporaneous limiting instruction helps channel the jury immediately: it places a verbal fence around the material and reduces the risk that jurors will treat the hearsay as substantive proof.

2) A witness’s hearsay statements are introduced before that witness testifies. Here the defense faces two concerns: (a) jurors may treat the hearsay as substantive if no later testimonial clarification occurs, and (b) if the declarant does testify, the Confrontation Clause issues are triggered only by the sequence of events in trial. If the declarant ultimately takes the stand, the Confrontation Clause concern may be alleviated (Charboneau, 613 F.3d at 861), but the jury’s interim exposure remains. A contemporaneous instruction advising the jury of the limited purpose avoids an unfair first impression.

3) Impeachment‑only material. Prior inconsistent statements admitted solely for impeachment (not for truth) can be easy to misconstrue. If counsel plans to introduce the inconsistent statement early to attack a witness’s credibility, a contemporaneous limiting instruction is prudent because the impeachment comes into the jury’s thinking at the precise moment they are forming impressions. A later instruction may not cure an initial, powerful impression.

In each of these contexts, the defense should make a particularized record explaining why a later instruction will be insufficient. General references to Rule 105 without explanation are less likely to persuade a trial judge to deviate from usual practice.

Cutbank shows the flip side: when the statement’s purpose is plainly explanatory of police conduct, when the declarant later testifies, and when no immediate, undeniable prejudice will occur, the court may reasonably defer. The trial judge is in the best position to assess how likely the jury is to misuse the evidence.

## Drafting precise instructions for intrinsic evidence, non‑hearsay purposes, and impeachment‑only uses

Not all limiting instructions are the same. The permissible content of a limiting instruction depends on why the evidence was admitted. Confusion often arises about three categories of evidence judges encounter frequently: intrinsic evidence (evidence that is part of the charged scheme or the act itself), evidence admitted for a non‑hearsay purpose (for example, to explain a police officer’s state of mind), and evidence admitted for impeachment only (prior inconsistent statements).

Intrinsic evidence. When evidence is “intrinsic” to the crime charged—evidence of acts that are part of the charged offense or that explain the immediate context of the charged conduct—trial courts and appellate courts treat the material differently than they treat typical Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014) (cited in Cutbank), recognizes that prior possession of the same firearm is highly relevant to proving later possession: it has direct probative value because it tends to show ownership or control, and is not merely propensity evidence. For intrinsic evidence, an instruction that unnecessarily calls attention to an absence of propensity problems (for example, by saying “this evidence is not to be considered as showing propensity”) can confuse more than it helps. A better approach is a narrowly tailored instruction that explains what the evidence tends to prove: “You may consider testimony that the defendant possessed a firearm on prior occasions only as evidence that he had knowledge of, access to, or control over that firearm, and not as proof of any bad character or disposition to commit crimes.”

Non‑hearsay explanatory uses. Frequently, an out‑of‑court statement is admissible not for its truth, but to explain why a police officer acted in a certain way—why an officer entered a location, detained a person, or escalated force. In that circumstance the limiting instruction must tell jurors to construe the statement only for the narrow purpose of explaining the agent’s state of mind or the reason for their actions. Sample wording: “Certain testimony about statements made to police has been admitted only to show why the officers acted as they did and what they knew at the time. You must not consider those statements as proof that the facts asserted in them are true.”

Impeachment‑only statements. Prior inconsistent statements admitted solely to impeach the witness’s credibility pose a particular danger because jurors may treat the earlier statement as substantive proof of the matter asserted. The preferred instruction makes the impeachment purpose explicit and warns the jury that the prior statement is not independent proof of the facts. For example: “You have heard evidence that [witness] made a prior statement inconsistent with [his/her] testimony here. That prior statement was admitted only to help you evaluate [witness]’s credibility. It is not evidence that the events described in the prior statement actually occurred.”

Practical drafting tips. Whatever the category, limiting instructions should be (1) succinct—long, multi‑layered legal explanations confuse jurors; (2) concrete—use plain language and concrete verbs (e.g., “do not consider,” “only to explain”); (3) connected to the fact pattern—tailor the instruction to the specific witness and statements; and (4) given in writing if requested—jurors may appreciate having a printed copy of the instruction. When possible, propose instruction language in writing and obtain a ruling on the record so appellate courts can determine preservation and reviewability.

Cutbank demonstrates the importance of tailoring: the government argued the victim’s statements were explanatory of officer conduct and that a later instruction would suffice. The court accepted that position. If the defense believed the statements were likely to be misused as substantive proof or were too inflammatory to permit delayed instruction, it should have articulated that position to the district court and requested specific contemporaneous wording.

## Preservation, requests, and standards on appeal

Preservation matters. If a party wants a limiting instruction, Rule 103(a) requires a timely objection if the party believes the admission of evidence was improper and a timely request for an instruction under Rule 105 to preserve the argument that the judge erred in failing to give the instruction. If counsel merely objects to the evidence without asking for a limiting instruction, the failure to request the instruction can be treated as forfeiture of the right to complain on appeal about the absence or timing of a limiting instruction. In Cutbank the defense made a specific pretrial request that the jury be instructed not to consider the victim’s out‑of‑court statements as substantive evidence until the witness testified. That clear request preserved the issue for review, even though the district court denied it.

Standard of review. Appellate courts generally review a trial court’s refusal to give or its timing of limiting instructions for abuse of discretion. See Bennett, 765 F.3d at 899 (reviewing denial of limiting instruction for abuse of discretion). If the defendant did not preserve the issue, the appellate court will apply plain‑error review, which requires the defendant to show (1) error, (2) that is plain (clear or obvious), and (3) that affects substantial rights; the court may correct the error only if it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. See Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b). Courts affirm district courts that have thoughtfully considered the request and either tailored an instruction later in trial or concluded the risk of juror misuse was minimal in context.

Harmless error and cumulative error. Even if a limiting instruction was erroneously omitted or mistimed, reversal is not automatic. The defendant must show the error was not harmless—i.e., the evidentiary ruling (or its timing) substantially swayed the jury. The Eighth Circuit has emphasized that where there is substantial evidence of guilt, courts will not overturn a conviction for an erroneously admitted or uncurtailed piece of evidence unless the defendant can show the improper evidence likely affected the verdict. See United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997–98 (8th Cir. 2009) (describing harmless error standard); Keys, 918 F.3d at 985 (cumulative errors). Cutbank’s appellate challenge failed because the record contained strong testimonial and physical evidence—DNA, contemporaneous identifications, and the violent context of the offense—so any limiting‑instruction error, if there had been one, was harmless.

Authentication and impeachment extrinsic evidence. Preservation of a right to cross‑examine and to use extrinsic evidence for impeachment must be navigated carefully. If counsel seeks to read portions of an investigator’s report into the record to impeach a witness, the defense must lay a foundation for admission of the report (authentication) and demonstrate the testimony’s foundation links the content to the witness who allegedly made the prior statement. The Eighth Circuit has held that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated (United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)), and a district court does not abuse its discretion in denying the reading of parts of a police report where the defense fails to establish that foundation. In Cutbank the court required proper authentication before allowing police‑report material to be read in, a ruling the appellate court sustained.

Practical preservation tips. When you want a limiting instruction: (1) request one on the record at the earliest possible moment, (2) explain precisely why the limiting instruction must be contemporaneous (e.g., extreme prejudice, likely jury misuse, declarant may not testify), (3) submit proposed wording, and (4) if the court denies a contemporaneous instruction, ask that a written limiting instruction be given at the close and that the denial and the requested language be placed on the record so appellate courts can review the decision. If you plan to use extrinsic impeachment, lay a clear foundation for authentication before attempting to read from investigative reports or other third‑party documents.

## Risks of over‑emphasis and jury confusion

Limiting instructions are not risk‑free remedies. Poorly worded or repetitious instructions can do more harm than good. Judges must balance the benefit of immediate guidance against the danger of calling emotional or sensational facts to the jury’s attention and thereby magnifying their impact.

Two risks are particularly salient:

1) Spotlighting the evidence. A contemporaneous instruction declaring “this statement is not to be considered as proof” can paradoxically draw attention to the statement in a way that makes it more memorable. Jurors often recall what a court deems noteworthy. If the statement is inflammatory, a contemporaneous instruction may unintentionally highlight the inflammatory content. In close cases, counsel should weigh whether requesting an immediate instruction might do more to reinforce a damaging piece of testimony than to blunt its effect.

2) Overly legalistic or confusing instructions. Legalese or multi‑part instructions may confuse jurors. For example, telling jurors a statement is admissible for “state‑of‑mind” purposes but inadmissible for “truth” can be difficult to parse under the stress of trial. Plain language is essential. Judges should avoid long exegeses on evidentiary doctrine and instead give short, concrete directions about how jurors should use the testimony in their deliberations.

Because of these risks, a judicious approach often works best. When evidence is clearly intrinsic and its probative value plain, an instruction that explains the limited purpose of the evidence without invoking “propensity” labels is often sufficient. When a statement is inflammatory and likely to be treated as substantive—especially if the declarant may not testify or there is no later chance to correct the jury’s impression—a contemporaneous, plain‑spoken instruction is prudent despite the risk of spotlighting. And when counsel fears that any instruction will highlight the harmful testimony, they should consider alternative strategies: move to strike the testimony, move for a mistrial (in extreme cases), or use cross‑examination to neutralize the content so that a limiting instruction becomes less necessary.

Strategy summary for counsel:

- Defense counsel should anticipate the possible misuse of evidence and seek a contemporaneous limiting instruction when the prejudice is immediate and likely (inflammatory hearsay, refusal of confrontation, or impeachment introduced before the declarant testifies). Put the request and proposed language on the record.

- Prosecutors should be prepared to explain why a limiting instruction can be deferred (e.g., the evidence is explanatory of police conduct, the declarant will testify later, the evidence is intrinsic). If the government believes a contemporaneous instruction would be counterproductive, it should offer alternative remedial steps (e.g., brief cautionary statements by witness/examining officer or a deliberate plan to have the declarant testify promptly).

- Both sides should submit proposed limiting instructions in writing and seek a ruling on the record so appellate courts can review the trial court’s judgment and exercise of discretion.

Conclusion

Limiting instructions perform an important gatekeeping and explanatory function in trials, but Rule 105 does not impose a one‑size‑fits‑all rule about timing or content. Cutbank exemplifies the ordinary discretion district courts possess to defer limiting instructions until the close of evidence when doing so will not prejudice the defendant. But that discretionary power has limits: when the risk of misuse is immediate and substantial, a contemporaneous instruction—or other curative measures—may be required to preserve a fair trial. Counsel must be proactive: make a timely, particularized request; propose clear, plain‑language wording tailored to the admissible purpose; preserve the record; and weigh the tactical risk that an instruction may inadvertently spotlight the very evidence it attempts to tame. Appellate review will ask whether the trial court’s approach was a reasoned exercise of discretion and whether any error was harmless in light of the entire trial record.

# Harmless Error and Cumulative Error in Criminal Appeals

The appellate inquiry into whether trial errors require reversal often determines the fate of a criminal conviction. Appellate courts must balance two competing imperatives: protecting a defendant’s constitutional and statutory rights to a fair trial, and avoiding needless retrials when errors were harmless in the sense that they did not affect the outcome. This chapter explains the principles governing harmless-error review and the related cumulative-error doctrine, illustrates how the allocation of burdens varies depending on the nature of the error, and shows how courts apply these principles in practice—particularly in firearms prosecutions in which physical evidence and strong testimonial proof often diminish the prejudicial effect of trial errors. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank, decided October 7, 2025, provides a recent, concrete example of these doctrines in action and will be used throughout the chapter to illuminate the tests and their application.

## Non‑constitutional harmless error: “substantially swayed” / “substantially injurious effect” standard

Not all errors at trial are constitutional errors. Many claimed errors involve the trial court’s discretionary evidentiary rulings, the admission of certain witnesses or documents, and other non‑constitutional matters. When the claimed error is non‑constitutional, appellate courts apply a harmless‑error inquiry designed to determine whether the error affected the fairness, integrity, or outcome of the trial.

Eighth Circuit precedent (and the Cutbank opinion) frames this inquiry in pragmatic terms: a non‑constitutional evidentiary error warrants reversal only if the defendant shows the jury “may have been substantially swayed” by the improperly admitted evidence or, said another way, that the error had a substantial or injurious effect on the jury’s verdict. This language echoes the Supreme Court’s harm formulations (for direct review and habeas contexts) such as the “substantial and injurious effect or influence” standard articulated in Kotteakos v. United States and used in habeas review decisions like Brecht v. Abrahamson. The Eighth Circuit often uses the shorthand that reversal is required only if the improperly admitted evidence “substantially swayed” the jury in reaching its verdict (see United States v. DeMarce and Keys). This is an inherently case‑specific judgment that turns on the record as a whole.

Applying this test requires the appellate court to assess the nature of the error, the centrality of the erroneously admitted or excluded evidence to the government’s theory, and the strength and character of the remaining evidence supporting guilt. Factors that mitigate the prejudicial impact of an error include (1) overwhelming independent evidence of guilt (for example, reliable physical evidence such as DNA or fingerprints linking the defendant to the instrumentality of the crime), (2) other admissible testimony that established the same facts, (3) curative jury instructions, and (4) the peripheral or cumulative nature of the erroneously admitted material. Conversely, errors that affect the prosecution’s central theory or relate to a contested factual issue are more likely to be deemed prejudicial.

Cutbank illustrates this approach. The defendant mounted a cluster of challenges to evidentiary rulings—whether lay witnesses could testify they had seen him possess the sawed‑off rifle before the charged incident; whether the jury should have been instructed about hearsay; whether testimony about a witness’s sister being killed by a gun was prejudicial; and whether a police report could be read into evidence for impeachment. The Eighth Circuit did not accept the defendant’s cursory objections as showing prejudice. The court held that the district judge’s rulings did not “substantially sway” the jury because the government’s testimonial and physical evidence—DNA on the firearm, victim identifications, the discovery of the rifle on the likely path between two houses, the victim’s phone recovered nearby, and officers’ testimony about the defendant’s conduct—was substantial. Thus, even if one or more evidentiary rulings were erroneous, the error was harmless under the “substantially swayed” standard.

Practical lesson for practitioners and students: when pressing a harmless‑error claim on appeal in a non‑constitutional setting, the appellant must show more than that a trial ruling was mistaken; the appellant must demonstrate that the error likely had a meaningful effect on the jury’s verdict. Appellate briefing should therefore focus tightly on the record and on why the remaining evidence was insufficient to sustain the verdict in light of the error.

## Constitutional harmless‑error standard: harmless beyond a reasonable doubt

Constitutional errors—violations of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel, the Confrontation Clause, the privilege against self‑incrimination, the right to an impartial judge, or other fundamental protections—trigger a more exacting standard. Where a constitutional error is properly preserved at trial, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the guilty verdict. This is the Chapman v. California standard: only when the government demonstrates that there is no “reasonable possibility” that the error affected the verdict will the conviction stand.

Why the stricter standard? Constitutional protections are treated as so fundamental to a fair trial that a higher standard guards against nullifying those protections by mere evidentiary happenstance. Chapman draws a bright line: preserved constitutional errors require the government to overcome a heavy burden.

There are two important practical wrinkles. First, the label “constitutional error” matters—some trial errors implicate statutory or evidentiary rules rather than constitutional rights, and those invoke the less demanding “substantial prejudice” inquiry discussed above. Second, when a defendant fails to preserve a constitutional objection at trial, the appellate court ordinarily reviews for plain error under the standard of United States v. Olano: the defendant must show (1) an error, (2) that is plain (clear or obvious at the time of appeal), (3) that affects substantial rights (usually showing a reasonable probability of a different outcome), and (4) that the court should exercise its discretion to correct the error because it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Plain‑error review, while demanding, still permits reversal of constitutional error in exceptional cases even when not preserved.

Finally, not all constitutional errors are subject to harmless‑beyond‑a‑reasonable‑doubt review. Structural errors—those that defy analysis by harmless‑error principles because they affect the framework within which the trial proceeds (for example, total deprivation of counsel, a biased judge, racial discrimination in jury selection)—are treated as reversible per se. A defendant need not show prejudice for structural errors.

In Cutbank, the defendant raised Confrontation Clause and hearsay concerns. The Eighth Circuit found no Confrontation Clause problem because the relevant declarant (the victim) testified in person at trial, satisfying the constitutional guarantee. Thus, the Chapman standard did not come into play. This shows that courts often decide threshold constitutional questions before addressing harmlessness.

## Cumulative‑error doctrine: aggregation and the requirement of substantial prejudice

Multiple errors that are individually harmless may, in combination, require reversal if their cumulative effect undermines confidence in the verdict. The cumulative‑error doctrine embodies the intuition that several small errors can, when aggregated, amount to a trial so flawed that the conviction cannot stand. Nonetheless, courts are cautious in applying this doctrine. The central question is whether the aggregate effect of the errors “substantially swayed” the jury or otherwise deprived the defendant of a fundamentally fair trial.

The doctrine has boundaries. First, cumulative‑error review is applied only to errors; it does not aggregate non‑errors (i.e., correct rulings). Second, courts will not aggregate multiple harmless discretionary rulings if those rulings were correct in law or limited in effect. Third, the cumulative effect must be meaningful: courts typically require a showing of substantial prejudice comparable to that demanded when considering a single harmful error. The Eighth Circuit’s decisions—illustrated in Keys and invoked in Cutbank—are representative: aggregation leads to reversal only when the combined errors produced substantial prejudice to the defendant’s right to a fair trial.

How do courts evaluate cumulative prejudice? Analysts look at the number and type of errors, whether they implicated the same factual issue or different portions of the government’s case, whether overlapping admissible evidence re‑established the points the errors undermined, and whether the errors involved constitutional rights (which raises the bar for harmlessness). Courts also place heavy weight on the strength of the government’s evidence—the stronger the independent proof of guilt, the less likely that the collective effect of errors altered the verdict.

Cutbank demonstrates the cumulative‑error analysis in practice. The defendant claimed multiple evidentiary rulings were erroneous, but the Eighth Circuit concluded the cumulative effect did not produce substantial prejudice. The panel emphasized the substantial physical and testimonial evidence of guilt and reasoned that even if some rulings were marginally erroneous, the aggregate effect did not undermine confidence in the verdict. The court’s conclusion exemplifies the typical outcome in cases where the government has strong independent proof: the aggregate of trivial or peripheral errors rarely compels reversal.

Practical lesson: when asserting a cumulative‑error claim, defense counsel must identify not only multiple rulings that were improper but also explain how those rulings together altered the evidentiary landscape in a way that raises a serious doubt about the result. General assertions that “errors stacked up” without record‑based analysis are unlikely to succeed.

## Burden allocation and record‑driven analysis

A hallmark of harmless‑error and cumulative‑error review is that it is intensely record‑driven. Appellate courts decide whether an error was prejudicial by examining the trial transcript, exhibits, jury instructions, and the nature and timing of objections. The procedural posture and preservation of objections determine both the standard of review and the party that bears the burden.

The allocation of burdens proceeds along these lines:

- Preserved constitutional error: government bears the burden to prove harmless beyond a reasonable doubt (Chapman).
- Preserved non‑constitutional error: appellant must show prejudice under the applicable harmless standard (circuit variations exist, but Eighth Circuit law frames this as showing the jury may have been “substantially swayed”).
- Unpreserved error (constitutional or non‑constitutional): plain‑error review applies; appellant must show the error was plain, affected substantial rights, and that correction is necessary for fairness—this is the most demanding route for defendants on appeal.

Appellate courts give substantial deference to trial courts’ factual findings and to discretionary evidentiary rulings. When a district court adopts a presentence report or other factual statements without objection, appellate courts accept those adopted facts as a proper part of the record; the Cutbank panel relied on the district court’s adoption of the PSR for sentencing findings. Similarly, when a trial judge gives a contemporaneous or curative instruction, appellate review recognizes the trial judge’s superior position to assess how the jury received the evidence.

Because harmless‑error review is fact‑intensive, appellant briefs must marshal the trial record; assertions divorced from record citations are unsatisfactory. An appellate court has no basis to find prejudice where the record demonstrates otherwise, and conversely, a well‑developed record of error and its consequences creates a path to reversal.

Cutbank affords multiple examples of record‑driven reasoning. The defendant’s appellate brief was criticized for lacking legal authority and for failing to develop arguments supported by record citations; the court treated the contentions as undeveloped and disposed of them on the merits by reference to the record (victim testimony, DNA, physical recovery of the rifle, and the PSR). The lesson is plain: appellate courts expect a careful, record‑based showing of how a disputed ruling affected the outcome.

## Application in firearms trials with strong physical and testimonial evidence

Firearms prosecutions often present the classic setting in which harmless‑error and cumulative‑error doctrines play out. A typical federal felon‑in‑possession case will feature a mix of physical evidence (the firearm itself, DNA or fingerprints on the weapon, photographs, shell casings, serial numbers) and testimonial evidence (victim identifications, eyewitness accounts of possession, officers’ observations). When the physical and testimonial evidence is strong and consistent, courts are less likely to find that an evidentiary error—such as a narrowly admitted prior‑possession statement by a witness or limited testimony about a witness’s prior statements—had a substantial effect on the verdict.

Two practical points about firearms cases deserve emphasis:

1. Prior possession testimony often lies at the heart of the government’s case. Federal courts routinely recognize that testimony that a defendant possessed the same weapon on previous occasions is not necessarily “other‑acts” propensity evidence under Rule 404(b); rather, it may be intrinsic and probative of possession, ownership, and continuity. The Eighth Circuit in United States v. Battle (and the Cutbank opinion following Battle) explains that prior possession of the same firearm tends to establish later possession because it illuminates control and continuity. Consequently, such testimony is less likely to be deemed unfairly prejudicial and more likely to survive harmless‑error scrutiny.

2. Physical evidence such as DNA linking a defendant to the firearm is highly probative and strongly mitigates the prejudicial effect of peripheral evidentiary errors. If DNA or fingerprints on the gun match the defendant, if the weapon was recovered along the defendant’s likely path of flight, and if multiple witnesses identify the weapon and the defendant’s prior familiarity with it, appellate courts will often find that any isolated evidentiary misstep did not “substantially sway” the jury.

Cutbank illustrates both points. The government presented DNA evidence connecting the defendant to the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle, eyewitness identification by the victim and another housemate, recovery of the gun and the victim’s phone along the likely path between locations, and testimony regarding the defendant’s flight and resistance—including biting an officer and grabbing a weapon. Against that record, the Eighth Circuit found that the aggregate effect of the defendant’s complaints about evidentiary rulings did not produce substantial prejudice. The court also affirmed the district court’s pretrial ruling that lay testimony about prior observations of the same sawed‑off rifle was intrinsic and admissible to establish possession and continuity, underscoring that prior possession testimony in firearms cases often serves a permissible and probative function.

For practitioners: in a firearms prosecution, challenge to an evidentiary ruling will have a higher chance of reversal when the contested evidence bears directly on a disputed element that is not otherwise corroborated by physical or other strong evidence. Conversely, where the government has multiple independent proofs of possession, appellate courts will usually treat peripheral evidentiary errors as harmless.

## Conclusion: practical strategy for litigants and students

Harmless‑error and cumulative‑error doctrines require careful, record‑bound argumentation. For defense counsel:

- Preserve objections where possible, and make clear the constitutional basis of any objection that implicates fundamental rights.
- If an objection is preserved, develop the record to show how the admitted evidence impacted the jury’s view of contested issues; if the error is not preserved, be prepared to show plain error and its effect on substantial rights.
- In appellate briefs, focus on the specific portions of the record that demonstrate prejudice; bare assertions will fail.
- In cases with strong physical evidence (common in firearms prosecutions), tailor arguments to show why even overwhelming evidence may not cure particular errors—e.g., demonstrate that the error affected a threshold credibility finding or eliminated a legitimate defense theory.

For appellate courts and students studying their work, the Cutbank opinion is a model of typical appellate analysis: the court identified the nature of each claimed error, characterized it as constitutional or non‑constitutional (or not error at all), applied the appropriate standard, and reviewed the full record to decide whether the error (alone or in combination) undermined confidence in the outcome. The central thread through all of these analyses is the same: harmless‑error and cumulative‑error review is not abstract; it is an outcome‑sensitive, record‑bound inquiry that asks whether the asserted errors, individually or collectively, were likely to change the result. Where the record contains strong, independent proof of guilt, courts will generally find that errors—particularly peripheral evidentiary rulings—were harmless.

# Guidelines for Firearms Offenses: The § 2K2.1 Framework

United States Sentencing Guideline § 2K2.1 governs most sentences for unlawful possession, transportation, or trafficking in firearms and ammunition. For a law student learning to apply the Guidelines to a felon‑in‑possession case, § 2K2.1 is usefully analyzed in layers: (1) a base offense level that depends on the defendant’s status and the type of weapon involved; (2) a set of specific offense characteristics (SOCs) that increase the offense level for aggravating factors such as altered serial numbers, the number or the stolen status of firearms, or possession in connection with other felonies; (3) cross‑references and adjustments in other Chapters (for example, adjustments for reckless endangerment in flight and the interaction with career‑offender or Armed Career Criminal computations); and (4) the practical sentencing procedures — the relevant‑conduct rules, the evidentiary standards at sentencing, the role of the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), preservation of objections, and appellate review. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank provides a concise vehicle for illustrating many of these points in a modern felon‑in‑possession prosecution and sentencing.

This chapter maps that structure and explains how judges, advocates, and appellate courts apply the rules in practice. The goal is not merely to recite numbers; it is to equip you to (a) identify which provisions plausibly apply from the charging and factual record, (b) frame and preserve objections, (c) marshal factual proof or concessions to support or oppose enhancements, and (d) anticipate appellate standards of review.

## Base offense levels tied to prior convictions and weapon type (including § 5845 firearms)

Section 2K2.1 begins each calculation with a base offense level that reflects both (i) the defendant’s status and criminal history and (ii) the type of weapon involved. In practice, the starting point for many § 2K2.1 cases is an intermediate base level that may be raised if the weapon is one of the firearms specially identified in the National Firearms Act (NFA) — for example, sawed‑off shotguns or rifles described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a). The guidelines treat possession of those NFA weapons more severely because Congress has determined they are particularly dangerous and subject to separate federal regulation.

Cutbank illustrates this principle. The PSR recommended a base offense level of 26 because the weapon was a Marlin .22 rifle with a sawed‑off barrel and stock — a firearm described in § 5845(a). The practical lesson: in a weapons case the practitioner must identify the weapon’s legal classification early. Is it an ordinary rifle or handgun? Is it a short‑barreled rifle or sawed‑off shotgun? Is it a machinegun? Different classifications trigger different base offense levels and may open the door to statutory violations under other statutes as well (NFA registration statutes, tax code provisions, etc.).

Two procedural points follow. First, the factual predicate for classifying the weapon (for example, measurements proving a sawed‑off barrel) is sentencing‑stage factfinding when the classification is not admitted and not resolved by a jury: courts may resolve it by a preponderance of the evidence. Second, the Guidelines sometimes interact with statutory mandatory penalties (for example, ACCA). When another provision (e.g., the career‑offender rule or the Armed Career Criminal Guideline) yields a higher offense level, the Guidelines provide rules (e.g., § 4B1.4(b)) to apply the greater level. In Cutbank, the district court relied on the PSR’s base level tied to the § 5845 firearm and then compared that calculation to the alternative ACCA/Chapter Four computation; the Guidelines mandate that the greater offense level govern.

Always check the controlling edition of the Guidelines because numeric levels and cross‑references have changed over time; but the analytic steps — classify the weapon, determine whether NFA treatment applies, and then compute the comparative Chapter Four career offender/ACCA numbers — remain constant.

## Specific offense characteristics: altered/obliterated serial number, number of firearms, stolen firearm, trafficking

After selecting the appropriate base offense level, § 2K2.1(b) lists numerous specific offense characteristics (SOCs) that adjust the offense level upward for aggravating conduct. A sentencing advocate should evaluate each SOC as applied to the factual record; commonly contested SOCs in felon‑in‑possession cases include the following.

- Altered or obliterated serial number. The Guidelines penalize tampering with a firearm’s identifying marks because obliterated serial numbers indicate an intent to conceal provenance and evade tracing. In Cutbank the PSR recommended a three‑level increase for an altered/obliterated serial number, and the district court adopted that increase. Practically, proof may include forensic testimony about serial numbers, photographs, or testimony about an attempt to grind off identifying marks. At sentencing, the court may rely on such evidence by a preponderance.

- Number of firearms. § 2K2.1 contains increases tied to the number of weapons involved. The policy rationale is straightforward: the more weapons a defendant possesses, the greater the potential for harm and the greater need for incapacitation or deterrence. Thus, both defense counsel and prosecutors should identify every firearm found or connected to the defendant and argue for or against quantity‑based enhancements.

- Stolen firearms. Possession of a stolen firearm often triggers an enhancement. Theft signals a separate criminal objective (dispossession of the owner) and, frequently, a nexus to trafficking or distribution. Proof may come from a victim’s report, law enforcement records, or evidence linking the weapon to a theft report.

- Trafficking. Possession with intent to distribute or evidence of sales activity can trigger trafficking enhancements under § 2K2.1. These enhancements can transform a possession case into an essentially drug‑distribution‑style sentencing profile. Courts will look to communications, unexplained wealth, repeated transfers, and the context of the possession.

- Possession in connection with another felony (§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)). One of the more litigated SOCs is the four‑level enhancement for use or possession of a firearm in connection with another felony, regardless of whether a separate charge was brought or a conviction obtained (see the Guidelines’ commentary, e.g., comment n.14(C)). In Cutbank the PSR recommended and the court applied this four‑level enhancement after finding the firearm was used in connection with other felonious conduct (the domestic‑violence/kidnapping episode), even though there was no separate conviction for a firearm‑in‑furtherance offense. The lesson: prosecutors need only prove the requisite nexus by a preponderance at sentencing, and they may rely on the PSR and trial record; defense counsel must timely object and present countervailing evidence or argument if the facts do not support the nexus.

When these SOCs are contested, litigants should identify the precise factual predicate the Guidelines require — for example, whether the enhancement requires proof that the firearm “facilitated” another felony, or simply that it was “in connection with” another felony — and then present evidence tailored to whether the nexus exists. Courts evaluate the temporal and spatial relationships, the role of the firearm in the other wrongdoing, and reasonable inferences from circumstantial proof. Cutbank’s facts — eyewitness testimony that the defendant had the gun when he took the victim’s phone, threatened her and others, and fled with the weapon — provided the temporal and functional connection the district court relied upon.

## Cross‑references and interaction with other chapters (e.g., § 3C1.2 reckless endangerment during flight)

Section 2K2.1 does not operate in a vacuum. It contains internal cross‑references and interacts with adjustments from other chapters. Two types of cross‑reference frequently arise.

First, § 2K2.1 itself sometimes cross‑references Chapter Two provisions. For example, where a defendant’s conduct during flight or resistance to arrest creates an added danger, § 2K2.1 may authorize a two‑level increase for reckless endangerment while fleeing (or require application of § 3C1.2 in appropriate cases). Cutbank’s PSR recommended a two‑level increase because the defendant recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police — conduct that the district court found and adopted. In practice, the prosecutor must marshal facts showing that flight (or other conduct) produced a realistic risk of serious harm; the court then decides whether that evidence meets the applicable guideline standard.

Second, Chapter Four career–offender rules (and the Booker era interplay between Guidelines and statutory sentencing ranges) can change the result. When a defendant is a career offender under Chapter Four (or otherwise subject to ACCA), the Chapter Four offense level sometimes exceeds the Chapter Two computation. The Guidelines instruct the sentencing judge to apply the greater offense level. Cutbank demonstrates this: the court calculated the § 2K2.1 result and then recognized that the Armed Career Criminal Guideline computation might produce a different total offense level; the Guidelines’ rule (USSG § 4B1.4(b)) directs application of the greater of the two.

These cross‑references make sentencing a multi‑step process: calculate § 2K2.1 base level; add SOCs; consider Chapter Three adjustments and cross‑references; compute any alternative Chapter Four career/ACCA offense level; take the higher total; determine criminal history category; and arrive at the Guidelines range. Each step is an occasion for evidence and argument.

## Relevant conduct and uncharged behavior at sentencing

A perennial and important principle is that sentencing courts may consider relevant conduct beyond the offense of conviction. The Guidelines’ relevant‑conduct rules and 18 U.S.C. § 3661 permit judges to consider “any information” in imposing sentence, subject to the limits of reasonableness review and, for factfinding that increases statutory minimums, the demands of Apprendi/Blakely/Alleyne when applicable.

Cutbank’s PSR included factual statements about violent conduct in the home, the kidnapping and assault of the victim in the presence of her children, the flight and resistance to officers (including biting an officer), the discovery of the sawed‑off rifle along the likely route, and DNA on the weapon. Although some of this conduct was not the subject of separate charges, the PSR used it as relevant conduct to support several enhancements — including the possession‑in‑connection‑with‑another‑felony enhancement and the flight‑related increase. The Eighth Circuit affirmed because the district court adopted the PSR findings and the defendant had not preserved objections that would create reversible error.

Practical implications for counsel: (1) at trial, make clear what conduct the government will rely on solely for sentencing and make appropriate hearsay and confrontation arguments (under current law, many sentencing facts may be found by the judge by a preponderance of the evidence); (2) during the PSR process, vigorously contest any factual allegations not supported by admissible evidence if you want to avoid enhancements based on that conduct; and (3) preserve objections at sentencing so appellate review is not limited to plain‑error standards.

## Evidentiary standards at sentencing: preponderance and reliance on undisputed PSR facts

Sentencing factfinding under the Guidelines follows different evidentiary rules than a criminal trial. The governing principles are:

- Preponderance standard. Except where a judge’s factfinding would increase a statutory mandatory minimum (or otherwise implicate Apprendi line limits), courts typically apply the preponderance of the evidence standard at sentencing to any contested fact that increases the Guidelines range. The Supreme Court and the circuits have accepted this standard in the advisory Guidelines era after Booker.

- PSR as primary vehicle. PSRs are the primary record for sentencing facts. When the defendant objects, the probation office and the parties present evidence at the sentencing hearing. When the defendant does not object to PSR factual statements, a district court may adopt those facts as true; appellate courts review such factual findings for clear error. Cutbank’s appellate difficulty exemplifies this consequence: the court stated that the district court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions,” and relied on those adopted facts to uphold the enhancements. The appellate standard of review for sentencing facts is typically “clear error,” which is deferential.

- Use of hearsay. Hearsay and out‑of‑court statements that might be inadmissible at trial are often admissible for sentencing. The court may consider police reports, victim impact statements, and other hearsay evidence as long as it bears “sufficient indicia of reliability.” But note two caveats: (1) the defense should still contest unreliable or highly prejudicial hearsay, and (2) where the finding would increase a statutory minimum, Sixth Amendment jury rights or related doctrines may restrict judge‑found facts.

- Authentication and extrinsic evidence. At trial, impeachment with extrinsic evidence (for example, reading content from a police report not authored by the witness) requires authentication; the same principles of foundation govern witnesses’ prior statements during cross‑examination. Cutbank shows this difference in contexts: at trial, the district court sustained objections to defense counsel’s attempts to read portions of a police report into the record without proper foundation; the appellate court affirmed the district court’s discretion. At sentencing, by contrast, properly authenticated reports and police testimony are often admissible to support guideline enhancements.

Because courts may rely on PSR findings, an early procedural strategy for defense counsel is to attack the PSR vigorously, create a record, and present contrary evidence. Where a defendant fails to object to PSR statements, appellate courts will treat those facts as admitted for sentencing purposes.

## Procedural posture, preservation, harmless error, and appellate review

Two sentencing process rules that emerge from Cutbank are worth emphasizing.

First, preservation matters. Failure to object to PSR facts or to preserve trial objections limits appellate relief. The Eighth Circuit repeatedly noted that Cutbank “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements” and therefore could not prevail on appeal. The district court’s adoption of unobjected facts is a powerful procedural weapon for the government; defense counsel must preserve issues with precision and timeliness.

Second, appellate standards are deferential. For Guidelines calculations, appellate courts first require that the district court committed no significant procedural error (including a correct Guidelines computation) before they address substantive reasonableness. Gall v. United States instructs courts to start with the correct Guidelines range; Rosales‑Mireles reiterates that an appellate court must ensure no significant procedural error occurred before reviewing reasonableness. On factual findings at sentencing, appellate review is typically for clear error. On the ultimate sentence’s substantive reasonableness, appellate courts give broad deference to district courts, presuming within‑Guidelines sentences are reasonable (as the Eighth Circuit observed in Cutbank). Consequently, attacking the Guidelines calculation and procedural regularity is usually the best appellate path.

Moreover, harmless‑error principles apply to trial evidentiary rulings that might affect a conviction: the Eighth Circuit in Cutbank reiterated that reversal requires showing that the jury “may have been substantially swayed” by improperly admitted evidence and that cumulative errors must produce substantial prejudice to justify reversal. At sentencing, harmless‑error doctrine is similarly operative in reviewing particular hearsay or PSR errors, but the presence of a preserved objection and a clear error in factfinding is the decisive factor on appeal.

## Practical checklist for litigating § 2K2.1 cases

To synthesize, here is a practical step‑by‑step approach a practitioner should follow in a felon‑in‑possession case governed by § 2K2.1:

1. Weapon classification. Immediately identify and document the weapon’s type and any features that might invoke NFA treatment (e.g., sawed‑off barrel/stock, short‑barreled rifle). Preserve measurements, photographs, and expert testimony.

2. Base level calculation. Read § 2K2.1(a) carefully to determine the applicable base offense level given the weapon type and the defendant’s status. Compute any alternative Chapter Four (career offender/ACCA) levels and determine which yields the higher offense level per § 4B1.4(b).

3. Specific offense characteristics. Inventory each SOC listed in § 2K2.1(b) and the government’s potential evidence for them (altered serial number, number of firearms, stolen status, possession in connection with another felony, reckless flight). For each contested SOC, prepare a factual and legal response, and be ready to present testimony or documentary evidence at the PSR hearing.

4. Relevant conduct. Anticipate what uncharged conduct the PSR will include as relevant conduct. Challenge unreliable or prejudicial allegations early in PSR discussions and at the sentencing hearing.

5. Evidentiary strategy. Know that sentencing uses a preponderance standard and that PSR statements not objected to may be adopted. Present reliable evidence and, where appropriate, advocate for an evidentiary hearing rather than accepting hearsay in the PSR without testing it.

6. Preservation. Object timely and on the record to PSR facts and to the application of specific Guidelines provisions. If you fail to object, be prepared to show plain error on appeal — a steep hill.

7. § 3553(a) arguments. After the Guidelines computation, prepare arguments for a variance under § 3553(a). Remember that a district court need not mechanically recite each factor, but the record must show the court considered the factors in imposing a sentence.

8. Appellate posture. If your client is appealing, frame issues as legal errors in Guidelines computation, preserved evidentiary errors, or procedural sentencing errors. Recognize that factual findings adopted from the PSR will be reviewed for clear error and that within‑Guidelines sentences are presumptively reasonable on appeal.

Conclusion

Section 2K2.1 contains many doctrinal moving parts. Cutbank illustrates several of them: the NFA weapon base level, specific offense characteristics for altered serial number and possession in connection with another felony, flight‑related increases, and the practical force of unobjected PSR findings. The core lessons are procedural as much as substantive: identify weapon classification early; contest PSR facts with specificity; understand the preponderance standard and the court’s discretion to rely on reliable hearsay at sentencing; and preserve objections at trial and sentencing so appellate courts review errors for more than plain error. Mastery of § 2K2.1 therefore requires both a firm grasp of the substantive SOCs and disciplined attention to the PSR process and preservation practice.

# The ‘In Connection With’ Enhancement: § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

The Sentencing Guidelines impose a four‑level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) when “a firearm or ammunition was possessed in connection with another felony offense.” The enhancement is one of the most frequently litigated sentencing issues in firearm cases because it turns on the presence of a factual nexus between the weapon and some separate criminal wrongdoing. This chapter explains the text and commentary of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the typical factual situations in which courts find (or refuse to find) the required nexus, the doctrinal tests courts apply to separate facilitation from mere presence, the standards that govern sentencing factfinding and appellate review, and practical proof and defense strategies. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank—both at trial and at sentencing—illustrates how these principles operate in practice.

## Text and commentary: facilitation, potential, and absence of a separate conviction requirement

The Guideline text provides the enhancement when the defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense.” The Commentary supplies essential interpretive guidance. It explains that “in connection with” requires some nexus between the firearm and the other felony—specifically, that the firearm “facilitated, or had the potential of facilitating, another felony offense.” The Commentary also makes clear that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” for that other felony. Thus, the enhancement does not depend on a separate indictment or conviction for the underlying felony; the sentencing court’s factual finding that the weapon facilitated, or could have facilitated, a felony is sufficient.

Two interpretive points follow directly from that text and commentary. First, the focus is functional, not formal: courts look to what the firearm did (or could do) in relation to the other wrongdoing. A weapon that actually enables an assault, intimidates victims, protects contraband, or facilitates flight plainly meets the facilitation test. Second, the Commentary’s “potential” language prevents prosecutors from having to show actual use in a particular transaction. If the weapon’s presence created a realistic capacity to assist in committing a felony, the enhancement can apply even if the defendant never fired a shot or used the gun to threaten someone on the spot.

The Cutbank case demonstrates both ideas. The PSR and trial record showed a domestic episode in which the defendant used a gun to threaten and control his girlfriend, led victims to identify prior possession of that same sawed‑off firearm, and later fled and resisted arrest during which he injured an officer. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements, and the court applied the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on those adopted facts. The Eighth Circuit affirmed: adoption of the PSR’s facts sufficed to establish the necessary connection, and the Commentary permits application even when no separate conviction was charged.

## Common contexts where courts find (or litigate) the enhancement

Certain fact patterns recur in successful § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) claims, and courts have developed practical descriptive labels for them. Understanding these contexts helps counsel anticipate how sentencing courts will view the nexus question.

- Drug offenses and the “fortress” or “stash” theory. Courts frequently apply the enhancement where a gun is found near narcotics, drug paraphernalia, or packaging materials. The rationale is that a firearm protects the drug operation or stash, deters theft or robbery, and therefore facilitates the drug felony. Physical proximity (weapons in the same container, room, or vehicle as drugs) is commonly persuasive to a sentencing judge.

- Assaults and domestic violence. When a gun is present during an assault or threat, or when a defendant brandishes a weapon to control or terrorize a victim, the facilitation nexus is straightforward. Domestic contexts are especially common: a weapon used or displayed during a home confrontation typically provides both demonstrable facilitation (immediate threat) and a realistic potential to enable further crime.

- Burglary, robbery, and other property crimes. A firearm found near burglary tools, in a burglar’s vehicle, or used to threaten victims during a robbery will ordinarily be treated as facilitating the property offense.

- Flight from police and resistance. Courts have sometimes applied the enhancement where the presence or use of a firearm materially increased the likelihood of successful flight or resistance—particularly where the defendant used the gun to force entry, to remove an officer’s access to a weapon, or otherwise to impede arrest. Cutbank’s facts—flight, violent resistance, and an officer bitten and grabbed while the defendant threatened to have a weapon—fit comfortably within this category.

These categories are not exhaustive. What matters is the functional relationship: did the weapon play a role in enabling or increasing the danger of the other felony? That determination turns on the totality of the circumstances.

## Causation and nexus: temporal and spatial proximity; facilitation versus mere presence

The critical inquiry under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is whether there is a sufficient nexus between the firearm and the other felony to show facilitation or potential facilitation. Courts do not apply a single formulaic test; rather they weigh several related factors, taken together:

- Temporal proximity. How close in time was the possession or display of the firearm to the other felony conduct? A gun found immediately before, during, or shortly after an assault or drug sale points toward facilitation. A long temporal gap undermines the inference that the weapon aided the offense.

- Spatial proximity. Where was the gun relative to the location of the other felony conduct? Co‑location in a vehicle, residence, or the immediate scene supports a nexus; possession in a distant or unrelated place weakens it.

- Functional relationship. Did the gun materially increase the likelihood of the felony’s success or the defendant’s ability to commit it? Examples include guns used to threaten or coerce victims, protect contraband, facilitate escape, or keep others from interfering. Courts ask whether the firearm had a role—actual or potential—in advancing the criminative purpose.

- Peculiarity of the firearm and circumstances. Saws‑off shotguns, illegal conversion or modification, or other indicia of readiness for violent misuse can support inference of facilitation. The presence of paraphernalia suggesting an illicit enterprise (packaging, large cash, distribution records) also aids the government’s case.

- Statements and conduct. Victim testimony, admissions by the defendant, witness observations that the gun was displayed to threaten or was habitually used in connection with criminal activity are powerful evidence of nexus.

Crucially, mere presence of a firearm is not enough. Sentencing courts and appellate courts distinguish between a weapon that lay in the vicinity with no reasonable functional relationship to the felony and a weapon that meaningfully supported or could have supported criminal conduct. The court in Cutbank viewed the totality of the evidence—victim testimony of prior possession, DNA on the gun, contemporaneous use to threaten, and flight/resistance—as sufficient to find the required nexus. The Eighth Circuit noted that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements establishing that connection and that the enhancement may be applied even when no separate charge was brought.

## Standards of proof and appellate review

Two distinct thresholds govern: (1) the evidentiary standard the district court applies in resolving disputed sentencing facts, and (2) the standard of appellate review of those district court findings.

- Burden of proof at sentencing. For disputed facts relevant to Guidelines calculations, the sentencing court generally resolves disputes by a preponderance of the evidence. That means the court must find it is more likely than not that the asserted fact is true. This preponderance standard governs typical guideline factfinding under advisory-booker jurisprudence.

- Appellate review of factual findings. Appellate courts review a district court’s factual determinations regarding sentencing enhancements for clear error. A finding is clearly erroneous only if, after reviewing the whole record, the appellate court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. The Cutbank opinion cites this standard when explaining that the district court did not clearly err in adopting the PSR facts to support the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.

- Appellate review of legal interpretation. Questions of law—how to interpret the Guidelines’ text or commentary—are reviewed de novo. If a defendant argues that the commentary’s “potential to facilitate” language has a certain legal meaning, the appellate court reviews that interpretation without deference.

These standards together mean that once a district court makes a reasoned factual finding (e.g., that the gun facilitated another felony) supported by evidence and within the range of reasonable inferences, an appellate court will typically defer unless the finding is unsupported by the record.

## Practical proof at sentencing: witnesses, physical evidence, and PSR narratives

The government typically relies on a combination of testimonial, physical, and documentary evidence to prove the “in connection with” enhancement:

- Victim and eyewitness testimony. Victims who say the defendant brandished or used a gun in the course of committing an assault or in connection with a robbery or domestic incident provide compelling evidence of facilitation. Cutbank’s trial demonstrated this: D.F. and C.F. identified the sawed‑off rifle as the same weapon they had seen with the defendant in the past, and D.F. testified to being threatened and kept in a closet at gunpoint.

- Physical and forensic evidence. Location of the gun relative to contraband, fingerprints or DNA linking the weapon to the defendant, shell casings at a crime scene, or alterations to the weapon can all support a finding that the firearm had a realistic potential to facilitate felony activity. In Cutbank, DNA matched to the defendant and the discovery of the gun along the likely path between the victim’s home and the garage where he barricaded himself strengthened the nexus inference.

- Police reports and investigative narratives. The PSR often collects and synthesizes trial evidence, police reports, and other investigative materials. If the district court adopts or relies upon the PSR’s factual recitation and the defendant fails to challenge those statements, the PSR can supply an adequate factual basis for applying the enhancement. The Cutbank court emphasized that the district court adopted the PSR statements and that such adoption supports the enhancement absent clear error.

- Defendant statements and admissions. Statements made by the defendant—either to police or to witnesses—admitting to possessing a firearm during the commission of another felony are straightforward proof. Counsel should be alert to hearsay and confrontation issues at trial, but at sentencing the court may consider a broader array of admissible materials under the Guidelines’ sentencing procedures.

Defense counsel facing a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement should actively contest the factual basis in the PSR, object to adoption of unverified narratives, and present contrary evidence—witnesses, alibi proof, forensic experts—to undermine temporal or spatial proximity or the functional relationship between the firearm and the alleged felony. Cross‑examination of prosecution witnesses regarding inconsistencies, timing, and location of the weapon can weaken the court’s inference of facilitation.

## Procedural considerations and appellate posture

Several procedural points are important for practitioners:

- Preserve objections at sentencing. If a defendant does not timely object to PSR factual statements that form the basis for the enhancement, courts often treat those facts as adopted and review them only for clear error. Cutbank demonstrates the practical consequence: the district court accepted the PSR facts without objection, and the appellate court affirmed.

- Use of trial evidence at sentencing. Evidence admitted at trial—victim testimony, DNA testing, witness identifications—may be used again at sentencing. If a defendant believes the trial record does not support the enhancement, the proper time to press that point is at sentencing and on appeal with preserved objections.

- Harmless error and cumulative effect. Even if a particular evidentiary ruling at trial was erroneous, appellate courts will not vacate a sentence where the error was harmless in light of the substantial remaining evidence linking the weapon to the felony. The Cutbank opinion reflects this approach in its review of prior evidentiary rulings and the ultimate sentencing outcome.

- Variance arguments under § 3553(a). A district court must correctly calculate the Guidelines range before considering a variance under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). A defendant unhappy with application of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement can argue on the record that, even if correctly calculated, the Guidelines range yields a greater sentence than necessary to satisfy the sentencing factors. Appellate courts will presume a within‑Guidelines sentence reasonable, but they will review whether the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors and whether the sentence was substantively reasonable.

## Applying the doctrine: hypothetical illustrations

- Hypothetical A — drugs and fortress facts. Police search a home and find a semiautomatic pistol in a safe next to kilogram brick packaging materials, scales, and distribution ledgers. The defendant admits the safe belongs to him. Temporal and spatial proximity and the presence of drug distribution indicia support a finding that the firearm facilitated the drug enterprise; courts commonly apply the enhancement in these circumstances.

- Hypothetical B — mere presence. A defendant lawfully keeps a rifle unloaded and secured in a closet in his apartment. That apartment is the scene of a neighbor’s theft of a bicycle from the hallway. The defendant’s locked, stored rifle bears no relationship to the theft. Absent evidence that the weapon was used to facilitate the theft, courts will be reluctant to apply the enhancement.

- Hypothetical C — domestic threat and prior possession. A defendant is charged with felon in possession of a handgun found in his car after he fled a domestic disturbance. The victim testifies that the defendant previously displayed that same handgun to scare her and later threatened to shoot her in the incident. Even if the defendant was not separately charged with aggravated assault, the factual record supports the inference that the gun facilitated the domestic felony, and the enhancement is likely to be applied.

These examples illustrate the flexible, fact‑intensive nature of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) inquiry.

## Conclusion

The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement turns on whether a firearm “facilitated, or had the potential of facilitating” another felony. Courts weigh temporal and spatial proximity, the functional relationship between weapon and crime, the nature of the firearm and surrounding circumstances, and the strength of testimonial and physical evidence. The sentencing court resolves disputed facts by a preponderance of the evidence, factual findings are reviewed for clear error, and legal interpretations of the guideline are reviewed de novo. Cutbank exemplifies how the combination of victim identification, forensic linkage, contemporaneous misconduct (flight and resistance), and an adopted PSR can establish the enhancement even when the defendant was not separately convicted of a related felony. For defense counsel, preserving objections to PSR facts, challenging the nexus with concrete contrary evidence, and pressing § 3553(a) arguments remain the principal means to contest the application or consequences of this frequently dispositive enhancement.

# ACCA and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline (§ 4B1.4): Interaction and Calculations

The interaction between the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), and the Sentencing Guidelines provision that implements it (USSG § 4B1.4) is one of the frequent sources of dispute at sentencing in firearms cases. Courts must determine (1) whether a defendant qualifies as an “armed career criminal” under § 924(e); (2) how that statutory determination interacts with the Guidelines’ directions; and (3) which offense‑level calculation controls when § 2K2.1 (firearms) enhancements and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline both point in different directions. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank, No. 24‑1225 (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), provides a compact illustration of these issues: the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements, applied § 2K2.1 enhancements (including a four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement), computed the Guidelines range, and—because § 4B1.4 instructs the sentencing court to apply the greater of two offense‑level calculations—arrived at a within‑Guidelines sentence that the court of appeals affirmed. This chapter explains the doctrinal rules a student must master to predict and analyze such outcomes.

## ACCA predicates: ‘violent felony’ after Johnson’s residual‑clause invalidation; enumerated and elements clauses (overview)

ACCA’s sentence‑enhancing machinery turns on whether the defendant has three prior convictions for a “violent felony” or serious drug offense. The statutory definition of “violent felony” contains three parts: (1) the elements clause (often called the “force clause”)—crimes that “have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another”; (2) the enumerated offenses clause—certain named offenses (burglary, arson, extortion, and crimes involving explosives); and (3) the residual clause that covered “otherwise similar” crimes that “involve conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” In Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), the Supreme Court held the residual clause void for vagueness. Since Johnson, only the elements clause and the enumerated‑offenses clause are available to qualify an earlier conviction as a violent felony under ACCA.

That ruling has three practical consequences. First, sentencing courts may no longer bootstrap ill‑defined analogies to sweep in many prior offenses; after Johnson the inquiry is narrower. Second, courts apply the categorical approach (from Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990)) to determine whether a prior conviction qualifies. Under the categorical approach the sentencing court compares (on paper) the elements of the prior statute with the elements of the generic offense that constitutes a violent felony. If the statute’s elements are the same as, or narrower than, the generic offense, the prior conviction qualifies. If the statute is broader, the conviction does not qualify—unless the statute is divisible into alternative elements, in which case the court may use the modified categorical approach to consult a limited set of judicial documents (Shepard materials) to identify which statutory alternative the defendant was convicted under. Shepard v. United States, 544 U.S. 13 (2005), restricts permissible documents to the charging papers, plea agreement, transcript of colloquy, or comparable judicially‑authorized records that definitively show which alternative formed the basis of conviction.

Third, because Johnson excised the residual clause, many convictions that courts once treated as ACCA predicates no longer qualify. That can affect whether a defendant is statutorily an armed career criminal at all—an issue that substantially changes both statutory sentencing exposure (ACCA carries a mandatory minimum of 15 years) and the Guidelines inputs discussed below.

## Guideline mechanics under § 4B1.4: offense level alternatives and automatic Criminal History Category VI

USSG § 4B1.4 is the Sentencing Guidelines’ specific rule for defendants subject to ACCA’s statutory enhancement. The guideline has two key mechanical features that every student must understand.

First, § 4B1.4 creates alternative offense‑level calculations and instructs the sentencing court to apply the greater of those alternatives. In practice the guideline requires the court to compare (A) the offense level calculated under the ordinary firearms Guideline, § 2K2.1 (including any specific‑offense characteristic enhancements and adjustments), and (B) the offense level determined under the career‑criminal/ACCA bracket in § 4B1.4. The guideline text resolves which offense level controls by directing the court to use whichever results in a higher total offense level. The Cutbank opinion demonstrates this rule in operation: the PSR’s § 2K2.1 calculations (base level for a sawed‑off firearm, plus adjustments and enhancements) produced a total offense level of 35; the calculation under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34. Because § 4B1.4 required use of the greater offense level, the district court adopted level 35, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

Second, § 4B1.4 fixes the defendant’s criminal‑history category at VI. For sentencing purposes the Guidelines treat an ACCA defendant as having the highest default criminal history category. This automatic elevation often pushes the Guidelines range substantially higher than the range that would obtain absent ACCA—because offense level is combined with Criminal History Category to determine the sentenced range in the Guidelines table.

These features mean that two separate influences flow from ACCA at sentencing: the statutory mandatory minimum and the Guidelines mechanics. The statute itself creates higher statutory exposure; § 4B1.4 ensures the defendant also receives the more severe Guidelines inputs (greater of § 2K2.1 or § 4B1.4 calculation and CHC VI). The district court must calculate both alternatives and take the greater offense level; it must set CHC at VI. Once the Guidelines range is correctly computed, the court then turns to § 3553(a) factors to determine whether a within‑range sentence is appropriate or whether a variance is warranted.

## Choosing the greater offense level when § 2K2.1 and § 4B1.4 both apply

A common source of litigation is that § 2K2.1 enhancements often can push the § 2K2.1 offense level above the level that would result from treating the defendant solely as an armed career criminal under § 4B1.4. The Guidelines’ answer—apply the greater offense level—resolves that conflict, but the answer generates subsidiary questions about factfinding and proof.

The first practical question is how the § 2K2.1 level is calculated. § 2K2.1 provides a base offense level tied to characteristics of the weapon (e.g., a sawed‑off rifle), then lists numerous specific‑offense characteristics (altered serial number, possession in connection with drug trafficking or other felony, reckless endangerment, etc.). Those enhancements, when supported by the record, raise the § 2K2.1 total offense level. In Cutbank the PSR included a four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possession in connection with another felony (the defendant’s violent conduct against his girlfriend), and additional enhancements for altered serial number and reckless flight. Those adjustments raised the § 2K2.1 total above what the Armed Career Criminal Guideline alone would have produced; § 4B1.4(b) required use of the higher figure.

The second question is whether and how those § 2K2.1 enhancements must be proven. Legal standards divide the inquiry. Purely legal interpretations of the Guidelines (e.g., whether a listed offense characteristic applies in a given legal situation) are reviewed de novo; factual findings to support a specific enhancement are reviewed for clear error. Where a contentious enhancement depends on disputed conduct, the district court may rely on the PSR if the PSR’s facts are adopted (and not sufficiently disputed) or on evidence at trial. In Cutbank the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements—unchallenged—and thus the court did not clearly err in finding the § 2K2.1 enhancements established.

The third question is constitutional: whether a sentence is governed by facts that must be submitted to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The Supreme Court’s Apprendi line requires that any fact (other than a prior conviction) that increases the statutory maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. But ACCA’s predicate is a prior conviction, and the Supreme Court has allowed judicial factfinding on prior convictions in Almendarez‑Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224 (1998). That principle still governs the use of prior convictions for enhancing statutory exposure under ACCA. By contrast, specific‑offense‑characteristic enhancements in the Guidelines (for example, for possession in connection with another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)) are sentencing facts. Unless they increase the statutory maximum or mandatory minimum, they do not implicate Apprendi. When the enhancement does, however, constitutional rules may apply (see Alleyne). In practice, many § 2K2.1 enhancements affect only the Guidelines offense level (and thus the advisory range) and are therefore judicially determined under the ordinary standards of proof for sentencing (preponderance of the evidence in the federal system, subject to circuit variation and Booker-related principles). The Cutbank court, invoking the customary standard and the district court’s adoption of the PSR, treated the § 2K2.1 enhancements as properly established for Guidelines purposes.

The practical upshot: in every case where a defendant is an armed career criminal, the sentencing court computes two offense‑level outcomes (§ 2K2.1 total and the § 4B1.4 calculation), applies the greater, fixes CHC at VI, and then moves to § 3553(a) analysis. The court’s factual findings to support § 2K2.1 enhancements must be supported in the record; they are reviewed for clear error if based on PSR findings the court adopts.

## Charging practice vs. sentencing reality: jury findings on ACCA and judicial factfinding for enhancements

There is a structural split between criminal charging/proof rules and sentencing realities. Charging and proof rules guard the Sixth Amendment right to jury trial and the requirement that the government prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Sentencing rules, by contrast, permit a broader range of judicial factfinding for Guidelines calculations, subject to constitutional limits.

ACCA predicates occupy an unusual middle ground. Whether a defendant has three prior qualifying convictions can be established by certified conviction records—something the government can do without presenting witnesses—and courts have long permitted a trial judge to find prior convictions (Almendarez‑Torres). Many prosecutors nevertheless include a special verdict or a plea to the prior convictions in the indictment so that the jury can—or must—resolve the predicate question. That procedural choice affects appellate posture, but it does not alter the essential rule: prior convictions are treated differently for sentencing than other facts. In Cutbank, by way of example, the jury found Cutbank had at least three prior violent felonies—an affirmative jury finding. But that jury finding was not strictly necessary to the district court’s authority to rely on the convictions at sentencing if they could be established by records and PSR material. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion notes that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements regarding prior convictions without objection—an approach courts often use to avoid separate trials or mini‑trials on prior records.

By contrast, enhancements under § 2K2.1 that involve conduct (e.g., possession “in connection with” another felony, reckless endangerment during flight) are sentencing facts. Although courts generally may decide those facts at sentencing, the defendant is entitled to notice and a meaningful opportunity to contest the alleged conduct. The defense in Cutbank raised evidentiary objections at trial and at sentencing, but the district court resolved them and adopted the PSR. The Eighth Circuit applied the ordinary standards: the district court’s factual findings at sentencing were not clearly erroneous, and its guidelines calculation was procedurally correct. The court then affirmed the substantive reasonableness of a within‑range sentence because the record shows the district court considered § 3553(a) factors.

For practitioners this means two things. First, charging the ACCA predicates in the indictment and obtaining jury findings on predicates may have strategic value, but it is not constitutionally required, and many recidivist predicates are resolved through judicial factfinding using certified records. Second, litigating § 2K2.1 enhancements requires careful preparation to contest the factual basis the PSR will likely use—because those contested facts may control whether § 2K2.1 or § 4B1.4 yields the greater offense level.

## Illustration: when § 2K2.1 enhancements push above the ACCA default offense level

Concrete examples help students see how these rules play out numerically. Consider the Cutbank fact pattern as an instructive model.

- Imagine the base offense level for unlawful possession of a firearm (because the gun is a sawed‑off rifle) is 26 under § 2K2.1. The PSR adds a three‑level enhancement for an altered serial number and a two‑level enhancement for reckless creation of risk of death while fleeing from police. That would produce a subtotal of 31. If the PSR then adds a four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement because the firearm was possessed in connection with another felony—domestic‑violence, kidnapping, or assault—then the § 2K2.1 total offense level becomes 35. With an automatic Criminal History Category VI under § 4B1.4, that offense level produces a Guidelines range (in the Cutbank opinion) of 292–365 months.

- By contrast, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline alternative might produce a different offense level (in the opinion the ACCA guideline calculation produced level 34). Because § 4B1.4 directs the court to use the greater offense level, the court applies level 35 (the § 2K2.1 total) rather than 34 (ACCA alternative), producing the larger Guidelines range.

Two lessons emerge. First, enhancements under § 2K2.1—especially the broad “in connection with” enhancement found in comment n.14(C) and the specific‑offense characteristic that applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained”—can add substantial levels and may be decisive. The Commentary defines “in connection with” broadly, and courts often apply it where the firearm facilitated, or had the potential to facilitate, other felony conduct. Cutbank’s possession of the sawed‑off in the course of a violent domestic episode supported such an enhancement in the PSR and at sentencing. Second, because the Guidelines require selection of the greater offense level, aggressive pursuit of § 2K2.1 enhancements at sentencing can result in higher advisory ranges than the bare ACCA calculation would produce. Practitioners must therefore litigate both the qualification as an ACCA defendant and the factual basis for any § 2K2.1 enhancements.

A final practical point is statutory minimums. Where ACCA imposes a statutory mandatory minimum (15 years), the Guidelines range must be computed in a manner that accounts for the statutory minimum—sometimes the Guidelines range is driven by the statutory minimum rather than the Guidelines offense level. Even then, § 4B1.4’s automatic CHC VI and the “greater of” rule remain part of the calculus. In Cutbank, the final sentence was within the Guidelines range computed after applying the greater offense level and CHC VI, and the appellate court treated the within‑range sentence as presumptively reasonable but still reviewed for procedural correctness (Gall, Rosales‑Mireles) and for clear error in factfinding.

Conclusion

The Cutbank decision illustrates the doctrinal architecture students must master when ACCA and the firearms Guidelines collide. Johnson narrowed ACCA predicates to the elements and enumerated clauses; the categorical approach and Shepard constrain how prior convictions are deemed qualifying. For Guidelines purposes § 4B1.4 requires (1) computation of the § 2K2.1 offense level (with any applicable enhancements), (2) computation of the Armed Career Criminal alternative, (3) selection of the greater offense level, and (4) assignment of Criminal History Category VI. Sentencing practice therefore revolves around two parallel contests: proving the ACCA predicates and proving the conduct that supports § 2K2.1 enhancements. Both sorts of findings have distinct procedural postures and standards of review (judge‑found sentencing facts reviewed for clear error; legal interpretations reviewed de novo), and both affect the ultimate advisory range that a court will consider in deciding whether a within‑range sentence comports with § 3553(a). Cutbank confirms that where the PSR’s facts supporting § 2K2.1 enhancements are adopted and not clearly erroneous, a district court may permissibly select the higher § 2K2.1 offense level under § 4B1.4 and impose a within‑range sentence that the court of appeals will generally presume reasonable.

# Procedural and Substantive Reasonableness: Gall, Rita, and Eighth Circuit Presumptions

This chapter examines the post-Booker appellate framework for reviewing federal sentences. It explains the two-part inquiry courts undertake—procedural reasonableness followed by substantive reasonableness—identifies the standards of review that apply to the principal kinds of sentencing determinations, and describes how the Eighth Circuit treats within-Guidelines sentences on appeal. The court of appeals’ opinion in United States v. Cutbank exemplifies the interaction of these doctrines: the district court’s Guidelines calculation and its adoption of the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) bear on procedural review; the district court’s recognition of § 3553(a) factors and its choice to impose a within-Guidelines sentence illustrate the presumption of reasonableness the Eighth Circuit affords such sentences.

The doctrinal architecture rests on Booker’s transformation of the Guidelines into advisory standards; on Gall and Rita, which govern the allocation of responsibilities and the scope of appellate deference; and on later Supreme Court and circuit cases that refine the sequence of appellate review. Students should be able to (1) distinguish procedural errors that require correction before the merits of a sentence can be reviewed, (2) identify what constitutes an adequate explanation and consideration of arguments and § 3553(a) factors, and (3) predict how an appellate court—particularly the Eighth Circuit—will treat a within-Guidelines sentence and preserved mitigation arguments.

## Procedural reasonableness: correct range, responding to arguments, adequate explanation (Gall; Rosales-Mireles)

Procedural reasonableness concerns the correctness of the process the district court used to arrive at a sentence. The principal procedural requirements are (1) correct calculation of the applicable Guidelines range, (2) consideration of the statutorily enumerated § 3553(a) factors, (3) meaningful response to any nonfrivolous arguments for a different sentence by the parties, and (4) an adequate explanation for the chosen sentence, particularly when a court imposes a variance or departure.

The Supreme Court in Gall v. United States distilled these requirements into a clear allocation of responsibilities. Gall instructs district courts to calculate the Guidelines range, to permit parties to argue for a sentence outside the range, to consider the § 3553(a) factors, and, if selecting a sentence outside the range, to explain adequately the chosen variance. Gall also sets forth the appellate approach: first, the appellate court must ensure there was no significant procedural error; second, it must review the substantive reasonableness of the sentence for abuse of discretion.

Rosales-Mireles v. United States reinforces the sequencing emphasized in Gall. Before an appellate court conducts a substantive-reasonableness inquiry, it must ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate the Guidelines range correctly or otherwise procedurally erring in a way that could affect the substantive outcome. This sequence matters because procedural defects—miscalculation of the Guidelines or failure to consider or respond to arguments under § 3553(a)—can render a sentence substantively unreasonable as well; therefore, they must be identified and corrected first.

How do courts apply these principles in concrete terms? They treat questions of law about the Guidelines (e.g., whether an enhancement applies) de novo, and review the district court’s factual findings (for example, whether the defendant possessed a firearm in connection with another offense) for clear error. Discretionary determinations—such as whether to grant a downward variance—are reviewed for abuse of discretion. In practice, this tripartite formulation means that an appellate court will carefully examine (a) whether the Guidelines calculation rested on legally correct interpretations and adequately supported factual findings, and (b) whether the district court acknowledged and addressed the critical sentencing arguments and § 3553(a) considerations raised by the parties. In Cutbank, for example, the district court adopted the PSR and its factual statements about prior convictions and enhancements; the court then reviewed § 3553(a) factors and expressly considered mitigation arguments. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit concluded there was no procedural error and therefore moved to the substantive-abuse-of-discretion inquiry.

## Substantive reasonableness: abuse-of-discretion review of length; Rita’s appellate presumption

Once procedural reasonableness is established, an appellate court reviews the substantive reasonableness of the sentence for abuse of discretion. That is a highly deferential standard: an appellate court will affirm unless the sentence “falls outside the broad range of choices reasonably available to the district court,” or unless the district court’s stated reasons are inadequate or irrational in light of the § 3553(a) factors.

Rita v. United States addressed how appellate courts may treat within-Guidelines sentences. Although Booker made the Guidelines advisory, Rita held that an appellate court may apply a presumption of reasonableness to a sentence within the properly calculated Guidelines range. The presumption is not an irrebuttable rule and does not relieve an appellate court of its obligation to review for abuse of discretion; rather, it recognizes that a within-Guidelines sentence is likely to be reasonable because the Guidelines reflect national sentencing practices and the Commission’s empirical work.

After Rita and Gall, the inquiry on appeal thus typically unfolds as follows: (1) did the district court correctly calculate the Guidelines range and address procedural requirements? (2) if so, did the district court impose a sentence within that range? If yes, many circuits—including the Eighth—apply a presumption that the sentence is reasonable, and the defendant bears a substantial burden to demonstrate the sentence is substantively unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors and the record. If the court imposed a variance, the sentencing judge must provide a sufficiently detailed explanation so an appellate court can determine whether the variance was justified.

It is important to remember that the Rita presumption operates at the appellate level and does not alter the district court’s duty to reason explicitly about the § 3553(a) factors if the chosen sentence departs materially from what those factors would otherwise indicate. District courts must still “consider” the statutory factors and must explain deviations sufficiently for meaningful appellate review.

## Eighth Circuit applications: presumption for within-range sentences (e.g., Goodhouse) and treatment of preserved mitigation (e.g., Masood; Maluoth)

The Eighth Circuit applies the Rita presumption and follows the Gall/Rosales-Mireles sequencing. The circuit treats within-Guidelines sentences as presumptively reasonable on appeal. In United States v. Goodhouse, the court stated that a within-Guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable; although this presumption is rebuttable, it allocates a substantial burden to the defendant on appeal. That presumption animated the outcome in Cutbank: because the district court imposed a within-Guidelines sentence and properly calculated the range (adopting the PSR), the Eighth Circuit began with a presumption that the sentence was reasonable and sustained the sentence absent a showing of procedural error or substantive abuse of discretion.

Two recent Eighth Circuit decisions illustrate how the court treats preserved mitigation arguments and determines whether the district court actually considered § 3553(a) factors. In United States v. Masood, the court refused to find an abuse of discretion where the record showed the district court was aware of and rejected the defendant’s arguments—an express statement that the court had considered the arguments sufficed for appellate review. Likewise, in United States v. Maluoth the court explained that a district court need not provide a “mechanical recitation” of each § 3553(a) factor; rather, it must be clear from the record that the district court considered the factors in choosing a sentence. The Cutbank opinion quotes both principles to uphold a within-range sentence where the district court had reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and noted the § 3553(a) factors. In short, the Eighth Circuit enforces procedural sufficiency—awareness, consideration, and a demonstrable link between facts and reasoning—without demanding a formal checklist recitation from the sentencing judge.

These cases send a clear message to practitioners: preserve objections and argue mitigation clearly at sentencing. If the defendant preserves an objection and the record shows the district court considered the argument and rejected it, the appellate court is unlikely to second-guess the district court’s judgment except in rare cases where the sentence is outside the available range of reasonable choices.

## Building a record: articulating § 3553(a) factors and variances

Because appellate review is heavily based on the written and oral record from sentencing, defense counsel seeking a non-Guidelines sentence must build a clear record. Here are practical rules and techniques:

- Preserve objections to the PSR and to any disputed Guidelines application. A timely objection in the district court is essential for de novo review of legal questions and for avoiding forfeiture. If the PSR contains disputed facts that support an enhancement, present contrary evidence and obtain an explicit ruling resolving the factual dispute.

- Present a focused § 3553(a) argument in writing and orally. A sentencing memorandum that ties particular facts to the statutory factors—such as family responsibilities, rehabilitation progress, disproportionate sentence relative to past conduct, or mitigating mental-health evidence—gives the court the material it needs to assess a variance. During allocution, ask the court to state on the record (or in the written judgment) how it evaluated the arguments and which factors it found persuasive or unpersuasive.

- If seeking a variance, explain the magnitude and rationale for the variance. The district court should know not only why a variance is warranted but also how large the proposed variance should be and why that magnitude is sufficient but not greater than necessary.

- Request specific findings if the record is deficient. Where a judge appears to rely on generic statements or to overlook a central mitigating fact, defense counsel should ask the judge to articulate its reasons. Asking for the court to explain its decision to the extent necessary for appellate review is a routine and appropriate practice.

- Use the opportunity to create evidentiary support: letters, expert reports, employment and treatment records, and other materials that demonstrate rehabilitation or disproportionality. The more the district court has before it, the easier it is for the court to engage § 3553(a) factors and for an appellate court to review the sufficiency of the explanation.

Cutbank illustrates the value of these practices: the PSR was adopted by the district court, the court stated that it had considered the sentencing memoranda and § 3553(a) factors, and the defendant’s arguments were on the record. That procedural posture made reversal unlikely on appeal.

## Common pitfalls: miscalculations vs. disagreements with policy

Sentencing practice generates recurrent errors and misunderstandings. Students and practitioners should be alert to these common pitfalls.

- Treating a policy disagreement with the Guidelines as a procedural error. Defendants often complain that the Guidelines’ calculation produces an unduly harsh sentence. Absent a legal misapplication of the Guidelines or an unsupported factual finding, such a complaint is a disagreement about the sentence’s substantive fairness. Appellate courts do not reweigh the § 3553(a) factors de novo because the standard is abuse of discretion for substantive reasonableness. Put another way: a mere belief that the sentence is too long does not establish a procedural error.

- Failing to preserve vital objections. If the defendant does not object to a Guidelines calculation or a PSR fact at sentencing, appellate review may be limited to plain-error review (or the appeal may be foreclosed), making reversal difficult. Preservation is especially important where factual findings in the PSR support an enhancement that materially affects the Guidelines range.

- Expecting a lengthy verbatim explanation from the district court. Appellate courts require enough of an explanation to permit meaningful review, but they do not demand a line-by-line recitation of every § 3553(a) factor. However, what a district court cannot do is plainly ignore or refuse to consider nonfrivolous arguments. A short but clear statement that the court considered the written submissions, the § 3553(a) factors, and the defendant’s allocution will frequently suffice.

- Confusing legal and factual standards of review. Errors of law (e.g., misinterpreting a Guidelines provision) are reviewed de novo and can lead to resentencing. Factual findings (e.g., whether the defendant possessed a weapon in connection with another felony) are reviewed for clear error. Counsel should identify immediately whether the dispute is legal, factual, or discretionary and preserve appropriate objections.

- Relying on cumulative-error arguments without showing prejudice. Appellate courts will not reverse based on multiple small evidentiary or procedural rulings unless the cumulative effect substantially influenced the verdict or the sentence. The burden of showing substantial prejudice is a high one.

## Applying the framework: lessons from Cutbank

United States v. Cutbank provides a compact illustration of these principles. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings (a procedural act that anchored the Guidelines calculation), applied the enhancements—including the four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony—and sentenced the defendant within the resulting Guidelines range. On appeal, Cutbank challenged evidentiary rulings and the application of the enhancement. The Eighth Circuit applied the Gall/Rosales-Mireles sequencing: it first confirmed that there was no procedural error in calculating the Guidelines range (the PSR was adopted, and the factual findings were not clearly erroneous); it then applied the Eighth Circuit’s presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable and reviewed for substantive abuse of discretion. Because the district court had considered the § 3553(a) factors and the defendant had a serious criminal history and violent conduct supporting a lengthy sentence, the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The case underscores these critical lessons for practitioners and students:

- Early and explicit objections to PSR facts and Guidelines calculations are crucial. Adopting the PSR without a ruling on a timely objection leaves those facts firmly in the record for appellate review.

- Preserve, present, and document § 3553(a) arguments. The Eighth Circuit will assume the district court considered the arguments if the record shows the court reviewed sentencing memoranda and discussed the factors; nevertheless, making the arguments plainly and supporting them with evidence increases the chance of prevailing at sentencing or on appeal.

- Expect appellate deference. In the Eighth Circuit, within-Guidelines sentences enjoy a presumption of reasonableness; overcoming that presumption requires demonstrating either a significant procedural error or that the sentence constitutes a clear abuse of discretion given the § 3553(a) factors.

Conclusion

The post-Booker landscape requires a disciplined, record-oriented approach at sentencing. District courts must calculate the Guidelines correctly, afford the parties the chance to argue for a deviation, and consider the § 3553(a) factors. Appellate courts must ensure the procedural baseline is satisfied before exercising the deferential abuse-of-discretion standard for substantive reasonableness. In the Eighth Circuit, a within-Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable; defendants who wish to challenge such a sentence on appeal need either to show procedural error in the Guidelines calculation or to persuade the appellate court that the sentence was substantively unreasonable in light of the record and the § 3553(a) factors. The Cutbank opinion provides a recent, concrete example of these principles in action.

# United States v. Cutbank as a Teaching Vehicle

This chapter uses United States v. Cutbank to illuminate several recurring evidentiary and sentencing doctrines that arise in federal criminal trials. Cutbank is a compact example: the case presents disputes about the admissibility of prior-possession evidence, the proper treatment of witness statements made to police, the bounds of cross-examination and impeachment with documentary police reports, and the application of a §2K2.1 enhancement at sentencing — all decided against the defendant under the standards that govern district courts and courts of appeals. Each section below explains the governing legal principles and then shows how the Eighth Circuit applied them in Cutbank.

## Intrinsic evidence of prior possession of the same sawed-off rifle: reliance on Battle; continuous possession rationale

A common prosecution theory in a felon-in-possession case is to prove ownership or control of the weapon by showing the defendant’s prior possession of that same firearm. Cutbank challenged testimony by two witnesses that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 on prior occasions, arguing that such evidence was improper propensity proof under Rule 404(b). The district court — and the Eighth Circuit on appeal — rejected that argument, treating the testimony as intrinsic evidence of possession rather than as extrinsic “other acts” evidence.

The doctrinal distinction matters. Rule 404(b) governs “other crimes, wrongs, or acts” offered for a purpose such as intent or absence of mistake; such evidence is admissible only if it meets the Rule 404(b) framework (notice, probative purpose other than propensity, and Rule 403 balancing, where required). But where the evidence is intrinsic — that is, it arises out of the same transaction or is necessary to complete the story of the offense, or where it directly proves an element such as possession or knowledge — it is not “other acts” evidence and need not be submitted under Rule 404(b). The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Battle illustrates the point: prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to establishing later possession because it helps prove control, access, or ownership. The court in Cutbank cited Battle and applied that logic: testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the night in question was directly probative of his possession of that weapon during the charged offense.

Students should be able to apply this distinction. Ask whether the prior-act evidence serves to explain the charged conduct or is being offered simply to show a defendant’s bad character or propensity. If the evidence makes it more likely that the defendant possessed the particular weapon at the relevant time (for example, because the weapon is distinctive and the witnesses saw the defendant with it previously), it is often intrinsic and admissible. If, by contrast, the evidence is remote in time and offered simply to show a defendant’s violent tendencies, it is more likely to fall within Rule 404(b). In Cutbank, the sawed-off rifle was a distinctive, identifiable item, and testimony that the defendant had been seen with it before the incident bore directly on who controlled the gun that later was found and recovered with the defendant’s DNA on it.

## Police-response testimony: non-hearsay context and Confrontation satisfied because declarant testified

Cutbank also sought a jury instruction forbidding the jury from considering D.F.’s out-of-court statements to police “as substantive evidence” until she testified, raising hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns. The district court declined to give the preemptive limiting instruction, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that ruling.

Two doctrinal points intersect here. First, not every statement made to police is offered for the truth of the matter asserted; many such statements are offered for a non-hearsay purpose — for example, to explain why officers took certain actions, to show their state of mind or knowledge at the time, or to provide context for the investigation. Statements admitted for such non-hearsay purposes do not offend the hearsay rule. Second, the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause is implicated when a testimonial out-of-court statement is offered against the accused and the declarant does not testify; but when the declarant appears and testifies at trial, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because the defendant has the opportunity to cross-examine the witness about the earlier statements.

In Cutbank the court noted both points. The government proffered officers’ testimony about what they were told to explain the officers’ investigative and tactical responses; the district court did not admit impermissible hearsay and, importantly, the declarant (D.F.) later testified at trial. Citing Charboneau, the Eighth Circuit reiterated the settled rule that confrontation is satisfied when the out-of-court declarant testifies in person. Practically, a pretrial limiting instruction that attempts to segregate every potentially problematic statement is unnecessary if the court correctly admits evidence for non-hearsay purposes and if the declarant testifies at trial; a bedrock appellate principle is that limiting instructions need not accompany the admission of each item of evidence as it is introduced so long as an appropriate instruction is given at closing or the evidence is otherwise properly admitted.

Students learning this material should focus on the analytical sequence a trial judge applies: identify the purpose for which the statement is offered (hearsay-for-truth or non-hearsay); if offered for truth, determine whether any hearsay exception or Confrontation Clause requirement applies; if offered for a non-hearsay purpose, admit subject to relevance and possible Rule 403 balancing. Also remember that if the declarant later testifies, prior police statements become less problematic under the Confrontation Clause, although they can still raise hearsay questions if admitted for their truth and the declarant’s testimony does not squarely place them before the jury as prior inconsistent statements or adopt them.

## Rule 403 and opening the door: allowing rehabilitative context about victim’s aversion to guns

During cross-examination about prior inconsistent statements, D.F. volunteered that she was “not familiar with guns” and that she “did not like guns” because her sister had been killed by a gun. Cutbank moved to strike the reference to the sister’s death; the district court denied the motion, and the Eighth Circuit found no abuse of discretion.

Two principles are important here. First, Rule 403 gives the court broad discretion to exclude evidence when its probative value is substantially outweighed by risks such as unfair prejudice, confusion, or wasting time. Second, cross-examination that elicits inconsistency often “opens the door” to rehabilitative or explanatory testimony. When a witness is asked on cross about inconsistency, the witness may be permitted to explain the inconsistency on redirect, or even to do so on cross, as a responsive answer; courts generally will not order a sweeping erasure of responsive testimony that fairly rebuts an impeachment attempt.

In Cutbank, D.F.’s reference to her sister’s death was invited by cross-examination aimed at her inconsistent statements about seeing the gun. The court reasonably treated the reference as context that explained why D.F. might react to or describe guns a certain way. The defendant pointed to no persuasive demonstration that the mention of the sister’s death had substantial unfair prejudice that would warrant exclusion under Rule 403. The Eighth Circuit’s conclusion reflects the deferential standard appellate courts apply to district courts’ Rule 403 decisions: absent a clear abuse of discretion, admission of such responsive testimony stands.

A practical lesson for students: timing and context matter. A collateral reference to a traumatic event may be admissible if it is responsive to impeachment and helps explain a witness’s perceptions or credibility, but the trial court may still exclude it if its prejudicial effect substantially outweighs probative value. The trial judge’s balancing call receives substantial deference on appeal.

## Impeachment via police reports: authentication required for extrinsic proof; permissible scope of cross-examination

Cutbank’s counsel attempted to read portions of a police report into the record to impeach C.F.’s testimony about whether she had previously seen the gun. The district court sustained objections because the report was not authored by C.F. and the defense lacked foundation to admit the report as an extrinsic document. The Eighth Circuit approved that ruling and invoked the general rule that extrinsic evidence used to impeach a witness must be authenticated.

Understanding when a party may use extrinsic evidence to impeach is essential. Federal Rule of Evidence 613(b) permits extrinsic evidence of a witness’s prior inconsistent statement only if the witness is afforded the opportunity to explain or deny the statement, and the opposing party is given an opportunity to interrogate the witness about it, except where the extrinsic evidence is a writing adopted or approved by the witness or where the prior statement is one of identification. Separately, extrinsic documents must be authenticated under Rule 901 (or tend to fall within a hearsay exception). A police report prepared by an officer is, at least initially, hearsay if offered for the truth of the matter asserted and may require either a hearsay exception (e.g., recorded recollection under Rule 803(5)) or proper foundation by the preparer or another witness with knowledge to qualify for admission.

In Cutbank the court’s enforcement of authentication and foundational requirements was routine. Defense counsel could have pursued impeachment in other permissible ways: for example, by asking the witness whether she had made certain statements to a particular officer and then using the officer’s live testimony or properly authenticated portions of the report (with the preparer on the stand) to impeach, or by laying the foundation that would permit the police report to be admitted under an applicable hearsay exception. The district court was not required to allow the reading of unauthenticated portions of a report to the jury. This enforcement of foundational rules strikes a balance between permitting meaningful impeachment and protecting the tribunal from unreliable documentary evidence.

Students should be able to identify the pathways by which extrinsic documentary impeachment may be accomplished (e.g., use of the author as a witness, establishing recorded recollection, or using a prior inconsistent statement that meets Rule 801(d)(1)(A)’s nonhearsay criteria), and the limits on a cross-examiner’s ability to simply “read in” documents without foundation.

## Harmless and cumulative error: strong physical (DNA, recovery path) and testimonial evidence foreclosing prejudice

When a defendant challenges multiple evidentiary rulings on appeal, courts often consider both whether any individual ruling was erroneous and whether the cumulative effect of any errors deprived the defendant of a fair trial. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank applied the harmless-error doctrine and the cumulative-error principle to reject the defendant’s attack.

Harmless-error analysis asks whether the erroneously admitted (or excluded) evidence likely had a substantial influence on the jury’s verdict. If the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, reversal is not required. The cumulative-error doctrine recognizes that multiple errors, none individually reversible, might in combination have a prejudicial effect that warrants reversal. But the threshold is high: the cumulative errors must produce substantial prejudice to the defendant’s right to a fair trial.

Cutbank’s case illustrates why many evidentiary errors are held harmless when the government’s proof is otherwise strong. The record included DNA on the gun matching Cutbank, physical items (the victim’s cellphone, a butcher knife) found along the likely path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself, eyewitness identifications of the sawed-off rifle belonging to Cutbank, and testimony about Cutbank’s threatening conduct that evening. Given that body of corroborating physical and testimonial evidence, the court concluded that any erroneous evidentiary rulings could not have substantially swayed the jury. On appeal, the defendant therefore could not meet the burden to show prejudice from the alleged errors, and the conviction stood.

For students: when evaluating harmlessness, analyze the strength and coherence of the admitted evidence apart from the challenged items. Look for independent proof that ties the defendant to the crime (DNA, fingerprints, the recovery of uniquely identifiable property, eyewitness testimony directly linking defendant and object), and consider whether the challenged evidence was cumulative of other properly admitted proof. Cumulative-error review requires viewing the trial as a whole; multiple minor errors may remain harmless if they do not together undermine confidence in the verdict.

## §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement: use “in connection with” another felony established via undisputed PSR; standard of review

At sentencing, the central dispute concerned the four-level enhancement under USSG §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when the defendant “used or possessed any firearm . . . in connection with another felony offense.” The Guidelines commentary clarifies that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (comment n.14(C)). Cutbank argued that the enhancement was improper; the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

Two sentencing principles are important here. First, disputes about the facts underlying guideline enhancements are reviewed for clear error when the district court adopts a PSR that contains undisputed facts. If the defendant does not object at sentencing, the PSR’s factual statements may become binding and are reviewed for clear error on appeal. Second, to trigger §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) the government must show a nexus between the firearm and another felony — a connection more than mere temporal coincidence but less than proof that the firearm facilitated the felony beyond all doubt. The enhancement’s focus is on whether possession of the firearm facilitated or had the potential to facilitate the commission of another felony (for example, a burglary, robbery, kidnapping, or assault), not whether the other felony was separately charged or resulted in a conviction.

In Cutbank the PSR recited facts — many of which the district court adopted without objection — showing that the sawed-off rifle, a knife, and the victim’s phone were found along the path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank was later detained, and that Cutbank had threatened and assaulted the victim and resisted arrest, biting an officer and grabbing his gun. These facts supported the district court’s finding of a nexus between the firearm and other felonious conduct (domestic assault, kidnapping, injuring an officer), and the Eighth Circuit applied the clear-error standard to affirm. Students should note the practical consequences: failing to object to PSR factual statements forfeits a defendant’s opportunity to contest them later on appeal except for clear error, elevating the importance of timely and specific sentencing objections.

## Guidelines calculation vs. ACCA guideline: higher §2K2.1-driven level controls over §4B1.4 default

The interaction between the specific firearm guideline (§2K2.1) and the career-offender/Armed Career Criminal Guideline (§4B1.4 or the career offender guideline in other contexts) can be a point of confusion. Cutbank had been found by the jury to have three or more prior violent felonies, thereby triggering ACCA’s statutory penalties. The PSR also applied enhancements under §2K2.1 that produced a total offense level higher than the offense level that would have resulted solely from application of §4B1.4. The Guidelines themselves resolve such conflicts: when two different provisions yield different offense levels, the greater offense level applies.

The Eighth Circuit noted this point: the Guidelines direct the sentencing court to apply the higher offense level when the §2K2.1 enhancements produce a higher offense level than the ACCA-guideline calculation. Because the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings and the PSR’s §2K2.1-driven calculations produced the higher total offense level, the court properly used the higher level. This practical rule is straightforward: a defendant confronting overlapping guideline provisions should calculate all applicable offense levels and apply the one that yields the higher range, subject of course to any objection and cross-checking at sentencing.

Students should also be aware of statutory constraints. ACCA itself prescribes mandatory minimums and a statutory framework that may operate independently of the Guidelines. But the Guidelines’ internal mechanism for resolving competing offense-level calculations is a helpful rule-of-thumb in ordinary guideline sentencing where multiple provisions might apply; one must still be attentive to statutory minima and maxima that govern the overall sentencing authority.

## Within-Guidelines sentence affirmed: presumption of reasonableness; adequate §3553(a) consideration on the record

Cutbank received a within-guidelines sentence of 292 months, and he argued on appeal that applying the §2K2.1 enhancement produced a sentence inconsistent with §3553(a)’s “not greater than necessary” principle. The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument and affirmed the sentence.

Two analytical steps frame appellate review of sentences after Gall and Rosales-Mireles. First, the appellate court must ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error — in particular, that it correctly calculated the Guidelines range. This requires that the district court begin by calculating the Guidelines range correctly and resolving disputed guideline facts without procedural error. Second, having confirmed procedural regularity, the appellate court reviews the substantive reasonableness of the sentence, weighing whether the district court considered the §3553(a) factors and whether the ultimate sentence was reasonable in light of those factors. Appellate courts give substantial deference to a within-Guidelines sentence and “presume” such a sentence reasonable (subject to rebuttal with evidence of unreasonableness).

In Cutbank the district court adopted the PSR without objection, calculated the Guidelines range that resulted from the §2K2.1 enhancements, and expressly considered the §3553(a) factors and sentencing memoranda. The Eighth Circuit emphasized that a district court need not deliver a mechanical recitation of each §3553(a) factor; rather, the record must show that the court considered the arguments and factors. Given Cutbank’s extensive criminal history (many violent and firearms-related felonies), the violent facts of the offense (domestic assault, kidnapping of the victim in her home in the presence of children, and injuring an officer during the arrest), and the court’s stated consideration of §3553(a), the within-guidelines sentence was presumed reasonable and was affirmed.

From a teaching perspective, students should take away the following: timely and specific objections to PSR facts preserve challenges to guideline calculations; absent procedural errors, an appellate court will give meaningful deference to a within-guidelines sentence; and a sentencing court should ensure a clear record of consideration of §3553(a) factors, even if the court does not recite each factor in rote fashion.

Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank offers a compact demonstration of how courts apply well-established evidentiary and sentencing doctrines in the federal criminal context. The case reaffirms that (1) prior possession of the same, identifiable firearm is intrinsic evidence probative of later possession; (2) police-response testimony is often admissible for non-hearsay purposes and poses no Confrontation problem when the declarant testifies; (3) courts have broad Rule 403 discretion and will permit responsive or rehabilitative testimony that explains impeaching questions; (4) extrinsic documentary impeachment must be authenticated and properly founded; (5) harmless-error and cumulative-error doctrines protect convictions when substantial independent proof exists; and (6) sentencing enhancements under §2K2.1 are fact-intensive, reviewed for clear error when based on adopted PSR facts, and will control guideline calculations when they yield a higher offense level — with within-guidelines sentences presumptively reasonable when the district court adequately considers §3553(a). Taken together, these lessons show both the practical importance of early and specific objections at trial and sentencing and the deferential standards appellate courts apply to district-court calls in close evidentiary and sentencing decisions.

# Problems, Hypotheticals, and Practice Tips

This chapter applies the doctrinal lessons illustrated in United States v. Cutbank to the practical work of litigating felon-in-possession cases. The Cutbank decision is useful because it brings together common evidentiary disputes—prior sightings of a weapon, witness statements to police, impeachment with investigative reports—and the interplay between trial rulings and sentencing, in particular the application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The hypotheticals below are designed to sharpen doctrinal understanding and to provide concrete drafting and courtroom techniques you can use when preparing motions in limine, cross-examining witnesses, preserving issues for appeal, and litigating contested sentencing enhancements.

## Hypo: Prior sighting of the firearm occurs months earlier—intrinsic or Rule 404(b)? Draft the motion and proposed limiting instruction

Doctrinal Framework. Rule 404(b) prohibits admission of evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a defendant’s propensity to commit the charged offense. But evidence of prior acts is not governed by Rule 404(b) if it is intrinsic to the charged offense—i.e., it is part of the same transaction or necessary to complete the story of the charged crime—or if it is relevant for a non‑propensity purpose (identity, knowledge, absence of mistake, intent, ownership, or another legitimate purpose) and satisfies the Rule 403 balancing test. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank followed United States v. Battle: prior possession of the same firearm can be intrinsic evidence because it helps prove control or ownership. Whether an earlier sighting months before qualifies as “intrinsic” depends on whether it forms part of the same course of conduct or shows continuity of possession or control that makes later possession more probable.

Practical Application to the Hypo. When the prior sighting is several months earlier, you must frame the evidence to the judge in terms that emphasize its non‑propensity relevance. Courts are receptive to prior sightings if the government can show a chain of possession (the same firearm reappears in circumstances linking it to the defendant), identity (the gun is distinctive), or absence of mistake (the defendant knew of and controlled the weapon). If months have passed without intervening owners or events that break the link, the evidence may still be intrinsic. If, however, the prior sighting is remote and unconnected, it risks exclusion as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence unless the government can tie it to one of the recognized non‑propensity purposes.

Model Motion in Limine (defense) — to exclude or limit testimony about prior sightings:
- State the relief sought: exclude testimony that witnesses “saw the defendant with the firearm months earlier,” or in the alternative, limit the testimony to non‑propensity uses and require a limiting instruction.
- Legal argument:
  1. Argue that the court must treat the evidence under Rule 404(b) because the prior sighting is temporally remote and not part of the same transaction as the charged possession. Cite Rule 404(b) and the necessity of a non‑propensity purpose.
  2. If the court treats it as admissible under the intrinsic evidence doctrine, request the court make explicit findings on the record explaining how the evidence is intrinsic—i.e., how it completes the story of the offense or shows continuity/ownership—as required to allow meaningful appellate review.
  3. Argue that even if relevant, the prior sighting should be excluded under Rule 403 because its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice (risk of impermissible propensity inference) and jury confusion, particularly given the remoteness and lack of corroborating evidence tying the earlier sighting to control on the night charged.
- Offer alternatives: redaction of time references, or limiting the scope of testimony to a brief statement that a witness “had previously observed a firearm in the defendant’s presence” without details that invite propensity inferences.

Model Government Motion in Limine (or response) — to admit:
- Show how the prior sighting relates to one of the legitimate non‑propensity purposes: ownership, identity (distinctive sawed‑off Marlin .22), absence of mistake, or continuity of possession.
- Provide factual proffers: e.g., the firearm recovered bears defendant’s DNA and has distinctive modifications (sawed‑off barrel and stock) that make it unlikely to have been possessed by someone else; witnesses observed the same distinctive weapon months earlier at the defendant’s residence; no intervening transfer is documented.
- Argue probative value outweighs prejudice under Rule 403 because the evidence directly supports an essential element—possession and control—rather than mere bad character evidence.

Proposed Limiting Instruction (if court admits the prior sighting):
- “You have heard testimony that [Witness name] previously observed [Defendant] with a firearm at an earlier time. You may consider that evidence only for the limited purpose of determining whether the defendant had knowledge of and control over the handgun that is the subject of the indictment, or for identifying the firearm recovered as the same weapon previously observed. You may not consider that testimony as evidence that the defendant has a predisposition to commit crimes, or for any purpose other than the limited purposes I have described.”

Practice Tips
- Seek a pretrial ruling. The trial judge in Cutbank addressed these issues before trial and relied on Battle; get the court to make specific factual and legal findings about intrinsicness or 404(b) admissibility to preserve the issue for appeal.
- Ask for limits on timing and detail. If the prior sighting is admissible, request the government not mention irrelevant details (e.g., stories of unrelated criminal conduct) that invite propensity inferences.
- If the defense expects the government to rely heavily on the prior sighting to prove ownership, consider an expert (forensic toolmark or DNA) or cross‑examine to test the chain of custody or identification of the weapon.

## Hypo: Declarant does not testify—police ‘context’ testimony and Confrontation Clause implications

Confrontation Doctrine Basics. Under the Sixth Amendment (Crawford v. Washington and its progeny), testimonial out‑of‑court statements cannot be admitted for their truth unless the declarant testifies subject to cross‑examination. If the declarant appears in court and testifies, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied, as the Eighth Circuit explained in Charboneau and as the Cutbank opinion noted when D.F. later testified. If the declarant does not testify, the admissibility of statements turns on whether those statements are “testimonial,” and whether a hearsay exception applies.

Officers’ “Context” Testimony. Police officers frequently testify to statements they heard from victims or witnesses to explain why officers did what they did—who they went to, why they searched, or why they arrested a particular person. There are two limits to that common practice:
1. If officers repeat an out‑of‑court statement that is testimonial and do so for its truth (not merely to explain their state of mind or actions), the Confrontation Clause may bar that testimony unless the declarant testifies.
2. If the officer’s testimony is offered not for the truth of the matter asserted but to explain police conduct (e.g., “the victim told me the shooter ran north, so we pursued that direction”), the testimony may be non‑testimonial or admissible for non‑hearsay purposes—but the court must be vigilant to ensure the jury is not using such testimony substantively.

Practical Strategies When Declarant Does Not Testify
- If the declarant won’t or can’t testify, counsel should litigate admissibility early. Request a Crawford analysis and a ruling on whether the statement is testimonial. Emphasize that simply labeling testimony “context” does not cure a Confrontation Clause problem when the content is the substance of the accusation.
- Where possible, use hearsay exceptions (excited utterance, present sense impression, dying declaration, or business records) to admit the statement for truth; make the foundation to establish the exception. Non‑testimonial statements are more likely to fall into exceptions.
- If the prosecution intends to elicit police testimony repeating the declarant’s words, ask for a limiting instruction and insist the court craft the officer’s testimony to minimize repetition of the exact content. For example, the officer can testify that the declarant “pointed out” a location or “said the suspect had a weapon” without reciting detailed accusatory phrases.
- Preserve objections: contemporaneously object under the Confrontation Clause and Rule 403. If the court admits the statement, make an offer of proof under Rule 103(a)(2) explaining the substance and why it prejudices the defense. If the court excludes the statement and the prosecution argues that an alternative reason justifies admission, request a specific limiting instruction.

Illustration from Cutbank. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank rejected a limiting‑instruction claim because D.F., the declarant, later testified and the court did not admit hearsay before that. That highlights a safe practice for prosecutors: if victim statements are potentially testimonial, delay eliciting them until the victim has testified or secure testimony from the victim early. For defense counsel, it shows the importance of moving promptly to exclude or obtain rulings about such testimony, and the risk that a late testifying declarant will moot Confrontation objections.

## Hypo: ‘In connection with’ where the other felony is a domestic assault without a separate charge—what evidence suffices?

Doctrinal Overview. USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) imposes a four‑level enhancement if a firearm was “used or possessed in connection with” another felony, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The comment (n.14(C)) makes clear that no separate charge is required, but it does not lower the evidentiary standard. At sentencing, the government must show by a preponderance of the evidence that the requisite nexus exists. The critical inquiry is whether possession of the firearm facilitated, or had the potential to facilitate, the commission of another felony—this is often described as a “temporal and causal nexus” or a “facilitative nexus.”

What Evidence Will Satisfy the Nexus Requirement?
- Contemporaneity: Evidence that the firearm was present during the assault, that the defendant pointed or brandished it, or that the assault occurred while the defendant possessed the firearm strongly supports the enhancement.
- Use or Threat: Testimony that the defendant held the gun to the victim, threatened to shoot, or struck the victim with the gun is powerful support for the enhancement because it demonstrates the weapon’s facilitative role.
- Physical and Forensic Evidence: DNA, fingerprints on the firearm, bullet holes, or blood/other physical traces linking the gun to the assault scene support a finding of connection.
- Victim and Witness Statements: Consistent victim testimony that the defendant used or threatened to use the gun during the assault, corroborated by third‑party witnesses or contemporaneous statements to police, is persuasive.
- Circumstantial Evidence: Recovery of the weapon nearby after the assault, lack of time for an intervening transfer, and the weapon’s distinctive nature—especially when combined—can establish the nexus.

Arguments for the Defense. To challenge the enhancement, the defense should attack either the temporal element or the causal link:
- Temporal gap: Show that the assault occurred at a different time or place from where the firearm was possessed or recovered. If the gun was found hours later or at another location and there is no evidence tying it to the assault, that undermines the enhancement.
- Lack of facilitation: Argue that the firearm did not facilitate the felony; for example, the defendant used fists or a knife; the firearm was inoperable; the victim was unaware of a weapon; or the defendant did not use or threaten the weapon in the assault.
- Alternative explanations: Demonstrate plausible innocent reasons for presence of the firearm that have no connection to the assault (storage in a common area, borrowing by another person, etc.).

Practice Tips for Sentencing Advocacy
- Create a detailed time‑line and corroborate it with physical evidence, witness statements, and cell‑phone geolocation if available. A clear timeline can separate possession from the felony.
- Obtain and present affidavits from witnesses who can attest that the gun was not used in the assault, or who saw someone else with the weapon.
- Cross‑examine officers at the sentencing hearing on chain of custody, time of recovery, and any gaps in the government’s story linking the firearm to the assault.
- If the government relies on uncorroborated statements (e.g., one victim report), highlight inconsistencies and impeach credibility. Remember that preponderance is the burden—weak evidence may be overcome by showing reasonable doubt, not required at sentencing, but weight matters.

Cutbank’s Lesson. Cutbank’s PSR presented facts establishing that the defendant threatened the victim with the sawed‑off rifle and retained the weapon while detaining multiple people in the house—classic indicia of facilitation. The court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection; that adoption significantly limits appellate review. Defense counsel should be proactive in contesting such PSR facts before sentencing.

## Authenticating impeachment: securing adoption of interview notes; calling the report author; using recordings

Relevant Rules and Principles. Authentication is governed by Rule 901 (sufficient evidence to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims) and the specific provisions of Rule 801 and 803 where hearsay is concerned. For impeachment with prior inconsistent statements, Rule 613 allows inquiry into prior inconsistent statements and permits extrinsic evidence subject to limits. But extrinsic evidence used to demonstrate a prior inconsistent statement must be adequately authenticated (Cumbie). Recorded statements are generally easier to authenticate than investigator’s notes, but either can be adequate with proper foundation.

Practical Methods to Authenticate Interview Materials
1. Witness Adoption of the Report or Notes
   - Before trial, obtain a stipulation, if possible, that the witness reviewed or orally adopted the investigative notes or report. At trial, lay foundation: ask the witness whether she spoke to the investigator, whether the report fairly and accurately reflects what she told the investigator, and whether she recognizes her own handwriting, statements, or recorded answers. An unambiguous adoption makes the document far more usable.
   - If the witness cannot recall, consider using the recorded interview or the recorded recollection rule (FRE 803(5)): if (a) the witness once had knowledge, (b) made a memorandum or record when the matter was fresh and the record accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge, and (c) the witness now cannot recall well enough to testify fully and accurately, the record may be read into evidence (but is not received as an exhibit unless offered by the adverse party).

2. Call the Report Author
   - The safest route to admitting a police report or investigative notes is to call the report author to lay a foundation: who prepared it, what sources the author used, whether it was created contemporaneously, and whether it fairly reflects the witness’s statements. The report author can authenticate the document under Rule 901(b)(1)–(4) and show the chain of custody where needed.

3. Use Recordings
   - Recordings are potent for impeachment. To authenticate an audio or video recording, lay a foundation for (a) who made the recording, (b) that it fairly and accurately reproduces what was said, (c) how it was preserved and stored, and (d) that the voice is the declarant’s (either by witness identification or expert voice identification if necessary). Where an officer recorded an interview on an agency device, it is often straightforward to establish chain of custody and authenticity.
   - Playbacks are effective: have the witness confirm that the words on the recording are hers and then impeach inconsistent trial testimony by playing the relevant segment.

4. Refreshing Recollection and Recorded Recollection
   - If the witness does not recall, use a written report to refresh recollection (FRE 612). If refreshing fails and the witness still cannot recall, seek admission under the recorded recollection exception (FRE 803(5)): the document may be read into evidence. Before moving to introduce the report as a recorded recollection, prepare a foundation showing the witness made the statement contemporaneously and that it was accurate.

Common Objections and How to Overcome Them
- “Hearsay” objections: explain that the impeachment is not offered for its truth but to impeach the witness’s credibility; if the proponent wants to put the report into evidence substantively, you must authenticate and satisfy an exception.
- “Not the witness’s statement” objection: produce the report author or get a stipulation; show witness made or adopted the statement.
- “Lack of foundation for handwriting/voice identification”: prepare for a handwriting or voice identification foundation and consider expert testimony if necessary.

Illustration from Cutbank. The district court excluded portions of a police report that defense counsel attempted to read into the record because the report was not authored by the witness being impeached and lacked proper foundation. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed that authentication is required for extrinsic impeachment evidence. That emphasizes the importance of laying the right foundation in advance: if you intend to read or offer parts of a report, either secure the witness’s adoption of it, call the report’s author, or rely on a recording.

Checklist for Trial Counsel When Seeking to Use Investigative Materials to Impeach
- Before trial, subpoena the investigator and the recording device logs; get a copy of any recordings and notes.
- Meet and confer, and attempt to secure a stipulation that the witness spoke to the investigator and that the report accurately reflects the witness’s statements.
- If no stipulation, plan to call the report author or introduce the recording with a voice identification foundation.
- Prepare to lay foundation for recorded recollection if the witness lacks present recollection.
- Be ready to explain to the court why impeachment material is admissible for non‑hearsay purposes and, if you seek substantive use, why an exception applies.

## Sentencing advocacy: objecting to PSR factual assertions; marshaling evidence against § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B); framing § 3553(a) mitigation

Procedural Preliminaries. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(f) requires that the probation office provide the defendant and counsel with the PSR and that both parties state objections at least 14 days before sentencing. Objections must be specific. If a factual dispute remains, the court must resolve it, and the defendant has the right to an evidentiary hearing when the disputed matter is material and has potential to affect the sentence. The district court in Cutbank adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection; that is fatal to most appellate challenges. Preserve the record.

Tactical Steps for Objecting to PSR Assertions
- Timely and specifically object to any PSR statement you contest. Vague or late objections are easily overruled or deemed forfeited.
- Submit written evidence supporting your objections: affidavits from alibi or character witnesses, relevant records, photos, phone records, or surveillance footage.
- If the PSR cites a prior conviction, request certified judgments and sentencing transcripts if there is any ambiguity about the date, nature, or legal characterization of the prior offense (violent felony vs. nonviolent).
- Request a hearing when the dispute is factual and material to guideline calculations (e.g., whether a firearm was possessed in connection with another felony, or whether an enhancement applies). An evidentiary hearing allows cross‑examination and the introduction of contrary evidence.

Marshaling Evidence Against a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement
- Attack the nexus: assemble chronology, witness affidavits, and physical evidence showing the firearm was not present at the time or place of the felony, or that it played no role in facilitating the felony.
- Provide expert testimony if useful (e.g., firearms experts to show the weapon could not have been fired, or that the weapon was not the one described by witnesses).
- Emphasize lack of corroboration: single uncorroborated statements in police reports are less persuasive at sentencing where the government bears a preponderance burden.
- Highlight alternative explanations for the firearm’s presence (ownership by third party, storage, or transfer before the felony).

Framing § 3553(a) Mitigation
- Develop a compelling story: build a sentencing package with mitigating letters, employment history, treatment records (substance abuse, mental health), evidence of childhood trauma, and post‑offense conduct (acceptance of responsibility, rehabilitation efforts). The Cutbank court noted the defendant’s extensive criminal history—mitigation must try to humanize the client and explain the factors that weigh against a severe sentence.
- Quantify collateral consequences and disparities: show sentencing disparity relative to codefendants or statistical norms, explain that the guidelines overstate culpability due to double counting, or highlight policy considerations (e.g., addiction-driven conduct).
- Propose concrete alternatives to a long custodial sentence where appropriate: halfway house, intensive supervised release with treatment conditions, or participation in specialized programs. Courts are more likely to deviate when specific, realistic alternatives are presented.
- Address public safety concretely: if release would not endanger the public (family support, stable housing, treatment plan), underscore that. Conversely, if public safety is a real concern, provide a realistic plan for supervised release that mitigates risks.

Form and Tone of Sentencing Memoranda
- Be evidence‑oriented, not merely aspirational. Include affidavits, records, and exhibits supporting each factual claim.
- Anticipate government arguments for enhancements and preemptively rebut them with facts and legal authorities.
- Ask the court for specific findings on disputed facts (so the record will support appellate review if necessary).

Cutbank’s Lesson. Cutbank demonstrates the high costs of failing to object to PSR statements. The district court adopted the PSR's factual assertions without objection; on appeal the record bound the defendant. Preserve objections and litigate factual disputes before sentencing.

## Preservation checklists: contemporaneous objections, offers of proof, and targeted requests for limiting instructions

Trial Preservation Checklist
- Make contemporaneous objections: You must object at the time the evidence or testimony is offered, stating the specific legal ground (e.g., “Objection: Rule 404(b) propensity; prior bad act not intrinsic,” or “Objection: Crawford/Confrontation Clause—testimonial statement”).
- When the court rules against you and excludes evidence, make an offer of proof (Rule 103(a)(2)). State on the record what the excluded witness would have said or what the document contains. An offer of proof preserves the issue for appeal and helps the appellate court assess prejudicial effect.
- When the court admits potentially prejudicial evidence, request a limiting instruction on the record immediately. Ask the court to state the purpose for which the jury may consider the evidence.
- Renew objections when evidence is later repeated or used in a different context; do not assume one objection covers all uses.
- Consider requesting a running transcript or bench conference to get a detailed record of evidentiary discussions.

Sentencing Preservation Checklist
- File written objections to the PSR within the time prescribed by local practice and Rule 32(f). Be precise about the factual assertions you challenge.
- Request a sentencing hearing and state whether you want to call witnesses. If you think a contested enhancement may be outcome determinative, request an evidentiary hearing under Rule 32(i)(3)(B).
- Make a record of all proffers and any documents submitted in support of objections. If the court adopts the PSR, preserve your objections for appeal by renewing them at the sentencing hearing.
- If you agree with the PSR in part, be explicit about which paragraphs you accept and which you contest; this avoids appellate waiver traps.

Strategic Preservation Tips
- Err on the side of objecting. Courts are more likely to find harmless error than to excuse an unpreserved objection; a contemporaneous, specific objection is cheap insurance.
- Use offers of proof strategically: do not simply recite that the excluded evidence is exculpatory—explain how it would undermine the government’s theory or mitigate sentencing enhancements.
- If you lose an objection and evidence is admitted, move to strike or ask for a limiting instruction to lessen prejudice and improve appellate prospects.

Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank underscores familiar themes that recur in felon‑in‑possession litigation: the fine line between intrinsic evidence and forbidden propensity evidence; the interaction of hearsay and confrontation concerns when victims make out‑of‑court statements; the evidentiary threshold for proving that a firearm was possessed “in connection with” another felony at sentencing; and the crucial importance of preserving objections to protect appellate rights. Trial and sentencing practice are litigation of details: factual timelines, carefully crafted foundation questions, timely written objections, offers of proof, and a robust sentencing record can make the difference between a lost battle and a winnable appeal.

Keep the practical checklists and model language above at hand when preparing a felon‑in‑possession case. Good preparation in the district court both advances your client’s immediate interests and, as Cutbank illustrates, preserves meaningful arguments for appeal.

# Significance and Future Directions

United States v. Cutbank sits at the intersection of two doctrinal tracks that routinely determine the fate of federal firearms prosecutions: evidentiary control at trial over prior or contemporaneous conduct involving weapons, and sentencing law that applies guideline and statutory enhancements based on prior convictions and uncharged conduct. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in Cutbank is doctrinally unsurprising in light of its recent decisions, but it is useful as a compact statement of how the circuit allocates decisionmaking power between trial courts and juries, how it treats evidentiary latitude in firearm cases, and how it disciplines appellate review of within-Guidelines sentences. This chapter explicates the ruling’s place in the law, shows the analytic tests courts apply, and identifies the likely lines of future litigation suggested by the opinion.

## Reinforcement of Eighth Circuit trends on intrinsic firearm-possession evidence and authentication rigor

Cutbank reinforces the Eighth Circuit’s established view that prior or repeated possession of the same firearm is frequently intrinsic to a prosecution for later possession and therefore need not be shoehorned into the Rule 404(b) framework. The court in Cutbank endorsed the district court’s pretrial ruling allowing testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle on prior occasions. The court relied on the principle that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon” (citing Battle). That statement encapsulates the typical inquiry: if the evidence is necessary to complete the narrative of the charged crime—showing identity, continuity of possession, or the provenance of the weapon—then it is intrinsic and admissible without the four-factor 404(b) analysis used for other-purpose evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts.

Understanding this distinction is essential for litigation strategy. Intrinsic evidence is not admitted to prove propensity; it explains the context in which the charged offense occurred. The test is functional rather than semantic: courts look to whether the proffered evidence and the charged offense are part of a single, continuous transaction or whether the evidence is probative of a fact material to the charged offense independent of propensity inference (for example, ownership, identity, or intent). When a weapon is uncommon (a sawed-off Marlin .22) or when DNA links the defendant to the specific item later recovered, courts are predisposed to find the prior-observation testimony intrinsic because it materially strengthens the probative link between defendant and weapon.

At the same time, Cutbank affirms that intrinsic classifications do not eliminate other admissibility checks. The opinion references Rule 403 and basic fairness considerations. If prior-possession testimony is cumulative, needlessly prejudicial, or likely to mislead the jury, a court still must weigh those concerns. The difference is procedural and practical: the burden of producing a proffer and triggering the formal Rule 404(b) notice-and-analysis procedure is relieved when the evidence is integral to the charged conduct, but the balancing obligations of Rule 403 continue to constrain admission. Practitioners should therefore prepare both: detailed proffers showing nexus to the charged offense (to justify intrinsic treatment) and a readiness to contest prejudice under Rule 403.

Cutbank also underscores Eighth Circuit insistence on authentication and foundational rigor when the defense seeks to use extrinsic materials for impeachment. The court approved the district court’s refusal to allow portions of a police report to be read into the record without a proper foundation. This follows Cumbie’s articulation that extrinsic impeachment evidence requires authentication. In practice, this means defense counsel must lay a foundation—typically through the witness who made the prior statement or through a custodian—before introducing reports or documents reflecting that witness’s prior statement. Courts are attentive to the distinction between impeaching a witness by cross-examining them about a prior inconsistent statement (permitted) and introducing unauthenticated documentary proof of a prior statement (which can be hearsay and needs authentication or an applicable hearsay exception).

Finally, Cutbank demonstrates a conservative approach to harmless-error analysis in the Eighth Circuit. Even when an evidentiary ruling is arguably incorrect, reversal requires a showing that the jury may have been substantially swayed by the error. Where, as here, the government presents multiple strands of corroborating physical and testimonial evidence—including DNA on the weapon, found items along the defendant’s likely flight path, and eyewitness identification—the appellate court will find it difficult to show the kind of prejudice necessary to reverse.

## Continued vitality of ‘in connection with’ enhancement across non-drug felonies

A central sentencing issue in Cutbank was the application of the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm “in connection with” another felony. The Sentencing Guidelines and their commentary (notably comment n.14(C)) contemplate a broad application: the enhancement can be applied “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” Cutbank confirms that in the Eighth Circuit this enhancement reaches beyond the familiar drug-distribution context and may apply where the firearm in question facilitated, or had the potential to facilitate, the defendant’s commission of another felony—here, a series of violent acts in a domestic setting and resisting arrest that caused injury.

The doctrinal focus in applying the “in connection with” enhancement is nexus: is there a sufficient relationship between the firearm and the other felony so that the possession meaningfully facilitated or was intended to facilitate the felony, or made the felony more dangerous? Lower courts commonly look for some temporal and functional relationship—possession contemporaneous with, and used to further, the felony. But the enhancement’s commentary and Eighth Circuit precedent give courts flexibility to find the required nexus without a separate conviction for the other felony. Dunn and cases like it reflect that courts review the factual finding of a nexus for clear error when the district court relies on the factual statements in the PSR that were not contested at sentencing.

Two implications flow from Cutbank for defense counseling and appellate litigation. First, contesting the factual content of the PSR during sentencing is crucial; an undisputed PSR becomes the factual baseline on appeal and substantially narrows grounds for reversal. Second, litigation over the enhancement is likely to concentrate on the sufficiency of the nexus rather than on formalities such as whether a separate prosecution was brought. Future challenges will press the boundary of “in connection with” where the relationship between weapon possession and another felony is attenuated—for example, where the other felony involves nonviolent property misconduct and the firearm was not used or exhibited. Appellate courts will continue to grapple with how much functional tie (use, exhibition, intimidation, facilitation) suffices for the enhancement, and whether conduct uncharged at trial can properly supply the “other felony.”

## Appellate deference to within‑Guidelines sentences and the strategic importance of undisputed PSRs

Cutbank exemplifies the modern post‑Gall and post‑Rosales-Mireles landscape in which district courts begin with the Guidelines calculation and appellate courts demand that the district court avoid procedural error before assessing substantive reasonableness. The Eighth Circuit presumes a within‑Guidelines sentence is reasonable, and on appeal the first inquiry is whether the Guidelines range was correctly calculated. This presumption matters strategically: a defendant’s chances of success on appeal nearly always improve if she can show procedural error in the calculation or consideration of the Guidelines at sentencing.

The practical lesson from Cutbank is unmistakable: state the objections at sentencing, and object precisely and on the record. The court’s acceptance “without objection” of the PSR’s statements effectively converts those statements into admitted facts for appellate review. APSR that includes uncontroverted assertions about the defendant’s prior crimes or the facts of the offense gives the sentencing court a reliable factual predicate for enhancements; defendants who do not timely object limit their appellate options. When challenging a Guidelines enhancement, a defendant should make clear which factual predicates are disputed and why. If the dispute is factual, the defendant must offer contrary proof or demand live testimony; if the dispute is legal (for example, whether the predicate offense qualifies as a “crime of violence” under a guidelines provision), the defendant must brief the legal issue and preserve objection.

On substantive reasonableness, Cutbank reminds practitioners that the appellate court will usually defer to the district court’s weighing of § 3553(a) factors so long as the district court considered arguments and articulated a coherent rationale. The circuit does not require a formulaic recitation of each factor; it looks for evidence in the record that the court “considered and rejected” mitigating arguments. This pragmatic standard underscores the importance of making a full record at sentencing—presenting mitigation, alternatives to incarceration, and arguments against particular enhancements—so that the district court’s decision not to vary can be reviewed as a reasoned exercise of discretion.

## Potential friction points: evolving Second Amendment challenges; scope of intrinsic evidence; limits of ‘context’ testimony

Although Cutbank does not involve a direct constitutional challenge to felon-in-possession statutes, the opinion sits against an unsettled constitutional backdrop. The Supreme Court’s post‑Bruen framework requires courts assessing restrictions on the exercise of the Second Amendment to look to historical analogues and tradition. That methodology has spawned litigation testing categorical disqualifications—particularly whether certain classes of persons (e.g., all felons or certain misdemeanor offenders) may constitutionally be disarmed as a categorical rule. Cutbank illustrates the doctrinal status quo—felon-in-possession convictions remain routine—but that status quo could be tested. Defendants may increasingly press as-applied or categorical Second Amendment challenges claiming insufficient historical analogue for broad disqualifications, and courts will have to decide whether the historic tradition of firearm regulation supports modern categorical bars for persons with the defendant’s particular record.

Evidence law offers further friction points not fully resolved by Cutbank. The opinion endorses relatively broad admission of prior observations of the same weapon as intrinsic evidence. But where the prior evidence is remote in time, involves different contexts, or risks creating a narrative of general criminality, defendants will press courts to construe prior possession as extrinsic conduct governed by Rule 404(b). Future cases will test the dividing line: when does prior possession cease to be part of a continuous narrative and become propensity evidence deserving of strict 404(b) scrutiny? The answer will turn on whether the prior conduct is logically necessary to link the defendant to the specific weapon or merely cumulative and suggestive of character.

A related issue is the permissible scope of “context” testimony—statements to police and officer testimony describing those statements. Cutbank approved the district court’s approach: no limiting instruction was necessary at the time the statements were admitted, and the Confrontation Clause did not bar admission because the declarant testified at trial. Yet close cases will arise. For example, when officers testify about multiple statements by a victim who later does not testify, or when officers’ testimony paraphrases a declarant’s narrative in a way that effectively supplies substantive facts, courts will need to police hearsay and confrontation concerns. The timing of limiting instructions also remains a tactical tool: requesting contemporaneous limiting instructions can preserve appellate issues and reduce risk of juror confusion, even though courts sometimes defer a single instruction at the end of trial.

Finally, authentication issues will continue to create litigation, especially with digital evidence and third-party reports. The district court’s insistence on foundation before permitting reading of a police report highlights that courts will not allow impeachment by unsworn, unauthenticated documentary excerpts merely because they appear in investigative files. Defense counsel must either authenticate the report through a witness, establish a hearsay exception (e.g., business records) where applicable, or secure trial testimony that allows impeachment without resorting to extrinsic documents.

## Policy considerations: proportionality under ACCA and robust use of uncharged conduct at sentencing

Cutbank raises enduring policy questions that appeal to lawmakers, sentencing reform advocates, and litigious defendants alike. The opinion illustrates how powerful statutory regimes—most notably the Armed Career Criminal Act—interact with Guidelines and judicial factfinding to produce very long sentences. The court accepted that the ACCA finding (three prior violent felonies) and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement together produce a near‑quarter‑century sentence. The Guidelines’ cross-referencing rules (e.g., USSG § 4B1.4(b)) provide mechanical answers when multiple enhancement theories apply, but they do not resolve whether such stacked punishment is proportionate.

Two policy debates are implicated. First, proportionality and the role of individualized sentencing: critics argue that heavy reliance on recidivist statutes and guideline enhancements can produce sentences that are excessive when considered against a defendant’s historic conduct, rehabilitation prospects, and the particular facts of the offense. Cutbank shows a circuit unwilling to second‑guess such outcomes if the court follows proper procedure and the sentence falls within the calculated Guidelines range. Second, the permissive use of uncharged conduct at sentencing—whether as factual predicates for guideline enhancements or as part of the § 3553(a) calculus—raises fairness and notice concerns. Although the Guidelines and the Sentencing Commission authorize reliance on uncharged conduct when established by a preponderance of the evidence, critics contend that this practice effectively penalizes defendants for conduct they were not prosecuted for and that may have been considered insufficiently provable or strategically uncharged.

These policy debates suggest avenues for legislative reform (narrower mandatory recidivist regimes, changes to guideline enhancement rules) and for litigation (Eighth Amendment proportionality challenges—generally difficult—or due process challenges to the use of uncharged conduct). Cutbank signals that, absent such reforms, the Eighth Circuit will continue to allow robust consideration of a defendant’s prior violent conduct and contemporaneous dangerous behavior in both verdict and sentencing.

## Conclusion: doctrinal stability with fertile grounds for future dispute

United States v. Cutbank is both confirmatory and instructive. It confirms Eighth Circuit lines favoring admission of prior or repeated possession evidence as intrinsic when it helps to prove control, insists on authentication for extrinsic impeachment, presumes the reasonableness of within‑Guidelines sentences, and applies the § 2K2.1 “in connection with” enhancement in a fact‑driven manner. At the same time, Cutbank highlights the fault lines that will generate future litigation: the outer limits of intrinsic evidence; the proper scope of “context” testimony and confrontation guarantees; the reach of the “in connection with” enhancement in non‑drug contexts; and the continuing constitutional and policy challenges to categorical firearms disabilities and heavy recidivist sentencing. For defense counsel, the case emphasizes preserving objections, contesting PSR facts at sentencing, and preparing evidence and argument on nexus and prejudice. For scholars and reformers, it crystallizes the competing values—public safety, evidentiary reliability, notice, and proportionality—that will shape the next round of disputes over firearms prosecutions and punishment.
