Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

### What appellate jurisdiction and briefing rules explain the court’s admonition that the government 'expanded the issues'?
Question:
The opinion notes the government framed an issue on appeal that the defendant hadn’t raised, and the court 'did not appreciate' that. In a criminal defendant’s appeal (jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291/§ 3742), what limits apply to an appellee’s ability to broaden issues absent a cross-appeal? Is this tied to the 'cross-appeal rule' barring enlargement of the judgment, or a prudential rule against raising new issues not briefed by the appellant?
Answer:
Short answer: The court’s rebuke is about the limits on an appellee’s briefing — a prudential/briefing rule that an appellee should not broaden the scope of the appeal by introducing new issues the appellant did not press — not the narrow “cross‑appeal” rule that formally limits a party from seeking to enlarge the judgment without filing a cross‑appeal. The opinion criticizes the government for “unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal” (slip op. § II.C) and then treats the government’s extra argument as improper and unnecessary to the disposition, addressing only authentication and harmless‑error points.  

Why that follows from appellate practice and the opinion

- The cross‑appeal rule (the principle that a party must cross‑appeal to obtain greater relief or an enlargement of the judgment) is a distinct doctrine that prevents an appellee from obtaining more favorable relief than the district court gave unless the appellee files a cross‑appeal. The opinion does not invoke that doctrine here — the government was not seeking to enlarge the judgment or obtain a different sentence; it was presenting a different phrasing/claim about a trial ruling the defendant did not itself press on appeal.

- Instead, the court’s language (again, “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal” (II.C)) reflects the ordinary appellate practice that parties should confine their briefs to the issues framed by the appellant’s opening brief. Appellate courts routinely decline to entertain issues not fairly presented by the appellant or not squarely briefed by the appellee because doing so would transform the appellee into a second appellant and would surprise the opposing party and the court. The opinion therefore treats the government’s extra argument as an “expans[ion]” of the record of issues and addresses only authentication and harmless‑error doctrine (Cumbie; DeMarce) to dispose of the matter.

Practical takeaway for briefing and jurisdictional posture

- If an appellee wants the court to consider a point the appellant did not raise, the appellee should (a) be mindful that courts may rebuke and ignore such expansion, and (b) recognize that the cross‑appeal rule applies only if the appellee’s argument would seek to enlarge or materially alter the judgment. If the appellee’s argument is merely a different theory in support of affirmance (not an attempt to obtain greater relief), courts have discretion to consider it, but may decline if it unfairly broadens the appellate dispute. The Cutbank opinion illustrates the court exercising that discretion and policing briefs that broaden the appeal beyond what the defendant raised (II.C).

References in the opinion supporting this explanation

- “the government’s brief argued that Cutback’s ‘next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross‑examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.’ We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” (Opinion § II.C.)

- The court then resolves the point on authentication and harmless‑error principles (citing United States v. Cumbie and United States v. DeMarce), showing it treated the government’s extra argument as unnecessary expansion rather than a jurisdictional cross‑appeal problem.

In short: the admonition stems from appellate briefing norms and the court’s discretion to refuse to entertain issues the appellee injects that the appellant did not press, not from the separate cross‑appeal/enlargement‑of‑judgment rule.
