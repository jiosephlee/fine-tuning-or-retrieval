### What exactly did the jury have to find about prior convictions to trigger ACCA after recent Supreme Court cases?
Question:
The panel states the 'jury convicted ... of being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies,' triggering § 924(e). Post-Wooden and Erlinger, some facts (like whether offenses occurred on different 'occasions') require jury findings beyond a reasonable doubt, but Almendarez-Torres still allows judicial fact-finding on the fact of a prior conviction. What ACCA-related facts did the jury actually have to find here (violent-felony status? occasions?), and which remain legal determinations for the judge? Why frame it as the jury convicting on 'being previously convicted' rather than a judicial finding?
Answer:
Short answer
- The only fact the Sixth Amendment compelled the jury to find in this case was the existence of the prior convictions in the sense the jury was asked and did convict Cutbank of “being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies.” The panel therefore treated the existence of three qualifying prior convictions as a jury-found fact, which is the dispositive predicate for § 924(e).
- What remains for the judge (when the court chooses to decide it) are legal characterizations of those prior convictions — e.g., whether a particular prior conviction meets the statute’s definition of a “violent felony” under the relevant categorical/modifiedcategorical analysis, or other legal issues about how a prior record counts (record authentication, merger, vacatur, etc.).
- The court’s language that “the jury convicted … of being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies” reflects either (a) the safe route of submitting the predicate-count to the jury (thus avoiding Almendarez‑Torres challenges), or (b) a plain statement of what the jury’s verdict accomplished: the jury found the threshold prior‑conviction fact that triggers ACCA. The opinion shows no need for the judge to make additional jury-level findings (such as whether offenses occurred on different “occasions”).

Longer explanation, tied to the opinion and relevant doctrine

1) Governing doctrinal points (as reflected in the opinion)
- Almendarez‑Torres v. United States remains good law to the extent it treats the fact of a prior conviction as an exception to the rule that any fact that increases the statutory penalty must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The opinion does not repudiate that principle; instead, the panel records that the jury “found … he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering the Armed Career Criminal Act’s enhanced penalties.” (Opinion, III, first para.)
- The panel treats the jury’s finding of the prior‑conviction predicate as the operative event that “trigger[s]” § 924(e). (Id.) That is consistent with Almendarez‑Torres’ core holding that the existence of prior convictions is a distinct inquiry and may be treated differently from other sentence‑augmenting facts.

2) What the jury actually had to find in this case (and did)
- The jury was asked and convicted Cutbank of “being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies,” and the panel repeats that as the basis for the ACCA enhancement. (Opinion, I; III.) So the constitutionally significant fact, as submitted to and found by the jury, was that Cutbank had at least three prior convictions that qualified as violent felonies.
- Because the verdict form (and the reported result) explicitly tied the jury to the predicate (three or more qualifying prior convictions), the judgment does not rest on a judge’s solitary finding that the defendant “had prior convictions.” The opinion therefore treats the prior‑conviction predicate as a jury-found fact.

3) What remains a judicial/legal determination
- Whether a given prior conviction “counts” as a violent felony typically requires a legal comparison between the statutory elements (or the record of conviction) and ACCA’s definition (the categorical or modified‑categorical approach). That sort of legal characterization — deciding whether the record of a prior conviction meets the statutory definition of a “violent felony” — is a legal determination courts commonly make at sentencing and is reflected in the opinion’s treatment of the PSR and the district court’s adoption of PSR facts. (See III, describing adoption of PSR factual statements without objection.)
- Other legal issues about priors (e.g., whether a conviction was vacated, whether multiple convictions merge for ACCA purposes, whether an out‑of‑state record is sufficiently authenticated) are also matters for court resolution using the record of conviction and related materials. The opinion’s discussion of the PSR and adoption of its facts (III) shows the court relied on those sentencing‐record mechanisms.

4) What about “occasions” or other subsidiary facts that some recent cases have required a jury to find?
- The opinion does not identify separate “occasion” findings as necessary here. The panel simply reports the jury’s finding of three or more prior violent felony convictions and treats that as triggering § 924(e). (III.)
- If a Supreme Court decision requires a jury to find a particular subsidiary, fact-based element (for example, an element that changes the statutory minimum or maximum and is not simply the fact of a prior conviction), the government must either submit that fact to the jury or rely on Almendarez‑Torres only where the fact asserted is properly characterized as the mere existence of prior convictions. The panel’s approach — submitting the prior‑conviction predicate to the jury and stating the jury’s verdict in those terms — avoids any close constitutional question about subsidiary factual findings such as “different occasions.” Because the panel does not rely on judge-only findings about distinct occasions, none are necessary to support the ACCA enhancement in this case.

5) Why the opinion frames it as “the jury convicted … of being previously convicted …”
- Two practical/legal reasons:
  a) Constitutional safety: framing and submitting the prior‑conviction predicate to the jury eliminates argument that sentencing relied on judge-found facts that should have been submitted to the jury post‑Wooden/Erlinger. The opinion’s repeated phrasing that the jury “found … he had three or more prior violent felony convictions” (III) and that the jury “convicted … of being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies” (I) reflects that safe posture.
  b) Reflecting what actually happened at trial: the record shows the jury returned a verdict on the ACCA predicate, so the panel accurately describes the jury’s role rather than treating the prior‑conviction determination as a purely judicial finding. The opinion also notes the PSR facts were adopted at sentencing, showing the district court relied on the presentence record where appropriate. (III.)

Bottom line
- In this case the essential constitutional requirement was satisfied: the jury found the threshold prior‑conviction predicate (that Cutbank had three or more prior violent‑felony convictions), and the panel rests the ACCA enhancement on that jury finding. The remaining work — legal classification of the priors, authentication, and other legal sentencing questions — are for the court to resolve using conviction records and the PSR. The opinion’s phrasing reflects both the record (a jury verdict on the predicate) and a prudent approach to the interplay of Almendarez‑Torres and more recent jury‑trial decisions.
