### Why couldn’t defense counsel read from the police report to impeach C.F., and what authentication was missing?
Question:
The trial court barred defense counsel from reading portions of a police report during cross of C.F., reasoning there was no proper foundation because C.F. didn’t author the report. The panel cited Cumbie about authenticating extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statements. Can someone explain precisely what foundation/authentication was lacking? Could counsel have used Rule 613(b) (extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statements), Rule 803(6) (business records), or called the officer who drafted the report? What’s the right way to get that impeachment before the jury in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer
The court excluded defense counsel’s “reading” of the police report because the report was not properly authenticated as the witness’s own prior statement (and so could not be admitted as extrinsic impeachment). Cumbie (which the panel cited) makes clear that extrinsic evidence used to impeach must be authenticated — the government and the district court were entitled to a foundation showing the document actually records what C.F. said. Absent that foundation, the document is hearsay and cannot simply be read into the record for impeachment.

Why authentication/foundation was missing (precisely)
- The panel relied on United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022): “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court sustained objections because the police report was not authored by C.F.; there was no foundation tying the written report to C.F.’s own statements.
- Authentication requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims (Fed. R. Evid. 901(a)). Here the proponent had to show the portions of the report accurately recorded statements made by C.F. — e.g., that C.F. told the officer X — or otherwise show that C.F. adopted or acknowledged the contents.
- Without testimony from C.F. adopting the report or other testimony connecting the words in the report to C.F.’s mouth, the report is merely a third-party document recounting what C.F. allegedly said. Reading that document into the record would introduce hearsay (a document offered for the truth of its contents) without the requisite foundation.

Could Rule 613(b) alone have done it?
- Rule 613(b) governs impeachment by prior inconsistent statement. It allows extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement only if the witness is afforded an opportunity to explain/deny or the subject is interrogated about it on cross (and the opponent has an opportunity to examine). It does not dispense with authentication requirements.
- Practically: defense can ask C.F. on cross, “Did you tell Officer Jones X?” If C.F. denies or equivocates, Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic proof — but that extrinsic proof must still be authenticated (Rule 901) before the jury may see/read a document that purports to memorialize the statement.

Could Rule 803(6) (business records) rescue the report?
- Rule 803(6) allows admission of regularly kept records if the record was made in the course of a regularly conducted activity and is trustworthy. But police reports present two problems:
  1) If the proponent seeks the report to prove the truth of a declarant-witness’s statements recorded in the report, many courts treat those portions as not falling squarely within 803(6) because the reporting officer was not the declarant of the substantive facts and the trustworthiness requirement is often questionable in criminal trials.
  2) Even if the report itself is a business record, the portions recording a witness’s out-of-court narrative may still be hearsay within hearsay; each layer needs a proper exception.
- The opinion does not admit the report under 803(6); instead it required authentication under Cumbie. So relying on 803(6) would have required additional foundation about how and when the report was prepared and why it is trustworthy — and would still likely face Confrontation/hearsay concerns if offered for the truth of what C.F. said.

Could defense have called the officer who drafted the report?
- Yes. Calling the officer who prepared the report is the straightforward route:
  - The officer can testify that C.F. told him X (officer testifies to the witness’s prior statement). That provides direct extrinsic impeachment and proper foundation.
  - The officer can authenticate the report as his or her own document if needed (Rule 901(b)(1): testimony of a witness with knowledge).
  - Using the officer avoids the layering/hearsay/authentication problems of reading the document into the record without foundation.

Practical, Eighth‑Circuit‑safe ways to get the impeachment before the jury
1. On cross, ask C.F. directly whether she told the officer the particular statement. If she denies, you may then offer extrinsic proof — but you must authenticate the source of that extrinsic proof.
2. Call the officer who took the statement. Have the officer testify that C.F. made the inconsistent statement and identify the report if necessary. That satisfies authentication and confrontation concerns.
3. If you want to use the written report itself, lay foundation: get C.F. to acknowledge the report or concede she made the statement contained in it; or have the officer authenticate the report as his/her record (and be prepared to argue the business-records exception for admissibility of the officer’s report for non-hearsay purposes). But do not rely solely on Rule 613(b) to bypass Rule 901 authentication.
4. If time permits, identify and proffer the exact foundation to the court before attempting to read the report — show who made it, when, by what procedure, and how it records C.F.’s statements.

Bottom line
The district court blocked the reading of the police report because defense counsel failed to lay the necessary authentication/foundation showing the report actually recorded C.F.’s statements. Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic impeachment but does not eliminate the need to authenticate a document; Rule 803(6) might help for parts of a police report but has its own limits and trustworthiness requirements; and the safest route in the Eighth Circuit is to obtain the impeaching prior statement through testimony (call the officer) or to get the witness to adopt/acknowledge the report on the record so the document is properly authenticated. The panel’s citation to Cumbie embodies that rule: extrinsic impeachment must be authenticated before it may be used.
