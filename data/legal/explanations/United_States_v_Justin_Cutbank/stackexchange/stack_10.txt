### Does using a firearm to commit assaults/false imprisonment qualify as 'in connection with' under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)?
Question:
Application Note 14(A) often focuses on facilitation or emboldening. Here, the firearm allegedly was used to hold D.F. in a closet, threaten her, and assault her while committing other felonies. Is that per se sufficient to satisfy 'in connection with' because the gun facilitated those separate felonies by enabling threats/force? Are there Eighth Circuit cases distinguishing mere contemporaneous possession from possession that 'facilitates' non-firearm felonies like assault or kidnapping?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — under the facts of this case the Eighth Circuit upheld the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four‑level enhancement because the rifle was used to facilitate other felonies (holding D.F. in a closet, threatening/assaulting her), not merely because it was contemporaneously present. But the enhancement still requires a nexus (the firearm “was used in connection with” another felony), not mere temporal proximity.

Why (doctrinally) and how the court applied it
- The Guidelines’ commentary makes clear that the enhancement applies when a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another . . . felony offense,” and it applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought.” U.S. v. Cutbank, slip op. at § III (citing USSG § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C))). That phrase has been read to require a nexus showing that the firearm facilitated, emboldened, or made the non‑firearm felony more likely/successful — i.e., not merely that the weapon was present while another crime occurred.
- The court accepted the PSR’s factual account (adopted by the district court) as establishing that nexus: Cutbank “pointed a gun at [D.F.]’s face, directed her into a closet, kept [her] in the closet at gunpoint . . . threatening her life and striking her across the face with the gun,” and later fled with the phone and the gun. Slip op. § I; see also § III. Those facts show the rifle was the means by which he restrained, threatened, and assaulted D.F., which is exactly the kind of facilitation/emboldening the Application Note contemplates.
- The court therefore concluded the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was properly applied: “the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” Slip op. § III. The court reviewed this for clear error and found none.

What this opinion does and does not hold for other cases
- This opinion applies the familiar nexus rule (firearm must facilitate or be used in furtherance of the other felony) to the concrete conduct here — pointing, confining, threatening, and striking with the gun. Those acts are sufficient to show the gun “facilitated” the kidnapping/assault, so the enhancement applies.
- The opinion does not hold that any contemporaneous possession of a gun automatically triggers the enhancement. It did not adopt a per se rule for mere contemporaneous possession. Rather, it relied on the specific facts showing the weapon was used to accomplish or advance other felonious conduct.
- The opinion cites United States v. Dunn (723 F.3d 919) for standard‑of‑review purposes but does not announce a new test or distinguish every variation of “mere possession” scenarios. If a case involved only passive, non‑functional presence of a firearm with no evidence it facilitated the other crime, Cutbank would not control that result.

Takeaway for a law student
- To qualify under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) the government must show a connection between the firearm and another felony — typically that the gun facilitated, emboldened, or was the means of committing the other offense.
- In Cutbank, pointing the rifle, confining the victim at gunpoint, threatening her life, and striking her with the gun constituted the necessary facilitation; the enhancement was therefore proper. See slip op. §§ I, III.
