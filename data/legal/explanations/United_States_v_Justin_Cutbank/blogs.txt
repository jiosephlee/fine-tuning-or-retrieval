Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

# Intrinsic Evidence After Battle: The Eighth Circuit’s Broad View in Felon-in-Possession Cases

## Why this matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank, handed down October 7, 2025, is a refreshingly straightforward reaffirmation of a common—but contested—trial practice in felon-in-possession prosecutions: testimony that a defendant was seen with the same firearm before the charged incident is often treated as intrinsic evidence, not character evidence governed by Rule 404(b). That classification lets prosecutors use such testimony without the special notice, limiting-instruction, and Rule 403 balancing that Rule 404(b) requires. For practitioners, the court’s ruling is important because it keeps open a powerful line of proof—prior possession tends to be highly persuasive circumstantial evidence of ownership or control. For defendants, it highlights an evidentiary battleground that can be decisive in close possession cases.

Cutbank’s conviction arose from a dramatic, violent incident in which the defendant allegedly held his girlfriend at gunpoint, fled, and was thereafter stopped after barricading himself in a garage. Officers recovered a sawed-off Marlin .22 and other items along a likely route, and DNA on the rifle matched the defendant. At trial, the girlfriend and another witness testified they had seen the defendant with that sawed-off rifle on multiple prior occasions. The district court admitted that testimony over a Rule 404(b) objection, treating it as intrinsic to proving possession. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, relying principally on United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), to say prior possession of the same weapon helps establish ownership or control and therefore is intrinsic, not propensity, evidence.

## The court’s reasoning, in context

The Eighth Circuit’s analysis in Cutbank is concise and doctrinally predictable. The panel reiterated the longstanding principle that evidence of prior possession of a particular weapon is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it bears on ownership or control. Battle supplies the controlling precedent in this circuit: when the prior and charged possession concern the same specific firearm, the evidence goes to identity, continuity, and dominion rather than to a defendant’s bad character. The panel rejected the defendant’s Rule 404(b) challenge for want of contrary authority and underscored that the record supported admission of the witnesses’ testimony that they had seen the defendant with the sawed-off rifle “a handful of times.”

The opinion is also careful to place this evidentiary ruling within ordinary appellate constraints: Cutbank’s brief offered little analysis or authority, and the court emphasized the district court’s broad discretion on 403 balancing and limiting-instruction decisions. It further treated any evidentiary missteps as harmless in light of substantial other evidence (DNA on the rifle, recovery along the route, testimony identifying the weapon during the incident, and the defendant’s own resistive conduct). On sentencing, the opinion is ancillary to the evidentiary discussion but illustrates the practical consequence: with the possession finding solid, enhancements and a within-guidelines sentence followed.

## How this fits with other circuits

Not all circuits draw the intrinsic/extrinsic line in precisely the same place. The Eighth Circuit’s approach in Cutbank aligns with a cluster of circuits that are permissive about prior-possession evidence when the prior incidents are closely connected to the weapon at issue. For example, courts in multiple circuits have allowed testimony that the defendant previously possessed or displayed the same gun when such testimony aids proof of identity, control, or the chain of events leading to the charged offense.

By contrast, some decisions in other circuits have been more skeptical about treating prior acts as intrinsic when the temporal or factual nexus to the charged offense is loose or when the prior acts risk doing little more than showing a propensity for carrying firearms. Those courts have required either a clearer nexus—same weapon, same location, same modus operandi—or have insisted on robust Rule 403 scrutiny (and, in some instances, limiting instructions) to prevent undue prejudice. The divergence is not absolute; rather it’s a matter of emphasis: the Eighth Circuit emphasizes the logical relevance of prior possession of the same weapon; more cautious panels emphasize the danger that jurors will use such testimony simply as proof that “he’s the sort of person who carries guns.”

Cutbank thus reinforces a functional, evidence-focused test in the Eighth Circuit: when prior possession involves the same firearm and helps establish ownership, control, or continuity, it is intrinsic. That keeps the door open to straightforward chronological narratives in felon-in-possession trials: witnesses describe seeing the defendant with the weapon before, during, and around the charged event, and the prosecution argues continuity of possession.

## Propensity risks and doctrinal tradeoffs

Treating prior-possession testimony as intrinsic eases the prosecutor’s path but raises real propensity concerns. The risk is not merely theoretical: jurors inferring “he had a gun before, so he must have had it that night” is precisely the evidentiary leap Rule 404 seeks to constrain when evidence is strictly character-based. The Eighth Circuit’s line depends on a close factual connection between the earlier sightings and the charged incident—same gun, recognizable features, or repeated prior control. That fact-intensive constraint performs an important gatekeeping function, but it can be porous in practice. Photographs, distinctive alterations (a sawed-off stock or serial-number obliteration), or DNA can strengthen the logical bridge between past and present possession; absent that connective tissue, the evidence becomes more marginal and more likely to carry undue prejudicial weight.

Cutbank illustrates both strengths and limits of the doctrine. Here, the weapon had distinct characteristics (a sawed-off Marlin .22), DNA linked the rifle to the defendant, and eyewitness testimony placed him with that same rifle before the charged episode. Those facts make the inference of continuity of possession highly probative and less likely to be merely impermissible propensity evidence. But when prior-possession evidence lacks such anchors—no distinctive firearm features, considerable temporal distance, or ambiguous witness descriptions—the risk that jurors will overvalue the testimony increases. Courts that accept the Eighth Circuit’s approach still must grapple with Fed. R. Evid. 403: even intrinsic evidence with legitimate relevance can be excluded if its prejudicial effect substantially outweighs its probative value.

## Litigation strategies going forward

For prosecutors in the Eighth Circuit, Cutbank is a green light to proffer carefully tethered evidence of prior possession when the weapon is the same and when other facts (distinctive alterations, DNA, location, continuity of control) corroborate the inference. Effective preparation will emphasize those connective facts in motions in limine and in examining witnesses—elicit distinguishing features of the firearm, witness familiarity, and the pattern of possession to preempt Rule 404(b) objections.

Defense counsel must push on every connective thread. Motions in limine should demand particularized findings that prior-possession testimony is truly intrinsic—show the temporal proximity, the specific identifying characteristics, or the witnesses’ basis for recognition. When those connections are thin, defense counsel should insist on the Rule 404(b) framework and on a Rule 403 balancing that foregrounds the risk of impermissible propensity reasoning. If the testimony comes in, defense lawyers should request contemporaneous limiting instructions and, if necessary, move to strike ambiguous or inflammatory testimony that does not directly describe the weapon’s identity or defendant’s control.

Cross-examination remains vital. Even when the testimony is admissible, probing inconsistencies, witness uncertainty about dates or identifying features, and potential motives for misidentification can blunt its force. And when physical evidence—DNA, unique markings, photographs—links a defendant to a weapon, defense counsel must be prepared to challenge chain-of-custody, testing methodologies, or interpretation of forensic results to prevent the prior-possession narrative from acquiring unassailable momentum.

Finally, appellate counsel should preserve Rule 403 and Rule 404(b) objections clearly and develop a record showing why the evidence’s probative value was outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The Eighth Circuit’s Cutbank opinion underscores that a thin brief or undeveloped argument is unlikely to succeed on appeal.

## Broader doctrinal and downstream implications

Cutbank does not create new law, but it consolidates and clarifies the path that prosecutors will follow in the Eighth Circuit. Its practical effect is to lower the barrier for use of prior-possession testimony in felon-in-possession cases, so long as the weapon can be tied to the charged incident with some specificity. That will likely encourage prosecutors to emphasize unique physical features and forensic links in investigative reports and PSRs so that district courts have a robust factual predicate for treating prior possession as intrinsic.

At the doctrinal level, Cutbank highlights the persistent tension between evidentiary rules designed to prevent character-based inferences and the evidentiary realities of possession cases, where continuity and identity often turn on precisely the kind of prior sightings that worry Rule 404’s drafters. The Eighth Circuit opts for a pragmatic resolution: permit the evidence when it materially advances proof of ownership or control, but remain mindful of Rule 403’s safeguard against undue prejudice. That balance will be tested in future cases where the similarities between the prior sightings and the charged incident are more subtle, and where juries might be tempted to conflate prior lawful or unlawful conduct with propensity.

Cutbank also serves as a reminder for practitioners in circuits that take a more cautious view: evidentiary outcomes can differ significantly from circuit to circuit, which affects charging strategy, plea negotiations, and trial planning. Defense teams operating in or across circuits should be keenly aware of these local doctrinal textures when advising clients about risks and trial prospects.

## Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank reaffirmed the Eighth Circuit’s pragmatic, evidence-centered approach: prior possession of the same firearm, when meaningfully connected to the charged offense, is intrinsic and admissible to show possession, ownership, or control. The decision keeps a common prosecutorial tool firmly available in the circuit, but it also underscores the continuing need for vigilant defense advocacy where the factual links are tenuous. Ultimately, Cutbank is less a doctrinal sea change than a clear restatement of the Eighth Circuit’s willingness to let juries hear prior-possession evidence so long as the evidence advances a legitimate, nonpropensity fact of consequence in the case.



# Context or Confrontation? Policing the Line on Officer Narrative Testimony

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank is routine in outcome but instructive in technique. At issue is a common evidentiary tension in criminal trials: when police testify about what a victim told them, are they simply explaining why officers did what they did — admissible “context” testimony — or are they offering substantive, hearsay-backed proof of the defendant’s conduct that raises Confrontation Clause problems? Cutbank reinforces a cautious-but-permissive approach that district courts across the country already use: courts need not give preemptive limiting instructions each time an officer relays an out-of-court statement so long as the declarant later testifies in court, and trial judges retain wide discretion to manage whether such testimony becomes substantive hearsay.

That question matters because officer narrative testimony — a police officer recounting what they were told on the scene — touches both Rule 801/802 hearsay rules and the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause as developed in Crawford v. Washington and its progeny. The balance courts strike affects the prosecution’s ability to tell a coherent story and the defense’s ability to challenge testimonial hearsay without forfeiture of confrontation protections. Cutbank illustrates how one federal appellate court navigates that balance after the declarant ultimately testifies.

## What the Eighth Circuit did

Cutbank argued the district court should have given the jury an upfront limiting instruction: that any statements D.F. (the victim) made to police could not be treated as substantive evidence against the defendant until D.F. herself testified. The district court declined to give that instruction preemptively, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

Two strands of the court’s analysis are worth noting. First, on hearsay and Rule 404(b) grounds the court treated testimony that D.F. and another witness had seen the defendant previously with the same sawed-off rifle as intrinsic evidence of continuity of possession. That made prior-observation testimony admissible without invoking 404(b)’s propensity limitations. Second, the court relied on Confrontation Clause doctrine to conclude there was no constitutional problem because D.F. eventually testified at trial: “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied ‘when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.’” The court also cited its precedent (United States v. Bennett) for the proposition that a district court need not instruct the jury about every item of evidence at the moment it is admitted; a closing instruction or the fact that the declarant testifies in person can be sufficient.

Put simply, the Eighth Circuit gave trial courts discretion to admit officer testimony that includes out-of-court statements, delay any limiting instruction until later, and rely on the declarant’s in-court testimony to cure any potential confrontation issue.

## How this fits Crawford-era Confrontation Clause doctrine

Crawford and the cases that followed created a bright-line rule about testimonial hearsay: testimonial out-of-court statements by witnesses who do not testify are generally inadmissible unless the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. When the declarant actually testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination, the Confrontation Clause concern evaporates. Cutbank applies that straightforward Crawford principle: D.F. testified in court, so officer recounting of her prior statements did not violate the Sixth Amendment.

What the opinion underscores, however, is that Crawford addresses constitutional exclusion; it does not automatically resolve evidentiary or jury-instruction questions. An officer’s narrative may be admissible for a non-hearsay purpose (explaining police conduct, context, or state of mind), and the presence of the declarant at trial removes constitutional bar to its use as substantive evidence. Still, courts must vigilantly guard against the practical risk that jury hearsay will morph into untested substantive proof when the declarant’s in-court testimony is incomplete or impeached in ways that leave the out-of-court statement as the more persuasive account.

## Risks of backdoor hearsay and why limiting instructions matter

There are several practical risks from permitting officer narrative testimony without an immediate limiting instruction:

- Jury confusion: Jurors may not distinguish between an officer’s explanation for an investigative decision and the substantive truth of the statement the officer recounts. Even later testimony from the declarant may be imperfectly assimilated in jurors’ minds if the officer’s narrative was emphatic or vivid.

- Impeachment asymmetry: Defendants sometimes face a situation where the declarant testifies but offers weak or evasive testimony, is successfully impeached, or recants, leaving the powerful, vivid account the officer delivered to hang in the jury’s mind untested. Crawford does not help in that scenario because the declarant did appear in court; the Confrontation Clause is satisfied even if the in-court testimony is unhelpful to the defense.

- Hearsay use by indirection: Even where a statement is framed as “context,” an officer’s testimony can supply independent proof of a key fact — for example, whether the defendant had a weapon or made a threat. If the statement’s evidentiary value is its truth, then it should be assessed under hearsay doctrine and, if necessary, excluded or admitted only with caution.

Cutbank recognizes these risks implicitly by emphasizing the district court’s evidentiary discretion and harmless-error analysis. The court did not find prejudice here because the government’s physical and testimonial proof — DNA on the rifle, eyewitness identification, corroborating items found along the route, and officer observations of defendant’s conduct — was substantial. But the close cases that make these doctrinal tensions live often lack such a robust corroborative record.

## Practical takeaways for trial courts and litigators

For district courts: Cutbank is a reminder that judges have substantial discretion in managing officer narrative testimony, but discretion must be exercised with an eye toward jury perceptions. A few practices can reduce the risk of inadvertent, constitutionally permissible but unfairly prejudicial hearsay:

- Use contemporaneous bench rulings to frame officer narratives narrowly. If officers are permitted to describe statements they heard, expressly limit the purpose of that testimony on the record (e.g., “for the limited purpose of explaining why officers took X action, not for the truth of the matter asserted”), and revisit the issue later if the witness who made the statement testifies.

- Give limiting instructions when the officer’s recounting is particularly narrative or emotionally salient. The Bennett line the Eighth Circuit cites does not forbid upfront instructions; it merely says they are not always required. In practice, an early brief instruction can prevent jury confusion without handicapping the prosecution.

- Consider sequestration and sequencing. If the declarant will testify, consider timing so that the officer’s testimony about the declarant’s statements occurs after the declarant’s in-court testimony, or at least ensure the jury hears the declarant’s version soon after the officer’s narrative.

For prosecutors: Cutbank reinforces that you can use officer narrative testimony to explain police conduct, but be mindful of Confrontation and hearsay concerns. Whenever possible, bring the declarant to court early so confrontation issues are moot. When relying on officer testimony for context, avoid phrasing that invites the jury to treat the officer as a proxy witness of substantive facts.

For defense counsel: Preserve the record. Ask for limiting instructions when appropriate and request contemporaneous clarifications on the record. When the declarant testifies, use cross-examination to highlight discrepancies between the officer’s account and the declarant’s in-court statements; if the declarant’s testimony is weak, ask the court to instruct the jury on the limited purpose of the officer’s earlier recounting to prevent lingering prejudice. And in close cases, press for a curative instruction at the time the discrepancy emerges.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Cutbank does not shift the axis of Confrontation Clause law. It applies settled Crawford doctrine — appearance and cross‑examination by the declarant cures the Sixth Amendment issue — and reiterates the appellate deference afforded to district courts in managing jury instructions and evidentiary rulings. But it also highlights an enduring doctrinal gap between Crawford’s constitutional floor and the evidentiary protections that trial courts can and sometimes must supply.

The larger doctrinal takeaway is practical rather than doctrinally novel: courts can and will allow some officer narrative testimony without immediate limiting instructions, but appellate courts will treat such decisions as reviewable only for abuse of discretion and will look for substantial prejudice on the record. That means the real battleground remains at the trial level, where the sequence of testimony, the vividness of the officer’s narrative, and the availability of corroborative evidence decide how much weight juries afford out-of-court statements.

In cases where the declarant later testifies but gives equivocal or impeached testimony, appellate courts will face difficult harmless-error assessments. Cutbank suggests those assessments will often favor affirmance if there is ample independent evidence of guilt. That pragmatic posture should encourage defense counsel to focus on building record-based objections and asking for limiting instructions early, and should encourage prosecutors to structure their case so declarations to police are corroborated and not relied on as the linchpin of proof.

## Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank is a useful exemplar of how appellate courts treat officer narrative testimony in the Crawford era: constitutional bars are straightforward — if the declarant testifies, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied — but evidentiary risks remain. Trial judges have broad discretion to admit and manage such testimony, and appellate review will generally be deferential unless the record shows the jury was substantially swayed by improperly admitted hearsay. Attorneys who want to control how a jury hears — and weighs — officer narratives should focus their efforts at the trial level: seek or resist limiting instructions, sequence witnesses strategically, and sharpen cross-examination so that the declarant’s in-court account, not the officer’s retelling, is what the jury ultimately relies upon.



# You Can’t Read From the Report: Authentication, FRE 613, and Impeaching With Police Paperwork

Why this matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank (No. 24‑1225) provides a crisp lesson for trial lawyers about a commonplace battlefield: impeachment by prior inconsistent statement and the limits on reading into the record portions of police reports that the witness did not author or adopt. The court’s handling of defense counsel’s effort to read parts of an investigator’s report into evidence — and its reliance on United States v. Cumbie — reinforces a basic, often overlooked point: extrinsic impeachment requires authentication and the right foundation. For trial counsel who regularly rely on police reports to impeach witnesses, the opinion is both a reminder and a roadmap: you can cross-examine about what the witness told an officer, but you generally cannot treat the officer’s report as a stand‑in for the witness unless you satisfy the rules.

What the court did

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession. At trial, a government witness (C.F.) gave testimony about seeing the gun “once or twice” before the incident. On cross, the defense attempted to impeach by using statements in the investigator’s report. The district court allowed defense counsel to question C.F. about what she had told the officer but sustained objections when counsel tried to read portions of the police report into evidence without laying the requisite foundation. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing the well‑established rule that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be authenticated. The court expressly invoked Cumbie and emphasized that a report not authored by the witness cannot be read into evidence as the witness’s prior inconsistent statement unless properly authenticated or otherwise admissible under a hearsay exception.

The doctrinal footing: FRE 613(b), 901, 801(d)(1)(A), and the hearsay exceptions

Cutbank is a practical application of several overlapping evidentiary rules — and the seams between them are where many trial disputes arise.

- FRE 613(b) allows extrinsic evidence of a witness’s prior inconsistent statement only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny the statement and the opposing party is given an opportunity to interrogate the witness about it. But 613(b) does not eliminate the separate requirement that extrinsic impeachment be authenticated when the extrinsic evidence is a document or recording. In other words, you can impeach the witness by asking about prior statements; whether you can then introduce a separate document reflecting that statement is a different question.

- FRE 901 requires authentication or identification sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. A police report is not self‑authenticating simply because it appears in the case file. If a portion of a report is offered as the witness’s prior statement, the proponent must show the report (or the specific portion) is truly the declarant’s statement — through adoption, signature, recorded interview, or other linking evidence.

- FRE 801(d)(1)(A) makes certain prior inconsistent statements non‑hearsay only if the declarant testifies and is subject to cross‑examination, and the statement is inconsistent with the declarant’s testimony. Section 801(d)(1)(A) covers prior statements of the witness, but it does not automatically convert someone else’s report into the witness’s prior statement absent adoption or other linking facts.

- FRE 803(5) (past recollection recorded) can admit a document as a recorded recollection when (1) the witness once had knowledge about the matter, (2) the witness now has insufficient recollection to testify fully and accurately, (3) the record was made or adopted by the witness when the matter was fresh in memory, and (4) the record accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge. Importantly, the “adopted” piece is often dispositive: the witness must have adopted the report (or the portion) as their record for 803(5) to apply.

Cutbank shows the court will not tolerate shortcuts: asking the officer’s scribe to stand in for the witness by simply reading aloud sections of a report written by someone else without foundation is vulnerable to exclusion.

Practical foundations that work (and how to get them)

If you want to use a police report in impeachment — whether to read it to the jury or to admit it — plan the foundation before cross. Here are concrete approaches that trial counsel should routinely employ.

1. Use the witness to lay the foundation for adoption (or denial)
Ask the witness on cross whether she spoke to the officer, whether she gave answers in the interview, whether the officer accurately recorded her words, whether she reviewed the report, and whether she signed or initialed it. A clear, contemporaneous adoption — “Yes, that report accurately states what I told Officer X” — can convert the report (or relevant portions) into the witness’s own prior statement for both 801(d)(1)(A) and 803(5).

2. Use the officer-scribe to authenticate the report
If the witness cannot or will not adopt the report, call the interviewing officer as a witness. Ask the officer to identify the report, describe the manner and timing of making it, confirm that the report comprises the witness’s recorded statements, and explain the routine practice for preparing interview reports. This authenticates the report and can establish that the document contains the witness’s prior statements (supporting 801(d)(1)(A) if the declarant testifies) or that the report is a recorded recollection (803(5)) if the witness lacks present recollection.

3. Invoke recorded recollection (FRE 803(5)) carefully
Recorded recollection is a powerful tool but strictly requires that the witness once had knowledge, that the witness now cannot fully recall, and that the report was made when the matter was fresh and accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge. That means you should: elicit that the witness reviewed the report shortly after the event; demonstrate the timing; and show the witness cannot now recall the details well enough to testify, making the recorded recollection necessary. If these elements are met, the writing may be read into evidence (and often received as an exhibit).

4. Use 801(d)(1)(A) when the witness adopts the statement
If the witness testifies at trial and admits she previously made the inconsistent statement, that statement becomes non‑hearsay. The safest path: before introducing the document, secure the witness’s acknowledgment that she made the earlier statement reflected in the report. Once the witness has been confronted and adopts the prior statement, admission is usually straightforward.

5. Prepare to authenticate hearsay exceptions or business records
Other pathways include the business‑records exception, but police investigative reports often pose problems there because they are prepared for litigation and contain opinions. If you hope to admit the report under Rule 803(6), prepare the officer or custodian to testify about regular record‑keeping, timing, and trustworthiness — but be cautious: many courts resist admitting investigative reports under 803(6).

Practical templates and questions to use at trial

Below are short, practical lines of questioning to build a foundation. Tailor language to the facts and sequence them so you can pivot depending on the witness’s answers.

A. Foundation for adoption by the witness
Q: Did you speak with Officer [Name] on [date] about the events at [location]?
Q: During that conversation, did Officer [Name] ask you questions about what you saw?
Q: Were your answers written down by Officer [Name] in a report?
Q: Did you review that report shortly after it was prepared?
Q: Do you recall whether the report accurately reflects the statements you gave to Officer [Name]?
If yes: Your Honor, I offer the portions of the report containing [quote] as a prior statement by this witness under Rule 801(d)(1)(A).

B. Foundation via the officer-scribe
Q (to officer): Are you the author of this report?
Q: When did you prepare this report in relation to your interview with [witness]?
Q: How did you prepare it — contemporaneous notes, recording, memory?
Q: To your knowledge, did [witness] review or sign this report?
Q: Does the report accurately reflect the statements made to you during the interview?
If yes: Your Honor, the defense offers this report under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) / 803(5) (as applicable), authenticated by the interviewing officer.

C. Foundation for recorded recollection (803(5))
Q: At the time of the interview, did you have a clear recollection of the events you were asked about?
Q: Did you record those recollections in a writing shortly after the interview?
Q: Is that writing the document marked [Exhibit X]?
Q: Do you now have a sufficient recollection to testify fully and accurately about [specific fact]?
If witness says “No” or “Not clearly”: Q: Would this writing help refresh your recollection? (If it refreshes, do not offer; if witness still cannot recall and testifies that the writing accurately reflects what they knew when it was fresh, you can move for admission as recorded recollection.)

Why courts exclude readings from reports without foundation

Cutbank is a reminder that a report is not a free‑standing vehicle for impeachment simply because it contains contradictory material. Reading from the investigator’s file without laying a foundation misstates who said what, risks unfair prejudice, and sidesteps the adversary process by letting an unsworn document speak for a witness. The authentication requirement is not mere formality — it protects both the jury’s factfinding and the Confrontation Clause interests when a document is used to prove prior statements.

Downstream effects and takeaways

This decision will not revolutionize impeachment practice, but it reinforces prevailing Eighth Circuit law and the practical discipline of trial craft. Defense counsel should not treat police reports as disposable props; instead, they should prepare a clear foundation for adoption, call the officer as a witness if necessary, or use recorded‑recollection mechanics when the witness cannot now remember. Prosecutors, conversely, should be ready to block unsourced readings of reports and to offer the appropriate witness foundation early to prevent trial disruption.

Finally, Cutbank illustrates a recurring appellate truth: evidentiary disputes are often preserved when trial counsel lays the proper predicate. But lacking that predicate, the appellate court will enforce the authentication rule rather than excuse the omission. For trial teams on both sides, the lesson is practical and immediate — plan your impeachment strategy around the rules that govern what the jury can actually read or hear, and be ready to prove the foundation in real time.

Conclusion

You can impeach a witness with what she told a police officer; you just can’t let the report do the impeachment for you unless you authenticate it or the witness adopts it. Cutbank and Cumbie together make that point forcefully. The roadmap is clear: obtain adoption, call the officer, or establish recorded recollection. Do that work at trial, and you avoid the predictable “sustained” and the predictable affirmation on appeal.



# Harmless Error as Endgame: Why Evidentiary Appeals Keep Failing in the Eighth Circuit

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is the latest iteration of a familiar story in the Eighth Circuit: a defendant mounts a multi-front challenge to trial rulings—evidentiary decisions, confrontation and hearsay questions, limits on impeachment material—and the court affirms the conviction not because the rulings were unimpeachable, but because any error was harmless in light of the “substantial” body of testimonial and physical proof. The panel’s opinion, drawing on DeMarce and Keys, reiterates the circuit’s high bar for reversal based on evidentiary error. For defense counsel, the practical takeaway is blunt: absent a preserved, compelling record showing a realistic risk that the jury was “substantially swayed,” appellate review is unlikely to undo a conviction supported by DNA, eyewitness testimony, and other corroboration.

Cutbank’s case is illustrative because it presents the typical mix that courts find hard to overturn: contested witness testimony about prior observation of the weapon, police statements and reports, disputed impeachment avenues, and emotionally charged collateral material (the victim’s sister’s death by gun). Yet the physical evidence—DNA on the rifle, the victim’s phone along the route Cutbank likely took, and corroborating witness identifications—anchored the verdict. The panel’s analysis shows how the harmless-error inquiry operates as an endgame—the last, often decisive step in preserving convictions following arguably contestable trial rulings.

## The panel’s reasoning — a close reading

The opinion resolutely proceeds in two parts: first, a rapid dismissal of several evidentiary challenges by characterizing the contested testimony as intrinsic or properly admissible; second, a conventional harmless-error analysis accepting that, even if any marginal mistakes occurred, they could not have meaningfully affected the jury’s verdict.

On admissibility, the court leans on long-standing principles. Prior sightings of the same sawed-off Marlin rifle were treated as intrinsic evidence—tending to show possession, control, or continuity of possession—which the court tied to Battle. The court likewise treated police recounting of the victim’s statements as essentially non-prejudicial where the declarant (D.F.) later testified in person, thereby satisfying Confrontation Clause concerns (Charboneau). Cross-examination and impeachment disputes—such as limits on reading segments of an investigator’s report into the record—were resolved under authentication and foundational rules (Cumbie), rather than as constitutional or per se reversible errors.

When it moves to harmlessness, the opinion relies on DeMarce and Keys for the pivotal formulation: reversal requires a showing that the jury "may have been substantially swayed" by the alleged error. The panel applied that standard with deference to the district court’s factual findings and the PSR’s adopted statements at sentencing. The cumulative-error doctrine received a brief, familiar dispatch: only substantial prejudice arising from multiple errors would justify reversal, and here the physical and testimonial record was too robust for such a showing.

## What the opinion shows about the “substantially swayed” standard in practice

Cutbank demonstrates three features of the Eighth Circuit’s harmless-error approach that trial lawyers must internalize.

First, the standard is outcome-sensitive and fact-intensive. Courts repeatedly point to DNA on the rifle, cell phone found on the likely route, victim and housemate identifications, and the defendant’s resistance and biting of an officer. When physical evidence and corroborating testimony are present, the appellate court is predisposed to treat most evidentiary disputes as unlikely to have moved the jury’s view.

Second, the opinion emphasizes preservation and evidentiary foundation at trial. The court enforced authentication requirements for impeachment with extrinsic documents and stressed that the district court had made pretrial rulings treating certain testimony as intrinsic evidence. In practice, this means appellate courts will scrutinize whether defense counsel (1) lodged precise contemporaneous objections; (2) tried to create a record by proffering excluded evidence or asking for limiting instructions; and (3) took available steps to authenticate material that the defense intended to use for impeachment. Absent those efforts, the appellate record is thin and harmless-error review will be correspondingly deferential.

Third, cumulative-error reversal is rare unless errors interact to create a realistic, aggregate likelihood of prejudice. The panel refused to aggregate the alleged mistakes into something that could have “substantially sway[ed]” the jury. Courts will not assume that separate, modest errors suddenly become prejudicial in the face of a strong evidentiary tableau.

## Doctrinal implications and the downstream effect on trial strategy

Doctrinally, Cutbank reinforces two settled but consequential rules. One is that evidence of prior possession or knowledge about an instrumentality (here, the rifle) is often intrinsic and therefore admissible under Rule 404(b) concerns, at least where it tends directly to show control or continuity. The second is that appellate harmless-error analysis will be heavily informed by the existence of independent physical proof—DNA, recovered items, forensic linkage—which functions as a kind of appellate insurance.

The practical downstream effects for defense attorneys are significant. If you are defending a client in a case with strong physical corroboration, the realistic goal at trial shifts from hoping for a clean, unassailable record for appellate reversal to being aggressive about preservation and record-building:

- Make contemporaneous, specific objections and obtain rulings on the record. Vague or belated objections are easy to fold into harmlessness rationales.
- When the court excludes or limits impeachment materials (police reports, notes), move to proffer the excluded content outside the jury’s presence and obtain a ruling that precisely identifies the basis for exclusion. That makes clear whether the exclusion was foundational (authentication) or substantive (hearsay), and it gives an appellate court something concrete to evaluate.
- Seek limiting instructions promptly when evidence carries potential for prejudice, and, if the court refuses, ask for a running record indicating how the evidence was offered and for what purpose.
- For incidents involving emotionally fraught collateral facts (e.g., a relative being killed by a gun), be mindful that spontaneous, responsive testimony elicited on cross-examination is frequently allowed; if you want exclusion, get a pretrial ruling or a motion in limine with full briefing.
- If a forensic or DNA link exists, anticipate that appellate harmless-error review will be skeptical; allocate trial resources accordingly (e.g., focus on undermining the reliability or chain of custody of forensics rather than litigating ancillary evidentiary minutiae that the court may deem non-prejudicial).

## An analytical perspective: is this healthy for appellate review?

From an institutional perspective, the Eighth Circuit’s approach is defensible. Harmless-error review exists to avoid undermining verdicts where the totality of evidence establishes guilt beyond other reasonable dispute. The appellate court’s role is not to retry the facts and overturn convictions merely because defendants can point to one or two debatable trial rulings. Cutbank follows that logic.

But there is a tension. When appellate courts dispose of claims primarily on harmlessness rather than on the correctness of legal rulings, two problems arise. First, the law’s precedential value is blunted. A ruling that “any error was harmless” does not develop fine-grained standards about how trial courts should handle close evidentiary questions. Second, the standard can, at times, shift the burden toward defendants to produce a counterfactual showing of how the jury might have been persuaded otherwise—a hard task when the trial record is silent because objections were not fully pressed or proffers not made.

Cutbank therefore does double duty: it reminds trial counsel that diligent preservation matters and it signals to district courts that evidentiary discretion will be upheld if the verdict is tethered to corroborative physical or eyewitness evidence. For appellate courts, the case is an invitation to remain pragmatic. But for those concerned about consistency and doctrinal development, there is a countervailing need for appellate opinions that squarely address and, where appropriate, correct troubling trial law errors rather than dismissing them as harmless in cases with strong factual records.

## Conclusion — what defense counsel should take away

Cutbank is not novel. It reiterates established Eighth Circuit lines: prior possession can be intrinsic; an in-court recantation or later testimony cures Confrontation Clause problems; extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated; and absent clear prejudice, evidentiary rulings rarely require reversal when corroborative proof exists. The guiding lesson is procedural and pragmatic: to preserve appellate avenues, defense counsel must build a meticulous trial record—timely, specific objections; detailed proffers of excluded evidence; requests for limiting instructions; and explicit preservation of cumulative-error arguments.

If your client’s case rests on contesting forensic linkage or other central proofs, litigate those core issues vigorously at trial. If, instead, you are forced to wage a war on peripheral evidentiary skirmishes, recognize that appellate courts will likely classify many such skirmishes as harmless in the face of DNA, eyewitnesses, and a coherent narrative. In short: at the Eighth Circuit, harmless error is often the endgame—defense counsel should plan accordingly.



## Stacking Enhancements on Top of ACCA: The Reach of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) After Kisor

Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank is an important, if understated, contribution to two recurring sentencing fights: (1) how readily a district court may apply a four‑level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession “in connection with another felony,” even when the defendant is also an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) defendant; and (2) the inference courts will draw from unobjected‑to facts in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) — and from commentary to the Guidelines — when calculating the advisory range. The opinion affirms a within‑range 292‑month sentence reached by layering § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) on top of the ACCA result and other firearm adjustments, relying in significant part on the PSR and the Guidelines’ commentary note 14(C). That posture crystallizes several doctrinal tensions: potential double‑counting of conduct across enhancements, the force of Guidelines commentary in the post‑Kisor era, and the practical necessity of preserving “in connection with” objections for further review.

What the court held and why

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and found to be an armed career criminal under § 924(e). The PSR recommended several enhancements under USSG § 2K2.1, including a four‑level enhancement under subsection (b)(6)(B) because the firearm was “possessed in connection with another felony offense.” The district court adopted the PSR’s factual recitation without objection. On appeal Cutbank argued that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement in addition to ACCA (and the other enhancements) produced an excessive sentence and that the enhancement should not be applied in these circumstances.

The Eighth Circuit rejected those contentions. Two features of the court’s analysis are notable. First, the court emphasized that the district court adopted the PSR’s statements of fact and that Cutbank failed to preserve a meaningful objection to the PSR findings at sentencing. Where factual findings are adopted without objection, the appellate standard is highly deferential and reversal requires clear error. Second, the court pointed to the Guidelines commentary — specifically the note that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” — as supporting the enhancement’s applicability even when other sentencing regimes (like ACCA) are also in play. Finally, the court framed the issue as largely one of procedural preservation and sentencing discretion under § 3553(a): the district court calculated the Guidelines range and said it considered the § 3553(a) factors, and a within‑range sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal.

How the court’s reasoning fits into the larger doctrinal landscape

Two doctrinal axes intersect here.

First, the double‑counting or “overlap” concern. Defendants have long argued that applying multiple enhancements or separate sentencing regimes based on the same underlying conduct—stacking a Guidelines enhancement that punishes possession in connection with another felony on top of ACCA’s severe penalty that rests on prior violent felonies—can amount to impermissible double punishment or produce an unreasonable sentence. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank treats this primarily as a discretionary problem for the district court to weigh within the § 3553(a) calculus (and as one a defendant must preserve). The court does not treat the potential overlap as a per se legal bar to applying both regimes, especially when the Guidelines commentary and the PSR support the factual predicate for the enhancement.

Second, the interpretive force of Guidelines commentary after Kisor v. Wilkie. For decades, courts often treated commentary to the United States Sentencing Guidelines as highly persuasive (or even controlling) when reasonably construed. Kisor limited the deference owed to administrative interpretations in the Auer context, insisting on careful steps before any deference is granted and pushing courts to apply traditional tools of statutory (or text) construction first. While Kisor addressed agency interpretive deference in the administrative law arena, its doctrinal ripples affect how courts approach the Sentencing Commission’s commentary: courts are less likely to treat commentary as commanding unless the commentary is a reasonable construction of an ambiguous Guideline and the usual Kisor predicates are satisfied.

Cutbank, however, does not wade into a full Kisor‑style interpretive battle. The panel leans on the PSR and the plain language of the commentary’s note (n.14(C)) to justify the enhancement — and the defendant’s failure to object at sentencing meant the court did not need to decide hard questions about the permissible scope of the commentary under Kisor. That posture makes the decision an important example of how procedural default and adoption of PSR facts can blunt or avoid broader interpretive fights.

Analytical perspective: strengths, weaknesses, and preservation strategy

The Eighth Circuit’s result is defensible on conventional sentencing and procedural grounds. If a defendant does not contest the PSR findings and the district court properly calculates the Guidelines range, appellate courts are rightly reluctant to upset within‑range sentences. The court also correctly notes that § 2K2.1’s commentary expressly contemplates application of the enhancement even without a companion conviction for the “other felony,” which forecloses granular arguments that the government must have separately charged or proved contemporaneous other‑felony convictions beyond a preponderance of the evidence.

But the decision leaves open important questions that litigants — and higher courts — may want to resolve. First, there is a real doctrinal tension between ACCA and Guidelines enhancements tailored to conduct that also constitutes the predicate for ACCA: when does layering a Guidelines enhancement meaningfully double count? The Eighth Circuit treats this as a matter for the sentencing court to consider in exercising discretion under § 3553(a), but a persuasive legal argument can be mounted that some enhancements should be categorically inapplicable when they directly replicate conduct for which a statutory enhanced penalty has already been imposed. Absent such a rule, district courts must be vigilant in explaining how multiple regimes serve distinct deterrent or incapacitative goals and are not merely cumulative punishment for the same conduct.

Second, plaintiffs who want to challenge the interpretive force of commentary like note 14(C) post‑Kisor must preserve that fight at sentencing. Because Kisor calls for a textured analysis before affording deference to an agency’s (or Commission’s) interpretation, appellate review will be easier if the district court has a record on whether the Guideline text is genuinely ambiguous, whether the commentary is a reasonable construction of that text, and whether any special circumstances weigh against deference. Cutbank’s failure to make those arguments below effectively insulated the enhancement from appellate scrutiny.

Circuit splits and the road ahead

Circuits differ in their willingness to apply Guidelines commentary to support enhancements and in their treatment of “in connection with” enhancements. Some courts focus on the objective causal relationship between the firearm and the other felony conduct (looking for a temporal or functional nexus), while others accept broader linkages. Similarly, courts have varied in how much weight to give PSR statements adopted without objection. The practical upshot is predictable: a defendant seeking to overturn a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement should (1) object at sentencing to the PSR’s factual recitations and demand live proof of the “in connection with” predicate, (2) press the district court to articulate reasons why applying the enhancement does not improperly double count conduct punished by ACCA (or other statutes), and (3) preserve a Kisor‑style challenge to the Guidelines commentary by asking the court to analyze textual ambiguity and the reasonableness (or not) of the Commission’s construction.

For appellate advocates eyeing en banc review or certiorari, that preserved record is essential. A fully developed record would allow a court to confront squarely whether the Sentencing Commission’s commentary — when it says the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought” — should carry dispositive interpretive weight in cases where other, overlapping sentencing regimes are in play. It would also let higher courts clarify whether application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in ACCA cases presents a structural problem of double counting, or merely a discretionary judgment that district courts must address under § 3553(a).

Downstream effects

The practical lesson from Cutbank is immediate and procedural: defendants facing possible § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) exposure must litigate the factual and legal predicates at sentencing, not save them for appeal. More substantively, unless and until a circuit or the Supreme Court embraces a categorical limit on stacking Guidelines enhancements with ACCA, districts will continue to apply both regimes, provided they make at least a minimal record showing they considered § 3553(a) factors and that the PSR or evidence supports the enhancement. That reality increases the importance of rigorous PSR challenges and of obtaining explicit district‑court findings on how multiple enhancements operate without producing impermissible double punishment.

Conclusion

Cutbank underscores a simple but powerful dynamic in modern federal sentencing: procedural choices at sentencing — adoption of the PSR, failure to object to Guidelines treatment, and the absence of a Kisor‑style interpretive record — often determine the outcome as much as the substantive merits of an enhancement claim. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion does not resolve the harder doctrinal questions about double counting and the interpretive status of Guidelines commentary; it merely shows how those questions can be avoided when defendants do not preserve them. For practitioners, the takeaway is plain: litigate these issues early, make the court decide them expressly, and build a record that will allow an appellate court (or the Supreme Court) to confront the larger tensions left open by decisions like Cutbank.



# Who Finds ACCA Predicates? The Curious Use of a Jury for Prior Convictions

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank is, on its face, a garden‑variety affirmation of a felon‑in‑possession conviction and an ACCA sentence. Read more closely, however, and it highlights a strategic procedural choice that has been quietly gaining traction: having a jury, rather than a judge, make the factual finding that a defendant has the requisite number and type of prior convictions for the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). That choice sits at the intersection of Sixth Amendment doctrine (think Apprendi and Almendarez‑Torres), the stubbornly tricky “categorical” approach to predicate offenses (made harder by recent decisions like Borden), and Shepard/Shepard‑adjacent limits on what evidence courts may consult to establish the content of prior convictions.

Cutbank reminds us that—even though Almendarez‑Torres long has been read to allow judges to find prior convictions for sentence‑enhancement purposes—prosecutors can (and sometimes do) ask juries to make those findings. The decision offers a useful occasion to think about why governments would do that, what it means for ACCA litigation in the post‑Borden era, and whether jury findings buy appellate stability or merely shift dispute from one doctrinal battleground to another.

## The doctrinal background in brief

Two doctrinal strands frame the choice: first, Apprendi v. New Jersey (and its progeny) established that any fact that increases the statutory maximum penalty must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Almendarez‑Torres v. United States, though, created an important exception for the “fact” of prior convictions—permitting judges to find priors for sentence‑enhancement statutes without offending the Sixth Amendment. Thus, many ACCA cases historically proceeded with judges making the prior‑conviction findings at sentencing.

Second, the categorical (and modified categorical) approach governs whether a prior conviction counts as an ACCA “violent felony” or “serious drug offense.” Decisional law—most notably Borden v. United States—has narrowed the universe of convictions that qualify as violent felonies under ACCA’s force clause by excluding offenses that can be committed with reckless mens rea. The interplay of these rules often forces courts to look carefully at the statutory elements of prior offenses and, when statutes are “divisible,” to examine a limited set of documents (the Shepard line) to determine which statutory variant produced the conviction.

That combination—(a) Almendarez‑Torres allowing judicial factfinding about priors; (b) a categorical approach that can require fine‑grained parsing of prior statutes; and (c) limits on what materials a sentencing court can consult about those convictions—creates a set of hard choices for both prosecutors and defense counsel about where to resolve disputes: at trial, or at sentencing?

## Why a prosecutor might put priors to a jury

Cutbank illustrates one persuasive strategic reason: liability and sentencing are connected, and some facts that bear on whether a prior conviction qualifies under ACCA are genuinely disputed in ways not easily resolvable under the Shepard regime at sentencing. By asking the jury to find that a defendant has three prior violent felonies, the government avoids reliance on a judge parsing record‑of‑conviction materials after the fact. A jury verdict that a defendant “was previously convicted” of specific offenses can foreclose, or at least narrow, collateral arguments that a sentencing judge improperly considered extrinsic evidence about a conviction.

There are several other practical motivations behind putting priors to juries:

- Sixth Amendment insulation: Although Almendarez‑Torres remains good law, prosecuting through the jury reduces the banner argument a defendant might mount later—namely, an Apprendi‑based challenge that a judicial finding enlarged the statutory maximum. Putting the facts before the jury makes the § 3553 landscape less vulnerable to attack on procedural constitutional grounds.

- Fact disputes about identity or occurrence: Some sentencing disputes are not about statutory elements but about whether the defendant is the same person convicted previously, whether the conviction actually occurred, or other provenance questions (e.g., a vacated conviction). Those are classic jury facts and sometimes easier to prove to a jury through witnesses and documentary proof admitted in evidence at trial than to a judge at sentencing under Shepard constraints.

- Avoiding ambiguous record problems: When a prior statute is divisible in ways that create doubt under the categorical approach, a government might prefer a jury to hear testimonial evidence that clarifies which statutory variant was used, rather than having a sentencing judge try to glean dispositive facts from incomplete or ambiguous paperwork.

Cutbank’s jury finding of three or more violent felonies fits that mold: the government asked the jury to make the predicate‑count finding, and the jury did so. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion treated that outcome as a settled fact and focused on other evidentiary and guidelines issues.

## Interaction with the categorical approach after Borden (and coexisting doctrinal friction)

Borden stripped out crimes that can be committed recklessly from the ACCA force clause’s “violent felony” definition. That narrowing made many prior statutes—especially broadly written assault statutes—dangerous predicates at sentencing. When a sentencing judge is required to perform the categorical analysis, the judge is often limited to Shepard‑type documents (charging papers, plea agreements, transcripts, jury instructions) to determine which statutory variant the conviction rested on; anything beyond that risks running afoul of the rule that sentencing courts cannot hold later factfinding trials about elements of prior convictions.

Because Borden reduced the universe of qualifying priors and, in doing so, increased the likelihood that a prior conviction’s status will depend on subtle questions about mens rea and statute divisibility, the government sometimes faces an uncomfortable choice: leave the matter to a judge constrained by Shepard and risk losing a predicate, or have the jury decide and thereby make the predicate an express part of the verdict.

That choice, however, is not a doctrinal panacea. If the government puts predicates to a jury, questions arise about what exactly the jury is being asked to find and what evidentiary materials the jury may consider to reach that finding. Unlike a sentencing judge bound by Shepard, a jury at trial may hear broader testimonial and documentary evidence. That can be both an advantage (because the jury can fairly resolve factual disputes) and a source of new legal challenges—about whether the jury was permitted to consider evidence that at sentencing would be off‑limits, or whether the conviction record the jury relied upon was appropriately authenticated.

Thus, jury findings can bypass some categorical approach constraints by making the question of “did the defendant have these convictions?” one of the jury’s fact findings rather than a judicial legal parsing at sentencing. But they also raise new issues: did the government elicit facts about the prior convictions that go beyond the permissible Shepard universe? If so, does that create Confrontation or hearsay concerns, or open the door to claims that the jury’s predicate determination was based on evidence the law would not allow at sentencing?

Cutbank does not resolve these tensions; the opinion simply accepts the jury finding and moves on. But the case illustrates the practical tug toward jury involvement that Borden and its kin have produced.

## Do jury findings reduce appellate risk—or invite new complexities?

Jury findings can reduce certain appellate arguments: a jury verdict that includes a finding of qualifying priors is harder to frame as a judicial factfinding violation under Apprendi or Ring. Put differently, having the jury do the heavy lifting can blunt procedural constitutional arguments that a judge improperly exceeded the Sixth Amendment in making factual determinations that increase punishment.

But that is only half the equation. Shifting predicate‑determination to the jury can invite other appeals:

- Evidentiary and confrontation claims. Trials where juries hear testimony or documents about prior convictions can create new grounds for appeal if, for example, the defendant argues the evidence was hearsay or violated the Confrontation Clause. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in Cutbank expressly considered and rejected such challenges, but those issues remain live in other cases.

- Ambiguity about what the jury really decided. A general verdict that the defendant committed the charged offense and also had three prior violent felonies may not reveal which prior convictions the jury relied on, or which statutory variants. That can complicate later collateral attacks or eligibility analyses for resentencing. A jury’s finding may be broad on the record, leaving lingering legal questions on remand or appeal.

- Shepard‑line friction. If a jury reaches its predicate determination based on broader evidence than Shepard would allow at sentencing, defendants may contend that the conviction rested on evidence that the law otherwise disallows for defining judicial sentencing enhancements—creating a new species of challenge about the permissible universe of proof for prior‑conviction predicates at trial versus at sentencing.

- Greater trial complexity and risk of jury confusion. Presenting prior‑conviction proof to the jury can lengthen trial, risk confusing jurors with collateral matters, and expose non‑jury issues (vacatur of prior convictions, identity disputes) to a jury that may not be equipped to parse them fairly.

So jury findings are not a guaranteed shield; they trade one set of appellate risks for others. Whether that exchange is worthwhile depends on case‑specific features: the clarity of the prior statutes, the availability and persuasiveness of Shepard‑type records, the strength of alternative evidence about identity and chronology, and the prosecutor’s appetite for a perhaps longer, more complex proceeding.

## A practical and doctrinal takeaway

Cutbank signals a pragmatic approach that we should expect to see more often: prosecutors will use the jury when doing so makes the government’s path to an ACCA enhancement more reliable or simpler in the short run, especially in the post‑Borden world where mens rea questions have become dispositive for many potential predicates. But that strategy is not free of tradeoffs. It can bring confrontation and hearsay issues into play, complicate the appellate record with ambiguous jury findings, and create fights about what evidence a jury may lawfully consider in making those predicate determinations.

For courts, the smarter route would be clearer guidance about the permissible evidence and jury instructions when juries are asked to find priors for sentencing enhancements. For practitioners, the lesson is strategic: consider early whether to submit priors to the jury, and if you do, build a trial record that anticipates the Shepard limitations and future appellate scrutiny—be explicit about which prior convictions are at issue, how identity is established, and why the jury may consider the particular evidence offered. That way, a jury finding becomes not just a procedural workaround but a durable resolution of the predicate question.

Cutbank does not settle these debates, but it is a helpful data point in the evolving practice of ACCA litigation. As the law continues to roam between Apprendi, Almendarez‑Torres, the categorical approach, and Shepard constraints, expect more creative procedural moves—and more appeals testing the limits of where, and how, ACCA predicates should be found.



# Presuming Reasonableness, Explaining Less: The Eighth Circuit’s § 3553(a) Minimalism

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is a compact but telling decision about how the Eighth Circuit treats within-Guidelines sentences and the level of explanation a district court must provide under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Faced with a near‑25‑year sentence for a felon‑in‑possession conviction amplified by ACCA and multiple Guidelines enhancements, the panel affirmed without demanding a detailed on‑the‑record breakdown of the § 3553(a) factors. Instead, the court relied on a line of recent precedent — Goodhouse, Masood, and Maluoth — to reinforce the principle that when a district court calculates the Guidelines correctly, hears argument, reviews the presentence report, and acknowledges the § 3553(a) framework, a terse statement that the court considered the factors is generally sufficient to allow appellate review of substantive reasonableness.

That mode of appellate review matters in two ways. First, it shapes district‑court behavior at sentencing: how much narrative explanation must judges give when imposing a within‑Guidelines sentence? Second, it shapes defense advocacy: what record-building strategies will preserve and press claims that a Guidelines sentence is greater than necessary? Cutbank offers an instructive example of the Eighth Circuit’s current tolerance for abbreviated § 3553(a) reasoning — and the tactical implications that follow.

## What the Eighth Circuit did

The court’s procedural posture was straightforward. Cutbank challenged, among other things, the four‑level USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement and the substantive reasonableness of a within‑Guidelines 292‑month sentence. On the § 3553(a) point, the panel affirmed, emphasizing three elements the district court had performed: it adopted the PSR, reviewed sentencing memoranda, and explicitly stated it had considered the § 3553(a) factors. The court cited Gall’s starting point — that sentencing must begin with a correct Guidelines calculation — and the line of Eighth Circuit cases holding that a district court need not recite a “mechanical” checklist so long as the record makes clear the court considered the relevant factors. Because Cutbank’s criminal history and the offense circumstances were serious, and because the record reflected consideration of mitigating arguments, the panel concluded the within‑Guidelines sentence was substantively reasonable and presumptively so on appeal.

Importantly, the court treated Cutbank’s challenge to the § 2K2.1 enhancement as a separate procedural calculation issue and resolved it against him largely on the PSR/adoption record. That decision reinforced the interplay between factual adoption at sentencing (the PSR and its adoption by the district court) and later appellate deference.

## An analytical perspective: deference, minimalism, and the burden of explanation

Cutbank fits a pattern in which the Eighth Circuit gives substantial deference to within‑Guidelines sentences and accepts relatively minimal on‑the‑record explanation from district courts. The panel’s approach is pragmatic: when the Guidelines calculation is undisputed and the court demonstrates awareness of § 3553(a), the appellate court will presume substantive reasonableness absent an obvious disconnect between the sentence and the statutory factors. This approach promotes sentencing predictability and reduces appellate second‑guessing of discretionary choices informed by a Guidelines framework.

At the same time, that minimalism raises doctrinal friction. The Supreme Court in Gall and Kimbrough requires that sentencing be individualized and that district courts consider the statutory factors — but those decisions stopped short of prescribing a fixed verbal formula. The tension is this: appellate courts must ensure that courts actually engaged in individualized sentencing, yet they must avoid imposing rigid verbal requirements that would transform § 3553(a) review into an exercise in stylistic compliance. Cutbank demonstrates the Eighth Circuit’s current equilibrium point — courts get credit for consideration when they show, in a few discrete ways, that they did the work.

But the equilibrium has consequences. The presumption of reasonableness for within‑Guidelines sentences can become, in practice, difficult to rebut unless defense counsel extracts a more detailed record of the district court’s balancing of § 3553(a) factors, or unless the sentence is plainly excessive relative to the offense and offender. That raises costs for defendants who seek non‑Guidelines outcomes: they must litigate the fact issues (contest PSR adoption, present mitigating evidence, and press for a reasoned explanation), or else face a heavy uphill on appeal.

## Comparing the Eighth Circuit to other circuits

Cutbank should be understood against a national backdrop where circuits diverge on how much explanation suffices. Some circuits have demanded more explicit engagement with non‑frivolous mitigation arguments when imposing within‑Guidelines sentences, especially in cases involving unusual facts, novel mitigation claims, or significant numerical disparities between the Guidelines range and what defense considers appropriate. Others, like the Eighth Circuit here, remain comfortable with a concise record so long as it shows the district court’s awareness and consideration of § 3553(a).

That variation matters in practice: in jurisdictions where courts are expected to articulate a more detailed § 3553(a) analysis, defendants may obtain fuller oral rulings and more extended colloquies explaining why a probationary, below‑Guidelines, or variance sentence is not warranted. In the Eighth Circuit today, by contrast, district judges receive greater leeway to be economically written in their justifications, and appellants face a stronger presumption that an unexplained within‑Guidelines sentence is reasonable.

## Downstream effects and doctrinal implications

Two downstream effects follow. First, plea bargaining and sentencing practice may further tilt toward Guidelines reliance. If appellate courts are unlikely to reverse within‑Guidelines sentences unless the record is plainly defective, both prosecutors and defense counsel have a clearer expectation that Guideline outcomes will stand. Second, defense strategy must adapt. Where the appellate safety valve is narrower, the defense needs to litigate and preserve mitigation more aggressively at sentencing itself.

Doctrinally, the Eighth Circuit’s posture highlights the continuing tension between procedural sufficiency and substantive review. The court’s minimalism is defensible as an administrable rule: it avoids formulaic recitations and acknowledges district courts’ frontline role. Yet it also risks eroding the Supreme Court’s emphasis on individualized consideration if appellate review routinely stops at a boilerplate statement that the court considered the § 3553(a) factors. Over time, if other circuits trend the opposite way, the resulting circuit split could invite higher‑court attention to calibrate what constitutionally and prudentially suffices to ensure real, not merely formal, individualized sentencing.

## Practical advice for defense counsel

Cutbank is a reminder that securing a meaningful variance—or preserving a compelling record for appeal—requires active steps at sentencing.

First, litigate PSR adoption carefully. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank relied on the district court’s adoption of the PSR to sustain factual findings tied to Guidelines enhancements. Where possible, identify disputed PSR facts and secure factual objections on the record, including witness testimony or exhibits to contradict inaccurate PSR assertions. Do not allow adverse facts to be adopted without contemporaneous challenge.

Second, present an organized, evidence‑based mitigation package. Bring witnesses, affidavits, treatment records, employment history, and any rehabilitative plans that make a variance plausible. Demonstrative sentencing memoranda that quantify collateral consequences and propose specific alternatives give the district court a framework to engage the § 3553(a) factors meaningfully.

Third, press for an explicit on‑the‑record explanation when the guidelines produce an unusually severe result relative to the offense or offender. Ask the court to articulate how it weighed specific § 3553(a) factors — e.g., the need for deterrence, the seriousness of the offense, rehabilitation prospects, and disparities with similarly situated defendants — and to state why the Guidelines sentence is appropriate. Even if the judge is inclined to be concise, a short, targeted colloquy that links mitigators to statutory factors strengthens appellate review.

Fourth, preserve objections and articulate why the sentence would be greater than necessary. Do not rely on the hope that brevity will be treated as sufficient on appeal; instead, make precise arguments and note the record gaps you believe the court failed to consider.

Finally, if advocating for downward variance, provide concrete, individualized alternatives (treatment plans, programming, halfway‑house placement, or supervised release conditions) so the judge can meaningfully weigh a non‑custodial or reduced prison option.

## Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank reaffirms the Eighth Circuit’s practical approach to § 3553(a): when a district court correctly calculates the Guidelines, reviews the PSR and memoranda, and asserts that it has considered the statutory factors, appellate courts will, in most cases, accept a concise explanation and uphold a within‑Guidelines sentence as substantively reasonable. For defense counsel, that reality imposes a structural imperative — if the objective is a meaningful variance or a preserved basis for appeal, the record must be developed and pressed at sentencing rather than hoped for on appeal. Whether the balance struck by Cutbank and its Eighth Circuit brethren will prove durable — or prompt doctrinal correction by higher courts demanding fuller, more individualized sentencing narratives — is a question whose answer will shape sentencing practice for years to come.



# A Quiet Bench-Slap on Appellate Briefing: Don’t Inflate the Issues

## Why this opinion matters

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank is unremarkable on its facts: a defendant with a lengthy criminal history was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and sentenced under the Armed Career Criminal Act. What makes the decision noteworthy for appellate practitioners, however, is an offhand—but pointed—rebuke to an appellee who “unnecessarily expand[ed] the issues on appeal.” That admonition is terse, but it reflects an important, often underappreciated norm of appellate practice: precision and restraint in framing the issues you press or defend. The remark is not a new doctrinal rule; rather, it is a reminder from the bench about advocacy norms that can influence how a panel perceives a party’s credibility and the court’s willingness to entertain arguments that lack proper preservation or adequate briefing.

This episode matters because many appeals are decided on procedural grounds—preservation, standards of review, harmless-error analysis—or on narrow legal questions. Appellees who try to broaden the litigation by raising issues that were not clearly framed by the appellant or not preserved below risk distracting the panel and undermining their own position. Cutbank offers a compact lesson in appellate discipline: make your record and your argument match the issues you actually need to resolve.

## What the court said and how it fits into familiar doctrines

The court’s comment appears in the context of rejecting a contention that the district court had “thwarted” the defense’s attempt to impeach a witness by reading portions of a police report into the record. Although the government framed and defended an expanded claim on appeal, the panel tersely demurred: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” The court then proceeded to resolve the question on the narrower ground of authentication and the district court’s discretion, citing United States v. Cumbie for the proposition that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated.

That sequence is doctrinally coherent. Appellate courts routinely police the scope of review through several established tools: preservation rules (what objections were made below), standards of review (deference to trial-court discretion), and harmless-error analysis (whether any error affected the verdict). When an appellee—often the government—tries to enlarge the scope of argument beyond what the appellant properly preserved or briefed, the court can and often does respond by (a) treating the novel claim as forfeited; (b) addressing it only to the extent necessary in light of the existing record; or (c) resolving it on the appellee’s narrower fabrication of the record without rewarding expansion. Cutbank demonstrates the third approach: the court pushed the government back into the well-worn path of authentication and harmlessness rather than indulging an overbroad appellate framing.

This posture is consistent with controlling precedents the opinion otherwise relies on—Battle on intrinsic vs. 404(b) evidence, Charboneau on Confrontation Clause satisfaction when a declarant testifies, and Cumbie on the authentication needed for extrinsic impeachment material. The court’s remark is not an invocation of a novel rule of waiver; it is an exhortation to litigants to align their appellate posture with the record and with issues actually briefed and preserved.

## Analytical perspective: why the bench dislikes issue inflation

Judges have practical reasons for disfavoring expansive appellate framing. Panels have limited time and are accustomed to parsing the narrow legal questions presented by the appellant. When appellees try to inject additional arguments or defenses, three undesirable consequences often follow.

First, it creates perceptional costs. A party that enlarges the dispute—especially in ways that appear opportunistic after an adverse ruling below—risks looking like a gamesman rather than a straight advocate. That perception can subtly affect credibility with the panel, particularly in close cases where deference to the district court’s factfinding plays a role.

Second, expansive argumentation strains appellate resources and can muddle the court’s analysis. Courts prefer clarity about what legal question they are resolving; when the record does not squarely present a newly-invoked claim, the court may have to perform its own factfinding (which it generally avoids), apply deferential review, or dispose of the extra argument summarily—outcomes that rarely benefit the party who created the mess.

Third, issue inflation can invite procedural holds such as waiver or forfeiture. Even where an appellate court decides to consider a late-blooming contention, it will do so through the filter of preservation principles and the applicable standard of review. That typically reduces the substantive prospects for relief.

Cutbank thus should be read as a reminder that appellate advocacy is not just a contest of ideas but a craft of restraint. Good advocacy frames the fight where the record is strongest and the law is clearest; it does not attempt wholesale reinvention on appeal.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

From a doctrinal standpoint, Cutbank does not change the law. It does, however, signal how the Eighth Circuit expects litigants—government and defense alike—to behave. Several downstream effects are likely:

- For government counsel, the opinion is a caution: defending a conviction is not a license to broaden the record or raise novel theories in the court of appeals without clear preservation. The court will be skeptical of after-the-fact expansions, and such tactics may make the difference between a measured, deferential review and a sharp, summary disposition.

- For defense counsel, the decision underscores the importance of preserving objections and framing appellate issues narrowly and rigorously. When an appellee tries to expand issues, the defense can counter by calling attention to preservation gaps and the proper standard of review.

- For district courts and panels, the opinion reinforces an institutional norm favoring procedural regularity over ad hoc appellate theater. We can expect future panels to echo this admonition in close cases where outcome may turn on how neatly the parties confined themselves to the preserved record and the issues briefed.

- For litigation managers and appellate shops, Cutbank is a reminder to coordinate trial and appellate strategy. Anticipate avenues the other side might pursue on appeal, and create a clear, preserved record that prevents opportunistic after-the-fact pivots.

None of these consequences requires doctrinal recalibration; rather, they reflect a pragmatic enforcement of existing preservation, waiver, and harmless-error doctrines.

## Practical guidance for practitioners

Cutbank’s admonition is short, but it translates into straightforward practice guidance for both government and defense counsel. Below are practical principles distilled into prose form to help preserve credibility with an appellate panel.

First, be disciplined about the scope of your arguments. On appeal, make the fight the one you actually won below or the narrow question that is squarely preserved. Resist the temptation to invent new theories simply because they might look good at oral argument.

Second, preserve the record meticulously at trial. If you might rely on a particular theory on appeal, make the necessary objections, move to strike, offer limiting instructions, and proffer the evidentiary basis at trial. Absent a clear record, appellate courts will be loath to entertain novel claims.

Third, attend to standards of review. If the district court made a discretionary call—on authentication, limiting instructions, or Rule 403 balancing—remember that appellate courts apply abuse-of-discretion review. Appellees should not act as if such calls invite de novo second-guessing; rather, show how the trial record supports the district court’s reasoned exercise of discretion.

Fourth, be precise in briefing. Appellate briefs should identify the preserved issue, the standard of review, and the exact relief sought. When responding, avoid adding new claims that are not necessary to defeat the appeal; if you must, clearly label them as alternative arguments and explain their preservation status.

Fifth, anticipate and preempt the “credibility tax.” Judges notice gamesmanship. When you present an argument, do so with humility about the record and with candor about the limits of appellate review. That posture can make a persuasive difference in close calls.

Finally, coordinate between trial and appellate counsel. Appellate success often turns on decisions made weeks or months earlier in the district court. Train trial teams to think forward about preservation and to document rulings in ways that make appellate review efficient and straightforward.

## Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank does not alter federal appellate doctrine. What it does is deliver a judicially quiet—but unmistakable—reminder that appellate advocacy rewards restraint, precision, and fidelity to the record. Appellees who “unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal” do so at their own peril: they invite a wary court to pare back the argument, apply hard preservation rules, and perhaps discount the advocate’s credibility. For practitioners who want their briefs to receive a measured, attentive read from an appellate panel, the lesson is simple: keep the issues tight, preserve the record, and let the court decide the narrow questions it was asked to resolve.
