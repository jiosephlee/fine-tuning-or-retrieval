Law Stack Exchange Q&A: United_States_v_Justin_Cutbank

### What makes prior sightings of the same gun “intrinsic” evidence rather than Rule 404(b) propensity evidence?
Question:
The panel affirmed allowing D.F. and C.F. to testify that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the night in question, calling it intrinsic evidence relevant to continuous possession and citing United States v. Battle. I'm confused about the boundary between intrinsic evidence and 404(b) 'other acts' propensity evidence. Why is prior possession of the same weapon considered intrinsic rather than a prior bad act offered to show character? What factors does the Eighth Circuit look at (same weapon, temporal proximity, narrative completeness) to label such evidence intrinsic, and how is that consistent with Rule 404(b)'s prohibition on propensity reasoning?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the witnesses’ testimony about having seen Cutbank with that same sawed-off Marlin rifle before the night in question went to a fact “intrinsic” to the charged offense—identity, ownership/control, and continuous possession of the very weapon at issue—rather than to some independent bad-act or character trait. The Eighth Circuit treated the testimony as directly relevant to proving possession of that specific gun (citing United States v. Battle), not as “other acts” offered to show propensity, so Rule 404(b) did not bar it.

Why that matters (legal doctrine and standard)
- Rule 404(b) governs “other acts” evidence offered for non‑propensity purposes (e.g., motive, intent, absence of mistake). It is exclusionary when prosecutors try to use prior bad acts to show the defendant’s character or propensity to commit the crime.
- By contrast, evidence that is part of the same transaction or that is “intrinsic” to the charged offense is not governed by Rule 404(b) and is admissible if relevant and not unfairly prejudicial. Courts ask whether the evidence is probative of an element of the charged offense (or necessary to give the jury a complete, non‑misleading narrative), rather than whether it merely shows the defendant acted in conformity with propensity.

What the opinion says and how the Eighth Circuit applies it
- The panel framed the issue explicitly: Cutbank argued D.F. and C.F. seeing him with the rifle earlier was propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), while the government and the district court said it was intrinsic to the possession charge. The court agreed with the government: “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” Opinion § II.A (quoting Battle, 774 F.3d at 511).
- The court pointed to the witnesses’ testimony that they had seen the same sawed‑off rifle with Cutbank a “handful of times” (D.F.) and “once or twice” (C.F.) before the incident. See ¶¶ II.A, II.C. That testimony ties the defendant to this particular weapon across time, supporting continuous possession/ownership rather than serving merely to show bad character.

Factors the Eighth Circuit treats as making such evidence intrinsic (as shown in this opinion and Battle)
- Same weapon/identity: The testimony concerned the exact gun recovered and DNA‑linked to Cutbank. Evidence about prior possession of that same item is probative of ownership/control and identity of the weapon at the time of the offense.
- Relevance to an element (possession/continuous control): The prior sightings were offered to prove later possession of the same firearm (an element of § 922(g)), not to prove he was the kind of person who carries guns. The court emphasized the direct probative link: “helps establish ownership or control.”
- Narrative completeness/temporal nexus: The testimony filled in the background that the defendant had possessed or exercised control over that particular firearm before the charged incident—i.e., continuity of possession. The witnesses’ temporal statements (“handful of times,” “once or twice”) are close enough to establish a pattern of possession relevant to the charged event.
- Not an independent bad act: The testimony was not about a separate, uncharged criminal episode offered to prove, for example, intent, motive, or propensity; it concerned the same object and the same course of conduct leading to the charged offense.

How that fits Rule 404(b)’s ban on propensity reasoning
- Rule 404(b) seeks to prevent juries from inferring guilt because a defendant is a “bad person.” The Eighth Circuit’s intrinsic/404(b) line focuses on whether the evidence is probative of an element of the charged crime (or necessary to tell the story) rather than whether it shows bad character.
- Here, because the prior sightings tied Cutbank to the identical weapon and helped prove control/continuous possession (a direct element of the prosecution’s case), the testimony was not being used as propensity evidence and thus was not subject to 404(b)’s exclusionary rule. The court relied on that doctrinal distinction when affirming the district court (¶ II.A), citing Battle.

Practical delimitation (what would make similar evidence 404(b) instead)
- If the government introduced testimony about other guns or other separate violent episodes merely to show Cutbank’s character as violent or as someone who carries weapons generally, that would be classic 404(b) territory.
- If the prior incident involved a different weapon or no clear link to the charged firearm, or if the prior act was temporally remote and not probative of ownership/control of the specific gun, the court would more likely treat it as “other acts” evidence requiring 404(b) analysis.

Conclusion
The panel treated the prior sightings as intrinsic because they directly connected Cutbank to the very weapon charged in the indictment and helped prove continuous possession/ownership—precisely the sort of direct, probative evidence that Rule 404(b) does not reach. The opinion rests on that nexus to the element of possession (citing Battle) and the witnesses’ testimony that they had seen the same sawed‑off rifle with Cutbank before the night in question.

### How does the Eighth Circuit’s 'context for police conduct' rationale avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems?
Question:
The defense sought a pretrial instruction limiting the jury’s use of D.F.’s statements to police until she testified, invoking hearsay and the Confrontation Clause. The court declined, accepting the government’s position that officers would use the statements only to explain their actions. Why is this 'context' use not hearsay (given it still communicates accusatory content to the jury), and how does the Confrontation Clause analysis treat such testimony? Are there Eighth Circuit guardrails to prevent 'backdoor hearsay' when officers recount out-of-court statements 'for context'?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit treated the officers’ recounting of D.F.’s statements as non‑hearsay when offered to explain why officers did what they did (their knowledge, perceptions, and subsequent actions), not to prove the truth of D.F.’s out‑of‑court accusations. That use ordinarily does not trigger the Confrontation Clause problem because D.F. later testified at trial; under Charboneau the Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant actually appears and testifies. The opinion identifies and applies the ordinary Eighth Circuit guardrails: limiting the purpose of the testimony, refusing to admit out‑of‑court reports without authentication, preserving the right to a limiting instruction, and applying harmless‑error review if any hearsay slips in.

Relevant legal doctrine and how it was applied in this opinion
1) Hearsay doctrine — purpose matters.  
- Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c) defines hearsay as an out‑of‑court statement “offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted.” Statements offered for another purpose — e.g., to show the declarant’s effect on a listener, the declarant’s state of mind, or why a police officer acted a certain way — are not hearsay.  
- The district court accepted the government’s representation that officers would testify about D.F.’s statements only to explain their actions/knowledge (i.e., to provide the context for officers’ conduct), not to prove the truth of the accusatory content. The Court of Appeals upheld that approach, saying “the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial.” (Opinion II.B.)

2) Confrontation Clause — testimonial statements and in‑court testimony.  
- Under the Confrontation Clause line of cases (Crawford and its progeny), testimonial out‑of‑court statements by a witness are barred at criminal trial unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross‑examine. But when the declarant actually testifies in court, the Clause is satisfied: they can be cross‑examined then. The opinion cites United States v. Charboneau for that proposition: “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied ‘when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.’” (Opinion II.B.)  
- Here D.F. did testify at trial, so even if officers recounted what she had said earlier, the Confrontation Clause concern was addressed because the declarant was available to be confronted.

Eighth Circuit “guardrails” reflected in the opinion
The opinion demonstrates several limiting principles Eighth Circuit courts apply to prevent backdoor hearsay:

- Purpose limitation and judicial oversight: the court accepted the government’s representation that officers’ testimony would be contextual (to explain officers’ knowledge/actions). That is a classic guardrail: permitting officer testimony only for a non‑hearsay purpose and requiring the trial court to police that purpose during testimony. (Opinion II.B.)

- Limiting instruction doctrine: the court has discretion whether to give contemporaneous or closing limiting instructions; appellate review of refusal to give a limiting instruction is deferential (citing United States v. Bennett). The opinion notes that a bench instruction at the close of trial is “customary and sufficient.” (Opinion II.B.)

- Authentication requirement for extrinsic documents/statements: the court sustained objections where defense counsel sought to read parts of a police report not authored by the witness without proper foundation, citing the requirement that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be authenticated (citing Cumbie). That prevents introduction of unsworn written summaries of a declarant’s statements as a way around hearsay rules. (Opinion II.E.)

- Remedy/harm analysis: even if an evidentiary ruling is erroneous, reversal requires showing substantial prejudice — the harmless‑error standard. The opinion applies that standard to all claimed evidentiary errors. (Opinion II, last paragraph.)

Practical effect in this case
- Officers could testify about what D.F. told them insofar as that testimony explained their subsequent conduct (e.g., why they searched a route, why they detained someone), because offered for context not for the truth of the accusatory content.  
- Because D.F. eventually testified, the Confrontation Clause was not violated — she appeared and was cross‑examined, curing any Clause concern. (Opinion II.B., citing Charboneau.)  
- The court also prevented backdoor admission of unsworn police‑report material without authentication and retained the ability to instruct the jury about the limited purpose of such testimony; and any remaining error would be subject to harmless‑error review.

Bottom line
The opinion follows well‑established rules: out‑of‑court statements that are offered to explain an officer’s state of mind or actions are not hearsay; testimonial concerns under the Confrontation Clause are resolved when the declarant testifies; and Eighth Circuit practice imposes sensible safeguards (purpose limitation, authentication for documents, limiting instructions, and harmless‑error review) to prevent the “backdoor” admission of hearsay.

### When must a district court give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, and why wasn’t one required here?
Question:
The panel cited Bennett for the proposition that the court need not instruct the jury about each item of evidence at the time it’s admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial. What are the circumstances in which failure to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction is an abuse of discretion? Here, why did the court find no error in deferring or omitting an immediate instruction regarding D.F.’s statements, and what record factors typically drive that determination?
Answer:
Short answer
A district court must give a contemporaneous limiting instruction only when admission of out‑of‑court statements (or other potentially prejudicial evidence) at that moment is likely to mislead the jury into using the material for an improper substantive purpose and that risk cannot be cured later. Failure to give an immediate limiting instruction is an abuse of discretion where the record shows actual prejudicial admission (or a serious risk of prejudice) that a later instruction would not cure. In Cutbank the court did not err because (1) no hearsay was admitted at the time, (2) D.F. later testified in person (so Confrontation concerns were cured), and (3) the challenged statements were used legitimately to explain police officers’ actions — so any contemporaneous instruction was unnecessary.

Legal standard and when contemporaneous instructions are required
- Contemporaneous limiting instructions are prophylactic: they prevent jurors from treating testimony or documents as substantive proof when the law allows that material only for a limited purpose (for example, impeachment, state of mind, or non‑hearsay context).
- The appellate standard is abuse of discretion. See United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014): “The court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” The converse follows: an abuse occurs when evidence is admitted in a way that poses a real risk of impermissible use and that risk is not cured by later instructions or by the record (e.g., the declarant never testifies).
- Record factors that typically indicate an abuse of discretion:
  - Whether the challenged material was actually admitted as substantive evidence (vs. admitted for a non‑hearsay purpose).
  - Whether the declarant later testifies or is otherwise available (Confrontation Clause issues are cured if the declarant testifies: United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)).
  - The timing and context of the admission (did the jury hear the material long before any curative instruction?).
  - The centrality and prejudicial nature of the material — whether the improperly admitted matter was likely to be decisive or highly inflammatory.
  - Whether the court gave a limiting instruction later (at close of trial) and whether counsel requested an immediate instruction.
  - Whether any subsequent testimony or evidence rendered the earlier matter cumulative or harmless.

Why the court found no error here
- The panel applied Bennett and found no abuse of discretion. Two controlling points from the opinion:
  1. “Here, the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial.” The court concluded the statements the defense feared were not admitted as substantive hearsay at the time they were elicited (Opinion § II.B). If no hearsay was admitted, there was no need for a contemporaneous limiting instruction.
  2. “In addition, D.F. testified. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied ‘when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.’” (quoting Charboneau). Because D.F. later testified in person, any Confrontation or hearsay concern was plainly cured by her in‑court testimony.
- The government’s proffered purpose for the statements — to show the context for police officers’ actions and their knowledge at the time — is a recognized non‑hearsay or limiting purpose. The panel accepted that explanation and the district court’s exercise of discretion in declining a contemporaneous instruction.
- Finally, the court emphasized harmlessness and the overall strength of the government’s case: even if there had been an error, the conviction would not be reversed absent substantial prejudice. The record contained strong testimonial and physical evidence (DNA on the gun, witnesses identifying prior possession, items found along the likely path), so any failure to instruct contemporaneously did not substantially sway the verdict.

Takeaway for practice
- If a party fears that a statement will be used substantively before the declarant testifies, it should (a) object at the time and (b) ask for a contemporaneous limiting instruction on the record. That preserves the issue.
- But courts are entitled to manage trial flow; they need not instruct at every evidentiary interjection if the statement is not admitted as hearsay, the declarant will testify, a later limiting instruction is available, or the statement is used for a proper contextual purpose. The combination of those record facts is exactly what the panel relied on to affirm here.

### Was the harmless-error framework correctly applied given the potential constitutional nature of some claims?
Question:
The court said it would reverse only if the jury was 'substantially swayed' by improperly admitted evidence (DeMarce), then concluded any errors were harmless. If the alleged error implicated the Confrontation Clause (a constitutional error), shouldn’t the standard be 'harmless beyond a reasonable doubt' under Chapman? How does the Eighth Circuit decide which harmless-error standard applies when the court first declares 'no hearsay was admitted' but addresses harmlessness in the alternative?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the Eighth Circuit applied the harmless‑error framework consistent with how it resolved the claim. The panel first concluded there was no Confrontation/hearsay problem because the declarant testified, and then (in the alternative) applied the Circuit’s nonconstitutional harmlessness formulation from DeMarce. That sequence is what explains the “substantially swayed” phrasing in the opinion.

Why: the controlling legal point in the opinion is twofold.

- The court found no hearsay admission and no Confrontation Clause violation: “the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial. In addition, D.F. testified. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied ‘when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.’ ” (Op. § II.B, citing Charboneau). Because the declarant appeared and testified, the court treated the Confrontation issue as resolved against the defendant.

- For the alternative harmlessness analysis the court relied on its ordinary evidentiary‑error standard: “we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” (Op. § II, quoting DeMarce). The opinion then explains why the evidence was overwhelming and any error did not influence the verdict.

Putting those pieces together: the panel did not accept that a constitutional Confrontation Clause error occurred; having disposed of that threshold issue, it properly applied the Circuit’s nonconstitutional harmlessness test in assessing any residual evidentiary rulings. If the court had instead concluded there was a Confrontation Clause violation, it would have had to apply the more exacting constitutional harmlessness review — but the opinion’s explicit factual and legal finding (declarant testified, Charboneau) is what controls the choice of standard here.

Citations from the opinion supporting this reading:
- “the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial. In addition, D.F. testified. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied ‘when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.’ ” (Op. § II.B).
- “we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” (Op. § II, quoting DeMarce), followed by the court’s conclusion that the evidence was substantial and any error had only a slight influence.

So the Eighth Circuit’s application was consistent with its own reasoning: because it concluded there was no Confrontation/hearsay violation, it used its standard nonconstitutional harmless‑error test (DeMarce) in the alternative.

### Why couldn’t defense counsel read from the police report to impeach C.F., and what authentication was missing?
Question:
The trial court barred defense counsel from reading portions of a police report during cross of C.F., reasoning there was no proper foundation because C.F. didn’t author the report. The panel cited Cumbie about authenticating extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statements. Can someone explain precisely what foundation/authentication was lacking? Could counsel have used Rule 613(b) (extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statements), Rule 803(6) (business records), or called the officer who drafted the report? What’s the right way to get that impeachment before the jury in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer
The court excluded defense counsel’s “reading” of the police report because the report was not properly authenticated as the witness’s own prior statement (and so could not be admitted as extrinsic impeachment). Cumbie (which the panel cited) makes clear that extrinsic evidence used to impeach must be authenticated — the government and the district court were entitled to a foundation showing the document actually records what C.F. said. Absent that foundation, the document is hearsay and cannot simply be read into the record for impeachment.

Why authentication/foundation was missing (precisely)
- The panel relied on United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022): “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court sustained objections because the police report was not authored by C.F.; there was no foundation tying the written report to C.F.’s own statements.
- Authentication requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims (Fed. R. Evid. 901(a)). Here the proponent had to show the portions of the report accurately recorded statements made by C.F. — e.g., that C.F. told the officer X — or otherwise show that C.F. adopted or acknowledged the contents.
- Without testimony from C.F. adopting the report or other testimony connecting the words in the report to C.F.’s mouth, the report is merely a third-party document recounting what C.F. allegedly said. Reading that document into the record would introduce hearsay (a document offered for the truth of its contents) without the requisite foundation.

Could Rule 613(b) alone have done it?
- Rule 613(b) governs impeachment by prior inconsistent statement. It allows extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement only if the witness is afforded an opportunity to explain/deny or the subject is interrogated about it on cross (and the opponent has an opportunity to examine). It does not dispense with authentication requirements.
- Practically: defense can ask C.F. on cross, “Did you tell Officer Jones X?” If C.F. denies or equivocates, Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic proof — but that extrinsic proof must still be authenticated (Rule 901) before the jury may see/read a document that purports to memorialize the statement.

Could Rule 803(6) (business records) rescue the report?
- Rule 803(6) allows admission of regularly kept records if the record was made in the course of a regularly conducted activity and is trustworthy. But police reports present two problems:
  1) If the proponent seeks the report to prove the truth of a declarant-witness’s statements recorded in the report, many courts treat those portions as not falling squarely within 803(6) because the reporting officer was not the declarant of the substantive facts and the trustworthiness requirement is often questionable in criminal trials.
  2) Even if the report itself is a business record, the portions recording a witness’s out-of-court narrative may still be hearsay within hearsay; each layer needs a proper exception.
- The opinion does not admit the report under 803(6); instead it required authentication under Cumbie. So relying on 803(6) would have required additional foundation about how and when the report was prepared and why it is trustworthy — and would still likely face Confrontation/hearsay concerns if offered for the truth of what C.F. said.

Could defense have called the officer who drafted the report?
- Yes. Calling the officer who prepared the report is the straightforward route:
  - The officer can testify that C.F. told him X (officer testifies to the witness’s prior statement). That provides direct extrinsic impeachment and proper foundation.
  - The officer can authenticate the report as his or her own document if needed (Rule 901(b)(1): testimony of a witness with knowledge).
  - Using the officer avoids the layering/hearsay/authentication problems of reading the document into the record without foundation.

Practical, Eighth‑Circuit‑safe ways to get the impeachment before the jury
1. On cross, ask C.F. directly whether she told the officer the particular statement. If she denies, you may then offer extrinsic proof — but you must authenticate the source of that extrinsic proof.
2. Call the officer who took the statement. Have the officer testify that C.F. made the inconsistent statement and identify the report if necessary. That satisfies authentication and confrontation concerns.
3. If you want to use the written report itself, lay foundation: get C.F. to acknowledge the report or concede she made the statement contained in it; or have the officer authenticate the report as his/her record (and be prepared to argue the business-records exception for admissibility of the officer’s report for non-hearsay purposes). But do not rely solely on Rule 613(b) to bypass Rule 901 authentication.
4. If time permits, identify and proffer the exact foundation to the court before attempting to read the report — show who made it, when, by what procedure, and how it records C.F.’s statements.

Bottom line
The district court blocked the reading of the police report because defense counsel failed to lay the necessary authentication/foundation showing the report actually recorded C.F.’s statements. Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic impeachment but does not eliminate the need to authenticate a document; Rule 803(6) might help for parts of a police report but has its own limits and trustworthiness requirements; and the safest route in the Eighth Circuit is to obtain the impeaching prior statement through testimony (call the officer) or to get the witness to adopt/acknowledge the report on the record so the document is properly authenticated. The panel’s citation to Cumbie embodies that rule: extrinsic impeachment must be authenticated before it may be used.

### How does the court reconcile 'continuous possession' probative value with Rule 403 unfair prejudice concerns?
Question:
The opinion accepts testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen the rifle multiple times before as intrinsic and relevant to possession. But repeated mentions risk propensity inferences ('once a gun possessor, always a gun possessor'). How does the Eighth Circuit balance the probative value of showing identity/ownership of the same gun against the danger of unfair prejudice or confusion under Rule 403? Did the district court need to give a limiting instruction tailored to this risk?
Answer:
Short answer: The Eighth Circuit treats testimony that witnesses had seen the same rifle before as intrinsic, highly probative evidence of ownership/control (i.e., continuous possession), and it upheld the district court’s decision to admit that testimony without giving a specially tailored limiting instruction because (1) the testimony was admissible as intrinsic evidence (Battle), (2) the district court has broad discretion under Rules 401–403 to admit probative evidence unless unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value, and (3) the court did not abuse its discretion in declining a contemporaneous limiting instruction or any special jury instruction here (Bennett and related harmless-error principles).

How the court reasons (by the opinion’s text)
- Probative value as intrinsic evidence: The panel relied on the principle that “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon,” citing United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014). The opinion repeats that D.F.’s testimony that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a “handful of times” was intrinsic and relevant to proving continuous possession (Opinion § II.A–C).
- Rule 403/unfair prejudice: The court evaluated the Rule 403 concern under the usual abuse-of-discretion standard and found no showing that unfair prejudice, confusion, or propensity inference “substantially” outweighed the probative value. The opinion notes Cutbank “points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from” related testimony and stresses the court’s broad evidentiary discretion (Opinion § II.D).
- Limiting instruction: The court applied the standard from United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014): a district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” The panel found no abuse of discretion because (a) the testimony was admissible (not hearsay here), (b) the declarant (D.F.) testified so Confrontation concerns were satisfied, and (c) the record did not show a need for a specially tailored limiting instruction (Opinion § II.B).
- Harmless-error/backstop: Even if any marginal error occurred, the court stressed the overwhelming strength of the government’s physical and testimonial evidence and applied the familiar harmless-error approach—reversal requires that the jury may have been “substantially swayed” by the challenged evidence. The court concluded Cutbank could not meet that burden (Opinion § II, final paragraphs).

What this means for the Rule 403/propensity concern
- The panel treats repeated prior sightings of the same gun not as propensity evidence (Rule 404(b)) but as intrinsic evidence tending to prove identity/ownership/control. That re-classification significantly reduces the Rule 404/propensity problem.
- Once the evidence is intrinsic and highly probative on an element (possession/ownership), the district court reasonably may admit it unless there is a clear showing that unfair prejudice substantially outweighs the probative value. The opinion finds no such showing here.
- A limiting instruction is discretionary; the district court’s failure to give a special contemporaneous instruction was not reversible error under the court’s precedents (Bennett) given the admissibility of the testimony and the overall record.

Bottom line answer to your two questions
- How does the Eighth Circuit balance probative value against unfair prejudice? By treating prior sightings as intrinsic evidence of possession (citing Battle), applying the ordinary Rule 403 balancing under an abuse-of-discretion standard, and refusing to exclude or treat the testimony as improper propensity evidence absent a showing that its prejudicial effect substantially outweighs its probative value.
- Did the district court need to give a tailored limiting instruction? No. Under Bennett and the opinion’s analysis, the district court did not abuse its discretion by not giving a special limiting instruction here: the testimony was admissible, the declarant testified (so Confrontation concerns were not implicated), and the record contained no showing that the jury was unfairly swayed such that reversal would be required.

### What exactly did the jury have to find about prior convictions to trigger ACCA after recent Supreme Court cases?
Question:
The panel states the 'jury convicted ... of being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies,' triggering § 924(e). Post-Wooden and Erlinger, some facts (like whether offenses occurred on different 'occasions') require jury findings beyond a reasonable doubt, but Almendarez-Torres still allows judicial fact-finding on the fact of a prior conviction. What ACCA-related facts did the jury actually have to find here (violent-felony status? occasions?), and which remain legal determinations for the judge? Why frame it as the jury convicting on 'being previously convicted' rather than a judicial finding?
Answer:
Short answer
- The only fact the Sixth Amendment compelled the jury to find in this case was the existence of the prior convictions in the sense the jury was asked and did convict Cutbank of “being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies.” The panel therefore treated the existence of three qualifying prior convictions as a jury-found fact, which is the dispositive predicate for § 924(e).
- What remains for the judge (when the court chooses to decide it) are legal characterizations of those prior convictions — e.g., whether a particular prior conviction meets the statute’s definition of a “violent felony” under the relevant categorical/modifiedcategorical analysis, or other legal issues about how a prior record counts (record authentication, merger, vacatur, etc.).
- The court’s language that “the jury convicted … of being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies” reflects either (a) the safe route of submitting the predicate-count to the jury (thus avoiding Almendarez‑Torres challenges), or (b) a plain statement of what the jury’s verdict accomplished: the jury found the threshold prior‑conviction fact that triggers ACCA. The opinion shows no need for the judge to make additional jury-level findings (such as whether offenses occurred on different “occasions”).

Longer explanation, tied to the opinion and relevant doctrine

1) Governing doctrinal points (as reflected in the opinion)
- Almendarez‑Torres v. United States remains good law to the extent it treats the fact of a prior conviction as an exception to the rule that any fact that increases the statutory penalty must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The opinion does not repudiate that principle; instead, the panel records that the jury “found … he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering the Armed Career Criminal Act’s enhanced penalties.” (Opinion, III, first para.)
- The panel treats the jury’s finding of the prior‑conviction predicate as the operative event that “trigger[s]” § 924(e). (Id.) That is consistent with Almendarez‑Torres’ core holding that the existence of prior convictions is a distinct inquiry and may be treated differently from other sentence‑augmenting facts.

2) What the jury actually had to find in this case (and did)
- The jury was asked and convicted Cutbank of “being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies,” and the panel repeats that as the basis for the ACCA enhancement. (Opinion, I; III.) So the constitutionally significant fact, as submitted to and found by the jury, was that Cutbank had at least three prior convictions that qualified as violent felonies.
- Because the verdict form (and the reported result) explicitly tied the jury to the predicate (three or more qualifying prior convictions), the judgment does not rest on a judge’s solitary finding that the defendant “had prior convictions.” The opinion therefore treats the prior‑conviction predicate as a jury-found fact.

3) What remains a judicial/legal determination
- Whether a given prior conviction “counts” as a violent felony typically requires a legal comparison between the statutory elements (or the record of conviction) and ACCA’s definition (the categorical or modified‑categorical approach). That sort of legal characterization — deciding whether the record of a prior conviction meets the statutory definition of a “violent felony” — is a legal determination courts commonly make at sentencing and is reflected in the opinion’s treatment of the PSR and the district court’s adoption of PSR facts. (See III, describing adoption of PSR factual statements without objection.)
- Other legal issues about priors (e.g., whether a conviction was vacated, whether multiple convictions merge for ACCA purposes, whether an out‑of‑state record is sufficiently authenticated) are also matters for court resolution using the record of conviction and related materials. The opinion’s discussion of the PSR and adoption of its facts (III) shows the court relied on those sentencing‐record mechanisms.

4) What about “occasions” or other subsidiary facts that some recent cases have required a jury to find?
- The opinion does not identify separate “occasion” findings as necessary here. The panel simply reports the jury’s finding of three or more prior violent felony convictions and treats that as triggering § 924(e). (III.)

### Why is a within-Guidelines sentence presumed reasonable on appeal despite § 3553(a)’s parsimony clause?
Question:
The court applied a presumption of reasonableness to a within-Guidelines sentence (Goodhouse), even though the defendant argued the enhancement conflicted with § 3553(a)’s 'sufficient but not greater than necessary' principle. How does this appellate presumption square with the parsimony clause? What types of arguments or record showings typically overcome the presumption in the Eighth Circuit, especially where the Guidelines are driven by enhancements like § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit treats a within-Guidelines sentence as presumptively reasonable on appeal, but that presumption is not absolute. It operates only after the court confirms the Guidelines were correctly calculated and the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors. To overcome the presumption a defendant must show (1) a significant procedural error (e.g., an incorrect Guidelines calculation or failure to consider § 3553(a)), or (2) record evidence that the within‑range sentence is substantively unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors or the particular enhancement at issue. The Cutbank opinion applies each of those principles: it (a) accepts the correct application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) based on the PSR and controlling precedent, (b) cites Goodhouse for the presumption, and (c) relies on Masood/Maluoth to say the district court needn’t give a rote checklist so long as the record shows it considered § 3553(a).

Relevant legal doctrine and how the opinion applies it
- Presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences. The court in Cutbank repeats the Eighth Circuit rule that a within-range sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal (citing United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)). That presumption is an appellate, not a substantive, rule: it governs the appellate review posture after the Guideline range is established.

- Two preconditions before appellate review of substantive reasonableness. The opinion follows Gall and Rosales‑Mireles: a sentencing court must first correctly calculate the Guidelines range and the court of appeals must ensure no significant procedural error before it addresses substantive reasonableness. Cutbank cites Gall, 552 U.S. at 49, and Rosales‑Mireles, 585 U.S. at 143–44, to make that point.

- What the district court must do on the record. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank reiterates that the district court need not mechanically recite each § 3553(a) factor; it is enough that the record makes clear the court considered the factors (citing United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024)). The court also relies on Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025), for the proposition that when the record shows the court was aware of a defendant’s arguments, the appellate court will presume the district court considered and rejected them.

How that presumption squares with § 3553(a)’s parsimony clause
- The presumption does not eliminate § 3553(a). Cutbank shows the circuit’s framework: (1) the appellate presumption applies only after the court confirms a correct Guidelines calculation and that the sentencing judge considered § 3553(a); (2) § 3553(a)’s “sufficient but not greater than necessary” principle remains available as a basis for a variance if the defendant can demonstrate why the Guidelines produce an unjust or excessive result in the particular case. In practice the appellate presumption simply shifts the burden on appeal: absent procedural error or a strong showing that the sentence is substantively unreasonable under § 3553(a), a within-Guidelines sentence stands.

What typically overcomes the presumption in the Eighth Circuit (and what the Cutbank record lacked)
Based on Cutbank and the Eighth Circuit authorities it cites, defendants commonly overcome the presumption by showing one (or both) of these paths:

1) Procedural error or incorrect Guidelines calculation
- A clear error in the Guidelines computation (Rosales‑Mireles standard) — e.g., mathematical error, misapplication of a particular enhancement, or reliance on unsupported factual findings. In Cutbank the court rejected that route because the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings without objection and the enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was supported by the PSR and consistent with Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929–30 (8th Cir. 2013).

2) A convincing substantive showing that the Guidelines result conflicts with § 3553(a)
- Showing the sentencing factors (nature of the offense, history and characteristics, need for the sentence) weigh heavily against the Guidelines range so that a within-range sentence is greater than necessary. Such showings often require concrete record evidence: unusually minor role in the offense, mitigating personal circumstances, risks of unwarranted disparity, or empirical mismatch between the Guidelines and the statutory purposes. Cutbank failed here because the record showed multiple violent priors, a violent home offense, injury to an officer, and the district court expressly reviewed the PSR and § 3553(a) arguments — the exact facts the court relied on to affirm reasonableness.

Special note about enhancements driven by § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)
- In Cutbank the court accepted the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement because the PSR supported the factual predicate and the district court adopted those facts without objection. The opinion emphasizes the standard of review (no clear error when the PSR is adopted) and cites Dunn to support that approach. That illustrates two practical points for challenging such an enhancement on appeal:
  - Challenge the factual foundation at sentencing (so the PSR cannot be adopted unchallenged), or preserve objections to the PSR’s relevant facts;
  - If preserved, focus on showing the enhancement’s factual predicate is unsupported or clearly erroneous (document gaps, contradicted testimony, or lack of nexus between firearm and other felony).

Practical takeaways for an appellant under Eighth Circuit law
- Preserve objections at sentencing to both Guidelines facts in the PSR and any specific enhancements (e.g., § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)). If the district court adopts the PSR without objection, Cutbank shows an uphill climb.
- Make a clear record that the district court failed to consider or misapplied § 3553(a) if that is the claim; merely asserting a conflict with the parsimony clause without pointing to a procedural flaw or exceptional mitigating circumstances is unlikely to overcome the Goodhouse presumption.
- When attacking an enhancement, develop record evidence (contradictory witness statements, lack of nexus, insufficiency of the underlying-felony predicate) so the appellate court can find clear error in the facts supporting the enhancement.

Conclusion
Cutbank applies the settled Eighth Circuit framework: a within-Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable (Goodhouse), but only after the court confirms correct Guidelines calculations and that the district court considered § 3553(a) (Gall, Rosales‑Mireles). The presumption can be rebutted by showing procedural error or a compelling substantive § 3553(a) case — for example, by showing the enhancement was unsupported in the record or that the Guidelines result is disproportionate in the specific circumstances. Cutbank failed on both fronts: the PSR supported § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the district court adopted the PSR, and the record shows the court considered § 3553(a), so the within‑range sentence was affirmed.

(Opinion § III, with citations to Gall, Rosales‑Mireles, Masood, and Maluoth.)

### How does cumulative-error review operate when individual evidentiary rulings are harmless or non-errors?
Question:
The court cited Keys to say it won’t overturn a conviction based on cumulative effect absent substantial prejudice. If each ruling is either not error or harmless, how can the cumulative-error doctrine ever provide relief? What threshold showing of combined prejudice is required in the Eighth Circuit, and how is it assessed when many rulings involve similar themes (e.g., prior sightings of the gun)?
Answer:
Short answer
In the Eighth Circuit cumulative-error review only helps a defendant who can show that the combined effect of trial errors produced “substantial prejudice” — i.e., the errors, taken together, “may have been substantially swayed” the jury. If each ruling is either correct or harmless (meaning it did not influence the verdict or had only a slight effect), the cumulative‑error doctrine ordinarily does not provide relief unless the aggregate of those rulings meaningfully changed the trial’s outcome. The Cutbank opinion applies this exact standard: because the government’s testimonial and physical evidence was substantial, any individual evidentiary rulings were harmless or non‑prejudicial, and the court concluded the cumulative effect did not produce the requisite substantial prejudice. See DeMarce and Keys citations in the opinion.

What the opinion says (and the governing standard)
- The panel reiterates the harmless‑error framing: the court “will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quoted in the opinion). The opinion repeats that an error is harmless if it “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998 (quoted).
- For cumulative error the opinion cites Keys: “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quoted in the opinion).

How that operates in practice (as shown here)
- Burden and showing: the defendant must do more than point to several rulings that were close calls. He must show the cumulative impact plausibly changed the verdict — not merely that there were multiple, technically imperfect rulings. Cutbank could not “clear this hurdle” because the record showed substantial government proof (witness identifications, DNA on the gun, physical items found along the route), so any individual evidentiary rulings “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” (Opinion, Section II, last two paragraphs.)
- When each error is harmless: if the court finds each challenged ruling was either correct or harmless, cumulative‑error review typically adds nothing. The opinion reflects that approach: it treats the individual rulings under ordinary abuse‑of‑discretion/harmless‑error review first, and then rejects cumulative prejudice because the overall record was strong.
- Repetitive/similar rulings (e.g., multiple references to prior sightings of the gun): repetition can matter only if the repetitions, taken together, materially increased the risk of an unfair trial. But in Cutbank the court accepted that prior sightings were intrinsic and admissible (Battle, quoted) and, even if there had been borderline errors, the physical evidence and DNA made any cumulative effect immaterial. Thus repetitive testimony that merely reinforced a strong, independently supported theory of guilt did not meet the “substantial prejudice” threshold.

Bottom line for litigation strategy
- To prevail on cumulative‑error review in the Eighth Circuit you should (1) preserve and identify the individual errors; (2) show that each error had at least some prejudicial tendency; and (3) explain how those tendencies combined to produce a realistic probability the jury’s verdict was affected. If the government’s proof is strong and the errors were minor or correctable, the court will conclude the cumulative effect was insufficient, as the Cutbank court did.

### What appellate jurisdiction and briefing rules explain the court’s admonition that the government 'expanded the issues'?
Question:
The opinion notes the government framed an issue on appeal that the defendant hadn’t raised, and the court 'did not appreciate' that. Short answer: The court’s rebuke is about the limits on an appellee’s briefing — a prudential/briefing rule that an appellee should not broaden the scope of the appeal by introducing new issues the appellant did not press — not the narrow “cross‑appeal” rule that formally limits a party from seeking to enlarge the judgment without filing a cross‑appeal. Why that follows from appellate practice and the opinion

- The cross‑appeal rule (the principle that a party must cross‑appeal to obtain greater relief or an enlargement of the judgment) is a distinct doctrine that prevents an appellee from obtaining more favorable relief than the district court gave unless the appellee files a cross‑appeal. The opinion does not invoke that doctrine here — the government was not seeking to enlarge the judgment or obtain a different sentence; it was presenting a different phrasing/claim about a trial ruling the defendant did not itself press on appeal.

If the appellee’s argument is merely a different theory in support of affirmance (not an attempt to obtain greater relief), courts have discretion to consider it, but may decline if it unfairly broadens the appellate dispute. The Cutbank opinion illustrates the court exercising that discretion and policing briefs that broaden the appeal beyond what the defendant raised (II.C).

References in the opinion supporting this explanation

- “the government’s brief argued that Cutback’s ‘next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross‑examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.’ We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” (Opinion § II.C.)

- The court then resolves the point on authentication and harmless‑error principles (citing United States v. Cumbie and United States v. DeMarce), showing it treated the government’s extra argument as unnecessary expansion rather than a jurisdictional cross‑appeal problem.

In short: the admonition stems from appellate briefing norms and the court’s discretion to refuse to entertain issues the appellee injects that the appellant did not press, not from the separate cross‑appeal/enlargement‑of‑judgment rule.

- The NFA definition (26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)) will produce the higher base offense level in USSG § 2K2.1 if the sentencing court finds the defendant’s firearm qualifies as an NFA “sawed‑off” weapon (e.g., a short‑barreled rifle). The Cutbank opinion illustrates that the district court may rely on the PSR’s factual description (and other trial/physical evidence) to make that finding, and the adoption of unobjected‑to PSR facts is dispositive on appeal.
- The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank does not articulate a special evidentiary formula (measurements, photos, or expert testimony) for establishing NFA status; instead it treats the issue as a factual determination the district court may resolve at sentencing (here by adopting the PSR), with appellate review limited by the usual standards (see below).

What the opinion actually says (and why it matters)
- The PSR recommended a base offense level of 26 because “the offense involved a sawed‑off firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a).” The district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements “without objection,” and the Eighth Circuit held that the court therefore did not err in its findings. (Opinion, Background and III. The Sentencing Issue.)
- The opinion relies on the settled principle that a district court may adopt the PSR’s factual statements when calculating the Guidelines, and that such adopted facts are controlling where there was no objection. The court cites United States v. Dunn for the applicable standard of review for sentencing fact findings. (Opinion, III; citing Dunn, 723 F.3d at 929–30.)

What this means for proving NFA (practical points for sentencing)
- The sentencing court needs a factual finding that the firearm meets the statutory definition in § 5845(a) to apply the § 2K2.1 higher base level. How the court reaches that finding can vary: it may rely on trial testimony, the physical evidence, photos, measurements, expert testimony, or the PSR’s recitation of such facts. Cutbank shows the court may accept the PSR description if the defendant does not object.
- If the defendant does object to the PSR’s description (or otherwise contests NFA status), the district court must resolve the factual dispute at sentencing. Cutbank does not spell out the quantum of proof required, but under the governing practice reflected in the opinion: (1) the district court makes the factual finding; and (2) on appeal the finding is reviewed under the usual sentencing‑fact standard (the court cites Dunn on the standard of review). In Cutbank, because the PSR facts were adopted and not challenged, the court needed no further evidentiary showings and the appellate court found no error.

Eighth Circuit authorities the opinion uses or references
- Cutbank: adoption of PSR facts without objection = no error in sentencing findings (Opinion, III).
- Dunn: cited for the standard of review of sentencing fact findings (Opinion, III).
- Battle and other cited cases address evidentiary issues at trial (e.g., prior possession as intrinsic evidence), supporting the government’s use of witness testimony about the sawed‑off rifle at trial. (Opinion, II.A & II.C.)

Bottom line for practice
- If you want the § 2K2.1 higher base level to be disputed at sentencing, you should (a) obtain precise, authenticated evidence (measurements, photos, expert opinion, or stipulation) supporting or refuting the firearm’s dimensions/configuration, and (b) object to any PSR statement asserting NFA status so the district court must make an explicit finding. If you do not object, Cutbank shows the district court may simply adopt the PSR and the appellate court will not disturb that finding.
