Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

Chapter 14: # United States v. Cutbank as a Teaching Vehicle

This chapter uses United States v. Cutbank to illuminate several recurring evidentiary and sentencing doctrines that arise in federal criminal trials. Cutbank is a compact example: the case presents disputes about the admissibility of prior-possession evidence, the proper treatment of witness statements made to police, the bounds of cross-examination and impeachment with documentary police reports, and the application of a §2K2.1 enhancement at sentencing — all decided against the defendant under the standards that govern district courts and courts of appeals. Each section below explains the governing legal principles and then shows how the Eighth Circuit applied them in Cutbank.

## Intrinsic evidence of prior possession of the same sawed-off rifle: reliance on Battle; continuous possession rationale

A common prosecution theory in a felon-in-possession case is to prove ownership or control of the weapon by showing the defendant’s prior possession of that same firearm. Cutbank challenged testimony by two witnesses that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 on prior occasions, arguing that such evidence was improper propensity proof under Rule 404(b). The district court — and the Eighth Circuit on appeal — rejected that argument, treating the testimony as intrinsic evidence of possession rather than as extrinsic “other acts” evidence.

The doctrinal distinction matters. Rule 404(b) governs “other crimes, wrongs, or acts” offered for a purpose such as intent or absence of mistake; such evidence is admissible only if it meets the Rule 404(b) framework (notice, probative purpose other than propensity, and Rule 403 balancing, where required). But where the evidence is intrinsic — that is, it arises out of the same transaction or is necessary to complete the story of the offense, or where it directly proves an element such as possession or knowledge — it is not “other acts” evidence and need not be submitted under Rule 404(b). The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Battle illustrates the point: prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to establishing later possession because it helps prove control, access, or ownership. The court in Cutbank cited Battle and applied that logic: testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the night in question was directly probative of his possession of that weapon during the charged offense.

Students should be able to apply this distinction. Ask whether the prior-act evidence serves to explain the charged conduct or is being offered simply to show a defendant’s bad character or propensity. If the evidence makes it more likely that the defendant possessed the particular weapon at the relevant time (for example, because the weapon is distinctive and the witnesses saw the defendant with it previously), it is often intrinsic and admissible. If, by contrast, the evidence is remote in time and offered simply to show a defendant’s violent tendencies, it is more likely to fall within Rule 404(b). In Cutbank, the sawed-off rifle was a distinctive, identifiable item, and testimony that the defendant had been seen with it before the incident bore directly on who controlled the gun that later was found and recovered with the defendant’s DNA on it.

## Police-response testimony: non-hearsay context and Confrontation satisfied because declarant testified

Cutbank also sought a jury instruction forbidding the jury from considering D.F.’s out-of-court statements to police “as substantive evidence” until she testified, raising hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns. The district court declined to give the preemptive limiting instruction, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that ruling.

Two doctrinal points intersect here. First, not every statement made to police is offered for the truth of the matter asserted; many such statements are offered for a non-hearsay purpose — for example, to explain why officers took certain actions, to show their state of mind or knowledge at the time, or to provide context for the investigation. Statements admitted for such non-hearsay purposes do not offend the hearsay rule. Second, the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause is implicated when a testimonial out-of-court statement is offered against the accused and the declarant does not testify; but when the declarant appears and testifies at trial, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because the defendant has the opportunity to cross-examine the witness about the earlier statements.

In Cutbank the court noted both points. The government proffered officers’ testimony about what they were told to explain the officers’ investigative and tactical responses; the district court did not admit impermissible hearsay and, importantly, the declarant (D.F.) later testified at trial. Citing Charboneau, the Eighth Circuit reiterated the settled rule that confrontation is satisfied when the out-of-court declarant testifies in person. Practically, a pretrial limiting instruction that attempts to segregate every potentially problematic statement is unnecessary if the court correctly admits evidence for non-hearsay purposes and if the declarant testifies at trial; a bedrock appellate principle is that limiting instructions need not accompany the admission of each item of evidence as it is introduced so long as an appropriate instruction is given at closing or the evidence is otherwise properly admitted.

Students learning this material should focus on the analytical sequence a trial judge applies: identify the purpose for which the statement is offered (hearsay-for-truth or non-hearsay); if offered for truth, determine whether any hearsay exception or Confrontation Clause requirement applies; if offered for a non-hearsay purpose, admit subject to relevance and possible Rule 403 balancing. Also remember that if the declarant later testifies, prior police statements become less problematic under the Confrontation Clause, although they can still raise hearsay questions if admitted for their truth and the declarant’s testimony does not squarely place them before the jury as prior inconsistent statements or adopt them.

## Rule 403 and opening the door: allowing rehabilitative context about victim’s aversion to guns

During cross-examination about prior inconsistent statements, D.F. volunteered that she was “not familiar with guns” and that she “did not like guns” because her sister had been killed by a gun. Cutbank moved to strike the reference to the sister’s death; the district court denied the motion, and the Eighth Circuit found no abuse of discretion.

Two principles are important here. First, Rule 403 gives the court broad discretion to exclude evidence when its probative value is substantially outweighed by risks such as unfair prejudice, confusion, or wasting time. Second, cross-examination that elicits inconsistency often “opens the door” to rehabilitative or explanatory testimony. When a witness is asked on cross about inconsistency, the witness may be permitted to explain the inconsistency on redirect, or even to do so on cross, as a responsive answer; courts generally will not order a sweeping erasure of responsive testimony that fairly rebuts an impeachment attempt.

In Cutbank, D.F.’s reference to her sister’s death was invited by cross-examination aimed at her inconsistent statements about seeing the gun. The court reasonably treated the reference as context that explained why D.F. might react to or describe guns a certain way. The defendant pointed to no persuasive demonstration that the mention of the sister’s death had substantial unfair prejudice that would warrant exclusion under Rule 403. The Eighth Circuit’s conclusion reflects the deferential standard appellate courts apply to district courts’ Rule 403 decisions: absent a clear abuse of discretion, admission of such responsive testimony stands.

A practical lesson for students: timing and context matter. A collateral reference to a traumatic event may be admissible if it is responsive to impeachment and helps explain a witness’s perceptions or credibility, but the trial court may still exclude it if its prejudicial effect substantially outweighs probative value. The trial judge’s balancing call receives substantial deference on appeal.

## Impeachment via police reports: authentication required for extrinsic proof; permissible scope of cross-examination

Cutbank’s counsel attempted to read portions of a police report into the record to impeach C.F.’s testimony about whether she had previously seen the gun. The district court sustained objections because the report was not authored by C.F. and the defense lacked foundation to admit the report as an extrinsic document. The Eighth Circuit approved that ruling and invoked the general rule that extrinsic evidence used to impeach a witness must be authenticated.

Understanding when a party may use extrinsic evidence to impeach is essential. Federal Rule of Evidence 613(b) permits extrinsic evidence of a witness’s prior inconsistent statement only if the witness is afforded the opportunity to explain or deny the statement, and the opposing party is given an opportunity to interrogate the witness about it, except where the extrinsic evidence is a writing adopted or approved by the witness or where the prior statement is one of identification. Separately, extrinsic documents must be authenticated under Rule 901 (or tend to fall within a hearsay exception). A police report prepared by an officer is, at least initially, hearsay if offered for the truth of the matter asserted and may require either a hearsay exception (e.g., recorded recollection under Rule 803(5)) or proper foundation by the preparer or another witness with knowledge to qualify for admission.

In Cutbank the court’s enforcement of authentication and foundational requirements was routine. Defense counsel could have pursued impeachment in other permissible ways: for example, by asking the witness whether she had made certain statements to a particular officer and then using the officer’s live testimony or properly authenticated portions of the report (with the preparer on the stand) to impeach, or by laying the foundation that would permit the police report to be admitted under an applicable hearsay exception. The district court was not required to allow the reading of unauthenticated portions of a report to the jury. This enforcement of foundational rules strikes a balance between permitting meaningful impeachment and protecting the tribunal from unreliable documentary evidence.

Students should be able to identify the pathways by which extrinsic documentary impeachment may be accomplished (e.g., use of the author as a witness, establishing recorded recollection, or using a prior inconsistent statement that meets Rule 801(d)(1)(A)’s nonhearsay criteria), and the limits on a cross-examiner’s ability to simply “read in” documents without foundation.

## Harmless and cumulative error: strong physical (DNA, recovery path) and testimonial evidence foreclosing prejudice

When a defendant challenges multiple evidentiary rulings on appeal, courts often consider both whether any individual ruling was erroneous and whether the cumulative effect of any errors deprived the defendant of a fair trial. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank applied the harmless-error doctrine and the cumulative-error principle to reject the defendant’s attack.

Harmless-error analysis asks whether the erroneously admitted (or excluded) evidence likely had a substantial influence on the jury’s verdict. If the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, reversal is not required. The cumulative-error doctrine recognizes that multiple errors, none individually reversible, might in combination have a prejudicial effect that warrants reversal. But the threshold is high: the cumulative errors must produce substantial prejudice to the defendant’s right to a fair trial.

Cutbank’s case illustrates why many evidentiary errors are held harmless when the government’s proof is otherwise strong. The record included DNA on the gun matching Cutbank, physical items (the victim’s cellphone, a butcher knife) found along the likely path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself, eyewitness identifications of the sawed-off rifle belonging to Cutbank, and testimony about Cutbank’s threatening conduct that evening. Given that body of corroborating physical and testimonial evidence, the court concluded that any erroneous evidentiary rulings could not have substantially swayed the jury. On appeal, the defendant therefore could not meet the burden to show prejudice from the alleged errors, and the conviction stood.

For students: when evaluating harmlessness, analyze the strength and coherence of the admitted evidence apart from the challenged items. Look for independent proof that ties the defendant to the crime (DNA, fingerprints, the recovery of uniquely identifiable property, eyewitness testimony directly linking defendant and object), and consider whether the challenged evidence was cumulative of other properly admitted proof. Cumulative-error review requires viewing the trial as a whole; multiple minor errors may remain harmless if they do not together undermine confidence in the verdict.

## §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement: use “in connection with” another felony established via undisputed PSR; standard of review

At sentencing, the central dispute concerned the four-level enhancement under USSG §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when the defendant “used or possessed any firearm . . . in connection with another felony offense.” The Guidelines commentary clarifies that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (comment n.14(C)). Cutbank argued that the enhancement was improper; the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

Two sentencing principles are important here. First, disputes about the facts underlying guideline enhancements are reviewed for clear error when the district court adopts a PSR that contains undisputed facts. If the defendant does not object at sentencing, the PSR’s factual statements may become binding and are reviewed for clear error on appeal. Second, to trigger §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) the government must show a nexus between the firearm and another felony — a connection more than mere temporal coincidence but less than proof that the firearm facilitated the felony beyond all doubt. The enhancement’s focus is on whether possession of the firearm facilitated or had the potential to facilitate the commission of another felony (for example, a burglary, robbery, kidnapping, or assault), not whether the other felony was separately charged or resulted in a conviction.

In Cutbank the PSR recited facts — many of which the district court adopted without objection — showing that the sawed-off rifle, a knife, and the victim’s phone were found along the path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank was later detained, and that Cutbank had threatened and assaulted the victim and resisted arrest, biting an officer and grabbing his gun. These facts supported the district court’s finding of a nexus between the firearm and other felonious conduct (domestic assault, kidnapping, injuring an officer), and the Eighth Circuit applied the clear-error standard to affirm. Students should note the practical consequences: failing to object to PSR factual statements forfeits a defendant’s opportunity to contest them later on appeal except for clear error, elevating the importance of timely and specific sentencing objections.

## Guidelines calculation vs. ACCA guideline: higher §2K2.1-driven level controls over §4B1.4 default

The interaction between the specific firearm guideline (§2K2.1) and the career-offender/Armed Career Criminal Guideline (§4B1.4 or the career offender guideline in other contexts) can be a point of confusion. Cutbank had been found by the jury to have three or more prior violent felonies, thereby triggering ACCA’s statutory penalties. The PSR also applied enhancements under §2K2.1 that produced a total offense level higher than the offense level that would have resulted solely from application of §4B1.4. The Guidelines themselves resolve such conflicts: when two different provisions yield different offense levels, the greater offense level applies.

The Eighth Circuit noted this point: the Guidelines direct the sentencing court to apply the higher offense level when the §2K2.1 enhancements produce a higher offense level than the ACCA-guideline calculation. Because the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings and the PSR’s §2K2.1-driven calculations produced the higher total offense level, the court properly used the higher level. This practical rule is straightforward: a defendant confronting overlapping guideline provisions should calculate all applicable offense levels and apply the one that yields the higher range, subject of course to any objection and cross-checking at sentencing.

Students should also be aware of statutory constraints. ACCA itself prescribes mandatory minimums and a statutory framework that may operate independently of the Guidelines. But the Guidelines’ internal mechanism for resolving competing offense-level calculations is a helpful rule-of-thumb in ordinary guideline sentencing where multiple provisions might apply; one must still be attentive to statutory minima and maxima that govern the overall sentencing authority.

## Within-Guidelines sentence affirmed: presumption of reasonableness; adequate §3553(a) consideration on the record

Cutbank received a within-guidelines sentence of 292 months, and he argued on appeal that applying the §2K2.1 enhancement produced a sentence inconsistent with §3553(a)’s “not greater than necessary” principle. The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument and affirmed the sentence.

Two analytical steps frame appellate review of sentences after Gall and Rosales-Mireles. First, the appellate court must ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error — in particular, that it correctly calculated the Guidelines range. This requires that the district court begin by calculating the Guidelines range correctly and resolving disputed guideline facts without procedural error. Second, having confirmed procedural regularity, the appellate court reviews the substantive reasonableness of the sentence, weighing whether the district court considered the §3553(a) factors and whether the ultimate sentence was reasonable in light of those factors. Appellate courts give substantial deference to a within-Guidelines sentence and “presume” such a sentence reasonable (subject to rebuttal with evidence of unreasonableness).

In Cutbank the district court adopted the PSR without objection, calculated the Guidelines range that resulted from the §2K2.1 enhancements, and expressly considered the §3553(a) factors and sentencing memoranda. The Eighth Circuit emphasized that a district court need not deliver a mechanical recitation of each §3553(a) factor; rather, the record must show that the court considered the arguments and factors. Given Cutbank’s extensive criminal history (many violent and firearms-related felonies), the violent facts of the offense (domestic assault, kidnapping of the victim in her home in the presence of children, and injuring an officer during the arrest), and the court’s stated consideration of §3553(a), the within-guidelines sentence was presumed reasonable and was affirmed.

From a teaching perspective, students should take away the following: timely and specific objections to PSR facts preserve challenges to guideline calculations; absent procedural errors, an appellate court will give meaningful deference to a within-guidelines sentence; and a sentencing court should ensure a clear record of consideration of §3553(a) factors, even if the court does not recite each factor in rote fashion.

Conclusion

United States v. Cutbank offers a compact demonstration of how courts apply well-established evidentiary and sentencing doctrines in the federal criminal context. The case reaffirms that (1) prior possession of the same, identifiable firearm is intrinsic evidence probative of later possession; (2) police-response testimony is often admissible for non-hearsay purposes and poses no Confrontation problem when the declarant testifies; (3) courts have broad Rule 403 discretion and will permit responsive or rehabilitative testimony that explains impeaching questions; (4) extrinsic documentary impeachment must be authenticated and properly founded; (5) harmless-error and cumulative-error doctrines protect convictions when substantial independent proof exists; and (6) sentencing enhancements under §2K2.1 are fact-intensive, reviewed for clear error when based on adopted PSR facts, and will control guideline calculations when they yield a higher offense level — with within-guidelines sentences presumptively reasonable when the district court adequately considers §3553(a). Taken together, these lessons show both the practical importance of early and specific objections at trial and sentencing and the deferential standards appellate courts apply to district-court calls in close evidentiary and sentencing decisions.
