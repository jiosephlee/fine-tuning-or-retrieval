Chapter 11: # The ‘In Connection With’ Enhancement: § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

The Sentencing Guidelines impose a four‑level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) when “a firearm or ammunition was possessed in connection with another felony offense.” The enhancement is one of the most frequently litigated sentencing issues in firearm cases because it turns on the presence of a factual nexus between the weapon and some separate criminal wrongdoing. This chapter explains the text and commentary of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the typical factual situations in which courts find (or refuse to find) the required nexus, the doctrinal tests courts apply to separate facilitation from mere presence, the standards that govern sentencing factfinding and appellate review, and practical proof and defense strategies. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank—both at trial and at sentencing—illustrates how these principles operate in practice.

## Text and commentary: facilitation, potential, and absence of a separate conviction requirement

The Guideline text provides the enhancement when the defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense.” The Commentary supplies essential interpretive guidance. It explains that “in connection with” requires some nexus between the firearm and the other felony—specifically, that the firearm “facilitated, or had the potential of facilitating, another felony offense.” The Commentary also makes clear that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” for that other felony. Thus, the enhancement does not depend on a separate indictment or conviction for the underlying felony; the sentencing court’s factual finding that the weapon facilitated, or could have facilitated, a felony is sufficient.

Two interpretive points follow directly from that text and commentary. First, the focus is functional, not formal: courts look to what the firearm did (or could do) in relation to the other wrongdoing. A weapon that actually enables an assault, intimidates victims, protects contraband, or facilitates flight plainly meets the facilitation test. Second, the Commentary’s “potential” language prevents prosecutors from having to show actual use in a particular transaction. If the weapon’s presence created a realistic capacity to assist in committing a felony, the enhancement can apply even if the defendant never fired a shot or used the gun to threaten someone on the spot.

The Cutbank case demonstrates both ideas. The PSR and trial record showed a domestic episode in which the defendant used a gun to threaten and control his girlfriend, led victims to identify prior possession of that same sawed‑off firearm, and later fled and resisted arrest during which he injured an officer. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements, and the court applied the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on those adopted facts. The Eighth Circuit affirmed: adoption of the PSR’s facts sufficed to establish the necessary connection, and the Commentary permits application even when no separate conviction was charged.

## Common contexts where courts find (or litigate) the enhancement

Certain fact patterns recur in successful § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) claims, and courts have developed practical descriptive labels for them. Understanding these contexts helps counsel anticipate how sentencing courts will view the nexus question.

- Drug offenses and the “fortress” or “stash” theory. Courts frequently apply the enhancement where a gun is found near narcotics, drug paraphernalia, or packaging materials. The rationale is that a firearm protects the drug operation or stash, deters theft or robbery, and therefore facilitates the drug felony. Physical proximity (weapons in the same container, room, or vehicle as drugs) is commonly persuasive to a sentencing judge.

- Assaults and domestic violence. When a gun is present during an assault or threat, or when a defendant brandishes a weapon to control or terrorize a victim, the facilitation nexus is straightforward. Domestic contexts are especially common: a weapon used or displayed during a home confrontation typically provides both demonstrable facilitation (immediate threat) and a realistic potential to enable further crime.

- Burglary, robbery, and other property crimes. A firearm found near burglary tools, in a burglar’s vehicle, or used to threaten victims during a robbery will ordinarily be treated as facilitating the property offense.

- Flight from police and resistance. Courts have sometimes applied the enhancement where the presence or use of a firearm materially increased the likelihood of successful flight or resistance—particularly where the defendant used the gun to force entry, to remove an officer’s access to a weapon, or otherwise to impede arrest. Cutbank’s facts—flight, violent resistance, and an officer bitten and grabbed while the defendant threatened to have a weapon—fit comfortably within this category.

These categories are not exhaustive. What matters is the functional relationship: did the weapon play a role in enabling or increasing the danger of the other felony? That determination turns on the totality of the circumstances.

## Causation and nexus: temporal and spatial proximity; facilitation versus mere presence

The critical inquiry under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is whether there is a sufficient nexus between the firearm and the other felony to show facilitation or potential facilitation. Courts do not apply a single formulaic test; rather they weigh several related factors, taken together:

- Temporal proximity. How close in time was the possession or display of the firearm to the other felony conduct? A gun found immediately before, during, or shortly after an assault or drug sale points toward facilitation. A long temporal gap undermines the inference that the weapon aided the offense.

- Spatial proximity. Where was the gun relative to the location of the other felony conduct? Co‑location in a vehicle, residence, or the immediate scene supports a nexus; possession in a distant or unrelated place weakens it.

- Functional relationship. Did the gun materially increase the likelihood of the felony’s success or the defendant’s ability to commit it? Examples include guns used to threaten or coerce victims, protect contraband, facilitate escape, or keep others from interfering. Courts ask whether the firearm had a role—actual or potential—in advancing the criminative purpose.

- Peculiarity of the firearm and circumstances. Saws‑off shotguns, illegal conversion or modification, or other indicia of readiness for violent misuse can support inference of facilitation. The presence of paraphernalia suggesting an illicit enterprise (packaging, large cash, distribution records) also aids the government’s case.

- Statements and conduct. Victim testimony, admissions by the defendant, witness observations that the gun was displayed to threaten or was habitually used in connection with criminal activity are powerful evidence of nexus.

Crucially, mere presence of a firearm is not enough. Sentencing courts and appellate courts distinguish between a weapon that lay in the vicinity with no reasonable functional relationship to the felony and a weapon that meaningfully supported or could have supported criminal conduct. The court in Cutbank viewed the totality of the evidence—victim testimony of prior possession, DNA on the gun, contemporaneous use to threaten, and flight/resistance—as sufficient to find the required nexus. The Eighth Circuit noted that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements establishing that connection and that the enhancement may be applied even when no separate charge was brought.

## Standards of proof and appellate review

Two distinct thresholds govern: (1) the evidentiary standard the district court applies in resolving disputed sentencing facts, and (2) the standard of appellate review of those district court findings.

- Burden of proof at sentencing. For disputed facts relevant to Guidelines calculations, the sentencing court generally resolves disputes by a preponderance of the evidence. That means the court must find it is more likely than not that the asserted fact is true. This preponderance standard governs typical guideline factfinding under advisory-booker jurisprudence.

- Appellate review of factual findings. Appellate courts review a district court’s factual determinations regarding sentencing enhancements for clear error. A finding is clearly erroneous only if, after reviewing the whole record, the appellate court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. The Cutbank opinion cites this standard when explaining that the district court did not clearly err in adopting the PSR facts to support the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.

- Appellate review of legal interpretation. Questions of law—how to interpret the Guidelines’ text or commentary—are reviewed de novo. If a defendant argues that the commentary’s “potential to facilitate” language has a certain legal meaning, the appellate court reviews that interpretation without deference.

These standards together mean that once a district court makes a reasoned factual finding (e.g., that the gun facilitated another felony) supported by evidence and within the range of reasonable inferences, an appellate court will typically defer unless the finding is unsupported by the record.

## Practical proof at sentencing: witnesses, physical evidence, and PSR narratives

The government typically relies on a combination of testimonial, physical, and documentary evidence to prove the “in connection with” enhancement:

- Victim and eyewitness testimony. Victims who say the defendant brandished or used a gun in the course of committing an assault or in connection with a robbery or domestic incident provide compelling evidence of facilitation. Cutbank’s trial demonstrated this: D.F. and C.F. identified the sawed‑off rifle as the same weapon they had seen with the defendant in the past, and D.F. testified to being threatened and kept in a closet at gunpoint.

- Physical and forensic evidence. Location of the gun relative to contraband, fingerprints or DNA linking the weapon to the defendant, shell casings at a crime scene, or alterations to the weapon can all support a finding that the firearm had a realistic potential to facilitate felony activity. In Cutbank, DNA matched to the defendant and the discovery of the gun along the likely path between the victim’s home and the garage where he barricaded himself strengthened the nexus inference.

- Police reports and investigative narratives. The PSR often collects and synthesizes trial evidence, police reports, and other investigative materials. If the district court adopts or relies upon the PSR’s factual recitation and the defendant fails to challenge those statements, the PSR can supply an adequate factual basis for applying the enhancement. The Cutbank court emphasized that the district court adopted the PSR statements and that such adoption supports the enhancement absent clear error.

- Defendant statements and admissions. Statements made by the defendant—either to police or to witnesses—admitting to possessing a firearm during the commission of another felony are straightforward proof. Counsel should be alert to hearsay and confrontation issues at trial, but at sentencing the court may consider a broader array of admissible materials under the Guidelines’ sentencing procedures.

Defense counsel facing a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement should actively contest the factual basis in the PSR, object to adoption of unverified narratives, and present contrary evidence—witnesses, alibi proof, forensic experts—to undermine temporal or spatial proximity or the functional relationship between the firearm and the alleged felony. Cross‑examination of prosecution witnesses regarding inconsistencies, timing, and location of the weapon can weaken the court’s inference of facilitation.

## Procedural considerations and appellate posture

Several procedural points are important for practitioners:

- Preserve objections at sentencing. If a defendant does not timely object to PSR factual statements that form the basis for the enhancement, courts often treat those facts as adopted and review them only for clear error. Cutbank demonstrates the practical consequence: the district court accepted the PSR facts without objection, and the appellate court affirmed.

- Use of trial evidence at sentencing. Evidence admitted at trial—victim testimony, DNA testing, witness identifications—may be used again at sentencing. If a defendant believes the trial record does not support the enhancement, the proper time to press that point is at sentencing and on appeal with preserved objections.

- Harmless error and cumulative effect. Even if a particular evidentiary ruling at trial was erroneous, appellate courts will not vacate a sentence where the error was harmless in light of the substantial remaining evidence linking the weapon to the felony. The Cutbank opinion reflects this approach in its review of prior evidentiary rulings and the ultimate sentencing outcome.

- Variance arguments under § 3553(a). A district court must correctly calculate the Guidelines range before considering a variance under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). A defendant unhappy with application of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement can argue on the record that, even if correctly calculated, the Guidelines range yields a greater sentence than necessary to satisfy the sentencing factors. Appellate courts will presume a within‑Guidelines sentence reasonable, but they will review whether the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors and whether the sentence was substantively reasonable.

## Applying the doctrine: hypothetical illustrations

- Hypothetical A — drugs and fortress facts. Police search a home and find a semiautomatic pistol in a safe next to kilogram brick packaging materials, scales, and distribution ledgers. The defendant admits the safe belongs to him. Temporal and spatial proximity and the presence of drug distribution indicia support a finding that the firearm facilitated the drug enterprise; courts commonly apply the enhancement in these circumstances.

- Hypothetical B — mere presence. A defendant lawfully keeps a rifle unloaded and secured in a closet in his apartment. That apartment is the scene of a neighbor’s theft of a bicycle from the hallway. The defendant’s locked, stored rifle bears no relationship to the theft. Absent evidence that the weapon was used to facilitate the theft, courts will be reluctant to apply the enhancement.

- Hypothetical C — domestic threat and prior possession. A defendant is charged with felon in possession of a handgun found in his car after he fled a domestic disturbance. The victim testifies that the defendant previously displayed that same handgun to scare her and later threatened to shoot her in the incident. Even if the defendant was not separately charged with aggravated assault, the factual record supports the inference that the gun facilitated the domestic felony, and the enhancement is likely to be applied.

These examples illustrate the flexible, fact‑intensive nature of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) inquiry.

## Conclusion

The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement turns on whether a firearm “facilitated, or had the potential of facilitating” another felony. Courts weigh temporal and spatial proximity, the functional relationship between weapon and crime, the nature of the firearm and surrounding circumstances, and the strength of testimonial and physical evidence. The sentencing court resolves disputed facts by a preponderance of the evidence, factual findings are reviewed for clear error, and legal interpretations of the guideline are reviewed de novo. Cutbank exemplifies how the combination of victim identification, forensic linkage, contemporaneous misconduct (flight and resistance), and an adopted PSR can establish the enhancement even when the defendant was not separately convicted of a related felony. For defense counsel, preserving objections to PSR facts, challenging the nexus with concrete contrary evidence, and pressing § 3553(a) arguments remain the principal means to contest the application or consequences of this frequently dispositive enhancement.
