Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

Chapter 5: # Hearsay, Non-Hearsay Uses, and the Confrontation Clause in Police-Response Testimony

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank furnishes a compact springboard into recurring trial problems: when may officers recount what a complaining witness or a 911 caller said without running afoul of the hearsay rule, and when does the Confrontation Clause require that out‑of‑court declarants be subject to cross‑examination before the jury may consider their prior statements for substantive truth? Cutbank also illustrates the trial‑management question of sequencing testimony and the district court’s latitude to delay a limiting instruction until the close of trial. This chapter explains the governing rules and tests, shows how they operate in practice, and highlights preservation and appellate‑review considerations that lawyers should not overlook.

## Defining hearsay and non‑hearsay purposes (effect on listener, background/context)

The basic starting point is Rule 801 of the Federal Rules of Evidence: hearsay is an out‑of‑court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. If an out‑of‑court utterance is offered for some other purpose — for example, to show why a listener acted as he did, to explain the circumstances confronting a witness or officer, or to show the declarant’s state of mind — it is not hearsay and is not excluded by Rule 802. This distinction is simple in principle but often difficult in practice: the same words can be offered either for their truth or for their effect on the listener.

Two common non‑hearsay uses arise frequently in police‑response testimony. First, officers may recount statements to explain the officers’ subsequent conduct: a 911 caller’s report that “he has a gun” explains why officers approached with weapons drawn. The purpose of this testimony is not to prove that the accused in fact possessed a gun, but to show the basis for the officers’ perception and decision‑making. Courts recognize this as a classic non‑hearsay purpose — the statement’s effect on the listener — and therefore admissible for that limited purpose. Second, out‑of‑court statements may be offered to provide background or context for a sequence of events so that the jury can understand testimony that otherwise would be a bare series of actions without explanation. Background/context testimony is treated similarly: admissible when used to explain why parties or officers did what they did, not to establish the truth of the assertions in those statements.

Both categories remain subject to the general constraints on admissibility. If the government intends to rely on the contents of the statement for its truth, it must either fit the statement within a hearsay exception or be prepared to show that the statement is non‑testimonial for Confrontation Clause purposes (discussed below). A trial court also retains its Rule 403 power to exclude otherwise admissible non‑hearsay statements if their probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or undue consumption of time. In practice, however, courts are generally tolerant of police testimony recounting statements for the explanatory purposes described above, provided the judge and counsel take steps — e.g., limiting instructions or redact-and-proffer approaches — to avoid juror confusion about the permissible uses of those statements.

Cutbank illustrates these principles. The defendant urged before trial that the witnesses’ testimony that they had seen him with the sawed‑off Marlin rifle before the charged night was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court (and the Eighth Circuit on appeal) treated prior possession testimony as intrinsic: prior observations that a defendant possessed the same weapon are directly relevant to proving contemporaneous possession or control of that weapon. Likewise, when officers testified about what they were told or found in responding to D.F.’s call, the court viewed that evidence as explaining officer actions and knowledge at the time, not as improper hearsay offered to prove the truth of contested facts.

## Crawford and Davis: testimonial versus non‑testimonial statements; the primary‑purpose test

While the hearsay doctrine governs admissibility in general, the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause imposes a constitutional limit when the government seeks to introduce an out‑of‑court statement that is “testimonial” and the declarant does not appear for cross‑examination. Crawford v. Washington established that testimonial hearsay is categorically barred unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination. But Crawford left open the definition of “testimonial.” The Supreme Court in Davis v. Washington and related cases (often grouped with Hammon and subsequent clarifications) developed the critical “primary‑purpose” test. Under Davis, statements are non‑testimonial when made in the course of police interrogation under circumstances objectively indicating that the primary purpose was to enable police assistance to meet an ongoing emergency. By contrast, statements are more likely testimonial when the circumstances indicate that the primary purpose was to produce evidence for prosecution — for example, formal statements to identify or blame a perpetrator after the emergency has ended.

Applying the Davis primary‑purpose test requires a practical, context‑sensitive inquiry — a fact‑driven assessment of whether the declarant’s statements and the questioner’s conduct were aimed principally at resolving an ongoing threat or principally at investigating past wrongdoing. Questions about timing (ongoing emergency vs. past event), formality (casual exclamation vs. structured interview), and audience (911 operator or police arriving at a scene vs. detective conducting a crime‑scene interview hours later) are central to the analysis.

Several consequences follow. First, if a statement is non‑testimonial under Davis, Crawford’s confrontation rule does not apply and the hearsay issue is controlled solely by the evidentiary rules. Thus, an officer may recount a 911 call that reports an active shooting to explain why officers acted as they did without offending Crawford if the call was made to meet an ongoing emergency. Second, where the primary purpose was investigative — a call made after the danger passes, or a sit‑down interview designed to collect evidence — the statement is testimonial; the Confrontation Clause bars its admission unless the declarant testifies or a Crawford exception applies.

Cutbank did not require a full Davis analysis because D.F., the declarant of the initial complaint, testified at trial. But the opinion highlights the usual pathway: the government argued the officers’ testimony was admissible to explain their actions and knowledge, and the district court admitted such testimony without contemporaneous limiting instructions. Because D.F. later testified, the Confrontation issue was not implicated in the Crawford sense.

## Confrontation satisfied when the declarant appears for cross‑examination (e.g., Charboneau)

The Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant “actually appear[s] in court and testify[s] in person,” so that the defendant has the opportunity to confront and cross‑examine the declarant. This is the practical implication of Crawford: the requirement of cross‑examination is met by trial testimony. The Eighth Circuit’s application of this principle in United States v. Charboneau (cited in Cutbank) reiterates the straightforward proposition that if the government calls the declarant and subjects her to cross‑examination, any prior out‑of‑court statements she made may be admitted subject to the ordinary hearsay rules and any applicable exceptions, and the constitutional objection evaporates.

That is why many prosecutions proceed by calling the complainant or 911 caller to testify. When the declarant testifies, the defense gets the constitutionally mandated opportunity to cross‑examine, and the risk that the jury will treat an out‑of‑court statement as the substituted evidence against the defendant is reduced. Courts, however, remain attentive to whether the government attempts to use the declarant’s prior statements as bootstrapping evidence before the declarant has been placed on the stand. If the declarant never testifies, the Davis primary‑purpose test and Crawford control the admissibility of the out‑of‑court statement.

In Cutbank the district court admitted officers’ testimony about the initial complaints and investigatory steps; the court later received D.F.’s testimony. On appeal the Eighth Circuit pointed to Charboneau for the proposition that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when a hearsay declarant later testifies in court, and found no abuse of discretion by the district court in admitting the officers’ testimony without giving the defendant’s requested contemporaneous limiting instruction.

## Managing trial flow: whether to preclude early reliance on declarant statements before the declarant testifies

Defense counsel often seek to prevent the jury from hearing a declarant’s out‑of‑court statements until after the declarant has testified, because jurors are naturally inclined to treat dramatic statements as evidence of the defendant’s guilt even when the court later instructs them on limiting purposes. On these grounds a defendant may move for an order that (1) limits the prosecution’s use of the declarant’s out‑of‑court statements until the declarant has testified, or (2) requires contemporaneous limiting instructions each time such a statement is admitted.

Trial judges generally have wide discretion in managing evidence and jury instructions. The Eighth Circuit in United States v. Bennett (quoted in Cutbank) held that a district court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time it is admitted if it provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial. That means a court may admit police testimony recounting out‑of‑court statements without an immediate limiting instruction, and give a single, comprehensive limiting instruction at the close of evidence, so long as that instruction adequately explains the limited purpose for which the statements were admitted.

But district courts must also be sensitive to practical realities. Judges balancing the concerns will consider the nature of the testimony, its potential for prejudice if heard before the declarant testifies, and the chance of confusion if jurors are later told to disregard the earlier evidence for substantive purposes. If the government intends to use the prior statement substantively before the declarant has been called, a court may be more likely to require a limiting instruction or even to restrict the timing of admission. Conversely, if the testimonial risk has been eliminated by the declarant’s appearance on the stand, the court’s discretion to manage sequencing is broad.

Cutbank demonstrates the common compromise. The district court admitted police testimony explaining their response to the complaint and denied the defendant’s pre‑trial request for a rigid instruction that the jury not consider D.F.’s statements as substantive until D.F. testified. The court instead allowed the evidence in context and provided — or could provide — the customary end‑of‑trial limiting instructions. Because D.F. did testify and because the court did not admit hearsay as such, the Circuit found no abuse of discretion in the trial court’s management of the trial flow.

Practical advice to defense counsel: To preserve any appellate claim, contemporaneously object and request the relief you want (a contemporaneous limiting instruction or exclusion pending the declarant’s testimony). If the court declines, make sure the record reflects the nature of the anticipated prejudice and the alternative remedy sought; a general book‑and‑claim on appeal will be less persuasive.

## Authentication, impeachment, and the use of police reports

When cross‑examining a witness about prior statements to police, trial counsel will sometimes attempt to read portions of a police report into the record or to admit the report as extrinsic impeachment evidence. This may be improper absent foundation and authentication: extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible to impeach, but it must be properly authenticated as the witness’s own statement or as accurately reporting her words. Rule 613 governs prior inconsistent statements and the procedures for introducing such impeachment evidence; Rule 901 requires authentication of extrinsic documents.

The Eighth Circuit’s recent decisions, cited in Cutbank, reinforce that district courts may require the proponent to lay a foundation before allowing portions of a police report to be read to the jury. Where the report is not authored by the witness, the defense must either call the report’s author or otherwise show that the portion accurately records the witness’s statements. United States v. Cumbie is a helpful modern example: the court upheld a district court’s requirement that prior statements used for impeachment be authenticated. Failure to do so enables the judge to bar the exhibit or the reading of the report as a proper exercise of discretion.

In Cutbank the trial court allowed the defense to question C.F. about her statements to police but sustained objections to defense counsel reading parts of the police report into evidence without foundation. The Circuit held that ruling was not an abuse of discretion.

## Appellate review of limiting‑instruction decisions (abuse of discretion; adequacy of end‑of‑trial instructions)

When a defendant challenges a district court’s refusal to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction or its decision to give only a later instruction, appellate courts apply an abuse‑of‑discretion standard to the district court’s evidentiary and instructional choices. The Eighth Circuit’s decisions — including Bennett and the Cutbank opinion — emphasize that a court need not provide an immediate instruction for each admission of evidence; it is sufficient that the court’s overall instructions adequately inform the jury of the permissible uses of the testimony.

If the defendant fails to preserve the issue, appellate courts may review for plain error. But when the issue was preserved, a defendant still faces the perennial appellate hurdle: reversal requires more than a mere error of judgment. An erroneous evidentiary ruling will lead to reversal only when the appellant shows that the error was not harmless — that is, that the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence. As the Eighth Circuit explained in DeMarce and reiterated in Cutbank, the inquiry focuses on whether the admitted evidence “influenced or had more than a slight influence on the verdict.” The cumulative‑error doctrine adds that reversal is required only when the combined effect of multiple errors produced substantial prejudice. United States v. Keys, cited in Cutbank, articulates that approach.

In practical terms, the defendant’s best appellate argument is to show how the timing or content of the police‑response testimony made later limiting instructions ineffective. Examples that courts have found persuasive include: (1) when the testimony was particularly inflammatory and admitted early so that jurors could not reasonably be expected to forget it; (2) when the government repeatedly returned to the inadmissible content in closing; or (3) when the declarant did not testify and a testimonial statement was admitted without satisfying Crawford/Davis. Merely saying “I would have preferred an earlier instruction” rarely suffices.

Cutbank’s outcome reflects these standards. The Circuit found no abuse of discretion because (1) the record did not show that hearsay was improperly admitted during trial, (2) D.F., the declarant, testified in person (thus satisfying the Confrontation Clause), and (3) the government’s overall case was strong enough that any arguable instructional timing issue was harmless.

## Practical litigation and preservation strategies

The law described above presents both opportunities and pitfalls for advocating counsel:

- If you represent the defense and you anticipate police testimony recounting out‑of‑court statements, make a pretrial proffer explaining the context and explain to the judge why an immediate limiting instruction is necessary to avoid prejudice. Request contemporaneous limiting instructions, and if the court declines, make a timely objection on hearsay and Confrontation Clause grounds so the record is clear for appeal.

- If you represent the government, be prepared to articulate the non‑hearsay purpose of the testimony at the time it is offered: explain whether it is being offered to show the effect on the listener, background/context for officer conduct, or as a party‑opponent admission. Where a statement might be testimonial under Davis, consider calling the declarant to the stand to obviate Crawford concerns.

- Authentication issues: when you intend to impeach with a police report or other extrinsic written record, be ready to lay the foundation by showing the report accurately recorded the witness’s statement or by producing the author. The absence of authentication can prevent the use of impeachment documents and may disrupt defense cross‑examination plans.

- Closing and limiting instructions: request a specific jury instruction that explains both the limited purpose for which the jury may consider the declarant’s statements and that the jury cannot treat those statements as independent proof of the defendant’s guilt unless the declarant testifies and is subject to cross‑examination. If the court declines a contemporaneous instruction, make sure the jury receives a clear end‑of‑trial limiting instruction.

- Preservation: contemporaneous objections and clear alternative requests (e.g., exclusion of the statement, redaction, instruction, or sequestration until the declarant testifies) are essential to preserve issues for appeal.

## Conclusion

Police testimony about what victims or callers told them sits at the intersection of the hearsay doctrine and the Confrontation Clause. The modern framework requires judges to identify the purpose for which such testimony is offered, to analyze whether the statements are testimonial under the Crawford/Davis primary‑purpose test, and to manage the timing and instructions so the jury understands permissible uses. Cutbank typifies the routine outcome: officers may describe callers’ statements to explain their actions; if the declarant later testifies at trial, the Confrontation concern is generally resolved; and a district court’s decision to withhold a contemporaneous limiting instruction and instead give customary end‑of‑trial instructions will not be reversed absent abuse of discretion and a showing of prejudice. The practical lesson for advocates is straightforward — think ahead about sequencing, preserve objections, and be prepared to make the record about testimonial character and the necessity of limiting instructions.
