Chapter 2: # Elements of § 922(g)(1): Status, Possession, Knowledge, and Commerce

This chapter explains what the government must prove to obtain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (a felon-in-possession prosecution), how those elements have been reshaped by Rehaif and its progeny, and how courts treat common evidentiary disputes that arise at trial. The recent Eighth Circuit decision in United States v. Cutbank (No. 24-1225) provides a useful vehicle for discussing these issues in practice: Cutbank’s prosecution and appeal illustrate disputes over proof of possession, the admissibility of prior-possession testimony, the application of Rehaif’s knowledge requirement in the trial context, and the minimal interstate-commerce nexus required under federal law. This chapter organizes doctrine and practice around the four elemental questions a jury must resolve in a § 922(g)(1) prosecution: (1) was the defendant a person barred by § 922(g)(1) at the time of the charged conduct; (2) did the defendant know that he had that prohibited status (the Rehaif requirement); (3) did the defendant knowingly possess the firearm (actual, constructive, or joint possession); and (4) was there a sufficient interstate-commerce nexus.

## The Elements of a § 922(g)(1) Offense: An Overview

A § 922(g)(1) charge requires proof of four basic facts beyond a reasonable doubt:

- That the defendant has the disqualifying status—i.e., was previously convicted of a felony (or otherwise a person subject to a statutory prohibition) under § 922(g)(1);
- That the defendant knew that he had that status at the time of the possession (the mental-state element mandated by Rehaif v. United States);
- That the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm—possession in fact or constructive/joint possession plus knowledge of the presence of the firearm; and
- That the firearm was in or affected interstate commerce.

Each of these elements has particular legal tests and evidentiary rules that govern trial presentation, jury instructions, and appellate review. The remainder of this chapter treats each element in turn, with attention to practical proof issues and the interaction of these elements in evidentiary rulings like those in Cutbank.

## Prior-Felon Status and Proof After Rehaif

Historically, courts treated the status element of § 922(g)(1) as a relatively straightforward matter of record: the government proved a past felony conviction by introducing certified records, and that was enough to establish that the defendant belonged to the class of persons barred from possessing firearms. Rehaif v. United States, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), changed the mental-state calculus: Rehaif held that, for prosecutions under § 922(g) and § 924(a)(2), the government must prove not only that the defendant belonged to a prohibited class (e.g., had a prior felony conviction), but also that the defendant knew at the time of the firearm possession that he belonged to that class.

The immediate practical consequence of Rehaif is that the government must furnish proof—direct or circumstantial—that the defendant knew of his disqualifying status when he possessed the firearm. Proof can take many forms:

- Direct admissions by the defendant (e.g., statements acknowledging a prior conviction, admissions that he knew he could not possess firearms);
- Documentary evidence demonstrating that the defendant was informed of his conviction and its consequences (e.g., plea colloquies, written judgments, probation or parole notices);
- Circumstantial evidence from the defendant’s criminal history (e.g., a recent and numerous pattern of felony convictions, ongoing supervision, period of incarceration, or easily available public record informing the defendant of the conviction);
- Evidence of steps taken to conceal the firearm or other consciousness-of-guilt evidence probative of the defendant’s awareness of his status in context.

Rehaif did not prescribe a specific formula for proof; the Supreme Court required proof of knowledge of status as an element the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. In a practical trial setting prosecutors will therefore offer certified conviction records to establish the status element and support that evidence with witness testimony or documentary proof that the defendant knew about the conviction—plea colloquy transcripts, parole files, or the defendant’s own statements.

When a defendant fails to preserve a Rehaif objection at trial (for example, by failing to request a jury instruction that includes knowledge of status or failing to object to the omission of that element), appellate courts apply plain-error review. The Supreme Court’s treatment of Rehaif-related appellate claims (and the courts of appeals’ follow-up decisions) make clear that a defendant who attempts to raise a Rehaif claim for the first time on appeal must satisfy the demanding plain-error standard. Under that standard the defendant must show (1) there was an error, (2) the error is clear or obvious, (3) the error affected substantial rights—usually meaning there is a reasonable probability the error affected the outcome—and (4) the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Lower courts assessing prejudice look to the record evidence relating to knowledge of status and determine whether the evidence is sufficient to remove any reasonable doubt a jury could have had on that element. See, e.g., Greer v. United States, 141 S. Ct. 2090 (2021) (applying plain-error review to the omission of a Rehaif instruction and evaluating prejudice by reference to the record).

The Cutbank prosecution illustrates how courts treat proof of status at sentencing and at trial. At sentencing the district court adopted, without objection, the Presentence Investigation Report’s recitation of Cutbank’s prior convictions; that adoption supplied a firm factual basis for ACCA enhancement. For the guilt phase, the government relied on witnesses and documentary evidence of prior convictions and on testimony that connected Cutbank to the sawed-off firearm. Students should note the distinction: at trial the government must prove status and the defendant’s knowledge of that status beyond a reasonable doubt; at sentencing the district court may adopt facts in an uncontested PSR under a preponderance standard for guideline calculation, but any sentencing enhancement tied to status can have profound consequences (as in Cutbank, where ACCA applied). Defense counsel should therefore object and seek a Rehaif instruction whenever there is any colorable argument that the defendant lacked awareness of his felony status.

## The Rehaif Knowledge Requirement and Appellate Posture

Rehaif’s knowledge-of-status requirement created numerous litigation points about preservation and appellate review. If a defendant affirmatively objects at trial to the omission of a Rehaif element or requests an instruction tracking Rehaif’s requirement, the trial court must address that request; reversal is possible if the omission constitutes structural or preserved error. If the defendant fails to preserve the error, appellate review is for plain error, and the defendant shoulders the burden of demonstrating prejudice and the other plain-error factors.

In assessing prejudice under plain-error review, appellate courts examine the entire trial record to determine whether the omission of the knowledge-of-status instruction likely affected the verdict. Courts will examine, among other things, whether the defendant (1) admitted or otherwise conceded the felony conviction at trial, (2) had recent or numerous felony convictions or ongoing supervision suggesting he know of his status, (3) made statements or took actions indicating an awareness of his criminal history, or (4) had other circumstances that would have put the defendant on notice of his disqualifying status (e.g., pending parole, supervised release, or a recent plea colloquy). Where the record contains strong, uncontradicted evidence that the defendant knew about his status, appellate courts have generally concluded Rehaif omissions were not prejudicial under plain-error review. By contrast, a thin record on knowledge—e.g., limited or ambiguous evidence of a distant conviction without proof the defendant knew it had collateral consequences—may support a successful Rehaif claim when preserved and may satisfy plain error when the record shows reasonable probability of a different verdict.

Because appellate review turns on the record in each case, trial counsel should preserve Rehaif claims through request for a Rehaif instruction or through contemporaneous objection to avoid the heavy burden of plain-error review on appeal.

## Possession: Actual, Constructive, and Joint Possession; Proof and Evidentiary Indicia

A separate set of questions involves what it means to “possess” a firearm and how the government proves possession beyond a reasonable doubt. Possession in § 922(g)(1) cases is typically established by proof that the defendant had dominion and control over the weapon and knew of its presence. Courts recognize three paradigms of possession:

- Actual possession: the firearm is in the defendant’s immediate physical custody or control (e.g., found on the defendant’s person or in a container the defendant was carrying). Actual possession is typically the easiest form for the government to prove because the weapon’s physical presence with the defendant is direct evidence of control.

- Constructive possession: the firearm is not on the person but is in a place under the defendant’s dominion and control (e.g., a firearm found in the defendant’s home, vehicle, or an area the defendant exclusively controls). Constructive possession requires proof that the defendant had the ability and intent to exercise dominion and control over the firearm, even if someone else handled it at a particular moment.

- Joint possession: two or more people share dominion and control over the weapon (e.g., a firearm in a common area of a residence used by multiple occupants). The government may convict a defendant who has joint constructive possession if it proves that the defendant knowingly had the power and intent to exercise control over the firearm.

To sustain a possession conviction the government must prove not only the defendant’s relationship to the weapon (actual, constructive, or joint possession) but also that the defendant knew of the firearm’s existence. Evidence used to establish possession and knowledge can be direct or circumstantial and commonly includes:

- Eyewitness identification and testimony placing the firearm in the defendant’s hands or in places he controlled. In Cutbank, for example, the victim and a household member identified the sawed-off rifle as one they had seen Cutbank with before the night in question; the district court allowed this testimony as intrinsic evidence of continuous possession rather than extrinsic evidence of propensity.

- Physical evidence connecting the defendant to the weapon: DNA on the gun, fingerprints, possession of ammunition, or clothing fibers. In Cutbank, DNA on the sawed-off Marlin matched the defendant, a fact the jury could properly consider in assessing control and possession.

- Proximity and opportunity: testimony that the defendant was alone in a garage with the weapon, or that the weapon was found along a path the defendant likely used, are circumstantial indicators that the defendant possessed the weapon.

- Statements by the defendant: admissions, threats while holding the firearm, or statements acknowledging ownership are powerful evidence of possession and knowledge.

- Behavior indicative of control or concealment: fleeing with the firearm, changing the weapon, or placing it in a location to avoid detection are conductive of the required inference of dominion and knowledge.

Courts routinely instruct juries that possession may be actual, constructive, or joint and require the jury to find the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm. When multiple forms of possession are possible, the jury may be instructed in the disjunctive (the government may prove any of these modes), but the instruction should require specific findings—i.e., that the jury find beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant had dominion and control and knew of the firearm.

Practical evidence-law consequences follow. The government often seeks to admit testimony that a witness had seen a defendant with the same weapon on prior occasions to show ownership or continuity of control. Defense counsel frequently objects, arguing such evidence is improper propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). But courts often hold that testimony about prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic when it tends to prove an element of the charged offense—ownership, dominion, or continuous possession—and therefore is admissible without Rule 404(b) analysis. In the Eighth Circuit, United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), illustrates this principle: prior possession of the firearm is “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon.” Cutbank applied that principle in admitting testimony from the victim and a housemate that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the charged incident. The court treated those statements as intrinsic to proving possession rather than as prohibited character propensity evidence.

Defense counsel should be attentive, however, to the risk that prior-possession testimony will stray into inflammatory or collateral matters (e.g., other criminal acts); courts will exclude testimony that functions primarily to show bad character rather than to establish ownership or continuity. In cross-examination, the defense may probe witnesses’ inconsistent statements to the police, but extrinsic impeachment requires proper authentication; courts commonly prevent reading police reports into the record without foundation that the witness actually made the statements in the report. See United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), cited in Cutbank.

Evidence of actual threats, domestic violence, or a victim’s family trauma may arise in cross-examination— such as the victim’s testimony in Cutbank that she did not like guns because her sister had been killed. District courts have broad discretion to admit or exclude such testimony under Rules 401 and 403; absent an objection or a clear showing of prejudice, appellate courts defer to that discretion. Cutbank illustrates the practical limits of such challenges: the court denied motions to strike testimony about the victim’s sister’s death and found no unfair prejudice, a ruling the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

## Interstate Commerce Nexus: Minimal but Necessary

Section 922(g) is a federal statute; Congress’s power to regulate possession of firearms rests on the Commerce Clause. As a result, the government must prove that the firearm "was in or affecting commerce"—a requirement satisfied by showing a minimal interstate-commerce nexus. The Supreme Court and the courts of appeals have uniformly recognized that the nexus requirement is modest: proof that the weapon previously traveled in interstate commerce, that it was manufactured outside the state, or that it crossed state lines at some point in its history is typically sufficient.

The common ways the government proves the interstate-commerce element are: introducing testimony from the manufacturer or an expert that the firearm was produced out of state, introducing records showing interstate shipment, or establishing that the firearm’s make and model is known to be manufactured in another state or imported. Serial-number records, manufacturer catalogs, or Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) trace evidence often suffice. The government need not show the defendant personally transported the firearm across state lines or that the interstate transport occurred immediately before the offense; a prior passage in interstate commerce at any time in the firearm’s history is sufficient.

Defendants sometimes challenge interstate-commerce proof on the ground that the government has provided only conclusory testimony without documentary corroboration. Courts generally allow a minimal amount of proof at trial; if the government’s proof is weak but not nonexistent, the issue usually goes to the weight of the evidence rather than admissibility. Rarely will a court find the interstate-commerce element lacking when the government introduces basic evidence of manufacture or ownership outside the state.

In Cutbank, the court’s opinion does not dwell on the interstate-commerce evidence in detail, but the conviction record and the PSR treated the sawed-off rifle as within the statutory framework—an approach consistent with the modest showing the Commerce Clause requires.

## Indictments and Jury Instructions in the Post-Rehaif Era

The doctrine discussed above has practical consequences for the drafting of indictments and jury instructions and for trial preservation strategies.

Indictment. A § 922(g)(1) indictment typically pleads the statute’s elements: the defendant’s status as a convicted felon, the possession of the firearm, the interstate-commerce nexus, and the necessary mens rea (“knowingly” possessed). After Rehaif, prosecutors must ensure their charging documents and proposed instructions track the knowledge-of-status element, although a mere omission in the indictment is not invariably fatal if the government proves the element beyond a reasonable doubt at trial. To avoid appellate defense claims, prosecutors commonly allege both that the defendant “knowingly” possessed the firearm and that the defendant was knowingly a person who had previously been convicted of a felony.

Jury instructions. Trial judges must instruct juries on each element the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt, and after Rehaif, that obligation includes the knowledge-of-status element unless the defendant expressly waives the right or concedes status. A model § 922(g)(1) instruction in the post-Rehaif era will include language similar to this (simplified for illustration):

- The defendant has previously been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year;
- At the time of the possession alleged, the defendant knew he had that status (knew he had previously been convicted of a felony); and
- The defendant knowingly possessed the firearm, and the firearm had traveled in or affected interstate commerce.

The sequence and precise wording matter: courts should ensure the jury understands that the government must prove knowledge of status in addition to the objective fact of the prior conviction. Defense counsel should request a Rehaif instruction and object if the court declines, to preserve the claim for appeal.

Preservation and strategic considerations. Defense counsel should consider the following strategic points:

- Preservation: Object to any instruction that omits knowledge of status and request a Rehaif instruction. Failing to do so subjects the claim to plain-error review on appeal, which is a heavy burden.

- Stipulations: Sometimes the defense will stipulate to the prior conviction to avoid the admission of prejudicial records and testimony. A stipulation waives argument about the existence of the conviction but leaves open a Rehaif challenge only in the narrow circumstances where the defense asserts lack of knowledge of the conviction despite agreeing the record shows it. Careful colloquy is necessary so that the record reflects what the defendant concedes and what remains disputed.

- Cross-examination and impeachment: When the government offers victim and bystander testimony that the defendant had possessed the same firearm before the charged incident, defense counsel should evaluate whether the testimony is intrinsic (admissible to show ownership/continuous possession) or improper evidence of bad character. Cutbank demonstrates the courts’ inclination to treat prior use of the same weapon as probative of ownership and control rather than solely propensity.

- Authentication: To impeach government witnesses with prior inconsistent statements, the defense must authenticate extrinsic evidence before reading police reports or the like into evidence. Cutbank shows the district court refusing to allow the reading of portions of a police report without foundation; the point is fundamental to impeachment practice.

## Standards of Review: Evidentiary Rulings and Rehaif Errors

Courts review district courts’ evidentiary decisions under a deferential abuse-of-discretion standard. In Cutbank the defendant challenged multiple trial rulings—admission of prior-possession testimony, refusal to instruct the jury about hearsay limits at the moment of admission, and rulings on cross-examination and impeachment—but the Eighth Circuit affirmed under the abuse-of-discretion standard. Those rulings illustrate two important practical points: (1) judges have substantial latitude in admitting intrinsic evidence that bears on possession and ownership; and (2) when the record contains strong, independently persuasive evidence of possession and of status, even an error in admitting marginally relevant evidence will likely be deemed harmless under the harmless-error test.

When a defendant fails to object to a Rehaif omission at trial, appellate courts apply plain-error review (that same demanding deferential standard noted earlier). When the omission is preserved, the reviewing court examines whether the omission constituted legal error and whether that legal error warrants reversal under the usual standards for preserved error. Because Rehaif goes to the elements the government must prove, courts will reverse a preserved Rehaif omission unless the record shows the government proved the knowledge-of-status element beyond a reasonable doubt.

## Practical Takeaways and Trial Tips

- Preserve Rehaif challenges. Request a jury instruction that expressly requires the government to prove the defendant knew of his disqualifying status at the time of possession.

- Consider stipulation tradeoffs carefully. A stipulation to a prior conviction can reduce the risk of prejudice, but it may concede the status element; if the defense’s best Rehaif argument is lack of knowledge, do not stipulate away that issue without an on-the-record discussion with the client.

- Use admission and documentary proof. Prosecutors should introduce certified conviction records and any other materials (plea colloquies, parole/probation records, statements to police) that show the defendant knew of his conviction and its consequences.

- Prove possession across the spectrum. When actual possession is unavailable, marshal constructive-possession evidence: proximity, accessibility, physical evidence like DNA or fingerprints, eyewitness testimony linking the defendant to the weapon, and conduct (flight, concealment) showing conscious control.

- Anticipate 404(b) and intrinsic-evidence disputes. Prior sightings of the same firearm are commonly admissible to show ownership or continuity of possession; defense counsel should develop reasons to limit or contextualize such testimony when it threatens to spill into character evidence.

- Authenticate impeachment evidence. When impeaching witnesses with prior statements, be prepared to lay the foundation and authenticate police reports or other documents properly before attempting to read them into the record.

- Remember the minimal interstate-commerce element. The government’s burden on commerce is modest but real; prosecutors should be prepared to introduce readily-available evidence of the firearm’s out-of-state manufacture or shipment.

## Conclusion

A successful § 922(g)(1) prosecution requires the government to assemble proof on four elements: status, knowledge of status (Rehaif), knowing possession, and interstate commerce. Rehaif heightened the government’s burden by adding the knowledge-of-status element to the list of facts the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. Trial counsel—prosecution and defense alike—must plan accordingly: prosecutors must present direct or circumstantial evidence of knowledge of status; defense counsel must preserve Rehaif objections or consider strategic stipulations; and both sides must be prepared to litigate possession issues (actual, constructive, joint) with an eye to admissibility and error preservation. The Cutbank decision illustrates how these issues play out in practice: prior possession evidence was admissible as intrinsic proof of control; Confrontation concerns were satisfied where witnesses testified at trial; impeachment evidence required proper authentication; and Rehaif-related objections not raised at trial are subject to the demanding plain-error standard on appeal. Understanding these doctrinal contours will enable counsel to make informed strategic choices at every stage of a § 922(g)(1) prosecution.
