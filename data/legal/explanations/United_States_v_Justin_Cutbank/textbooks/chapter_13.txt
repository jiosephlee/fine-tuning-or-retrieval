Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

Chapter 13: # Procedural and Substantive Reasonableness: Gall, Rita, and Eighth Circuit Presumptions

This chapter examines the post-Booker appellate framework for reviewing federal sentences. It explains the two-part inquiry courts undertake—procedural reasonableness followed by substantive reasonableness—identifies the standards of review that apply to the principal kinds of sentencing determinations, and describes how the Eighth Circuit treats within-Guidelines sentences on appeal. The court of appeals’ opinion in United States v. Cutbank exemplifies the interaction of these doctrines: the district court’s Guidelines calculation and its adoption of the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) bear on procedural review; the district court’s recognition of § 3553(a) factors and its choice to impose a within-Guidelines sentence illustrate the presumption of reasonableness the Eighth Circuit affords such sentences.

The doctrinal architecture rests on Booker’s transformation of the Guidelines into advisory standards; on Gall and Rita, which govern the allocation of responsibilities and the scope of appellate deference; and on later Supreme Court and circuit cases that refine the sequence of appellate review. Students should be able to (1) distinguish procedural errors that require correction before the merits of a sentence can be reviewed, (2) identify what constitutes an adequate explanation and consideration of arguments and § 3553(a) factors, and (3) predict how an appellate court—particularly the Eighth Circuit—will treat a within-Guidelines sentence and preserved mitigation arguments.

## Procedural reasonableness: correct range, responding to arguments, adequate explanation (Gall; Rosales-Mireles)

Procedural reasonableness concerns the correctness of the process the district court used to arrive at a sentence. The principal procedural requirements are (1) correct calculation of the applicable Guidelines range, (2) consideration of the statutorily enumerated § 3553(a) factors, (3) meaningful response to any nonfrivolous arguments for a different sentence by the parties, and (4) an adequate explanation for the chosen sentence, particularly when a court imposes a variance or departure.

The Supreme Court in Gall v. United States distilled these requirements into a clear allocation of responsibilities. Gall instructs district courts to calculate the Guidelines range, to permit parties to argue for a sentence outside the range, to consider the § 3553(a) factors, and, if selecting a sentence outside the range, to explain adequately the chosen variance. Gall also sets forth the appellate approach: first, the appellate court must ensure there was no significant procedural error; second, it must review the substantive reasonableness of the sentence for abuse of discretion.

Rosales-Mireles v. United States reinforces the sequencing emphasized in Gall. Before an appellate court conducts a substantive-reasonableness inquiry, it must ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate the Guidelines range correctly or otherwise procedurally erring in a way that could affect the substantive outcome. This sequence matters because procedural defects—miscalculation of the Guidelines or failure to consider or respond to arguments under § 3553(a)—can render a sentence substantively unreasonable as well; therefore, they must be identified and corrected first.

How do courts apply these principles in concrete terms? They treat questions of law about the Guidelines (e.g., whether an enhancement applies) de novo, and review the district court’s factual findings (for example, whether the defendant possessed a firearm in connection with another offense) for clear error. Discretionary determinations—such as whether to grant a downward variance—are reviewed for abuse of discretion. In practice, this tripartite formulation means that an appellate court will carefully examine (a) whether the Guidelines calculation rested on legally correct interpretations and adequately supported factual findings, and (b) whether the district court acknowledged and addressed the critical sentencing arguments and § 3553(a) considerations raised by the parties. In Cutbank, for example, the district court adopted the PSR and its factual statements about prior convictions and enhancements; the court then reviewed § 3553(a) factors and expressly considered mitigation arguments. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit concluded there was no procedural error and therefore moved to the substantive-abuse-of-discretion inquiry.

## Substantive reasonableness: abuse-of-discretion review of length; Rita’s appellate presumption

Once procedural reasonableness is established, an appellate court reviews the substantive reasonableness of the sentence for abuse of discretion. That is a highly deferential standard: an appellate court will affirm unless the sentence “falls outside the broad range of choices reasonably available to the district court,” or unless the district court’s stated reasons are inadequate or irrational in light of the § 3553(a) factors.

Rita v. United States addressed how appellate courts may treat within-Guidelines sentences. Although Booker made the Guidelines advisory, Rita held that an appellate court may apply a presumption of reasonableness to a sentence within the properly calculated Guidelines range. The presumption is not an irrebuttable rule and does not relieve an appellate court of its obligation to review for abuse of discretion; rather, it recognizes that a within-Guidelines sentence is likely to be reasonable because the Guidelines reflect national sentencing practices and the Commission’s empirical work.

After Rita and Gall, the inquiry on appeal thus typically unfolds as follows: (1) did the district court correctly calculate the Guidelines range and address procedural requirements? (2) if so, did the district court impose a sentence within that range? If yes, many circuits—including the Eighth—apply a presumption that the sentence is reasonable, and the defendant bears a substantial burden to demonstrate the sentence is substantively unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors and the record. If the court imposed a variance, the sentencing judge must provide a sufficiently detailed explanation so an appellate court can determine whether the variance was justified.

It is important to remember that the Rita presumption operates at the appellate level and does not alter the district court’s duty to reason explicitly about the § 3553(a) factors if the chosen sentence departs materially from what those factors would otherwise indicate. District courts must still “consider” the statutory factors and must explain deviations sufficiently for meaningful appellate review.

## Eighth Circuit applications: presumption for within-range sentences (e.g., Goodhouse) and treatment of preserved mitigation (e.g., Masood; Maluoth)

The Eighth Circuit applies the Rita presumption and follows the Gall/Rosales-Mireles sequencing. The circuit treats within-Guidelines sentences as presumptively reasonable on appeal. In United States v. Goodhouse, the court stated that a within-Guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable; although this presumption is rebuttable, it allocates a substantial burden to the defendant on appeal. That presumption animated the outcome in Cutbank: because the district court imposed a within-Guidelines sentence and properly calculated the range (adopting the PSR), the Eighth Circuit began with a presumption that the sentence was reasonable and sustained the sentence absent a showing of procedural error or substantive abuse of discretion.

Two recent Eighth Circuit decisions illustrate how the court treats preserved mitigation arguments and determines whether the district court actually considered § 3553(a) factors. In United States v. Masood, the court refused to find an abuse of discretion where the record showed the district court was aware of and rejected the defendant’s arguments—an express statement that the court had considered the arguments sufficed for appellate review. Likewise, in United States v. Maluoth the court explained that a district court need not provide a “mechanical recitation” of each § 3553(a) factor; rather, it must be clear from the record that the district court considered the factors in choosing a sentence. The Cutbank opinion quotes both principles to uphold a within-range sentence where the district court had reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and noted the § 3553(a) factors. In short, the Eighth Circuit enforces procedural sufficiency—awareness, consideration, and a demonstrable link between facts and reasoning—without demanding a formal checklist recitation from the sentencing judge.

These cases send a clear message to practitioners: preserve objections and argue mitigation clearly at sentencing. If the defendant preserves an objection and the record shows the district court considered the argument and rejected it, the appellate court is unlikely to second-guess the district court’s judgment except in rare cases where the sentence is outside the available range of reasonable choices.

## Building a record: articulating § 3553(a) factors and variances

Because appellate review is heavily based on the written and oral record from sentencing, defense counsel seeking a non-Guidelines sentence must build a clear record. Here are practical rules and techniques:

- Preserve objections to the PSR and to any disputed Guidelines application. A timely objection in the district court is essential for de novo review of legal questions and for avoiding forfeiture. If the PSR contains disputed facts that support an enhancement, present contrary evidence and obtain an explicit ruling resolving the factual dispute.

- Present a focused § 3553(a) argument in writing and orally. A sentencing memorandum that ties particular facts to the statutory factors—such as family responsibilities, rehabilitation progress, disproportionate sentence relative to past conduct, or mitigating mental-health evidence—gives the court the material it needs to assess a variance. During allocution, ask the court to state on the record (or in the written judgment) how it evaluated the arguments and which factors it found persuasive or unpersuasive.

- If seeking a variance, explain the magnitude and rationale for the variance. The district court should know not only why a variance is warranted but also how large the proposed variance should be and why that magnitude is sufficient but not greater than necessary.

- Request specific findings if the record is deficient. Where a judge appears to rely on generic statements or to overlook a central mitigating fact, defense counsel should ask the judge to articulate its reasons. Asking for the court to explain its decision to the extent necessary for appellate review is a routine and appropriate practice.

- Use the opportunity to create evidentiary support: letters, expert reports, employment and treatment records, and other materials that demonstrate rehabilitation or disproportionality. The more the district court has before it, the easier it is for the court to engage § 3553(a) factors and for an appellate court to review the sufficiency of the explanation.

Cutbank illustrates the value of these practices: the PSR was adopted by the district court, the court stated that it had considered the sentencing memoranda and § 3553(a) factors, and the defendant’s arguments were on the record. That procedural posture made reversal unlikely on appeal.

## Common pitfalls: miscalculations vs. disagreements with policy

Sentencing practice generates recurrent errors and misunderstandings. Students and practitioners should be alert to these common pitfalls.

- Treating a policy disagreement with the Guidelines as a procedural error. Defendants often complain that the Guidelines’ calculation produces an unduly harsh sentence. Absent a legal misapplication of the Guidelines or an unsupported factual finding, such a complaint is a disagreement about the sentence’s substantive fairness. Appellate courts do not reweigh the § 3553(a) factors de novo because the standard is abuse of discretion for substantive reasonableness. Put another way: a mere belief that the sentence is too long does not establish a procedural error.

- Failing to preserve vital objections. If the defendant does not object to a Guidelines calculation or a PSR fact at sentencing, appellate review may be limited to plain-error review (or the appeal may be foreclosed), making reversal difficult. Preservation is especially important where factual findings in the PSR support an enhancement that materially affects the Guidelines range.

- Expecting a lengthy verbatim explanation from the district court. Appellate courts require enough of an explanation to permit meaningful review, but they do not demand a line-by-line recitation of every § 3553(a) factor. However, what a district court cannot do is plainly ignore or refuse to consider nonfrivolous arguments. A short but clear statement that the court considered the written submissions, the § 3553(a) factors, and the defendant’s allocution will frequently suffice.

- Confusing legal and factual standards of review. Errors of law (e.g., misinterpreting a Guidelines provision) are reviewed de novo and can lead to resentencing. Factual findings (e.g., whether the defendant possessed a weapon in connection with another felony) are reviewed for clear error. Counsel should identify immediately whether the dispute is legal, factual, or discretionary and preserve appropriate objections.

- Relying on cumulative-error arguments without showing prejudice. Appellate courts will not reverse based on multiple small evidentiary or procedural rulings unless the cumulative effect substantially influenced the verdict or the sentence. The burden of showing substantial prejudice is a high one.

## Applying the framework: lessons from Cutbank

United States v. Cutbank provides a compact illustration of these principles. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings (a procedural act that anchored the Guidelines calculation), applied the enhancements—including the four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony—and sentenced the defendant within the resulting Guidelines range. On appeal, Cutbank challenged evidentiary rulings and the application of the enhancement. The Eighth Circuit applied the Gall/Rosales-Mireles sequencing: it first confirmed that there was no procedural error in calculating the Guidelines range (the PSR was adopted, and the factual findings were not clearly erroneous); it then applied the Eighth Circuit’s presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable and reviewed for substantive abuse of discretion. Because the district court had considered the § 3553(a) factors and the defendant had a serious criminal history and violent conduct supporting a lengthy sentence, the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The case underscores these critical lessons for practitioners and students:

- Early and explicit objections to PSR facts and Guidelines calculations are crucial. Adopting the PSR without a ruling on a timely objection leaves those facts firmly in the record for appellate review.

- Preserve, present, and document § 3553(a) arguments. The Eighth Circuit will assume the district court considered the arguments if the record shows the court reviewed sentencing memoranda and discussed the factors; nevertheless, making the arguments plainly and supporting them with evidence increases the chance of prevailing at sentencing or on appeal.

- Expect appellate deference. In the Eighth Circuit, within-Guidelines sentences enjoy a presumption of reasonableness; overcoming that presumption requires demonstrating either a significant procedural error or that the sentence constitutes a clear abuse of discretion given the § 3553(a) factors.

Conclusion

The post-Booker landscape requires a disciplined, record-oriented approach at sentencing. District courts must calculate the Guidelines correctly, afford the parties the chance to argue for a deviation, and consider the § 3553(a) factors. Appellate courts must ensure the procedural baseline is satisfied before exercising the deferential abuse-of-discretion standard for substantive reasonableness. In the Eighth Circuit, a within-Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable; defendants who wish to challenge such a sentence on appeal need either to show procedural error in the Guidelines calculation or to persuade the appellate court that the sentence was substantively unreasonable in light of the record and the § 3553(a) factors. The Cutbank opinion provides a recent, concrete example of these principles in action.
