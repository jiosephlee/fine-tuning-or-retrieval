Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does the 'relaxed' standing standard for procedural injuries still require a direct causal link between the agency's inaction and the plaintiff's concrete harm?
Question:
The court mentions that when plaintiffs allege injury from a violation of a procedural right, courts 'relax' the requirements of imminence and redressability. However, the opinion states AFL fails even this relaxed standard. Specifically, why doesn't the relaxation allow for a more speculative chain of events? The court rejects AFL's argument that OSC *might* investigate, leading MSPB *might* sanction, leading DOJ *might* release records. Doesn't the 'relaxed' standard mean we don't need to prove that outcome is guaranteed, just probable enough? Why is this chain considered too speculative compared to typical environmental or zoning cases where relief often depends on multiple administrative steps?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Even under the "relaxed" standing standards applicable to procedural injuries, the plaintiff must still demonstrate a plausible causal link between the agency's violation and the specific concrete harm they suffer. The relaxation applies only to the strictness of proving *imminence* and *redressability*; it does not eliminate the requirement that the injury be fairly traceable to the defendant rather than dependent on a chain of speculative future decisions by independent third parties.

**Detailed Explanation**

The confusion stems from conflating "relaxed redressability" with "eliminated causation." While the D.C. Circuit acknowledges that courts relax imminence and redressability requirements when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a statutory procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest (*Center for Law & Education*), this relaxation operates within firm boundaries. As the opinion explicitly states, these relaxations apply "while not wholly eliminating... the issues of injury in fact or causation."

In *America First Legal Foundation*, the court rejected AFL's standing claim precisely because its proposed causal chain relied entirely on speculation regarding the discretionary choices of independent actors, which violates core standing principles regardless of whether procedural rights are at stake.

**1. The Limitation on Speculative Chains**
The court emphasizes that causation cannot rely on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." AFL's argument hinged on a multi-step hypothesis:
1.  If OSC investigated, it *might* pursue corrective or disciplinary action;
2.  If OSC pursued action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) *might* sanction employees;
3.  If sanctioned, DOJ *might* release records.

The court found this chain too attenuated to satisfy Article III. Unlike environmental cases where relief often involves regulating a single entity's ongoing behavior (e.g., halting emissions), AFL sought to force DOJ to act based on a series of potential future enforcement actions by agencies (OSC and MSPB) that are not parties to the suit and possess broad enforcement discretion. The opinion notes that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation," citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*.

**2. Discretion vs. Mandate**
The relaxation standard assumes that forcing an agency to follow procedure will likely lead to a change in outcome or protection of an interest. Here, however, even if OSC were compelled to investigate, there was no guarantee it would exercise its enforcement powers differently than it already had (which was declining action). Furthermore, even if OSC acted, subsequent decisions by non-parties (MSPB sanctions) remained uncertain. The court clarified that redressability requires showing that a favorable judgment would "very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the injury (*Hecate Energy*). Because AFL could not show that its desired result (record production) was likely triggered by correcting OSC's procedure, the "relaxed" standard offered no salvation against an impossible burden of proof regarding causation.

**Conclusion**
The 'relaxed' standard does not permit plaintiffs to bridge gaps in causation with mere possibilities ("might"). It merely lowers the bar for showing that an injury is sufficiently imminent or that relief is likely given existing constraints on agency discretion. When an injury depends entirely on a sequence of discretionary acts by multiple independent entities outside the litigation, as in AFL's case, the causal link remains broken despite any procedural allegations.

### Can a statutory mandate to 'investigate' create a cognizable informational injury if the statute doesn't explicitly grant rights to specific reports or notifications?
Question:
AFL argues that Section 1216 mandates OSC to investigate FOIA violations. They claim an informational injury because they aren't getting updates or results. The court says Section 1216 doesn't create an entitlement to receive information unless it triggers Section 1214 notice requirements. But isn't there a distinction between a right to *initiate* an investigation and a right to *information about* that investigation? If Congress wrote 'shall conduct an investigation,' shouldn't the complainant have a stake in knowing whether that duty was fulfilled? Why does the lack of explicit text granting 'notice rights' defeat the entire claim for informational standing here?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**  
No. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, a statutory command to "investigate" does not automatically create a cognizable informational injury where the statute fails to expressly grant the complainant a right to receive reports, updates, or findings from that investigation. Without an explicit statutory entitlement to information, the alleged harm remains abstract and non-concrete.

**Legal Analysis**  

The core of your confusion lies in conflating a **mandate to act** (the duty to investigate) with a **right to information** (the duty to report). While Section 1216 imposes a mandatory duty on the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to "conduct an investigation," the *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* opinion clarifies that this procedural duty does not inherently generate a corresponding substantive right for the complainant to know the outcome or progress of that investigation.

Under Article III standing principles, particularly regarding informational injuries, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they were denied information that a **"statute entitled him to receive."** *Am. Soc'y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011). The court's analysis hinges entirely on whether Section 1216 contains language creating such an entitlement.

Here is why the lack of explicit text defeats the claim:

1.  **Distinction Between Investigation and Notice Rights:**  
    The court notes that Section 1214 (which governs "prohibited personnel practices") explicitly includes provisions requiring OSC to notify complaining parties about the progress of an investigation (*e.g.*, § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). However, Section 1216—which covers FOIA violations—does not contain similar notification mandates. Although Section 1216(c) allows OSC to seek corrective action under Section 1214 if warranted, it does not make Section 1214's procedural notice requirements automatic for all Section 1216 investigations.

2.  **Discretion vs. Mandate:**  
    AFL attempted to argue that because OSC investigates under Section 1216 using mechanisms from Section 1214, it should be bound by Section 1214's notice rules. The court rejected this because AFL failed to allege that OSC is statutorily compelled to follow those rules in every instance. Instead, AFL acknowledged case law stating that OSC's decision whether to follow Section 1214's procedural requirements in a Section 1216 context is **"discretionary."** *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d at 495–96 (cited in *Jamieson Greer*).

3.  **Procedural Right in Vacuo:**  
    The court reaffirmed the principle from *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest... is insufficient" to confer standing. Because Congress did not write into Section 1216 a specific obligation for OSC to provide updates or final reports to complainants, AFL possesses no "legally protected interest" in receiving such information merely because an investigation was triggered by their complaint.

**Conclusion**  

The distinction you identified—between initiating an investigation and being informed about it—is legally significant precisely because Congress chose not to bridge them in this statute. By omitting explicit notice requirements found in analogous sections like Section 1214, Congress left the flow of information discretionary rather than mandatory for complainants under Section 1216. Consequently, the mere existence of a statutory duty for OSC to investigate does not transform into an individual right for AFL to demand transparency about that process absent express legislative authorization for such disclosure.

### How does the 'Heckler v. Chaney' presumption against judicial review of agency inaction interact with statutes using mandatory language like 'shall investigate'?
Question:
The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, stating courts generally cannot compel agency enforcement discretion. Yet Section 1216(a)(3) uses the word 'shall... conduct an investigation.' Usually, mandatory language ('shall') removes agency discretion regarding whether to act. Does this mean the OSC actually had no choice but to investigate under 1216(a)(3), making their refusal unlawful regardless of discretion? Or does *Heckler* still shield them because even if they must start an investigation, deciding *how* far to go remains discretionary? I'm confused about whether 'mandatory investigation' overrides general prosecutorial discretion.
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
Based on the D.C. Circuit's opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the "mandatory" language of 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) ("shall... conduct an investigation") **does not override** the *Heckler v. Chaney* presumption against judicial review of agency inaction when the relief sought effectively compels enforcement discretion. While the statute mandates that an investigation occur, the court implies that the scope, duration, and ultimate outcome of that investigation remain within OSC's enforcement discretion, which courts cannot compel. Consequently, even if OSC erred in refusing to investigate, a plaintiff likely lacks standing because they cannot demonstrate that a court order would redress their specific injury given the intervening discretionary choices of other agencies (like the MSPB) and OSC's own discretion over how far to take an investigation.

### Detailed Analysis

**1. The Interaction Between Mandatory Language and Discretion**
You correctly identify the tension between statutory commands ("shall") and prosecutorial discretion (*Heckler*). However, *Jamieson Greer* clarifies that even where a statute imposes a duty to act, judicial review is often barred if compelling that action requires reviewing discretionary decisions about *how* or *to what extent* to enforce the law.

The opinion notes that while § 1216(a)(3) states OSC "shall... conduct an investigation," it simultaneously grants OSC broad authority under § 1216(c) to seek corrective or disciplinary action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Crucially, the court points out that OSC's decision regarding whether to follow specific procedural requirements (like those in § 1214) during such an investigation is **discretionary**.

Therefore, a court ordering OSC to "investigate" would inevitably require reviewing how far that investigation goes—a classic area of enforcement discretion shielded by *Heckler*. The opinion suggests that because the statute allows OSC significant leeway in executing its duties (including whether to notify complainants or pursue specific remedies), a mandate to "investigate" does not strip away all discretion; rather, it merely triggers a process where discretion remains paramount regarding the outcome.

**2. Why Standing Fails Regardless of Statutory Mandates**
The primary reason you cannot force an investigation under this opinion is not necessarily a direct conflict between "shall" and *Heckler*, but rather **Article III standing**, specifically **redressability**.

Even assuming arguendo that § 1216(a)(3) leaves no room for OSC to refuse an initial inquiry entirely, the court holds that you cannot obtain standing because:
*   **Speculative Causation:** The chain of events required to redress your injury is too attenuated. An investigation by OSC $\rightarrow$ potential corrective action by MSPB $\rightarrow$ voluntary compliance by DOJ involves multiple independent actors making independent choices.
*   **Discretionary Intervening Acts:** As stated in Section II.B of the opinion, "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Since OSP has discretion over whether to recommend sanctions after an investigation, and MSPB has discretion over whether to impose them, a court order forcing an investigation does not guarantee redress.
*   **No Entitlement to Information:** Even if investigated, there is no statutory entitlement for you to receive specific information or notices unless OSC chooses to treat it as a § 1214 case (which is discretionary). Thus, denying you access doesn't constitute a concrete injury you can litigate away via mandamus.

**Conclusion for Your Exam/Analysis**
*Mandatory language ("shall") does not automatically create justiciable rights.* Under *Jamieson Greer*, when a statute mandates an agency function but delegates significant implementation details (scope of investigation, choice of remedy) to agency discretion, courts will typically find:
1.  The decision falls within *Heckler*'s presumption against review; and/or
2.  Plaintiffs lack standing because they cannot prove that compelling the initial step will reliably lead to redress given the subsequent discretionary steps required by law.

To succeed where AFL failed here, you would need a statute that eliminates all downstream discretion (e.g., "Shall investigate AND shall impose penalty X"), leaving no room for agency interpretation or choice once triggered.

### Is Article III standing defeated solely by the presence of intervening independent actors (like MSPB or DOJ) in the causal chain, even if those actors are not parties?
Question:
The core reason for dismissing AFL's first theory seems to be that redressing their injury requires speculating about what two non-party agencies (MSPB and DOJ) will do after OSC acts. Is this rule absolute? If I sue an agency for failing to enforce a law against another entity, am I automatically barred from standing whenever my desired outcome relies on that other entity changing its behavior voluntarily? How does this differ from cases where plaintiffs successfully challenge regulatory failures that indirectly cause market harms?
Answer:
No, the presence of intervening independent actors does not *automatically* defeat Article III standing in every scenario. However, under *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, standing is defeated when the causal chain linking the defendant's omission to the plaintiff's injury relies on **speculation regarding the "unfettered choices" of multiple independent actors** who are not parties to the suit.

The D.C. Circuit clarified that while intervening actors often appear in regulatory challenges, they become fatal to standing only when their participation breaks the direct link between the defendant's conduct and the redressability of the injury. In *America First*, Judge Katsas rejected AFL's attempt to force OSC to investigate DOJ by highlighting a specific causal sequence requiring three distinct, discretionary decisions:
1.  OSC must decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary action (an exercise of enforcement discretion generally immune from compulsion under *Heckler v. Chaney*).
2.  The Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) must independently decide to sanction DOJ employees based on OSC's referral.
3.  DOJ must voluntarily decide to produce records once sanctioned.

The court held that because this chain depends on "choices by two government agencies not parties," it constitutes impermissible speculation (*FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Med.*). Consequently, a favorable judgment against OSC could not be said to "very likely spur" the necessary steps to redress AFL's injury (*Hecate Energy*).

This distinguishes your hypothetical scenarios as follows:
*   **Direct Regulatory Failure:** If you sue an agency for failing to enforce a law where that agency has direct control over the remedy (e.g., an agency refusing to grant a license it is statutorily required to issue), standing usually exists because the court can order the agency directly to act, without needing intermediate third-party approval.
*   **Indirect Market Harms:** Cases involving indirect market harms often succeed when the challenged regulation directly alters market conditions or imposes costs upon the plaintiff without requiring a separate, voluntary decision by a non-party entity to effectuate relief. For example, if an agency fails to regulate emissions causing health damage, ordering compliance directly addresses the harm; no third party needs to "choose" to stop emitting pollution—it is simply compelled by court order.

In short, *America First* establishes that standing fails not merely because an actor intervenes in time or space, but when that intervention involves **unfettered discretion** exercised by non-parties whose subsequent actions are essential and unpredictable prerequisites for redressability.

### What is the practical difference between being denied a 'procedural right in vacuo' versus having a procedural right attached to a concrete interest in this specific FOIA context?
Question:
"Summers v. Earth Island Inst." says depriving someone of a procedural right without affecting a concrete interest is insufficient for standing ('procedural right in vacuo'). Here, AFL wants records (concrete interest) but claims injury because OSC won't help get them via procedure. The court says their injury isn't traceable to OSC because DOJ controls the records. So, was AFL's mistake trying to use OSC as a lever for DOJ compliance rather than seeking direct relief from DOJ? Did they fail because they sued the wrong defendant (OSC vs DOJ) despite claiming OSC has jurisdiction over FOIA violations?
Answer:
**Answer**

You have correctly identified the core tension in the *America First* decision: AFL attempted to leverage a statutory duty owed by the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to force compliance from a different agency, the Department of Justice (DOJ). However, the D.C. Circuit did not dismiss the case simply because AFL sued the "wrong" defendant in terms of name; rather, it held that AFL failed to demonstrate **standing** because its claimed injury lacked both **traceability** and **redressability**.

Here is the breakdown of why AFL's attempt to treat OSC as a "lever" failed under *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* and the specific reasoning in *America First*.

### 1. The Failure of Traceability: The Injury Belongs to DOJ, Not OSC
Under *Lujan*, a plaintiff must show that their injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct. The court explained that while AFL has a concrete interest in obtaining records from DOJ (a valid "concrete interest"), any failure to obtain those records is caused solely by DOJ's withholding policies, not OSC's refusal to investigate.

The court rejected AFL's argument that OSC's inaction caused the injury because doing so would require accepting a chain of speculation:
*   If OSC investigated...
*   ...and decided to seek corrective action...
*   ...and if the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) sanctioned employees...
*   ...then DOJ might finally release the records.

The court cited recent precedent (*FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Med.*) stating that causation cannot rely on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Since DOJ and MSPB are not parties, and OSC's enforcement decisions are discretionary (*Heckler v. Chaney*), there is no logical link between suing OSC and getting records from DOJ. Therefore, AFL could not prove that its injury (lack of records) was caused by OSC.

### 2. Why This Is Not Just "Procedural Right in Vacuo"
You asked if this distinction matters regarding *Summers*. In *Summers*, denying a procedural right without affecting a concrete interest is insufficient standing ("procedural right in vacuo"). Here, AFL actually **did** assert a concrete interest (the records). However, they failed because they tried to attach that concrete interest to a procedural right held against the wrong entity.

The court clarified that even when relaxing standing rules for procedural injuries where a statute protects a concrete interest (*Center for Law & Education*), two hurdles remain:
1.  **Causation:** The procedural violation must result in injury to that concrete interest. (It didn't here; DOJ caused it).
2.  **Redressability:** A favorable judgment must likely remedy the injury. Ordering OSC to investigate would not guarantee DOJ releases documents because OSC lacks direct authority over DOJ's record-keeping or disclosure decisions in this context.

### 3. Was It Suing the Wrong Defendant?
Technically, yes, but legally speaking, it was an error of **jurisdiction**, not just pleading strategy.

AFL sought relief from OSC based on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), arguing OSC had a mandatory duty to investigate FOIA violations regardless of whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s specific triggers were met. While the district court found this interpretation plausible on the merits, it dismissed anyway due to standing issues raised sua sponte by the appellate court upon ordering supplemental briefing.

The D.C. Circuit noted that even if § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not create an entitlement for AFL to receive information directly from OSC regarding that investigation unless § 1214 notice requirements apply—which they do not automatically (§ 1216 investigations are distinct from § 1214 proceedings). Consequently:
*   **Suing for Records:** You must sue DOE/DOJ directly under FOIA (which AFL already did).
*   **Suing for Investigation:** You can sue OSC only if you have an injury caused by their failure to investigate *that results in harm*. Since AFL couldn't show how forcing an investigation would fix their lack of records (due to the speculative chain described above), they had no standing against OSC either.

### Conclusion
AFL did not lose merely because they sued OSC instead of DOJ; they lost because they could not connect their desire for documents (concrete interest) to OSC's actions in a way that satisfies Article III standing requirements (*Lujan*). They effectively tried to use a procedural mechanism intended for civil service discipline as a backdoor method to enforce FOIA disclosure against an agency with no direct obligation to comply with an investigation order initiated by them personally against another executive branch entity without judicial oversight of that specific link first occurring elsewhere (*Ames First Legal Found.* at II.B).

To succeed procedurally here, one would need to show either:
1.  That § 1216 explicitly grants plaintiffs access to investigation files creating an immediate informational injury; or
2.  That there is no intervening step between OSC's investigation and DOJ's disclosure making causation non-speculative (which doesn't exist given MSPB involvement).

### If Section 1216(c) allows OSC to act under sections 1214/1215 "in the same way," does that implicitly incorporate all Section 1214 notification requirements into Section 1216 investigations?
Question:
The opinion notes that under Section 1216(c), OSC can seek corrective action "in the same way" as prohibited personnel practices under Section 1214. Section 1214 includes specific notice requirements (e.g., informing complainants of progress). The Sixth Circuit (*Carson*) held OSC has discretion on whether to follow these notices during Section 1216 investigations. Does this conflict with D.C. Circuit precedent on statutory interpretation where "same way" usually means identical procedures apply unless specified otherwise? Is it possible Congress intended full parity in procedure but courts have narrowed it due to resource constraints or separation of powers concerns?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No. While 5 U.S.C. § 1216(c) authorizes the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to seek corrective action "in the same way" as prohibited personnel practices, the *America First Legal Foundation* opinion confirms that this phrase does **not** automatically incorporate the specific notification requirements of § 1214 into § 1216 investigations. The D.C. Circuit explicitly recognized Sixth Circuit precedent (*Carson*) holding that OSC retains discretion over whether to follow § 1214's procedural rules when acting under § 1216. Consequently, a plaintiff cannot claim a concrete, particularized injury based solely on the denial of § 1214-style notices during a § 1216 investigation because no statutory entitlement to those specific notices exists in that context.

### Detailed Analysis

**Statutory Interpretation and Procedural Parity**
The core of your question lies in the interaction between § 1216(c)'s "in the same way" language and the distinct procedural mandates of § 1214. Section 1216(a)(3) directs OSC to investigate allegations regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings and permits it to seek corrective action "under section 1214... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved."

However, the *America First* opinion clarifies that this statutory cross-reference governs the *substantive scope* of OSC's authority (i.e., what actions they can take), not necessarily every procedural step attached to those actions. The court noted that while OSC may use § 1214 mechanisms, it is not compelled to adopt all ancillary procedures found exclusively within § 1214 unless expressly stated. Specifically, the opinion references *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), which holds that OSC's decision to follow § 1214's procedural requirements—including notice provisions—in a § 1216 investigation remains discretionary.

**Standing Consequences of Discretionary Procedures**
The practical implication of this interpretation is critical for litigants seeking judicial review based on procedural violations. Under Article III standing doctrine, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury-in-fact resulting from a deprivation of a legally protected interest (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*).

In *America First*, AFL attempted to argue that its refusal to receive notices required under § 1214 constituted a concrete informational injury during a § 1216 investigation. The D.C. Circuit rejected this argument precisely because of the discretionary nature identified above:
> "[AFL] does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section [ ] investigations... [It] acknowledges cases holding that OSC's 'decision whether to follow section [ ]'s procedural requirements' in a section [ ] investigation is 'discretionary.'"

Because there is no statutory mandate forcing OSC to provide these specific notifications during a § 1206 inquiry, AFL lacked an "alleged entitlement" to such information (*Am. Soc'y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*). Without an entitlement created by statute, there can be no cognizable injury from its absence, regardless of whether Congress intended "full parity" in procedure elsewhere in the text or if resource constraints influenced judicial deference to agency discretion (*Heckler v. Chaney*).

**Conclusion**
The *America First* opinion resolves your query by confirming that "in the same way" grants OSC broad enforcement authority without rigidly importing all administrative protocols from one section into another absent explicit congressional command regarding procedure. Therefore, Section 1205 does not implicitly incorporate Section 550 notification requirements into Section [ ] investigations; rather, courts continue to treat those specific procedural safeguards as discretionary matters left to the agency's judgment in non-prohibited personnel practice contexts.
