Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Chapter 1: Statutory Framework and Procedural History

To understand the appellate dispute in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, one must first navigate the complex statutory architecture governing federal whistleblower protections and freedom of information. The case centers on the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), an independent investigative and prosecutorial agency created by the Civil Service Reform Act to ensure a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The OSC possesses broad authority to investigate "prohibited personnel practices" by federal employees. When such practices are found, the OSC can initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to seek corrective actions under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary actions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. These sections establish detailed procedural safeguards, including specific requirements for notifying complaining parties about the progress of an investigation.

The crux of this case lies in how these statutes interact with the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). While FOIA provides its own mechanism for enforcing disclosure rights—including a specific provision where a court finding that an agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding information triggers a mandatory OSC proceeding under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—Congress also embedded FOIA-related oversight within the Civil Service Reform Act itself. Specifically, 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) directs that the OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" of allegations concerning "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." Furthermore, subsection (c) clarifies that for such alleged FOIA violations, the OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved," effectively granting it access to the enforcement tools of Sections 1214 and 1215 while maintaining its distinct investigative mandate over FOIA non-compliance.

The factual background involves America First Legal Foundation (AFL), which sought emails between an outside attorney and the Department of Justice's (DOJ) Civil Division regarding judicial recusals. The DOJ denied this request on grounds that it does not search employee emails without consent unless authorized by statute or regulation. AFL subsequently filed a direct lawsuit against the DOJ under FOIA; during this litigation, the DOJ began producing responsive records, rendering the scope of that production uncontested in this appeal. However, AFL simultaneously pursued a parallel track by requesting that the OSC investigate what it characterized as DOJ's arbitrary FOIA policy regarding email searches. Invoking Section 1216(a)(3), AFL argued that this provision imposed a mandatory duty on the OSC to investigate such withholdings regardless of whether a prior judicial determination existed under Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

The OSC rejected AFL's request for an investigation, asserting that it lacked authority because none of the three specific predicates required by Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—a court order for production, an award of attorneys' fees, and a judicial finding of arbitrary conduct—had been met. AFL countered that Section 1216 created an independent obligation to investigate FOIA violations irrespective of Section 552's narrower trigger conditions. This disagreement formed the basis of AFL's lawsuit against the OSC in district court. The district court partially agreed with AFL's statutory interpretation, ruling that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit the OSC's investigatory power under Section 1216; rather, it concluded that failing to investigate constituted a legal error on behalf of the OSC. However, despite finding this legal error, the district court refused to compel an investigation at that time because Section 1216(c) appeared to commit enforcement decisions to the agency's discretion. Instead, it remanded the matter back to the OSC for reconsideration consistent with its interpretation of mandatory authority.

Following this remand, once again declined to launch an investigation into AFL's allegations regarding DOJ's email policy. Consequently, AFL moved for entry of final judgment based on this renewed refusal. The district court granted this motion and dismissed all remaining claims against both agencies for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. This dismissal set the stage for AFL's appeal to the D.C. Circuit. On appeal, while reviewing de novo whether OSD had correctly interpreted its statutory obligations regarding mandatory investigations versus discretionary enforcement powers, neither party initially raised Article III standing issues until ordered by this Court to address them separately as a threshold jurisdictional question affecting subject-matter jurisdiction over merit-based claims like those involving statutory interpretation and administrative discretion.

# Chapter 2: The Threshold Requirement of Article III Standing

## Introduction: The Constitutional Barrier to Adjudication

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit faced a dispute centering on the statutory obligations of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) regarding Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests. However, before the appellate court could address the complex question of whether the Civil Service Reform Act mandated OSC to investigate specific FOIA allegations, it paused to address a fundamental constitutional threshold. The court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing to sue. This decision illustrates a critical principle in administrative law: standing is not merely a pleading requirement but a jurisdictional bar rooted in the Constitution itself. Under Article III, federal courts possess "judicial Power" limited strictly to resolving actual "Cases" and "Controversies." Consequently, if a plaintiff fails to demonstrate standing, the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction and must dismiss the case regardless of how meritorious its arguments on the merits might appear. In *Jamieson Greer*, this jurisdictional inquiry superseded all subsequent analysis of statutory interpretation or agency discretion.

## The Three-Prong Test for Standing

To survive this threshold inquiry, a plaintiff must satisfy three distinct elements derived from Supreme Court precedent: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. First, the plaintiff must have suffered an injury that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent," rather than conjectural or hypothetical. This injury must constitute an invasion of a legally protected interest. Second, there must be a causal connection between the injury and the conduct complained of; essentially, the injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's actions. Third, it must be "likely," not merely speculative, that a favorable judicial decision will redress the injury. In *Jamieson Greer*, AFL attempted to frame its grievance around these prongs by alleging two specific injuries: first, that it never received the records it sought via FOIA from the Department of Justice (DOJ); and second, that OSC failed to provide it with notices and information associated with an investigation into DOJ's conduct. While AFL argued these constituted concrete informational injuries, the court found both theories collapsed under scrutiny due to failures in causation and redressability.

## Procedural Rights vs. Substantive Interests

A central complication in *Jamieson Greer* arose from AFL's reliance on alleged procedural rights granted by statute rather than direct substantive harms. The court explained that while statutes can create new legal interests worthy of protection, they cannot transform abstract grievances into justiciable injuries on their own. Specifically, "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer standing. To succeed where a plaintiff alleges a procedural violation, they must still demonstrate that this violation resulted in harm to some underlying concrete interest. Furthermore, courts often apply relaxed standards for imminence and redressability when procedural rights are at stake; however, these relaxations do not eliminate the strict requirements for injury-in-fact or causation. AFL failed because it could not link OSC's refusal to investigate either to a tangible loss (like obtaining documents) or establish that forcing an investigation would guarantee relief for those losses. Thus, even though AFL claimed Congress intended for OSC to act procedurally upon FOIA complaints, those procedural mandates did not automatically generate standing absent proof of concrete downstream effects on AFL's interests.

## Causation and Redressability: Breaking Speculative Chains

The most rigorous application of standing doctrine in *Jamieson Greer* concerned the requirements of causation and redressability regarding AFL's claim that it wanted DOJ records released through FOIA. The court rejected AFL's argument because establishing liability would require relying on a long chain of speculation involving multiple independent actors beyond OSC's control. For OSC's failure to cause AFL's lack of access to records, one would have to assume: (1) OSC decides to pursue corrective or disciplinary action against DOJ employees; (2) The Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) subsequently sanctions those employees; and (3) DOJ then produces the requested records as a result of those sanctions. The court emphasized that causation cannot rest on speculation regarding "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Since both MSPB decisions and future DOJ compliance involve discretionary judgments by entities not bound by this litigation's outcome, any causal link between ordering an OSC investigation now and getting DOJ records later was too attenuated to satisfy Article III requirements similarly held in recent precedents like *FDA v Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. Additionally, redressability generally depends on how a judgment binds parties directly present in court; since an order compelling OSC cannot force MSPB or DOJ to act differently than they otherwise would choose independently, any potential benefit remains speculative rather than likely enough to cure AFP's alleged injury.

Similarly flawed was AFL's second theory—that it deserved informational updates from OSC during an investigation—even if such updates were technically required under different statutes governing prohibited personnel practices (*section 1214*). Here too stood broken links between statute obligation ("shall investigate") versus entitlement ("must inform"). # Chapter 3: Relaxed Standards for Procedural Injury Claims

When a plaintiff challenges an administrative agency's failure to follow a statutory procedure, the Supreme Court and lower courts often apply a modified version of the Article III standing analysis. This modification recognizes that denying a specific procedural step can sometimes cause harm distinct from the ultimate substantive outcome. However, this relaxation is not a free pass; it operates within strict boundaries designed to prevent litigants from suing merely because they dislike an agency's process without suffering actual damage. The opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* illustrates both the mechanics of this "relaxed" standard and its limits, demonstrating why a plaintiff must still prove that a procedural violation resulted in a concrete injury to a legally protected interest.

### 'Procedial Right in Vacuo': Why Violating a Procedure Alone Is Insufficient

The foundational premise of modern standing doctrine is that the mere deprivation of a procedural right, without more, does not constitute an Article III injury-in-fact. As the D.C. Circuit reiterated in *Jamieson Greer*, citing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer standing. This concept serves as a critical gatekeeper: Congress may grant citizens rights to participate in administrative processes or demand information, but those statutory grants do not automatically translate into constitutional standing unless the citizen can demonstrate that the loss of that procedure harmed them in a tangible way.

In *Jamieson Greer*, America First Legal Foundation (AFL) attempted to bypass this barrier by arguing that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) violated its statutory duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) to investigate alleged FOIA violations. AFL claimed this statutory mandate created an enforceable procedural right to an investigation. The court rejected the notion that this isolated denial constituted an injury sufficient for federal jurisdiction. Without linking the missed investigation to a specific, concrete harm suffered by AFL, the alleged violation remained "in vacuo." The court emphasized that standing requires an invasion of a "legally protected interest" that is "concrete and particularized," meaning the plaintiff must look beyond the broken rule itself and identify how they were actually hurt by it.

### The Relaxation Rule: How Courts Treat Imminence and Redressability in Procedural Injury Cases

While courts refuse to recognize bare procedural violations as standalone injuries, they acknowledge that proving injury becomes difficult when the harm stems from a future event or an indirect consequence of agency inaction. Consequently, where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from the violation of a procedural right afforded by statute—and where that procedure was designed specifically to protect their threatened concrete interests—courts apply a relaxed standard regarding two elements of standing: imminence and redressability.

As explained in *Center for Law & Education v. Dep't of Educ.*, cited extensively in *Jamieson Greer*, these relaxed requirements do not eliminate the core necessity of establishing injury-in-fact or causation; they simply ease the burden regarding timing and likelihood of remedy. In traditional substantive cases, plaintiffs must show their injury is "imminent" and that their victory will "very likely" redress it. However, when dealing with procedural rights like access to information or participation in decision-making, courts accept that waiting for the final substantive outcome might be too late or impossible if the procedure itself was denied upfront. Similarly, redressability is viewed more leniently because ordering an agency to perform its missing procedure may naturally lead to different outcomes than if it had been performed originally, even if those outcomes are not guaranteed immediately upon judgment. This framework allows courts to police agency compliance with congressional mandates without requiring plaintiffs to wait until irreversible harm occurs downstream.

### The Persistent Burden: Proving That A Procedural Violation Injured A Concrete, Particularized Interest

Despite this relaxation on imminence and redressability, the burden remains heavy on plaintiffs to demonstrate that their concrete interest was actually impaired by the procedural lapse. The D.C. Circuit clarified in *Jamieson Greer* that AFL failed even under this more forgiving standard because it could not bridge the gap between OSC's refusal to act and any actual harm suffered by AFL itself. The court identified two primary theories advanced by AFL but found both lacking because they relied on speculation rather than proven causation or entitlements.

First, AFL argued that OSC's failure to investigate caused its inability to obtain records from another agency (DOJ). The court dismantled this theory using strict causation principles applicable even under relaxed standards. Even assuming OSC should have investigated, there was no guarantee OSC would have pursued corrective action against DOJ employees via the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), nor was there certainty that such action would force DOJ to release documents immediately or ever release them at all since DOJ had already begun producing records voluntarily during parallel litigation. The chain of events required multiple speculative leaps involving independent actors (OSC and MSPB) whose discretionary choices were not before the court (*Heckler v. Chaney*). Because AFL could not show that its injury was fairly traceable to OSC's omission or likely redressed by compelling OSC to act later, this claim failed regardless of whether it qualified as procedural relief sought under relaxed rules.

Second, AFL contended it suffered informational injury because OSC refused to provide notices typically associated with investigations under section 1214 (which applies when section 1216 triggers similar procedures). Here too, AFL stumbled over its own admission: it acknowledged that following section 1214 notice requirements during section 1216 investigations is discretionary for OSC (*Carson v. U.S.*). Since there was no statutory entitlement granting AFL these specific notices during a section 1216 inquiry, there was no concrete interest invaded by their absence. Furthermore, because OSC retained discretion over whether or how much information to share even after being ordered back into action (via remand), compelling OSC now would not necessarily result in AFL receiving what it wanted today ("redressability"). Thus, despite invoking procedural protections, AFL could not prove it possessed a concrete stake affected by OSC's choice not to engage with section 1214 protocols during its section 1216 analysis.

### Application To AFL's Claim That Section 207 Created An Enforceable Procedural Right

Ultimately, *Jamieson Greer* demonstrates that asserting Congress granted you a procedural right does not automatically solve your Article III problems; you must still connect that right violation to your own personal stake in the matter's outcome or information flow. While section 1216(a)(3) uses mandatory language ("shall... conduct an investigation"), creating what might appear as an absolute duty for OSC does not transform every disappointed requester into an Article III plaintiff capable of enforcing it through litigation absent proof of specific harm derived from non-compliance. The court noted explicitly that because AFL could not satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements designed for procedural injuries—specifically failing on both causation/redressability regarding record disclosure and failing on entitlement/concreteness regarding investigative notices—it lacked standing entirely on either theory whether characterized as substantive or procedural (*Amerson* distinction). By dismissing on standing grounds alone before reaching whether section 552 limited section 1216 authority regarding mandatory investigations (*Kerner*), the D.C. Circuit reinforced that jurisdictional thresholds remain paramount; no amount of statutory interpretation regarding agency duties can cure a fundamental lack of constitutional standing rooted in unproven injury and speculative remedies (*Steel Co.*).

# Chapter 4: Failure of Redressability Theory I – The Chain of Speculation

Having established the basic elements of Article III standing—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—the D.C. Circuit in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* turned to the specific theories proposed by the plaintiff to demonstrate that a favorable ruling would remedy its harm. While the court acknowledged that statutory procedural rights can sometimes trigger "relaxed" standing requirements regarding imminence and redressability, AFL failed to meet even this lower bar. This chapter focuses on the court's rejection of AFL's primary theory: that compelling the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice (DOJ) would ultimately force DOJ to release the withheld records. The court dismantled this argument by demonstrating that the causal link between a court order against OSC and DOJ's eventual disclosure was too attenuated, relying entirely on speculation regarding the independent choices of non-parties and unreviewable executive discretion.

The core flaw in AFL's first standing theory lay in what the court termed a "fatal causation and redressability problem." AFL posited that if OSC were forced to conduct an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), it could subsequently initiate corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215 against DOJ employees. This sequence would theoretically lead to sanctions by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which would then pressure or require DOJ to produce the requested documents. However, this narrative relies on a multi-step chain of events where every subsequent step depends on discretionary choices made by entities not currently before the court. Specifically, the chain requires speculation that OSC would choose to pursue enforcement rather than close its file; speculation that the MSPB would agree with OSC and impose sanctions; and finally, speculation that DOJ would alter its behavior solely due to those sanctions. The court emphasized that Article III standing cannot rest on such a fragile foundation where "any informational injury... is plainly traceable to DOJ," not OSC. Because the immediate source of injury was DOJ's refusal to search emails, attributing a remedy to OSC's potential investigation severed the direct line of causation required by constitutional limits on judicial power.

Central to this rejection is the "independent actor" doctrine, which prevents plaintiffs from establishing standing based on predictions about how third parties will act absent a direct legal obligation binding them in the current litigation. The D.C. Circuit cited *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, noting that causation "generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Here, both MSPB and DOJ are independent actors whose decisions are not controlled by a judgment ordering OSC to investigate alone. A federal court cannot assume that an order directed at one agency will inevitably result in action by another agency unless there is a clear statutory mandate linking them directly in response to that specific order. By requiring AFL to speculate on how MSPB might rule or how DOJ might respond post-sanction, AFL attempted to bootstrap standing upon outcomes that remain entirely uncertain and outside the control of either plaintiff or defendant in this suit. The court clarified that redressability turns on whether a judgment binds parties effectively; it does not suffice if a judgment merely serves as persuasive rhetoric intended to convince unrelated third parties to change their course of action voluntarily.

Furthermore, even if one were willing to entertain speculation about third-party reactions, AFL's theory collapsed under *Heckler v. Chaney* principles regarding executive enforcement discretion. *Heckler* establishes a strong presumption against judicial review of an agency's decision not to take enforcement action unless Congress has explicitly limited that discretion with meaningful standards applicable here. In this context, even assuming OSC had a duty under § 1216(a)(3) to investigate once triggered, it retained broad discretion over *how* it enforced its findings—specifically whether to bring corrective actions under § 1214 or disciplinary actions under § 1215 after an investigation concluded. The court noted that courts generally may not compel an agency like OSC to exercise its enforcement powers in a particular manner when those powers are inherently discretionary. Therefore, asserting that an investigation *must* lead to sanctions ignores the reality that OSC could investigate thoroughly and still decline any further action based on its own assessment of resources or priorities. Without a guarantee that an investigation leads toward enforcement, let alone towards specific sanctions against DOJ employees, AFL could not prove that obtaining an investigation would make it "very likely" (*Hecate Energy LLC*) that they would receive their records.

Brackeen*, that redressability requires showing that a favorable decision will likely spur steps necessary to correct the injury through binding obligations on litigants before them or their agents # Chapter 5: Failure of Redressability Theory II – Lack of Entitlement to Information

Having rejected the plaintiff's first attempt to establish standing by linking the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) inaction to the ultimate release of documents from the Department of Justice, the D.C. Circuit turned to AFL's second theory of injury. This theory posited that AFL suffered a concrete, informational harm simply because OSC refused to conduct an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Under this framework, AFL argued that the denial of an investigation deprived it of specific "notices and information" that it believed it was owed. While this approach attempts to fit within the category of procedural injury where courts sometimes relax redressability requirements, the court found that this theory collapsed at the threshold because AFL could not demonstrate a statutory entitlement to the information it sought. Without such an entitlement, there was no "concrete interest" invaded, rendering the claim non-justiciable under Article III.

To understand why this theory failed, one must first grasp the baseline requirements for "informational standing" within the D.C. Circuit. As established in precedents like *FEC v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. Department of Justice*, a plaintiff alleging an informational injury must show that they were statutorily entitled to receive specific information and were denied access to it by the defendant's unlawful conduct. The Supreme Court and circuit courts have long held that a mere desire for information is insufficient; there must be a "legal right" to that data created by Congress. In *American Society for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Entertainment*, Inc., the court clarified that a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury only if they are denied information that "a statute entitled him to receive." Consequently, for AFL to succeed on its second theory, it needed to identify a specific statutory command requiring OSC to provide it with reports or updates during a § 1216 investigation into FOIA violations.

The text reveals a critical statutory gap in AFL's argument: while § 1216 mandates that OSC *conduct* an investigation into arbitrary FOIA withholdings, it does not explicitly grant complainants a right to receive notifications or investigatory reports during that process. The court noted that AFL attempted to bridge this gap by arguing that if OSC investigates under § 1216, it might voluntarily adopt the procedural safeguards applicable when investigating "prohibited personnel practices" under § 1214. Section 1214 does indeed contain detailed provisions requiring agencies and boards to notify complaining parties about the progress of an investigation (see 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). However, § 1216(c) merely states that OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." It does not automatically import every procedural notification requirement from § 1214 into § 1216 proceedings as a matter of right for the complainant.

The decisive blow to AFL's second theory came from recognizing the discretionary nature of applying § 1214 procedures in § 1216 contexts. The court pointed out that AFL itself acknowledged existing case law—specifically *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*—which holds that OSC possesses broad discretion in deciding whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements when conducting a § 1216 investigation. Because OSC is not compelled by statute or binding precedent to provide section-specific notices in every § 1216 inquiry, AFL could not claim a concrete interest in receiving such information absent actual notice being promised or granted by statute. Since no statute entitles AFL to these specific investigative updates during a § 1216 probe, there is no "legally protected interest" that has been invaded by OSC's silence or refusal to act beyond initiating an investigation. Without this foundational entitlement, the alleged deprivation remains abstract rather than concrete, failing even the most relaxed tests for procedural injury standing described in *Center for Law & Education*.

Finally, because no concrete injury existed due to the lack of statutory entitlement, the question of redressability became moot regarding this specific claim; however, even assuming arguendo that an injury existed, redressability would still fail. The court reasoned that ordering OSC to conduct an investigation would not guarantee relief because OSC retains discretion over whether and how much information to share with AFL during said investigation. A judicial order compelling an investigation does not bind OSC to provide specific reports or notices unless those reporting duties are themselves mandatory under law—which they are not in this context. Therefore, since AFL lacked both a concrete interest (due to no statutory entitlement) and redressability (because compliance with any potential order would remain discretionary), its second theory offered no basis for Article III standing. This reinforced the conclusion that federal courts cannot adjudicate claims based on generalized grievances or expectations unsupported by clear legislative commands granting private rights of action or receipt of information.
