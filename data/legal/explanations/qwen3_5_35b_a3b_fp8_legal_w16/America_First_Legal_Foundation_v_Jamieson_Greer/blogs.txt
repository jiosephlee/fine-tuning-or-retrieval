# The Speculative Chain: Why Procedural Rights Don't Confer Standing Without Concrete Harm

**By [Your Name/Analyst]**

The tension between statutory mandates and Article III limits has long been a fertile ground for litigation, but the D.C. Circuit's October 3 decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* draws a particularly sharp line in the sand. While the case nominally concerns the scope of the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) authority to investigate Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations, its true significance lies in how rigorously the court applied recent Supreme Court jurisprudence to sever the link between a procedural grievance and a concrete injury. By invoking *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*, Chief Judge Katsas's panel demonstrates that a plaintiff cannot manufacture standing simply by pointing to a statutory command they believe was ignored; they must still prove that ignoring that command actually hurt them in a way the court can fix.

At its core, this dispute arose from AFL's attempt to force OSC to investigate the Department of Justice's refusal to release specific emails regarding judicial recusal protocols. AFL argued that 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) created a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate "arbitrary or capricious" FOIA withholdings, regardless of whether the specific triggers in FOIA's own enforcement clause (§ 552(a)(4)(F)) were met. When OSC declined to act, citing those narrower statutory triggers, AFL sued. Although the district court acknowledged OSC might have erred on the merits, it ultimately dismissed the case because ordering an investigation remained within OSC's discretion under § 1216(c). On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the merits entirely, concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing from the outset.

The court's reasoning serves as a potent reminder that "procedural rights in vacuo" are insufficient for standing. AFL advanced two theories of injury: first, that OSC's failure to investigate prevented DOJ from releasing records; and second, that AFL was denied specific informational outputs generated during an investigation under § 1214 standards. Both theories collapsed under scrutiny regarding causation and redressability—a double whammy driven by what Judge Katsas terms a "speculative chain." Regarding the first theory, any delay in receiving documents is traceable directly to DOJ, not OSC. To find standing here would require assuming a complex cascade of future events: that OSC would have chosen to prosecute after an investigation; that the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have sanctioned DOJ employees; and finally, that DOJ would have complied once sanctioned. The court rightly noted that causation cannot rest on "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts," especially when those choices involve enforcement discretion which courts traditionally hesitate to compel under *Heckler v. Chaney*.

This analysis gains additional weight when viewed alongside last term's *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. Just as the Supreme Court there demanded a tighter nexus between agency action and injury-in-fact, Judge Katsas insists that redressability must turn on how a judgment binds parties present in court, rather than relying on persuasive rationales for non-parties like MSPB or DOJ. Similarly, under *Haaland*, speculative chains are insufficient even where statutes create broad duties. The rejection of AFL's second theory reinforces this strictness: even if § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not automatically grant AFL access to investigation reports or notices unless those rights are explicitly conferred by statute or regulation—which they are not in this context. Consequently, ordering an investigation would not necessarily yield any new information for AFL, failing both concreteness and redressability tests simultaneously.

Doctrinally, *Jamieson Greer* signals a continued tightening of procedural standing doctrines across administrative law circles. Courts appear increasingly unwilling to allow litigants to use statutory interpretation battles as backdoors into Article III jurisdiction when no tangible harm flows from the alleged violation of procedure alone. This approach protects agencies from being dragged into litigation over every perceived procedural misstep where the ultimate remedy remains speculative or dependent on third-party whimsy. For advocates seeking judicial oversight of agency non-enforcement or procedural failures, this ruling suggests that merely identifying a broken statutory promise is no longer enough; one must demonstrate exactly how that break concretely injured them and how fixing it will immediately cure that injury without requiring guesswork about what other government actors might do next.

Ultimately, while AFL may feel aggrieved by OSC's refusal to intervene in their FOIA struggle against DOJ, *Jamieson Greer* underscores a fundamental structural reality: federal courts are limited tribunals empowered only to resolve actual controversies where their judgments matter concretely today, not tomorrow via a chain reaction of discretionary choices elsewhere in government bureaucracy.



# Investigation vs. Information: The Limits of 'Informational Injury' in Administrative Law

**By [Your Name/Persona]** | *October 5, 2025*

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, handed down on October 3, offers a stark reminder that the judiciary remains the gatekeeper of administrative overreach, particularly when plaintiffs attempt to weaponize "informational standing." While the case technically involves the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and its duty to investigate Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations, the underlying legal victory belongs to the principle that a statutory right to trigger an investigation does not automatically create a cognizable injury when that investigation yields no tangible results or disclosures. By dismissing AFL's suit for lack of Article III standing, the court effectively drew a line between the procedural machinery of government accountability and the concrete interests required to access federal courts.

The factual backdrop is straightforward but procedurally complex. AFL sought emails regarding judicial recusals from the Department of Justice (DOJ). When DOJ delayed production citing privacy concerns, AFL turned to two avenues: suing DOJ directly under FOIA—which resulted in ongoing compliance—and petitioning the OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged arbitrary denial policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). That statute mandates OSC to investigate allegations of "arbitrary or capricious withholding" of information under FOIA. However, OSC declined to launch an investigation, arguing that its mandatory duty only triggers after a court has already found an arbitrary withholding and ordered disclosure—a condition precedent AFL had not met in this specific context. Even though the district court suggested OSC might have broader discretion than it claimed, it ultimately dismissed AFL's claims because ordering an investigation would commit discretionary enforcement powers to the executive branch. On appeal, Judge Katsas and the full panel sidestepped these merits entirely by focusing on standing.

The crux of today's analysis lies in how the D.C. Circuit dismantled AFL's second theory of injury: that being denied participation in or updates from an OSC investigation constitutes a concrete harm. AFL argued that if OSC were conducting a proper investigation under § 1216, it would be obligated to provide notice and information akin to those required under § 1214 for "prohibited personnel practices." The court rejected this premise with surgical precision. It noted that while § 1216 directs OSC to *investigate* FOIA violations, it does not explicitly mandate that OSC adopt § 1214's notification protocols during such inquiries. Indeed, existing precedent recognizes that OSC retains broad discretion over whether to graft those procedural requirements onto § 1216 investigations. Without a clear congressional command granting AFL a right to receive specific data *about* the investigation itself, there is no "legal interest" invaded by OSC's silence.

This distinction is vital for administrative law doctrine moving forward. For years, litigants have tested the boundaries of what constitutes an "informational injury," often seeking to transform mere frustration with bureaucratic opacity into justiciable harm. The Supreme Court has long held that denying information can constitute injury where a statute entitles a plaintiff to receive it (*Akins*), but *Jamieson Greer* reinforces that this entitlement must be explicit regarding the *output* demanded by the plaintiff, not just the *process* initiated by them. AFL wanted information; they could get it via FOIA against DOJ or potentially via discovery if they won their underlying case against DOJ. They could not get it simply because they wanted an outsider's view into an internal enforcement decision-making process where Congress left discretion intact. The court correctly identified that allowing standing here would effectively grant private citizens a veto over agency enforcement priorities disguised as a right to information.

Furthermore, this ruling highlights a critical separation-of-powers boundary regarding prosecutorial discretion. The opinion reiterates that courts cannot compel agencies to enforce laws in ways Congress did not mandate (*Heckler v. Chaney*). If courts allowed plaintiffs like AFL to sue based on their exclusion from an investigative process where no specific output was guaranteed by statute, they would inevitably be drawn into micromanaging agency resources and priorities. By requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate that they are owed specific information rather than just asserting an interest in general oversight, *Jamieson Greer* preserves the executive branch's ability to prioritize cases without fear of litigation from every aggrieved requester who feels ignored during preliminary reviews.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a cautionary tale for organizations hoping to use administrative statutes as backdoor mechanisms for forced disclosure or oversight beyond what Congress intended. The D.C. Circuit clarified that while agencies must follow statutory commands when they exist, plaintiffs cannot conjure rights out of thin air by assuming that every statutory duty implies a corresponding right for third parties to know everything about how those duties are executed—or even whether they are executed at all—absent explicit legislative direction. In doing so, the court reaffirmed that Article III standing remains a robust barrier against lawsuits driven by generalized grievances wrapped in procedural language.



# Enforcement Discretion and the Wall of Article III: Reaffirming *Heckler v. Chaney*

**By [Your Name]** | October 4, 2025

In the intricate dance between statutory mandates and constitutional limits, the D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that "shall" does not always mean "must," particularly when Article III stands guard at the courthouse door. Decided last week by Judge Neomi Rao's colleague, Judge Patrick Katsas, the panel held that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacked standing to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate allegations that the Department of Justice (DOJ) arbitrarily denied a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the underlying dispute involves complex statutory interpretation regarding civil service reform and transparency, the core holding rests on a fundamental separation-of-powers principle: federal courts cannot compel executive agencies to exercise their enforcement discretion, even when Congress uses seemingly mandatory language in a statute.

The factual backdrop is straightforward but procedurally tangled. AFL sought emails from DOJ regarding judicial recusals and was told they weren't searched without employee consent—a policy AFL challenged under FOIA. Simultaneously, AFL asked OSC to investigate this denial under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding." When OSC refused, citing that specific FOIA enforcement triggers hadn't been met elsewhere in the law, AFL sued to force an investigation. The district court found that § 1216 created a mandatory duty to investigate but declined to order relief because § 1216(c) left the ultimate decision on corrective action to OSC's discretion. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped the merits entirely, concluding that AFL simply could not get past Article III standing.

The court's reasoning is a masterclass in applying *Heckler v. Chaney*. Under traditional standing doctrine, a plaintiff must show an injury-in-fact that is fairly traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. AFL attempted to frame its injury as twofold: first, that it never received the DOJ records; second, that it was denied procedural notices associated with an OSC investigation. Both theories collapsed under scrutiny due to broken chains of causation and redressability.

Regarding the missing records, AFL tried to argue that if OSC had investigated DOJ's policy under § 1216, it might have pursued corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215 against DOJ employees via the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). Only after such sanctions might DOJ finally release the documents. The court rightly identified this logic as pure speculation. As Judge Katsas noted, requiring courts to adjudicate based on "unfettered choices made by independent actors"—here, OSC deciding whether to prosecute and MSPB deciding whether to sanction—violates basic standing principles recently reaffirmed in *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. You cannot manufacture standing by chaining together multiple layers of hypothetical agency decisions involving non-parties over whom you have no control.

More significantly for administrative law scholars is how the court treated OSD's refusal to act as a shield against judicial review through backdoor litigation strategies like this one. Even assuming § 1216 imposes a mandatory duty to *investigate*, enforcing that duty would effectively require a court to micromanage executive enforcement priorities. The opinion explicitly relies on *Heckler v. Chaney* (470 U.S. 821), which holds that an agency's decision not to enforce is presumptively unreviewable because it involves complex factors best left within agency expertise and discretion. By dismissing AFL for lack of standing rather than addressing whether § 1216 actually compels an investigation regardless of other statutory prerequisites (*i.e.*, whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s conditions are exclusive), Judge Katsas avoids creating a precedent where courts become super-agencies reviewing every instance of alleged non-enforcement. Instead, he treats the inability of AFL to prove redressability as dispositive: if a court orders an investigation today but has no mechanism to ensure those findings lead to record disclosure tomorrow because enforcement remains discretionary downstream, there is no concrete injury capable of being redressed by judicial intervention alone.

This distinction carries profound doctrinal weight for future challenges against federal agencies invoking broad investigative powers across various statutes—from environmental protection laws whistleblower protections alike—where private litigants often seek "mandatory" investigations only to find themselves stymied by agency discretion once those investigations begin or end without results.* The ruling suggests that unless Congress can draft statutes with explicit mechanisms tying specific investigative outcomes directly and automatically to tangible benefits for plaintiffs (bypassing intermediate discretionary steps), litigants will struggle to clear Article III hurdles even when statutes appear absolute on their face.*

Furthermore, this decision underscores a growing trend in D.C. Circuit jurisprudence emphasizing strict adherence to *Lujan*'s concreteness requirements in procedural injury contexts.* While courts sometimes relax redressability standards when procedural rights are violated in ways affecting concrete interests,* here those exceptions failed because AFL could not demonstrate any direct link between OSC's inaction and its desired outcome.* Without proof that ordering an investigation would "very likely spur" subsequent steps leading toward relief,* mere hopefulness about what might happen downstream fails constitutional muster.* Ultimately,* while Congress retains broad power,* it cannot easily circumvent Article III limitations by drafting statutes that create duties without providing viable pathways for judicial enforcement absent intervening discretionary judgments.* The wall remains intact:* until Congress clarifies exactly how its commands translate into actionable rights enforceable by individuals,* federal courts will remain powerless—and unwilling—to pull levers they do not fully understand or control.*
