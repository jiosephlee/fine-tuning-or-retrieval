Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Enforcement Discretion and the Wall of Article III: Reaffirming *Heckler v. Chaney*

**By [Your Name]** | October 4, 2025

In the intricate dance between statutory mandates and constitutional limits, the D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that "shall" does not always mean "must," particularly when Article III stands guard at the courthouse door. Decided last week by Judge Neomi Rao's colleague, Judge Patrick Katsas, the panel held that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacked standing to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate allegations that the Department of Justice (DOJ) arbitrarily denied a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the underlying dispute involves complex statutory interpretation regarding civil service reform and transparency, the core holding rests on a fundamental separation-of-powers principle: federal courts cannot compel executive agencies to exercise their enforcement discretion, even when Congress uses seemingly mandatory language in a statute.

The factual backdrop is straightforward but procedurally tangled. AFL sought emails from DOJ regarding judicial recusals and was told they weren't searched without employee consent—a policy AFL challenged under FOIA. Simultaneously, AFL asked OSC to investigate this denial under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding." When OSC refused, citing that specific FOIA enforcement triggers hadn't been met elsewhere in the law, AFL sued to force an investigation. The district court found that § 1216 created a mandatory duty to investigate but declined to order relief because § 1216(c) left the ultimate decision on corrective action to OSC's discretion. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped the merits entirely, concluding that AFL simply could not get past Article III standing.

The court's reasoning is a masterclass in applying *Heckler v. Chaney*. Under traditional standing doctrine, a plaintiff must show an injury-in-fact that is fairly traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. AFL attempted to frame its injury as twofold: first, that it never received the DOJ records; second, that it was denied procedural notices associated with an OSC investigation. Both theories collapsed under scrutiny due to broken chains of causation and redressability.

Regarding the missing records, AFL tried to argue that if OSC had investigated DOJ's policy under § 1216, it might have pursued corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215 against DOJ employees via the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). Only after such sanctions might DOJ finally release the documents. The court rightly identified this logic as pure speculation. As Judge Katsas noted, requiring courts to adjudicate based on "unfettered choices made by independent actors"—here, OSC deciding whether to prosecute and MSPB deciding whether to sanction—violates basic standing principles recently reaffirmed in *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. You cannot manufacture standing by chaining together multiple layers of hypothetical agency decisions involving non-parties over whom you have no control.

More significantly for administrative law scholars is how the court treated OSD's refusal to act as a shield against judicial review through backdoor litigation strategies like this one. Even assuming § 1216 imposes a mandatory duty to *investigate*, enforcing that duty would effectively require a court to micromanage executive enforcement priorities. The opinion explicitly relies on *Heckler v. Chaney* (470 U.S. 821), which holds that an agency's decision not to enforce is presumptively unreviewable because it involves complex factors best left within agency expertise and discretion. By dismissing AFL for lack of standing rather than addressing whether § 1216 actually compels an investigation regardless of other statutory prerequisites (*i.e.*, whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s conditions are exclusive), Judge Katsas avoids creating a precedent where courts become super-agencies reviewing every instance of alleged non-enforcement. Instead, he treats the inability of AFL to prove redressability as dispositive: if a court orders an investigation today but has no mechanism to ensure those findings lead to record disclosure tomorrow because enforcement remains discretionary downstream, there is no concrete injury capable of being redressed by judicial intervention alone.

This distinction carries profound doctrinal weight for future challenges against federal agencies invoking broad investigative powers across various statutes—from environmental protection laws whistleblower protections alike—where private litigants often seek "mandatory" investigations only to find themselves stymied by agency discretion once those investigations begin or end without results.* The ruling suggests that unless Congress can draft statutes with explicit mechanisms tying specific investigative outcomes directly and automatically to tangible benefits for plaintiffs (bypassing intermediate discretionary steps), litigants will struggle to clear Article III hurdles even when statutes appear absolute on their face.*

Furthermore, this decision underscores a growing trend in D.C. Circuit jurisprudence emphasizing strict adherence to *Lujan*'s concreteness requirements in procedural injury contexts.* While courts sometimes relax redressability standards when procedural rights are violated in ways affecting concrete interests,* here those exceptions failed because AFL could not demonstrate any direct link between OSC's inaction and its desired outcome.* Without proof that ordering an investigation would "very likely spur" subsequent steps leading toward relief,* mere hopefulness about what might happen downstream fails constitutional muster.* Ultimately,* while Congress retains broad power,* it cannot easily circumvent Article III limitations by drafting statutes that create duties without providing viable pathways for judicial enforcement absent intervening discretionary judgments.* The wall remains intact:* until Congress clarifies exactly how its commands translate into actionable rights enforceable by individuals,* federal courts will remain powerless—and unwilling—to pull levers they do not fully understand or control.*
