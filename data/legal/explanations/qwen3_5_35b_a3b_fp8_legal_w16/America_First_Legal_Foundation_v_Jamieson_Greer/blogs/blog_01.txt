Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Speculative Chain: Why Procedural Rights Don't Confer Standing Without Concrete Harm

**By [Your Name/Analyst]**

The tension between statutory mandates and Article III limits has long been a fertile ground for litigation, but the D.C. Circuit's October 3 decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* draws a particularly sharp line in the sand. While the case nominally concerns the scope of the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) authority to investigate Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations, its true significance lies in how rigorously the court applied recent Supreme Court jurisprudence to sever the link between a procedural grievance and a concrete injury. By invoking *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*, Chief Judge Katsas's panel demonstrates that a plaintiff cannot manufacture standing simply by pointing to a statutory command they believe was ignored; they must still prove that ignoring that command actually hurt them in a way the court can fix.

At its core, this dispute arose from AFL's attempt to force OSC to investigate the Department of Justice's refusal to release specific emails regarding judicial recusal protocols. AFL argued that 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) created a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate "arbitrary or capricious" FOIA withholdings, regardless of whether the specific triggers in FOIA's own enforcement clause (§ 552(a)(4)(F)) were met. When OSC declined to act, citing those narrower statutory triggers, AFL sued. Although the district court acknowledged OSC might have erred on the merits, it ultimately dismissed the case because ordering an investigation remained within OSC's discretion under § 1216(c). On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the merits entirely, concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing from the outset.

The court's reasoning serves as a potent reminder that "procedural rights in vacuo" are insufficient for standing. AFL advanced two theories of injury: first, that OSC's failure to investigate prevented DOJ from releasing records; and second, that AFL was denied specific informational outputs generated during an investigation under § 1214 standards. Both theories collapsed under scrutiny regarding causation and redressability—a double whammy driven by what Judge Katsas terms a "speculative chain." Regarding the first theory, any delay in receiving documents is traceable directly to DOJ, not OSC. To find standing here would require assuming a complex cascade of future events: that OSC would have chosen to prosecute after an investigation; that the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have sanctioned DOJ employees; and finally, that DOJ would have complied once sanctioned. The court rightly noted that causation cannot rest on "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts," especially when those choices involve enforcement discretion which courts traditionally hesitate to compel under *Heckler v. Chaney*.

This analysis gains additional weight when viewed alongside last term's *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. Just as the Supreme Court there demanded a tighter nexus between agency action and injury-in-fact, Judge Katsas insists that redressability must turn on how a judgment binds parties present in court, rather than relying on persuasive rationales for non-parties like MSPB or DOJ. Similarly, under *Haaland*, speculative chains are insufficient even where statutes create broad duties. The rejection of AFL's second theory reinforces this strictness: even if § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not automatically grant AFL access to investigation reports or notices unless those rights are explicitly conferred by statute or regulation—which they are not in this context. Consequently, ordering an investigation would not necessarily yield any new information for AFL, failing both concreteness and redressability tests simultaneously.

Doctrinally, *Jamieson Greer* signals a continued tightening of procedural standing doctrines across administrative law circles. Courts appear increasingly unwilling to allow litigants to use statutory interpretation battles as backdoors into Article III jurisdiction when no tangible harm flows from the alleged violation of procedure alone. This approach protects agencies from being dragged into litigation over every perceived procedural misstep where the ultimate remedy remains speculative or dependent on third-party whimsy. For advocates seeking judicial oversight of agency non-enforcement or procedural failures, this ruling suggests that merely identifying a broken statutory promise is no longer enough; one must demonstrate exactly how that break concretely injured them and how fixing it will immediately cure that injury without requiring guesswork about what other government actors might do next.

Ultimately, while AFL may feel aggrieved by OSC's refusal to intervene in their FOIA struggle against DOJ, *Jamieson Greer* underscores a fundamental structural reality: federal courts are limited tribunals empowered only to resolve actual controversies where their judgments matter concretely today, not tomorrow via a chain reaction of discretionary choices elsewhere in government bureaucracy.
