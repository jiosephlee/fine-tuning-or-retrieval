Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Investigation vs. Information: The Limits of 'Informational Injury' in Administrative Law

**By [Your Name/Persona]** | *October 5, 2025*

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, handed down on October 3, offers a stark reminder that the judiciary remains the gatekeeper of administrative overreach, particularly when plaintiffs attempt to weaponize "informational standing." While the case technically involves the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and its duty to investigate Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations, the underlying legal victory belongs to the principle that a statutory right to trigger an investigation does not automatically create a cognizable injury when that investigation yields no tangible results or disclosures. By dismissing AFL's suit for lack of Article III standing, the court effectively drew a line between the procedural machinery of government accountability and the concrete interests required to access federal courts.

The factual backdrop is straightforward but procedurally complex. AFL sought emails regarding judicial recusals from the Department of Justice (DOJ). When DOJ delayed production citing privacy concerns, AFL turned to two avenues: suing DOJ directly under FOIA—which resulted in ongoing compliance—and petitioning the OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged arbitrary denial policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). That statute mandates OSC to investigate allegations of "arbitrary or capricious withholding" of information under FOIA. However, OSC declined to launch an investigation, arguing that its mandatory duty only triggers after a court has already found an arbitrary withholding and ordered disclosure—a condition precedent AFL had not met in this specific context. Even though the district court suggested OSC might have broader discretion than it claimed, it ultimately dismissed AFL's claims because ordering an investigation would commit discretionary enforcement powers to the executive branch. On appeal, Judge Katsas and the full panel sidestepped these merits entirely by focusing on standing.

The crux of today's analysis lies in how the D.C. Circuit dismantled AFL's second theory of injury: that being denied participation in or updates from an OSC investigation constitutes a concrete harm. AFL argued that if OSC were conducting a proper investigation under § 1216, it would be obligated to provide notice and information akin to those required under § 1214 for "prohibited personnel practices." The court rejected this premise with surgical precision. It noted that while § 1216 directs OSC to *investigate* FOIA violations, it does not explicitly mandate that OSC adopt § 1214's notification protocols during such inquiries. Indeed, existing precedent recognizes that OSC retains broad discretion over whether to graft those procedural requirements onto § 1216 investigations. Without a clear congressional command granting AFL a right to receive specific data *about* the investigation itself, there is no "legal interest" invaded by OSC's silence.

This distinction is vital for administrative law doctrine moving forward. For years, litigants have tested the boundaries of what constitutes an "informational injury," often seeking to transform mere frustration with bureaucratic opacity into justiciable harm. The Supreme Court has long held that denying information can constitute injury where a statute entitles a plaintiff to receive it (*Akins*), but *Jamieson Greer* reinforces that this entitlement must be explicit regarding the *output* demanded by the plaintiff, not just the *process* initiated by them. AFL wanted information; they could get it via FOIA against DOJ or potentially via discovery if they won their underlying case against DOJ. They could not get it simply because they wanted an outsider's view into an internal enforcement decision-making process where Congress left discretion intact. The court correctly identified that allowing standing here would effectively grant private citizens a veto over agency enforcement priorities disguised as a right to information.

Furthermore, this ruling highlights a critical separation-of-powers boundary regarding prosecutorial discretion. The opinion reiterates that courts cannot compel agencies to enforce laws in ways Congress did not mandate (*Heckler v. Chaney*). If courts allowed plaintiffs like AFL to sue based on their exclusion from an investigative process where no specific output was guaranteed by statute, they would inevitably be drawn into micromanaging agency resources and priorities. By requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate that they are owed specific information rather than just asserting an interest in general oversight, *Jamieson Greer* preserves the executive branch's ability to prioritize cases without fear of litigation from every aggrieved requester who feels ignored during preliminary reviews.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a cautionary tale for organizations hoping to use administrative statutes as backdoor mechanisms for forced disclosure or oversight beyond what Congress intended. The D.C. Circuit clarified that while agencies must follow statutory commands when they exist, plaintiffs cannot conjure rights out of thin air by assuming that every statutory duty implies a corresponding right for third parties to know everything about how those duties are executed—or even whether they are executed at all—absent explicit legislative direction. In doing so, the court reaffirmed that Article III standing remains a robust barrier against lawsuits driven by generalized grievances wrapped in procedural language.
