Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The Subjective Intent Fallacy: How *Santos v. Kimmel* Reaffirms the Objective Standard in Transformative Fair Use

The recent Second Circuit decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a timely and necessary reaffirmation of a core principle in modern copyright jurisprudence: the "transformative" nature of a work is determined by what a reasonable observer sees, not by the subjective intentions of the parties involved. While former Representative George Santos attempted to leverage his celebrity status to enjoin Jimmy Kimmel's satirical segments, the appellate court's affirmation of the district court's dismissal serves as a potent reminder that the fair use doctrine remains robust against attempts to weaponize copyright claims based on personal grievance or perceived bad faith. In an era where public figures frequently attempt to control their digital image, *Santos* clarifies that satire and political commentary occupy a protected space precisely because they operate outside the creator's original subjective design.

At the heart of Santos's appeal was a contention that Kimmel's use of his Cameo videos lacked transformative quality because Kimmel had "instigated" the creation of those videos and thus could not claim to be commenting on them. Santos argued that since he originally recorded the clips with hopes of conveying "hope, strength, and positivity," Kimmel's recontextualization into a mockery segment titled "Will Santos Say It?" betrayed his original intent. The Second Circuit, led by Judge Lohier, decisively rejected this line of reasoning. Drawing heavily on the Supreme Court's guidance in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the court reiterated that fair use analysis is an objective inquiry focused on how a "reasonable observer" perceives the new work relative to the old one. Whether Santos subjectively intended his messages to be uplifting is irrelevant if a viewer reasonably interprets Kimmel's edit as a critique of Santos's willingness to sell absurd statements for money. This distinction is vital; it prevents copyright holders from freezing their own creative output into a single interpretive box, ensuring that subsequent works can reshape meaning without fear of liability simply because they diverge from the author's internal narrative.

Perhaps even more significant for First Amendment values was the court's handling of Santos's assertion that Kimmel acted in bad faith by using fictitious names to solicit the videos before airing them mockingly. Santos invoked *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, arguing that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing, thereby suggesting that deception should negate the defense entirely. The Second Circuit rightly noted that while *Harper & Row* made such statements decades ago, subsequent precedent has distanced itself from treating subjective bad faith as a dispositive bar to fair use in this context. More importantly, the court observed that Santos himself admitted—through his own complaint—that Kimmel was motivated by sarcasm and criticism rather than an intent to supplant his commercial market. When a defendant is engaged in commentary or parody, their methods of eliciting material do not transform their conduct into commercial exploitation if the ultimate product functions as social critique rather than a market substitute for the original video clips themselves.

Beyond copyright law, *Santos* provides important clarity on state law contract claims often bundled with intellectual property disputes involving celebrities and platforms like Cameo or TikTok. The court efficiently dismantled Santos's breach of contract and fraudulent inducement arguments by highlighting fundamental gaps in standing and pleading standards under both Illinois and New York law. Crucially, because Santos was not a signatory to Cameo's Terms of Service between users like Kimmel and performers like himself, he could not claim third-party beneficiary status absent an express declaration—a requirement clearly missing from the platform's agreements which distinguished between contracting parties and beneficiaries elsewhere in their textually explicit clauses. Furthermore, without alleging concrete out-of-pocket losses resulting directly from alleged fraud, his tort claims collapsed under New York law standards requiring proof of actual economic injury beyond mere reputational sting or emotional distress inherent in being mocked publicly.

The broader doctrinal implication of *Santos v. Kimmel* lies in its reinforcement of the separation between an artist's subjective intent and an audience's objective reception—a separation essential for free speech protections in our media landscape. If courts allowed plaintiffs to defeat fair use defenses based solely on their own recollection of why they created content or how they felt about being deceived during production, satire would become legally perilous whenever it relied on pre-existing footage obtained through subterfuge or trickery (as many investigative reports do). By anchoring its analysis firmly in what a reasonable observer concludes about the work's purpose rather than what went through someone else's mind during filming or recording sessions, the Second Circuit ensures that cultural commentary retains its ability to challenge power without needing permission from every source it critiques. Ultimately, *Santos* stands as another brick in the wall protecting parody from becoming copyright litigation fodder simply because it offends its target or employs unconventional means to gather material for expression.



# Behind the Cameo Curtain: Third-Party Beneficiaries and Contract Formation in Digital Platforms

While the headline grabber in *Santos v. Kimmel* was undoubtedly the Second Circuit's robust affirmation that late-night satire qualifies as fair use, the decision offers a more granular, yet equally instructive, lesson in the architecture of digital commerce contracts. Former Representative George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and ABC for airing clips from videos Santos recorded via the Cameo platform, arguing that Kimmel had tricked him into creating content specifically to mock him. Although the court quickly dispatched these copyright claims under *Andy Warhol Foundation*, the state law section of the opinion serves as a stern warning to anyone attempting to leverage platform Terms of Service (ToS) as a sword against non-signatory users. The panel's rejection of Santos's breach of contract and fraudulent inducement claims underscores a fundamental reality of modern litigation: being a "beneficiary" of a digital contract is far harder than simply alleging you were harmed by its breach.

The core of the contractual dispute turned on whether Santos could enforce Cameo's Terms of Service—a contract strictly between the platform and its paying users—against Kimmel, who signed them. Under Illinois law, which governed the ToS, there exists a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court noted that for such an exception to apply, the intent to benefit a third party must be so explicit that it amounts to an express declaration. Crucially, the court observed that Cameo's ToS contained third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere; their presence in some sections highlighted their absence regarding Santos. By failing to include an express declaration extending rights to video creators like Santos, Cameo effectively insulated itself from liability while simultaneously preventing Santos from stepping into the shoes of a contracting party. This distinction is vital for tech companies drafting user agreements: if you want to limit liability or define rights broadly among all participants, you must say so explicitly; silence leaves non-signatories outside the protective umbrella entirely.

Turning to New York law governing implied contracts, the Second Circuit reinforced that vague allegations of unfairness do not equate to a valid cause of action for breach of implied-in-fact contract. To survive a motion to dismiss under New York precedent, a plaintiff must allege specific facts demonstrating a "meeting of minds" regarding essential terms. The court found Santos's complaint wanting because he offered no concrete evidence that Kimmel agreed to abide by Cameo's rules as part of their interaction with Santos himself. Instead, Santos relied on general assertions that Kimmel acted dishonestly by using pseudonyms and requesting personal videos. Without alleging specific terms that both parties understood and accepted as binding conditions for their relationship, no contract existed independent of Cameo's written agreement. This ruling clarifies that in high-profile interactions involving digital intermediaries, courts will not easily infer contractual obligations where none were explicitly negotiated between the disputing parties.

Furthermore, the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim highlights another procedural hurdle often overlooked by litigants seeking redress in entertainment disputes: pecuniary loss. Under New York tort law, fraud requires proof of out-of-pocket losses resulting directly from reliance on misrepresentations. The court pointed out that while Santos felt mocked or manipulated emotionally or reputationally, he failed to allege any actual financial expenditure lost due to Kimmel's deception beyond what he voluntarily spent on producing videos for compensation under standard platform rates. Since Kimmel paid Santos per request according to standard fees before broadcasting them later (and arguably better) than anticipated by Santos's expectations, there was no distinct financial injury caused by fraud sufficient to sustain a claim at the pleading stage.

The broader doctrinal implication here extends well beyond celebrity feuds and late-night television roasts. *Santos v. Kimmel* signals that courts remain skeptical of attempts to bypass traditional privity requirements in complex digital ecosystems where multiple actors interact through standardized interfaces. For attorneys advising clients involved in online transactions where one party feels aggrieved by another user's conduct mediated through a platform, this opinion suggests a narrow path forward: do not rely on broad interpretations of "third-party beneficiaries" unless your contract text screams it; instead, look for direct negotiations or specific promises made between you and your adversary. As digital platforms increasingly mediate human interaction—from gig economy apps to social media content sharing—the Second Circuit has drawn a bright line: if you didn't sign it and weren't named as an intended beneficiary with crystal clarity, you likely cannot enforce it against others who did sign it but chose not to follow your lead on how those terms should affect you personally.
