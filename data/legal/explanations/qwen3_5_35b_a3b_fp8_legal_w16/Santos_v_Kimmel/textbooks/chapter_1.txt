Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and Factual Background

To understand *Santos v. Kimmel*, one must first situate the case within its specific procedural and factual framework. This appeal arises from a dispute between former Congressman George Santos and the producers of *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"), specifically Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), and The Walt Disney Company. The litigation centers on a satirical television segment where Kimmel utilized the Cameo video-message platform to solicit personalized clips from Santos, only to air them on national television in a manner designed to mock Santos's public persona. Before addressing the substantive legal questions of copyright fair use and state law preemption, it is essential to trace the procedural path taken by the case and the factual mechanics that generated the controversy.

The parties in this appeal represent distinct spheres of influence: on one side, a disgraced former federal legislator seeking redress for perceived intellectual property theft; on the other, a prominent late-night host and major media conglomerates asserting their right to engage in political satire and commentary. The jurisdiction lies with the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, reviewing a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York before Judge Cote. The core dispute emerged after Kimmel, utilizing various fictitious names to bypass identification protocols on Cameo, requested personalized video messages from Santos. Believing these requests came from genuine fans or interested parties, Santos recorded several videos fulfilling these requests. These recordings were subsequently broadcast by JKL as part of a recurring series titled "Will Santos Say It?" which highlighted instances where Santos appeared willing to say absurd things for compensation.

The Cameo platform serves as the technological linchpin of this narrative. Originally functioning as a marketplace where celebrities record short video greetings for paying customers, Cameo allows users to purchase personalized content from stars ranging from athletes to politicians. In this instance, Kimmel exploited the platform's anonymity features by adopting multiple fake personas to commission videos directly from Santos. Once received, Kimmel edited or presented these clips within his monologue segments, framing them not as sincere endorsements but as evidence of Santos's susceptibility to financial incentives for saying ridiculous lines. While Santos viewed these broadcasts as unauthorized reproductions violating his exclusive rights under federal copyright law and various state laws regarding contracts and fraud, Kimmel characterized the usage as protected speech—specifically parody and news commentary—that fell squarely within the boundaries of fair use.

Procedurally, this case began when Santos filed a complaint alleging copyright infringement alongside state-law causes of action for breach of contract (both direct and implied), breach of implied warranty, and fraudulent inducement based on Kimmel's deceptive use of fake identities during the solicitation process. The District Court did not proceed to discovery or trial; instead, it addressed these allegations via a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. This rule permits a defendant to challenge whether a plaintiff has pleaded sufficient facts to state a plausible claim for relief upon which relief can be granted. Judge Cote concluded that Santos failed to meet this threshold across all counts: she ruled that the copyright claims were barred ab initio by the fair use doctrine because Kimmel's use was transformative, and she determined that any remaining state law claims were either preempted by federal copyright law or independently insufficient under applicable state statutes governing contracts and fraud.

On appeal, George Santos challenged both aspects of this dismissal—the legal determination regarding fair use and the assessment that his state law claims lacked merit. Consequently, the Second Circuit reviewed these determinations de novo. De novo review signifies that the appellate court examines the district court's decision anew without deferring to its conclusions; they stand ready to reverse if they find an error in applying legal principles or interpreting facts contained in the record. When conducting this review at the pleading stage following a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, the Second Circuit considers three primary sources: (1) all well-pleaded facts alleged within Santos's complaint; (2) documents formally attached to that complaint as exhibits; and (3) documents incorporated by reference within those attachments or exhibits. Furthermore, consistent with precedent in intellectual property cases involving registered copyrights, such as *Island Software & Comput.* *Serv.*, Inc.* v.* Microsoft Corp.,* courts may take judicial notice of official records maintained by government agencies—in this instance, copyright registration data filed with the U.S. Copyright Office—to verify ownership status without requiring formal introduction into evidence at trial stages often reserved for later proceedings like summary judgment or trial proper.**
