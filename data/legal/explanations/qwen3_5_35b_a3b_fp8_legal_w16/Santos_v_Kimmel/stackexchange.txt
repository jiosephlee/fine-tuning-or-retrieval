Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can a plaintiff win a 'transformative' fair use argument when they themselves initiated the creation of the video content?
Question:
The court emphasizes that the 'purpose and character' factor hinges on whether the use is transformative, citing *Andy Warhol Foundation*. Santos argues that because Kimmel instigated the request via Cameo, the videos weren't truly transformative but rather just a replication of Santos's original commercial purpose. However, the court dismisses this by stating that 'subjective intent' doesn't matter—only what a 'reasonable observer' perceives. I am confused: If Santos explicitly asked to make these videos for money, how does the court justify that a reasonable observer sees them as 'commentary' rather than just a repurposing of his own commercial product? Does the context of being aired on a late-night talk show automatically override the origin story?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court resolves the tension between Santos's commercial motivation and the "transformative" requirement by strictly applying the **objective "reasonable observer" test**, explicitly rejecting the relevance of subjective intent regarding who originated the request.

Under the first fair use factor (*purpose and character*), the Supreme Court in *Andy Warhol* clarified that the inquiry focuses on whether the new work adds something new with a further purpose or different character, superseding the original. Crucially, as cited in *Santos*, this determination is based on **what a reasonable observer perceives**, not the subjective intent of either party. The court notes that while Santos argued Kimmel was merely replicating his own commercial purpose because Kimmel instigated the request, this factual background is irrelevant to the statutory analysis.

The justification lies in the discrepancy between Santos's original perception and Kimmel's secondary use. As acknowledged in Santos's own complaint, a reasonable viewer watching the videos originally thought they conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." By contrast, when aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking segment titled "Will Santos Say It?", a reasonable observer perceives a completely different message: **"commentary on... [Kimmel's] willingness to say absurd things for money."**

Therefore, the context of airing on a late-night talk show does not automatically override everything; rather, it provides the specific lens through which a reasonable observer interprets the work. The court holds that because the secondary use conveys a distinct satirical message (mockery/criticism) compared to the original perceived message (positivity), it is transformative regardless of who paid for or requested its initial creation. The fact that Santos sought profit initially does not prevent Kimmel from using that footage to critique that very profit-seeking behavior in a way that changes its character entirely.

### Does *Harper & Row*'s requirement of 'good faith' still bar fair use if a defendant lies to obtain copyrighted material?
Question:
Santos argued that Kimmel's deception (using fictitious names to trick him into creating content) demonstrated bad faith, which should theoretically negate the fair use defense under *Harper & Row*. The court mentions that the Supreme Court has since 'distanced itself' from the *Harper & Row* statement regarding good faith. Could someone clarify exactly how far that distancing went? Is lying now irrelevant to fair use analysis entirely, or does it only matter if it proves an intent to 'supplant' the market? Why didn't this bad faith behavior prevent dismissal at the pleading stage?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
No, *Harper & Row*'s "good faith" requirement no longer serves as a standalone bar to fair use simply because a defendant lied to obtain material. While the Supreme Court originally stated that fair use presupposes good faith, the Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* confirms that subsequent jurisprudence has effectively "distanced" itself from that categorical rule. Instead, deceptive conduct like lying is now analyzed strictly through the lens of **market substitution**. Unless the bad faith demonstrates an intent to "supplant" the copyright owner's commercial market—rather than merely engaging in criticism or parody—the deception does not defeat the fair use defense.

### Detailed Analysis

**1. The Shift Away from "Good Faith" as a Separate Element**
The Supreme Court's 1985 decision in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.* famously declared that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." However, as noted by the Second Circuit in *Santos*, later cases have moved away from treating this statement as a rigid prerequisite. The court explicitly rejects Santos's argument that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to induce video creation automatically negates fair use based solely on this principle. The modern approach treats "good faith" not as an independent threshold test, but rather as evidence relevant to the statutory factors, specifically whether the use harms the market for the original work.

**2. The Relevance of Bad Faith: Market Supplantation vs. Criticism**
Under current law, bad faith behavior (such as deception) matters only if it proves an intent to **supplant** the market for the original work. The court explains that if a secondary user acts with bad faith but their ultimate goal remains commentary or criticism—without seeking to act as a commercial competitor—they remain protected by fair use.

In *Santos*, the court found that despite Kimmel's deceptive tactics, the complaint did not allege an intent to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights. On the contrary, the record showed Kimmel was motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Therefore, the lack of good faith did not tip the balance against fair use because it did not transform Kimmel's activity into a market substitute for Santos's videos.

**3. Why Dismissal Was Proper at Pleading Stage**
You asked why this bad faith behavior prevented dismissal at the pleading stage (Rule 12(b)(6)). The answer lies in how courts evaluate allegations of market harm under Factor Four of § 107:
*   **Legal Standard:** Courts distinguish between harm caused by criticism/parody (which is expected and allowed) and harm caused by a competing substitute (which is prohibited). As cited in *Hachette Book Group*, "we ask not whether the second work would damage the market... but whether it usurps the market... by offering a competing substitute."
*   **Application:** Since Santos conceded his videos were created for positive messaging ("hope," "strength") while Kimmel used them for mockery ("absurd things"), a reasonable observer sees these as distinct purposes with no overlap in potential markets. Even assuming Kimmel lied to get there, he did not create a product that competes with Santos's product; he created a parody/commentary piece that happens to utilize Santos's material for satire. Consequently, Santos failed to plausibly allege that Kimmel's bad faith resulted in actionable market harm sufficient to overcome fair use at the outset of litigation.

### Conclusion
*Lying* is no longer fatal to a fair use defense on its own face. It is only dispositive if it serves as proof that the defendant intended to enter into direct competition with or replace the copyright holder's work (*supplant*). Because Santos could not allege that Kimmel sought to monetize or compete with his videos—only mock them—the deceptive acquisition method did not preclude dismissal via summary judgment or motion to dismiss based on fair use.

### Can a public figure sue for breach of contract based on platform Terms of Service when they never agreed to those terms?
Question:
The court affirmed dismissal of Santos's breach of contract claim because he wasn't a signatory to the Cameo Terms of Service. The opinion cites Illinois law regarding third-party beneficiaries, noting there is a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits.' Since Kimmel signed up as a user and Santos was essentially treated as a service provider on that platform, isn't there an expectation that both parties are bound by the same rules? How strict must an agreement be before non-signatories like Santos can enforce its terms, especially when they arguably relied on those terms to perform services?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No. Under Illinois law governing the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS), a non-signatory like Santos cannot enforce the contract unless there is an "express declaration" or language so strong it amounts to one. Mere participation as a service provider on the platform, or reliance on the terms to perform services, does not overcome the "strong presumption against conferring benefits" to third parties.

### Legal Analysis

The core issue is **third-party beneficiary status**. While you argue that there is an expectation that all participants—both requestors and providers—are bound by the same rules, contract law requires more than just functional interaction; it requires evidence of intent to create rights for non-signatories.

Under **Illinois law**, which controls the interpretation of the Cameo ToS, there is a **"strong presumption against conferring benefits"** on non-contracting third parties (*Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp.*). The court in *Santos* explains that for a third party to enforce a contract, "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

The opinion highlights two key reasons why Santos fails this test:

1.  **Lack of Express Declaration:** The court notes that if the drafters of the ToS intended for certain provisions to benefit third parties (such as content providers), they included specific third-party beneficiary language elsewhere in the document. Because no such clause explicitly extends these protections or obligations to Santos, he remains outside the contractual relationship between Kimmel and Cameo.
2.  **No Implied Contract:** Even looking at New York law (which governs some aspects of his claim), an implied-in-fact contract requires a "meeting of minds." The court found Santos failed to allege specific facts showing that Kimmel and Santos actually agreed to be bound by the ToS terms directly between themselves. Merely fulfilling requests on a platform where another party agreed to terms does not automatically create a bilateral contract between the requester and the performer absent explicit agreement or clear evidence of mutual assent to those specific terms.

Regarding your point about **reliance**: The court did not find that performing services creates an automatic contractual bond with every other user who signs up for the platform. Without an express term stating that service providers are intended beneficiaries, reliance alone is insufficient to pierce the privity barrier under Illinois law. The "expectation" that everyone follows the rules is a matter of platform policy, not necessarily binding contract law between private individuals who never exchanged promises directly governed by those terms.

Therefore, because Santos was not a signatory and could not point to an express declaration granting him standing as a third-party beneficiary, his breach of contract claim was correctly dismissed for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6).

### What constitutes an 'implied-in-fact contract' in digital interactions where no explicit agreement exists between opposing parties?
Question:
Regarding the implied contract claim under New York law, the court says Santos failed to allege 'essential terms' or a 'meeting of minds.' In my understanding, implied contracts often arise from conduct (like accepting payment for work). Here, Kimmel paid for videos via Cameo. Why did the court find no meeting of minds? Was it simply because they didn't agree on specific terms beyond what was in Cameo's boilerplate (which neither signed directly), or is there something deeper about why online platform transactions don't create mutual contractual obligations between random users who interact through intermediaries?
Answer:
**The Short Answer**
Under New York law, the court found no "meeting of the minds" because Santos failed to plead **specific factual allegations** demonstrating that Kimmel and Santos mutually agreed to be bound by the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS) outside of the formal platform agreement. The mere fact that Kimmel paid for a video and Santos accepted payment does not automatically create a binding contract between them regarding the *terms* of that transaction (such as copyright waivers or usage rights) unless the plaintiff can allege that both parties specifically intended to adopt those external terms into their interaction.

**Detailed Analysis**

**1. The Requirement for "Essential Terms"**
While implied-in-fact contracts generally arise from conduct rather than written signatures, New York law still requires a plaintiff to plead more than just the existence of a relationship. As noted in *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, a complaint alleging an implied-in-fact contract must specify **"the essential terms of the parties' ... contract."**

In *Santos*, the court emphasized that Santos's complaint failed to identify these essential terms in "non-conclusory language." Specifically, Santos could not point to specific provisions within the Cameo ToS that he and Kimmel explicitly agreed to follow during their individual exchange. Without identifying *what* exactly was agreed upon (beyond the general expectation that a video would be made), there is no enforceable contract to breach.

**2. The Absence of a "Meeting of Minds"**
An implied-in-fact contract requires an objective manifestation of mutual assent—a "meeting of minds"—that one party will perform according to specific conditions understood by both. The court cited *DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass'n Compliance & Legal Div.* to explain that Santos did not plausibly allege **"an indication of a meeting of minds... that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service."**

The court distinguished this scenario from cases where parties clearly intend to incorporate external terms by conduct alone. Here, the record showed:
*   **Kimmel's Perspective:** There was no evidence Kimmel read, reviewed, or intended to be bound by the full text of Cameo's ToS regarding third-party beneficiaries or copyright assignments during his specific interaction with Santos.
*   **Santos's Perspective:** While Santos knew he had signed up for Cameo, he could not prove that Kimmel (a separate user) shared an intent to adopt those same boilerplate terms for their private transaction.

**3. Why Platform Transactions Don't Automatically Create User-to-User Contracts**
You asked if there is something deeper about online platforms preventing these obligations between random users. The court addressed this indirectly through Illinois law applied to third-party beneficiaries but clarified via New York contract principles: **parties cannot be bound by terms they never discussed.**

The court rejected the argument that using an intermediary (Cameo) creates a web of contractual obligations between all participants automatically. Instead, it held that:
*   The ToS is primarily an agreement between the User (Kimmel) and the Platform (Cameo).
*   For a User-to-User implied contract to exist based on those ToS, there must be proof that both users intended for those specific terms to govern their direct interaction.
*   Mere payment and acceptance are insufficient if they do not indicate an agreement on *how* the content would be used or owned beyond basic compensation for services rendered.

**Conclusion**
The dismissal was not based on a rule that "online transactions never form contracts," but rather on procedural insufficiency under New York pleading standards (*Rule 12(b)(6)*). Santos failed to allege facts showing that Kimmel and Santos reached a mutual understanding ("meeting of minds") regarding specific contractual terms like copyright ownership or usage restrictions solely through their conduct on the platform. Without those pleaded facts, no implied-in-fact contract existed between them as a matter of law at the pleading stage.

### Is proving 'out-of-pocket loss' strictly necessary for fraudulent inducement claims involving intangible assets like personal videos?
Question:
The court dismissed Santos's fraudulent inducement claim solely because he failed to allege any 'out-of-pocket loss.' This seems counterintuitive given he was tricked into creating content he wouldn't have made otherwise. If I lie to get someone to create art I then sell or broadcast, shouldn't their lost opportunity or diminished value count as damages even if they didn't spend cash out-of-pocket during production? Or does New York law require tangible monetary expenditure regardless of whether fraud occurred?
Answer:
Under New York law, alleging "out-of-pocket loss" is indeed a strict requirement for stating a valid claim of fraudulent inducement, even when the plaintiff claims they were tricked into performing services or creating intangible assets they otherwise would not have created.

As affirmed in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit held that Santos's fraudulent inducement claim failed specifically because he did not allege any **actual out-of-pocket loss**. The court relied on the standard set forth in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), and reaffirmed in *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017), which mandates that a plaintiff must prove they actually spent money or parted with property as a result of the fraud to recover damages.

The court explicitly rejected the argument that the creation of personal videos constituted a sufficient loss simply because Santos claimed he would not have produced them absent the deception. While Santos argued he suffered a "lost opportunity" or that his labor had diminished value because it was used for mockery rather than his intended commercial purpose, New York law distinguishes between general economic injury and the specific element of out-of-pocket expenditure required for this tort. The appellate court noted that Santos "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss," thereby precluding recovery under this theory regardless of whether the fraud induced him to expend time or effort on intangible creations.

Therefore, unlike some jurisdictions that might recognize reliance damages based on the value of services rendered or opportunity costs alone, New York requires a tangible monetary expenditure (or similar asset transfer) that leaves the plaintiff poorer than they were before the fraud occurred. In *Santos*, because he received payment via Cameo for each video and incurred no direct financial cost beyond his time—which does not satisfy the out-of-pocket requirement—his claim could not survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6).

### At what point does preemption by federal copyright law eliminate state law remedies for unauthorized uses?
Question:
While the court avoided ruling on preemption for breach/implied contract claims (deciding them on other grounds), it noted that these claims were likely preempted. I'm trying to understand the boundary here: If someone steals your photo and posts it online, you usually have state tort claims too. But here, since it involves copyrighted videos aired on TV, why would state contract laws be preempted so easily? What specific element makes these state law claims fall squarely within the scope of federal copyright exclusivity versus being 'extra elements'? Is it purely because they relate exclusively to reproduction/distribution rights?
Answer:
The *Santos* opinion clarifies the boundary between state law and federal copyright preemption not by establishing a bright-line rule for all contract claims, but by illustrating how **state claims based solely on the exclusive rights granted by the Copyright Act** lack the "extra elements" necessary to survive preemption.

Under 17 U.S.C. § 301(a), state law is preempted if the work falls within the subject matter of copyright and the state right is equivalent to one of the exclusive rights protected by federal law. The key distinction lies in whether the state cause of action requires an **"extra element"** beyond mere reproduction, distribution, or performance—the core rights held by a copyright owner.

In *Santos*, the court notes that while it did not definitively rule on preemption for breach of contract or implied contract claims (dismissing them on other grounds), it observed that these claims were **"likely preempted."** This observation suggests that when a plaintiff's state law claim arises strictly from the defendant's unauthorized copying, broadcasting, or distribution of a video—activities that constitute the exclusive rights of a copyright holder—the claim is essentially a re-packaged copyright infringement suit.

The specific element that causes these claims to fall within federal exclusivity is the **absence of a distinct contractual relationship or independent duty**.
*   **Reproduction/Distribution Focus:** As you noted, Santos's claims turned entirely on Kimmel taking his videos and airing them. If the "breach" alleged is simply that Kimmel used the videos without permission (reproduction/distribution), there are no extra elements; the claim is functionally identical to copyright infringement.
*   **The Missing Extra Element:** For a state contract claim to survive preemption, it typically must rely on a specific promise or consideration that exists outside the scope of general intellectual property rights (e.g., a confidentiality agreement regarding unpublished ideas, or a specific payment obligation unrelated to licensing fees). In *Santos*, because Kimmel was not a signatory to Cameo's Terms of Service and no separate agreement existed directly between Santos and Kimmel regarding those specific videos, Santos could not point to an "extra element" like a mutual exchange of promises distinct from the act of copying itself.

Therefore, as *Santos* implies, if your state law remedy seeks redress for an unauthorized use that constitutes an exercise of an exclusive right under § 106 (such as public performance or reproduction) and relies only on general principles of fairness rather than a specific, independent contractual duty, it lacks the requisite extra element and will be preempted. The "boundary" you seek is drawn where the state claim ceases to be about protecting an investment in expression (which federal law covers) and becomes about enforcing unique obligations (like confidentiality or specific performance) that exist independently of copyright status.
