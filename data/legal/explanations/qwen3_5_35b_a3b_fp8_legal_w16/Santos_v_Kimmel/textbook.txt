Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and Factual Background

To understand *Santos v. Kimmel*, one must first situate the case within its specific procedural and factual framework. This appeal arises from a dispute between former Congressman George Santos and the producers of *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"), specifically Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), and The Walt Disney Company. The litigation centers on a satirical television segment where Kimmel utilized the Cameo video-message platform to solicit personalized clips from Santos, only to air them on national television in a manner designed to mock Santos's public persona. Before addressing the substantive legal questions of copyright fair use and state law preemption, it is essential to trace the procedural path taken by the case and the factual mechanics that generated the controversy.

The parties in this appeal represent distinct spheres of influence: on one side, a disgraced former federal legislator seeking redress for perceived intellectual property theft; on the other, a prominent late-night host and major media conglomerates asserting their right to engage in political satire and commentary. The jurisdiction lies with the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, reviewing a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York before Judge Cote. The core dispute emerged after Kimmel, utilizing various fictitious names to bypass identification protocols on Cameo, requested personalized video messages from Santos. Believing these requests came from genuine fans or interested parties, Santos recorded several videos fulfilling these requests. These recordings were subsequently broadcast by JKL as part of a recurring series titled "Will Santos Say It?" which highlighted instances where Santos appeared willing to say absurd things for compensation.

The Cameo platform serves as the technological linchpin of this narrative. Originally functioning as a marketplace where celebrities record short video greetings for paying customers, Cameo allows users to purchase personalized content from stars ranging from athletes to politicians. In this instance, Kimmel exploited the platform's anonymity features by adopting multiple fake personas to commission videos directly from Santos. Once received, Kimmel edited or presented these clips within his monologue segments, framing them not as sincere endorsements but as evidence of Santos's susceptibility to financial incentives for saying ridiculous lines. While Santos viewed these broadcasts as unauthorized reproductions violating his exclusive rights under federal copyright law and various state laws regarding contracts and fraud, Kimmel characterized the usage as protected speech—specifically parody and news commentary—that fell squarely within the boundaries of fair use.

Procedurally, this case began when Santos filed a complaint alleging copyright infringement alongside state-law causes of action for breach of contract (both direct and implied), breach of implied warranty, and fraudulent inducement based on Kimmel's deceptive use of fake identities during the solicitation process. The District Court did not proceed to discovery or trial; instead, it addressed these allegations via a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. This rule permits a defendant to challenge whether a plaintiff has pleaded sufficient facts to state a plausible claim for relief upon which relief can be granted. Judge Cote concluded that Santos failed to meet this threshold across all counts: she ruled that the copyright claims were barred ab initio by the fair use doctrine because Kimmel's use was transformative, and she determined that any remaining state law claims were either preempted by federal copyright law or independently insufficient under applicable state statutes governing contracts and fraud.

On appeal, George Santos challenged both aspects of this dismissal—the legal determination regarding fair use and the assessment that his state law claims lacked merit. Consequently, the Second Circuit reviewed these determinations de novo. De novo review signifies that the appellate court examines the district court's decision anew without deferring to its conclusions; they stand ready to reverse if they find an error in applying legal principles or interpreting facts contained in the record. When conducting this review at the pleading stage following a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, the Second Circuit considers three primary sources: (1) all well-pleaded facts alleged within Santos's complaint; (2) documents formally attached to that complaint as exhibits; and (3) documents incorporated by reference within those attachments or exhibits. Furthermore, consistent with precedent in intellectual property cases involving registered copyrights, such as *Island Software & Comput.* *Serv.*, Inc.* v.* Microsoft Corp.,* courts may take judicial notice of official records maintained by government agencies—in this instance, copyright registration data filed with the U.S. Copyright Office—to verify ownership status without requiring formal introduction into evidence at trial stages often reserved for later proceedings like summary judgment or trial proper.**

# Chapter 2: The Four-Factor Fair Use Analysis

## Overview of Fair Use Doctrine as an Affirmative Defense
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit addressed a classic tension in intellectual property law: the protection of an author's exclusive rights versus the public interest in free expression and commentary. Former Representative George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and ABC for copyright infringement after Kimmel aired video clips Santos had recorded on the Cameo platform, using them in a satirical segment mocking Santos's political statements. The District Court dismissed the complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), ruling that Kimmel's actions constituted "fair use." On appeal, the Second Circuit affirmed, applying the four statutory factors set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107 to determine that the use was fair.

Fair use operates as an affirmative defense that excuses conduct that would otherwise constitute infringement. Its primary function is to prevent the Copyright Act from stifling the very creativity it aims to promote by allowing courts flexibility in applying rigid rules. As noted in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, fair use is a "flexible concept" where no single factor is dispositive; rather, all four factors must be weighed together within the specific context of the case. However, when a defendant can demonstrate that fair use is "clearly established" based on the facts alleged in a complaint, a plaintiff cannot survive a motion to dismiss. In *Santos*, the appellate court found that Kimmel's parody fell squarely within these bounds, rendering further litigation unnecessary at this stage.

## Factor One (Purpose/Character): Transformative Use Defined
The first factor of the fair use test examines "the purpose and character of the use," with a particular focus on whether the new work is "transformative." Citing *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the court explained that a use is transformative if it adds something new with a further purpose or different character, rather than merely superseding or replacing the original object. In *Santos*, Santos argued that Kimmel's use was not transformative because Kimmel had instigated the creation of the videos and was essentially repurposing them for his own commercial ends ("Will Santos Say It?"). Specifically, Santos contended that since he originally made the videos to convey messages of hope and positivity, using them to mock his willingness to say absurd things for money distorted their original intent.

The Second Circuit rejected this argument by drawing a sharp distinction between subjective intent and objective perception. The court held that whether a secondary use is transformative depends on what a "reasonable observer" perceives, not on what either party subjectively intended when creating or using the work. While Santos intended his videos to be inspirational, a reasonable viewer watching Kimmel's segment would perceive it as social commentary criticizing Santos's political persona and willingness to sell content for money—a distinct purpose from inspiration or encouragement. Consequently, despite Santos's assertion that he designed his videos for monetary gain just as Kimmel did later, the objective transformation of meaning satisfied this prong of Factor One strongly supporting fair use.

Furthermore, Santos attempted to argue that Kimmel acted in bad faith by falsely representing himself during his initial interactions on Cameo, citing *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* for the proposition that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing. The court acknowledged *Harper & Row* but clarified that subsequent jurisprudence has distanced itself from treating "bad faith" as an absolute bar to fair use in this context. More importantly, even assuming some deception occurred during acquisition, it did not alter the fundamental nature of Kimmel's ultimate purpose: sarcastic criticism and commentary on public figures like Santos does not serve to supplant commercially valuable rights but rather engages in protected speech under Section 230 principles often associated with media liability (though not explicitly cited here). Thus, any alleged bad faith did not negate the transformative character of Kimmel's parody nor defeat his claim to fair use under Factor One.

## Factor Two (Nature of Work)
The second factor considers "the nature of the copyrighted work." This inquiry generally favors fair use when dealing with factual works over highly creative ones and disfavors it when applied to unpublished materials where authors retain control over first publication rights (*Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*). In *Santos*, however, this factor played little role in shifting balance away from fair use because both parties agreed on key facts regarding publication status and creative nature derived directly from copyright registration records attached to the complaint (judicially noticed per local precedent). 

Specifically, evidence showed that Santos had registered copyrights for each video immediately upon creation—effectively publishing them simultaneously with their completion—and thus they were already disseminated publicly before being used by Kimmel. Since there was no suppression or delay inherent in private drafts or unpublished manuscripts at stake here, this factor neither significantly aided nor hindered either side’s position; it remained neutral or slightly favorable toward fairness given public availability prior to alleged misuse. # Chapter 3: Preemption Failure – Contractual Claims Against Non-Parties

Having resolved the federal copyright claims based on the fair use doctrine, the Second Circuit turned its attention to Former Representative George Santos's state law causes of action. While the District Court had initially dismissed Santos's breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims on the grounds that they were preempted by the Copyright Act, the appellate court found it unnecessary to reach that complex preemption question. Instead, the court affirmed the dismissal on independent and adequate grounds: Santos failed to state a valid claim for relief under applicable state law principles regarding contractual privity and standing. This section examines why Santos could not maintain these claims against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC/Disney, focusing specifically on the lack of privity between a content creator and a platform user who signed separate terms of service agreements.

## Direct Breach Claim Analysis: Privity Requirements Under Illinois Law for Third-Party Beneficiaries

Santos's primary state law challenge involved a direct claim for breach of contract based on the "Terms of Service" governing the Cameo platform. The core legal obstacle here was the doctrine of privity, which generally requires that only parties to a contract can enforce its terms or be held liable for its breach. The Cameo Terms of Service were governed by Illinois law, which adheres to a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to non-contracting third parties." *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). Under this standard, for a third party like Santos—who never agreed to or signed the Terms—to sue for their enforcement, there must be an express declaration within the contract stating that such provisions were intended to benefit him or others similarly situated.

The Second Circuit noted that while some provisions in Cameo's Terms explicitly included third-party beneficiary language, indicating that drafters knew how to create such rights when intended, no such language existed regarding the obligations Kimmel allegedly violated toward Santos. Consequently, Santos identified nothing approaching an "express declaration" extending these contractual protections to him as a non-signatory user of the platform. Without this explicit intent manifested in the text of the agreement, Illinois law presumes that Kimmel and Cameo did not intend to create enforceable rights in favor of Santos simply because he provided content through their system. Therefore, lacking privity and failing to meet the high bar for establishing third-party beneficiary status under Illinois law, Santos could not sustain a direct breach of contract claim against Kimmel or ABC based solely on violations of terms he never agreed to accept.

## Implied Contracts Under New York Law: Allegations Essential Terms and Meeting of Minds

Even if one attempted to bypass strict written contract requirements by arguing for an implied-in-fact contract between Santos and Kimmel/ABC, New York law presented another insurmountable hurdle for Santos's pleading sufficiency. When analyzing implied contracts under New York jurisdiction—which applied here—the court looks at whether there was an indication of a "meeting of minds" constituting an agreement between specific parties regarding specific terms. *Rocky Point Props., Inc.* v *Sear-Brown Grp.*, Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269 (4th Dep't 2002). To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a plaintiff must allege more than conclusory statements; they must plead with specificity "essential terms" of the alleged oral or implied agreement and identify exactly where mutual assent occurred between themselves and the defendant regarding those terms.

In *Santos*, the complaint failed catastrophically in this regard. The court observed that Santos did not allege any non-conclusory facts showing that he ever negotiated with Kimmel or ABC regarding specific performance expectations outside of what was mandated by public policy or existing statutory frameworks like copyright law. Furthermore, there was no plausible allegation suggesting a "meeting of minds" where Kimmel promised to adhere strictly to Cameo's Terms as if he were personally bound by them in his interactions with Santos as an individual contractor rather than as a platform user interacting via an automated interface. The absence of pleaded essential terms—such as specific promises made directly between Santos and Kimmel—meant that no implied contract could be reasonably inferred from the facts alleged in the complaint alone. Thus, regardless of whether preemption applied, these claims failed because they did not state legally cognizable causes of action under New York common law due to vagueness and lack of factual foundation regarding mutual assent.
