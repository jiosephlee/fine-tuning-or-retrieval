Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can a plaintiff win a 'transformative' fair use argument when they themselves initiated the creation of the video content?
Question:
The court emphasizes that the 'purpose and character' factor hinges on whether the use is transformative, citing *Andy Warhol Foundation*. Santos argues that because Kimmel instigated the request via Cameo, the videos weren't truly transformative but rather just a replication of Santos's original commercial purpose. However, the court dismisses this by stating that 'subjective intent' doesn't matter—only what a 'reasonable observer' perceives. I am confused: If Santos explicitly asked to make these videos for money, how does the court justify that a reasonable observer sees them as 'commentary' rather than just a repurposing of his own commercial product? Does the context of being aired on a late-night talk show automatically override the origin story?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court resolves the tension between Santos's commercial motivation and the "transformative" requirement by strictly applying the **objective "reasonable observer" test**, explicitly rejecting the relevance of subjective intent regarding who originated the request.

Under the first fair use factor (*purpose and character*), the Supreme Court in *Andy Warhol* clarified that the inquiry focuses on whether the new work adds something new with a further purpose or different character, superseding the original. Crucially, as cited in *Santos*, this determination is based on **what a reasonable observer perceives**, not the subjective intent of either party. The court notes that while Santos argued Kimmel was merely replicating his own commercial purpose because Kimmel instigated the request, this factual background is irrelevant to the statutory analysis.

The justification lies in the discrepancy between Santos's original perception and Kimmel's secondary use. As acknowledged in Santos's own complaint, a reasonable viewer watching the videos originally thought they conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." By contrast, when aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking segment titled "Will Santos Say It?", a reasonable observer perceives a completely different message: **"commentary on... [Kimmel's] willingness to say absurd things for money."**

Therefore, the context of airing on a late-night talk show does not automatically override everything; rather, it provides the specific lens through which a reasonable observer interprets the work. The court holds that because the secondary use conveys a distinct satirical message (mockery/criticism) compared to the original perceived message (positivity), it is transformative regardless of who paid for or requested its initial creation. The fact that Santos sought profit initially does not prevent Kimmel from using that footage to critique that very profit-seeking behavior in a way that changes its character entirely.
