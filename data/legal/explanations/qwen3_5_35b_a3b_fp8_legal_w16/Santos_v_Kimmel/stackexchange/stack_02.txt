Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does *Harper & Row*'s requirement of 'good faith' still bar fair use if a defendant lies to obtain copyrighted material?
Question:
Santos argued that Kimmel's deception (using fictitious names to trick him into creating content) demonstrated bad faith, which should theoretically negate the fair use defense under *Harper & Row*. The court mentions that the Supreme Court has since 'distanced itself' from the *Harper & Row* statement regarding good faith. Could someone clarify exactly how far that distancing went? Is lying now irrelevant to fair use analysis entirely, or does it only matter if it proves an intent to 'supplant' the market? Why didn't this bad faith behavior prevent dismissal at the pleading stage?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
No, *Harper & Row*'s "good faith" requirement no longer serves as a standalone bar to fair use simply because a defendant lied to obtain material. While the Supreme Court originally stated that fair use presupposes good faith, the Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* confirms that subsequent jurisprudence has effectively "distanced" itself from that categorical rule. Instead, deceptive conduct like lying is now analyzed strictly through the lens of **market substitution**. Unless the bad faith demonstrates an intent to "supplant" the copyright owner's commercial market—rather than merely engaging in criticism or parody—the deception does not defeat the fair use defense.

### Detailed Analysis

**1. The Shift Away from "Good Faith" as a Separate Element**
The Supreme Court's 1985 decision in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.* famously declared that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." However, as noted by the Second Circuit in *Santos*, later cases have moved away from treating this statement as a rigid prerequisite. The court explicitly rejects Santos's argument that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to induce video creation automatically negates fair use based solely on this principle. The modern approach treats "good faith" not as an independent threshold test, but rather as evidence relevant to the statutory factors, specifically whether the use harms the market for the original work.

**2. The Relevance of Bad Faith: Market Supplantation vs. Criticism**
Under current law, bad faith behavior (such as deception) matters only if it proves an intent to **supplant** the market for the original work. The court explains that if a secondary user acts with bad faith but their ultimate goal remains commentary or criticism—without seeking to act as a commercial competitor—they remain protected by fair use.

In *Santos*, the court found that despite Kimmel's deceptive tactics, the complaint did not allege an intent to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights. On the contrary, the record showed Kimmel was motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Therefore, the lack of good faith did not tip the balance against fair use because it did not transform Kimmel's activity into a market substitute for Santos's videos.

**3. Why Dismissal Was Proper at Pleading Stage**
You asked why this bad faith behavior prevented dismissal at the pleading stage (Rule 12(b)(6)). The answer lies in how courts evaluate allegations of market harm under Factor Four of § 107:
*   **Legal Standard:** Courts distinguish between harm caused by criticism/parody (which is expected and allowed) and harm caused by a competing substitute (which is prohibited). As cited in *Hachette Book Group*, "we ask not whether the second work would damage the market... but whether it usurps the market... by offering a competing substitute."
*   **Application:** Since Santos conceded his videos were created for positive messaging ("hope," "strength") while Kimmel used them for mockery ("absurd things"), a reasonable observer sees these as distinct purposes with no overlap in potential markets. Even assuming Kimmel lied to get there, he did not create a product that competes with Santos's product; he created a parody/commentary piece that happens to utilize Santos's material for satire. Consequently, Santos failed to plausibly allege that Kimmel's bad faith resulted in actionable market harm sufficient to overcome fair use at the outset of litigation.

### Conclusion
*Lying* is no longer fatal to a fair use defense on its own face. It is only dispositive if it serves as proof that the defendant intended to enter into direct competition with or replace the copyright holder's work (*supplant*). Because Santos could not allege that Kimmel sought to monetize or compete with his videos—only mock them—the deceptive acquisition method did not preclude dismissal via summary judgment or motion to dismiss based on fair use.
