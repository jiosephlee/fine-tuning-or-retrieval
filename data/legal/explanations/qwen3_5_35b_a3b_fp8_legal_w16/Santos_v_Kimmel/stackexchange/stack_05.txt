Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Is proving 'out-of-pocket loss' strictly necessary for fraudulent inducement claims involving intangible assets like personal videos?
Question:
The court dismissed Santos's fraudulent inducement claim solely because he failed to allege any 'out-of-pocket loss.' This seems counterintuitive given he was tricked into creating content he wouldn't have made otherwise. If I lie to get someone to create art I then sell or broadcast, shouldn't their lost opportunity or diminished value count as damages even if they didn't spend cash out-of-pocket during production? Or does New York law require tangible monetary expenditure regardless of whether fraud occurred?
Answer:
Under New York law, alleging "out-of-pocket loss" is indeed a strict requirement for stating a valid claim of fraudulent inducement, even when the plaintiff claims they were tricked into performing services or creating intangible assets they otherwise would not have created.

As affirmed in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit held that Santos's fraudulent inducement claim failed specifically because he did not allege any **actual out-of-pocket loss**. The court relied on the standard set forth in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), and reaffirmed in *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017), which mandates that a plaintiff must prove they actually spent money or parted with property as a result of the fraud to recover damages.

The court explicitly rejected the argument that the creation of personal videos constituted a sufficient loss simply because Santos claimed he would not have produced them absent the deception. While Santos argued he suffered a "lost opportunity" or that his labor had diminished value because it was used for mockery rather than his intended commercial purpose, New York law distinguishes between general economic injury and the specific element of out-of-pocket expenditure required for this tort. The appellate court noted that Santos "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss," thereby precluding recovery under this theory regardless of whether the fraud induced him to expend time or effort on intangible creations.

Therefore, unlike some jurisdictions that might recognize reliance damages based on the value of services rendered or opportunity costs alone, New York requires a tangible monetary expenditure (or similar asset transfer) that leaves the plaintiff poorer than they were before the fraud occurred. In *Santos*, because he received payment via Cameo for each video and incurred no direct financial cost beyond his time—which does not satisfy the out-of-pocket requirement—his claim could not survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6).
