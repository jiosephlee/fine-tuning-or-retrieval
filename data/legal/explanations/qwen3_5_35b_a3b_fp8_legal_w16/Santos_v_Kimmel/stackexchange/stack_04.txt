Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What constitutes an 'implied-in-fact contract' in digital interactions where no explicit agreement exists between opposing parties?
Question:
Regarding the implied contract claim under New York law, the court says Santos failed to allege 'essential terms' or a 'meeting of minds.' In my understanding, implied contracts often arise from conduct (like accepting payment for work). Here, Kimmel paid for videos via Cameo. Why did the court find no meeting of minds? Was it simply because they didn't agree on specific terms beyond what was in Cameo's boilerplate (which neither signed directly), or is there something deeper about why online platform transactions don't create mutual contractual obligations between random users who interact through intermediaries?
Answer:
**The Short Answer**
Under New York law, the court found no "meeting of the minds" because Santos failed to plead **specific factual allegations** demonstrating that Kimmel and Santos mutually agreed to be bound by the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS) outside of the formal platform agreement. The mere fact that Kimmel paid for a video and Santos accepted payment does not automatically create a binding contract between them regarding the *terms* of that transaction (such as copyright waivers or usage rights) unless the plaintiff can allege that both parties specifically intended to adopt those external terms into their interaction.

**Detailed Analysis**

**1. The Requirement for "Essential Terms"**
While implied-in-fact contracts generally arise from conduct rather than written signatures, New York law still requires a plaintiff to plead more than just the existence of a relationship. As noted in *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, a complaint alleging an implied-in-fact contract must specify **"the essential terms of the parties' ... contract."**

In *Santos*, the court emphasized that Santos's complaint failed to identify these essential terms in "non-conclusory language." Specifically, Santos could not point to specific provisions within the Cameo ToS that he and Kimmel explicitly agreed to follow during their individual exchange. Without identifying *what* exactly was agreed upon (beyond the general expectation that a video would be made), there is no enforceable contract to breach.

**2. The Absence of a "Meeting of Minds"**
An implied-in-fact contract requires an objective manifestation of mutual assent—a "meeting of minds"—that one party will perform according to specific conditions understood by both. The court cited *DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass'n Compliance & Legal Div.* to explain that Santos did not plausibly allege **"an indication of a meeting of minds... that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service."**

The court distinguished this scenario from cases where parties clearly intend to incorporate external terms by conduct alone. Here, the record showed:
*   **Kimmel's Perspective:** There was no evidence Kimmel read, reviewed, or intended to be bound by the full text of Cameo's ToS regarding third-party beneficiaries or copyright assignments during his specific interaction with Santos.
*   **Santos's Perspective:** While Santos knew he had signed up for Cameo, he could not prove that Kimmel (a separate user) shared an intent to adopt those same boilerplate terms for their private transaction.

**3. Why Platform Transactions Don't Automatically Create User-to-User Contracts**
You asked if there is something deeper about online platforms preventing these obligations between random users. The court addressed this indirectly through Illinois law applied to third-party beneficiaries but clarified via New York contract principles: **parties cannot be bound by terms they never discussed.**

The court rejected the argument that using an intermediary (Cameo) creates a web of contractual obligations between all participants automatically. Instead, it held that:
*   The ToS is primarily an agreement between the User (Kimmel) and the Platform (Cameo).
*   For a User-to-User implied contract to exist based on those ToS, there must be proof that both users intended for those specific terms to govern their direct interaction.
*   Mere payment and acceptance are insufficient if they do not indicate an agreement on *how* the content would be used or owned beyond basic compensation for services rendered.

**Conclusion**
The dismissal was not based on a rule that "online transactions never form contracts," but rather on procedural insufficiency under New York pleading standards (*Rule 12(b)(6)*). Santos failed to allege facts showing that Kimmel and Santos reached a mutual understanding ("meeting of minds") regarding specific contractual terms like copyright ownership or usage restrictions solely through their conduct on the platform. Without those pleaded facts, no implied-in-fact contract existed between them as a matter of law at the pleading stage.
