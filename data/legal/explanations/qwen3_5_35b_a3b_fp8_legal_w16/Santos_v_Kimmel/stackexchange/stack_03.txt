Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a public figure sue for breach of contract based on platform Terms of Service when they never agreed to those terms?
Question:
The court affirmed dismissal of Santos's breach of contract claim because he wasn't a signatory to the Cameo Terms of Service. The opinion cites Illinois law regarding third-party beneficiaries, noting there is a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits.' Since Kimmel signed up as a user and Santos was essentially treated as a service provider on that platform, isn't there an expectation that both parties are bound by the same rules? How strict must an agreement be before non-signatories like Santos can enforce its terms, especially when they arguably relied on those terms to perform services?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No. Under Illinois law governing the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS), a non-signatory like Santos cannot enforce the contract unless there is an "express declaration" or language so strong it amounts to one. Mere participation as a service provider on the platform, or reliance on the terms to perform services, does not overcome the "strong presumption against conferring benefits" to third parties.

### Legal Analysis

The core issue is **third-party beneficiary status**. While you argue that there is an expectation that all participants—both requestors and providers—are bound by the same rules, contract law requires more than just functional interaction; it requires evidence of intent to create rights for non-signatories.

Under **Illinois law**, which controls the interpretation of the Cameo ToS, there is a **"strong presumption against conferring benefits"** on non-contracting third parties (*Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp.*). The court in *Santos* explains that for a third party to enforce a contract, "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

The opinion highlights two key reasons why Santos fails this test:

1.  **Lack of Express Declaration:** The court notes that if the drafters of the ToS intended for certain provisions to benefit third parties (such as content providers), they included specific third-party beneficiary language elsewhere in the document. Because no such clause explicitly extends these protections or obligations to Santos, he remains outside the contractual relationship between Kimmel and Cameo.
2.  **No Implied Contract:** Even looking at New York law (which governs some aspects of his claim), an implied-in-fact contract requires a "meeting of minds." The court found Santos failed to allege specific facts showing that Kimmel and Santos actually agreed to be bound by the ToS terms directly between themselves. Merely fulfilling requests on a platform where another party agreed to terms does not automatically create a bilateral contract between the requester and the performer absent explicit agreement or clear evidence of mutual assent to those specific terms.

Regarding your point about **reliance**: The court did not find that performing services creates an automatic contractual bond with every other user who signs up for the platform. Without an express term stating that service providers are intended beneficiaries, reliance alone is insufficient to pierce the privity barrier under Illinois law. The "expectation" that everyone follows the rules is a matter of platform policy, not necessarily binding contract law between private individuals who never exchanged promises directly governed by those terms.

Therefore, because Santos was not a signatory and could not point to an express declaration granting him standing as a third-party beneficiary, his breach of contract claim was correctly dismissed for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6).
