Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the D.C. Circuit distinguish between a 'standalone' declaration and an 'update' when determining if Exemption 7(A) pendency requirements are met?
Question:
The court affirms the FBI's use of a brief 'Bender declaration' to confirm that an investigation remains ongoing, despite Farahi arguing it was conclusory. The court accepts this because it views the Bender declaration as merely updating the earlier, detailed 'Hardy declarations.' I am confused about the evidentiary threshold here: Does this mean that once an agency establishes pendency with a robust affidavit, subsequent updates can be significantly thinner? How far can an agency push this logic before a plaintiff can successfully argue that the agency has failed to meet its burden of proof regarding current pendency?
Answer:
In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit distinguishes between "standalone" and "update" declarations by analyzing the **contextual relationship** between the new affidavit and prior, detailed submissions, rather than evaluating the new affidavit in a vacuum. The court holds that an agency need not submit a fresh, comprehensive declaration every time it seeks to extend a claim of pendency if it has previously established a robust factual foundation for that claim.

The distinction turns on whether the new declaration serves as the primary vehicle for establishing pendent status or merely confirms that existing conditions persist. Here, the court reasoned that David Hardy's original declarations—both public and ex parte—had already satisfied the burden of proving that an investigation existed and detailing its nature. Consequently, Joseph Bender's subsequent "brief" declaration was treated not as a standalone attempt to prove pendency from scratch, but as a procedural mechanism to **"confirm[] that nothing had changed"** during the litigation gap. The court explicitly stated: *"The government submitted the Bender declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed... So, the Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations."*

This approach allows agencies to rely on "thin" updates once they have laid a sufficient evidentiary groundwork. However, this logic is not unlimited; it relies heavily on two factors present in *Farahi*:
1.  **Prior Specificity:** There must be an earlier record (like Hardy's) containing "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation."
2.  **Judicial Context:** The lower court must have already acknowledged that the agency had "seemingly" met its initial burden before ordering an update.

An agency pushes this logic too far if it attempts to use a short update declaration where no prior detailed affidavit exists, or where the passage of time suggests a fundamental change in circumstances that requires re-establishment of facts rather than mere confirmation. As noted in *CREW*, vague assertions alone are insufficient (*Farahi* cites *CREW* for this proposition). Therefore, while *Farahi* permits abbreviated updates, it does so only because those updates are tethered to a substantive historical record that validates their accuracy. If an agency lacks that anchor—or if the plaintiff presents evidence suggesting the investigation has actually terminated—the "update" theory collapses into an impermissible conclusory assertion.

### What is the practical application of 'inextricably intertwined' vs. 'random interspersion' in FOIA segregability analysis for large volumes of data?
Question:
Section V discusses segregability. The court rejects Farahi's argument that there must be some releasable info just because there are thousands of pages, citing *Mead*. They state that if non-exempt material is 'interspersed line-by-line,' it isn't reasonably segregable. But my confusion lies in the definition of 'meaningful information.' If I have 10,000 pages where 99% is redacted under 7(A), and the remaining 1% consists of isolated names or dates scattered throughout, why is extracting those specific fragments legally impossible or unreasonable? Is the test purely about whether the extracted text conveys independent meaning without revealing the exempt context?
Answer:
**Answer:**

The distinction between "inextricably intertwined" and "random interspersion" in *Farahi* turns entirely on **informational utility**, not merely the physical possibility of extraction. Under the D.C. Circuit's precedent cited in *Farahi*, specifically *Mead Data Cent.*, the core inquiry is whether the non-exempt fragments convey **"meaningful information"** independently of the exempt context.

If you have 10,000 pages where 99% is redacted and the remaining 1% consists of isolated names or dates, the court holds that extracting those fragments is legally unreasonable because they lack independent semantic value. As the opinion explains, "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." (*Mead*, quoted in *Farahi*). 

Here is how the doctrine applies to your hypothetical:

1.  **Random Interspersion = Not Reasonably Segregable:** The court explicitly states that if non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line," it is not reasonably segregable. In your scenario, isolated names or dates scattered throughout a file dominated by sensitive investigative details constitute "random interspersion." Even if a clerk could technically copy-paste those few words into a new document, doing so yields no "meaningful information" because the context required to understand their significance (and thus their relevance to a requester) has been excised along with the exempt material.
2.  **No Requirement for Volume-Based Disclosure:** The court rejects Farahi's argument that a massive volume of records (10,750 pages) automatically necessitates some disclosure simply because statistically some non-exempt text must exist. The holding clarifies that quantity does not equal quality; if every scrap of releasable text is so fragmented that it cannot stand alone as useful information, the agency need not release it.
3.  **The Practical Standard:** Therefore, the test is not whether you can physically separate the ink from the paper (which you can), but whether what remains constitutes **"information."** If extracting those specific fragments results in a list of unconnected proper nouns or dates that reveal nothing about Farahi's status or rights without revealing the redacted investigative context, they are deemed "inextricably intertwined" with the exempt portions for legal purposes.

In short, *Farahi* establishes that agencies are not required to perform "needle-in-a-haystack" operations where the resulting needles provide no usable intelligence. If the extracted text offers no independent informational value because it relies entirely on the redacted context to make sense, it falls under *Mead*'s exception and need not be released.

### Can a defendant effectively waive their right to challenge ex parte/sealed submissions by failing to explicitly oppose their admission into the record?
Question:
In paragraph V, the court notes that Farahi cannot complain about the district court relying heavily on Bender's ex parte declaration for segregability analysis because 'Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations.' This seems like a major procedural trap. If I am representing a FOIA requester and I suspect my opponent will submit sensitive classified info via ex parte affidavit, do I need to file a specific motion objecting to *any* potential reliance on sealed materials, even if I don't know exactly what they contain yet? Or does silence equal waiver for all future challenges regarding those specific sealed submissions?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Under *Farahi*, a FOIA plaintiff effectively waives the right to challenge the district court's reliance on *ex parte* or sealed submissions by failing to explicitly object to their admission into the record. Silence operates as a forfeiture of that specific procedural objection, regardless of whether the plaintiff knew the exact contents of the sealed material at the time.

**Legal Analysis**

The D.C. Circuit's decision in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* establishes that while *ex parte* declarations are "inherent" and sometimes necessary in national security FOIA cases, a litigant cannot remain silent regarding their introduction and then appeal based on the weight given to them later.

In **Section V**, the Court addresses Farahi's complaint that the district court relied "too much" on Agent Bender's *ex parte* declaration for its segregability analysis. The Court rejects this argument on two grounds:
1.  **Procedural Waiver:** The Court states plainly: *"For one thing, Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."* This language indicates that the failure to object to the *acceptance* of the sealed filing precludes challenging its subsequent *use*.
2.  **Necessity of Sealed Review:** The Court notes that while such filings disadvantage plaintiffs, they are often unavoidable when disclosure would reveal the very secrets being protected (*Perioperative Servs.*). Consequently, once a party fails to contest the entry of these materials, they accept the procedural reality that they will be part of the evidentiary record available to the judge for review.

**Practical Implication for Counsel**

You absolutely must file a specific objection if you wish to preserve this issue for appeal. You cannot argue on appeal that an undisclosed affidavit was improperly weighed if you never told the district court you objected to its inclusion in the first place.

As a matter of strategy, you do not necessarily need to know every word in a proposed sealed declaration before opposing it if you believe it violates discovery rules or exceeds statutory authority. A timely motion stating that you object to any consideration of unredacted, unopposed sealed materials—arguing either that they violate due process principles or that they should not be admitted without notice—is sufficient to avoid waiver under *Farahi*. By contrast, doing nothing signals consent ("did not oppose"), which binds you under appellate precedent.

### Does conceding involvement in terrorist activities during parallel immigration proceedings automatically satisfy the 'law enforcement purpose' nexus requirement for FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Section IV-A, the court relies partly on Farahi's own concession from his immigration case (where he admitted being linked to terrorists) to find that his FBI file was compiled for law enforcement purposes. Usually, courts require a specific nexus between the document creation and law enforcement duties. Here, it seems like admitting guilt/liability in another forum helped satisfy this element here. Does this create a precedent where a party's admissions in administrative or civil litigation can shortcut the government's burden to prove the original intent behind compiling records under Exemption 7?
Answer:
No, Farahi's concessions in his immigration proceedings did not "shortcut" the government's burden or create a precedent allowing admissions in other forums to automatically satisfy the "law enforcement purpose" nexus. Instead, the D.C. Circuit treated Farahi's admissions as **confirmatory evidence** that bolstered the FBI's existing declarations, rather than serving as a substitute for the agency's primary obligation to demonstrate a rational nexus between the file and its law enforcement duties.

Under *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998), the government must establish two elements for Exemption 7(A): (1) a "rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," and (2) a connection between an individual/incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. While courts grant deference to the FBI because it is a specialized law enforcement agency, that deference does not eliminate the need for a factual basis showing why records were compiled for those specific purposes (*Farahi* at Part IV-A).

In *Farahi*, the court explicitly stated that it agreed with the district court because **"The public Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled 'in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security...'"** The court then noted that Farahi's concession—that he had been investigated for ties to foreign terrorist organizations—provided **"broader context"** confirming that assertion. Crucially, the opinion frames the concession as supporting evidence ("And broader context confirms that assertion") rather than dispositive proof standing alone. The court affirmed that "we have no doubt that the FBI carried its burden," implying that the burden rested first on the FBI's declarations, which were then reinforced by Farahi's own admissions in prior litigation.

This distinction matters significantly for precedent. If an admission in another forum could independently satisfy Exemption 7(A), it would bypass the requirement for agencies to submit affidavits explaining their investigative rationale at the time of compilation. The *Farahi* opinion avoids this by relying on a combination of factors:
1.  **Agency Declarations:** The Hardy and Bender declarations establishing ongoing investigations into national security threats.
2.  **Subject Matter Context:** Public allegations regarding Farahi's role as a recruiter and his links to specific groups (Muslim Brotherhood, Al Qaeda).
3.  **Litigation Admissions:** Farahi's own stipulation regarding his status as an enemy combatant suspect or associate, which removed ambiguity about whether his file related to terrorism investigations.

Therefore, *Farahi* does not hold that admissions in administrative or civil litigation shortcut the government's burden. Rather, it illustrates that when an agency has already made prima facie assertions about an investigation's scope, a plaintiff's contradictory conduct or admissions can help resolve ambiguities regarding whether those assertions constitute a valid "law enforcement purpose." The ultimate holding rests on the totality of evidence—including specific agency affidavits—not merely on procedural concessions made in unrelated forums.

### Is there a difference between 'pending' and 'reasonably anticipated' proceedings when dealing with ongoing criminal investigations versus civil immigration hearings?
Question:
The opinion mentions that 'ongoing criminal investigations count as proceedings' under 7(A). However, Farahi's background involves both criminal investigations and immigration (civil) removal proceedings. The court cites *Juarez* regarding gathering evidence for a 'possible future criminal case.' My confusion is whether Exemption 7(A) treats an active internal FBI intelligence gathering phase differently than a formal indictment or an open immigration hearing. Specifically, does the threat of interference apply equally if no charges have been filed yet but only an internal intelligence investigation is underway?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Under D.C. Circuit precedent applied in *Farahi*, there is **no functional distinction** between "pending" and "reasonably anticipated" proceedings for the purpose of triggering Exemption 7(A) when an investigation is actively gathering evidence. An ongoing internal FBI intelligence investigation qualifies as a "proceeding," even absent a formal indictment or a filed civil charge, provided the investigation is concrete and its premature disclosure would jeopardize the potential future case.

### Legal Analysis

**1. The Scope of "Proceedings" Includes Active Investigations**
The core of your confusion lies in whether an internal intelligence-gathering phase counts as a "proceeding." The court explicitly resolves this by citing *Juarez v. DOJ*. The opinion states: *"Ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes... [and] so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."* (*Opinion* at IV.B).

This means the threshold is not the filing of a complaint or indictment, nor the initiation of a formal administrative hearing (like an immigration removal proceeding). Instead, the trigger is the **active status of the investigative process**. If agents are gathering evidence to build a potential future case, that phase constitutes a "proceeding" under Exemption 7(A).

**2. Application to Criminal vs. Civil Contexts**
While Farahi's situation involves both criminal investigations and civil immigration removal proceedings, *Farahi* clarifies that they need not be treated differently regarding the definition of "pending."
*   **Criminal Phase:** The court affirms that an internal FBI investigation into terrorism ties (even before charges are filed) satisfies the requirement. The threat of interference arises because releasing records now could tip off targets about the scope of the inquiry, thereby compromising evidence collection for a *possible future criminal case*.
*   **Civil Phase:** While Farahi has been in immigration removal proceedings since 2002, the court's affirmation relies heavily on the fact that the **criminal/law enforcement investigation remains ongoing**. The court notes that Farahi himself conceded he was investigated for ties to terrorist organizations and enemy combatants.

The opinion does not suggest that an internal intelligence gathering phase carries less weight than a formal hearing. Rather, it holds that if an agency declares an investigation is ongoing and explains that disclosure would harm that specific investigative effort (by alerting subjects), Exemption 7(A) applies regardless of whether a formal judicial or administrative proceeding has formally commenced against the subject at that exact moment.

**3. Sufficiency of Agency Declarations**
You asked if the threat applies equally if no charges are filed yet but only an internal investigation is underway. Yes. The court emphasizes deference to FBI declarations regarding national security investigations (*Campbell*; *CNSS*). Even though Farahi argued that Bender's declaration ("investigations remain ongoing") was conclusory given his age, the court rejected this because it viewed it as an update to earlier detailed declarations (Hardy) rather than a standalone claim.

The standard requires only that the government show:
1.  A rational nexus between the investigation and law enforcement duties; and
2.  That disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with those proceedings (i.e., tipping off targets).

Since *Juarez* establishes that gathering evidence for a "possible future criminal case" is sufficient, an active internal probe meets this standard just as effectively as a pre-indictment grand jury proceeding or an open immigration hearing would in terms of satisfying Exemption 7(A).

### Conclusion
In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit held that **an active internal FBI intelligence investigation is synonymous with a "pending or reasonably anticipated" proceeding.** You do not need waiting for charges to be filed or for a civil hearing to begin to satisfy Exemption 7(A). If the agency demonstrates through affidavit that they are currently gathering evidence for a potential case and release would compromise that effort, Exemption 7(A) shields those records from disclosure via FOIA.

### How strictly must an agency define document categories when withholding files on a categorical basis, especially when national security is involved?
Question:
In Section IV-C, Farahi argued that the FBI didn't define its document categories sufficiently distinctively to allow judicial review. The court dismissed this because Farahi offered no specific explanation of *why* they were overbroad ('forfeited his arguments'). This raises two questions: First, does this imply that if a plaintiff doesn't know enough about the content to critique categories (because they haven't seen them), they are stuck? Second, does deference increase so much in national security cases that generic categories (like 'investigative reports') are sufficient unless challenged with specific examples?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** You are not "stuck" forever if you don't know the content, but the burden is on you to articulate *how* a category is overbroad once the agency has provided a prima facie justification. In national security contexts involving Exemption 7(A), courts apply heightened deference, meaning generic descriptions (like "investigative reports") often suffice to satisfy the agency's burden of proof, provided they are tied to a specific investigation. If a plaintiff challenges such categories without offering specific examples or logical reasoning explaining why the category captures non-exempt material, the argument is typically deemed forfeited.

**Detailed Analysis**

Based on *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit addresses your two questions through its handling of Section IV-C regarding categorization and segregability.

**1. The Plaintiff's Burden vs. The "Catch-22"**
You asked if a plaintiff who hasn't seen the documents is stuck because they cannot critique categories. The court suggests that while lack of access creates a hurdle, it does not absolve the plaintiff of their duty to argue with specificity once the agency has made its case.

In Section IV-C, the court noted that when an agency withholds documents on a categorical basis, it must use categories "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied." However, the court immediately clarified that **Farahi failed because he offered no specific explanation for why the FBI's categories were overbroad.** By citing *Khine v. DHS*, the court ruled that Farahi "accordingly forfeited his arguments on this score."

This implies that while an agency cannot hide behind vague assertions indefinitely (it must provide "reasonable specificity"), a plaintiff cannot simply demand more detail without first articulating *what* is wrong with the current description. If the agency explains *why* a category fits within an exemption (e.g., linking "investigative reports" to ongoing counter-terrorism work), and the plaintiff merely asserts "this is too broad" without pointing out specific types of non-exempt data contained therein, the court will treat that objection as waived. The plaintiff isn't barred from arguing; they are barred from failing to meet their burden of persuasion given the information available in public declarations and ex parte submissions.

**2. Deference in National Security Cases and Generic Categories**
Regarding your second question: Yes, deference increases significantly in national security cases, making generic categories more likely to survive judicial scrutiny than in routine FOIA requests.

Section III explicitly states that while general deference applies to all FOIA cases involving law enforcement ("fair measure of deference"), **"Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security."** The court cites precedent (*Center for National Security Studies*) reiterating this principle specifically for Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigations.

Because of this heightened deference:
*   **Generic Descriptions Suffice:** The FBI's use of broad terms like "investigative reports" was upheld because they were contextualized by detailed public and ex parte declarations describing Farahi's alleged links to terrorist organizations (Al Qaeda, Muslim Brotherhood) and ongoing investigations into national security violations.
*   **The Nexus Requirement:** While agencies must show a nexus between the investigation and law enforcement duties (*Campbell*), in national security cases, the government need not itemize every single page type if it can demonstrate via affidavit that *all* documents in a broad category fall within that nexus or are inextricably intertwined with exempt material (Section V).
*   **Sealed Declarations Matter:** The court relied heavily on Joseph Bender's ex parte declaration and David Hardy's sealed submission to validate that even broad categories were necessary because releasing them would tip off targets about ongoing investigations (Section IV-B). Since these declarations were unchallenged effectively by Farahi (who did not contest their validity, only their breadth generally), their descriptive power stood unrefuted.

**Conclusion for Your Study**
To succeed in challenging document categories under Exemption 7(A):
1.  **Do not just assert overbreadth.** You must identify specific types of non-exempt information you believe are trapped inside those categories (e.g., "Category X includes administrative logs unrelated to enforcement").
2.  **Leverage Ex Parte Submissions.** If you suspect generic labels are hiding releasable info, look for opportunities to challenge the logic in ex parte filings or argue that even small amounts of text could be segregated without harm (though Section V notes this is difficult if redaction destroys meaning).
3.  **Recognize the Standard.** Remember that in national security cases, if the agency provides a plausible narrative linking broad categories to an active investigation (supported by affidavits), you bear a heavy burden to prove otherwise with concrete specifics rather than theoretical objections.
