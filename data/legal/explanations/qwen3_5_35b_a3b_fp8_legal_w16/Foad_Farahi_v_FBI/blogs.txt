# The 'Update' Doctrine: Sustaining Long-Pending FOIA Withholdings Without New Investigations

**By [Your Name]**

In the world of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, time is often the plaintiff's best friend and the agency's worst nightmare. Records gathered years ago can become stale; investigations can close; and the "reasonable expectation" of interference that justified a withholding today may evaporate tomorrow. Yet, in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals issued a ruling that significantly bolsters an agency's ability to maintain secrecy over decades-old files with minimal new effort. By accepting a brief "update" declaration to bridge a multi-year litigation gap, the court effectively created an "update doctrine" that lowers the barrier for sustaining indefinite withholdings under Exemption 7(A), raising serious questions about the durability of judicial oversight in national security cases.

At its core, *Farahi* addresses a procedural friction point common in complex FOIA suits: What happens when an agency wins on summary judgment but fails to act quickly enough? Here, the FBI initially moved for summary judgment in 2019 regarding Mr. Farahi's massive file—over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs containing sensitive intelligence. The district court granted leave for an update in late 2022, noting that nearly three years had passed since the initial filings. While the court acknowledged the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden initially, it refused to rubber-stamp a decade-old claim without current verification.

The FBI responded not with a new investigative report or a comprehensive re-evaluation of every page, but with two declarations from Joseph Bender, then-Acting Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). One was public; one was ex parte. The public declaration was notably sparse, stating only that RIDS had contacted the investigating agent and confirming that investigations remained "ongoing." It offered little new factual detail beyond what David Hardy had declared four years prior. Nevertheless, the D.C. Circuit affirmed this sufficiency.

Judge Katsas's opinion hinges on a pragmatic reading of administrative reality versus strict evidentiary demands. The court recognized that while *Citizens for Responsive & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW") requires affidavits to show with "reasonable specificity" why documents cannot be released, forcing an agency to generate entirely new evidence for every status check could be unduly burdensome—and perhaps counterproductive—in national security contexts where even acknowledging the existence of certain lines of inquiry can be damaging. Instead, Katsas reasoned that because the government had already established the existence and scope of the investigation in earlier declarations (which included detailed ex parte submissions), Bender's role was merely to confirm that "nothing had changed."

This distinction is crucial for understanding the downstream implications of *Farahi*. By treating Bender's declaration as a corroborative update rather than a standalone evidentiary foundation, the court sidestepped Farahi's argument that vague assertions like "investigations remain ongoing" are insufficient under *CREW*. The court essentially ruled that once an investigation is sufficiently detailed in an initial filing, subsequent updates need only confirm continuity, provided they are anchored by prior specificity and supported by ex parte context where necessary.

However, this reasoning invites scrutiny regarding the balance between transparency and secrecy. If an agency can satisfy its burden simply by pointing back to old declarations and adding a sentence confirming "no change," does Exemption 7(A) cease to function as a temporary shield and instead become a permanent lock? The D.C. Circuit has long emphasized that Exemption 7(A) applies only when proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." In theory, this requires active monitoring by courts to ensure agencies aren't hiding behind stale claims indefinitely. *Farahi* suggests that judicial skepticism should yield somewhat when an agency demonstrates good faith through sequential declarations rather than demanding fresh proof at every turn.

There is also a doctrinal tension regarding segregability highlighted in Part V of the opinion. Farahi argued that given the sheer volume of records (over 10,000 pages), some non-exempt information must exist somewhere within them. The court rejected this based on precedent (*Mead Data Cent.*) stating that random intermingling of exempt and non-exempt text renders segregation unreasonable if it would destroy meaning or reveal protected patterns even if individual words were redacted. Combined with the deference paid to Bender's ex parte submission on segregability issues—which were never fully aired publicly—the result is a robust protection for agencies facing voluminous requests involving national security subjects like terrorism associations or enemy combatant links.

From a policy perspective, *Farahi* offers agencies valuable breathing room but risks eroding public trust in FOIA's promise of accountability. The decision acknowledges that litigating national security cases takes time—often longer than standard civil disputes—but it places significant weight on trust in executive branch certifications over independent judicial fact-finding at every stage. While Katsas rightly notes that requiring constant re-investigation could compromise ongoing operations by forcing agencies to revisit closed files just to prove they haven't been abandoned, there is no mechanism described here for challenging those internal conclusions once they are deemed sufficient updates to prior filings.

Ultimately, *Farahi* signals that in high-stakes FOIA cases involving terrorism and national security, persistence pays dividends for both plaintiffs and defendants—but mostly for defendants who know how to manage their declaratory strategy wisely. As long as an agency can anchor its current position to specific past findings and provide timely confirmations via sworn statements (even brief ones), courts appear willing to let those withholdings stand without demanding fresh discovery into whether those investigations truly remain viable or have quietly stalled out of sight. For privacy advocates and journalists tracking government secrets over long timelines, this ruling suggests a steeper climb ahead: proving not just that records exist now, but actively dismantling the presumption that "ongoing" means exactly what it says without further proof beyond an attorney's word and an agent's nodding head in private chambers.



# The Ex Parte Shield: Reconciling Due Process Concerns with National Security Deference in FOIA Litigation

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *Farahi v. FBI* serves as a stark reminder of the immense procedural asymmetry that defines FOIA litigation when national security is at stake. While the court affirmed the FBI's right to withhold nearly 10,000 pages of records under Exemption 7(A), the path to that affirmation relied heavily on sealed, ex parte declarations that the plaintiff could neither read nor contest. By accepting a "supplemental" ex parte declaration merely to confirm that an investigation remained ongoing—without demanding a fresh, detailed public showing—the court reinforced a doctrine where executive deference often trumps adversarial testing. This ruling underscores a troubling reality: in the shadow of counterterrorism investigations, the traditional checks and balances of civil procedure are significantly blunted, leaving courts to act largely as rubber stamps for executive assertions of secrecy.

At the heart of *Farahi* is the interplay between two critical FOIA requirements: the need to prove that enforcement proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated," and the obligation to ensure that non-exempt information is reasonably segregable before withholding entire files. The district court initially signaled skepticism regarding the age of the FBI's original declarations, ordering an update because years had passed since the initial filing. The government responded not with a robust new public declaration detailing current investigative steps, but with a terse public affidavit from Joseph Bender confirming nothing had changed, backed by an ex parte supplement filled with classified specifics. The D.C. Circuit endorsed this approach, reasoning that because the initial public and sealed declarations had sufficiently established the investigation's existence, Bender's brief update was merely confirmatory rather than foundational. Consequently, Farahi was left unable to challenge the substantive claims within those sealed filings, even though they formed the bedrock of the government's defense against disclosure.

This deferential posture raises profound questions about procedural fairness in national security cases. Under standard administrative law principles, an agency must provide enough factual detail for a reviewing court—and ideally for an adversary—to evaluate whether a statutory exemption truly applies. Here, however, the court explicitly noted that while ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," such handicaps are "inherent" when disclosure itself would reveal what is being hidden (*Perioperative Servs.*). The result is a circular logic: because revealing details about an active terror investigation might tip off suspects, those details must be kept secret; because they are secret, they cannot be tested; and because they cannot be tested, courts accept them at face value if accompanied by a general assertion of authority. In *Farahi*, Farahi argued that Bender's declaration was conclusory on its own merits. While true in isolation, the court swept this argument aside by pointing to prior sealed submissions he couldn't access and public allegations from unrelated immigration proceedings where he was accused of recruiting for terrorist organizations and linking figures like Jose Padilla to his file.

The implications for document segregability are equally concerning for transparency advocates. One of FOIA's core promises is that redactions should be narrow and precise; if innocent data exists alongside secrets, it should be released. Yet, in *Farahi*, the district court relied almost exclusively on Bender's ex parte submission to conclude that no meaningful information could be separated from exempt material across thousands of pages. Without seeing those specific justifications—or having any opportunity to argue against them—the appellate court deferred entirely to the government's claim that non-exempt text was "inextricably intertwined" with sensitive intelligence. This effectively grants agencies broad discretion to withhold massive volumes of records simply by asserting complexity in their internal categorization schemes, provided they submit a sufficient-seeming seal-order explanation behind closed doors.

Looking forward, *Farahi* signals that litigants challenging access to counterterrorism files will face an uphill battle not just against substantive exemptions, but against procedural barriers designed to protect those same secrets from judicial scrutiny until after victory is assured (or lost). The decision suggests that once an agency establishes a prima facie case via sealed submissions early in litigation—even if done partially *ex parte*—subsequent updates require far less rigorous public justification than one might expect under normal administrative practice. For future plaintiffs facing similar hurdles in accessing intelligence files regarding themselves or third parties outside their immediate control (such as associates), this precedent narrows their ability to force agencies into corners where they must either produce records or offer detailed public explanations for doing otherwise. Ultimately, while national security demands confidentiality in many contexts, *Farahi* illustrates how easily those necessities can expand into a blanket shield against accountability when courts prioritize executive expertise over adversarial rigor.



# Interspersed Line-by-Line': The High Bar for Segregability in Massive Counterterrorism Files

The tension between the Freedom of Information Act's mandate for transparency and the government's need to protect active law enforcement investigations came into sharp focus last week in *Farahi v. FBI*. In a unanimous opinion authored by Judge Neomi Rao (joined by Judges Henderson and Griffith), the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court's ruling that the FBI could withhold nearly its entire file on Foad Farahi, an Iranian national accused of links to terrorist organizations. While the case initially turned on whether an investigation spanning two decades remained "pending" enough to trigger Exemption 7(A), the most legally significant takeaway lies in Section V: the court's rigorous application of the segregability doctrine. By rejecting Farahi's contention that some non-exempt information must exist within his massive 10,750-page file, the panel reinforced a strict standard that effectively grants functional immunity to large-scale investigative records, even where trivial amounts of unredacted text might technically exist.

The facts of *Farahi* present a classic FOIA struggle involving national security. Farahi, whose immigration proceedings have been fraught with allegations connecting him to figures like Jose Padilla and organizations designated as foreign terrorist groups, sought his FBI file. The agency located thousands of pages and dozens of CDs, ultimately releasing only a fraction before withholding the rest under Exemption 7(A). The district court had previously remanded part of the case because the original declarations regarding the status of ongoing investigations were dated. The FBI responded with updated declarations from current officials confirming that investigations into Farahi remained active. The appellate court agreed, deferring heavily to the agency's expertise on matters touching national security and accepting that disclosure could tip off targets regarding the scope of an inquiry still gathering evidence for a potential future prosecution.

However, it is Farahi's challenge to segregability that offers the most troubling precedent for privacy advocates and transparency proponents alike. Under FOIA, agencies must release any "reasonably segregable" portion of a record after deleting exempt material. Farahi argued that given the sheer volume of documents—over ten thousand pages—it was statistically impossible for every single page to be entirely exempt; surely, some names, dates, or innocuous administrative notes should have surfaced. The court dismissed this argument with a nod to *Mead Data Cent.*, establishing that if non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is not reasonably segregable.

This reasoning reflects a profound shift in how courts view context versus content in mass surveillance files. The logic follows that because intelligence reports often weave together sensitive operational details with mundane identifiers in dense paragraphs, extracting isolated words or short phrases would strip them of meaning or inadvertently reveal patterns about an investigation that remain hidden in full sentences. As Judge Rao noted, "the focus of the FOIA is information… not simply words." If reading a name or a date without its surrounding context yields no useful information—and worse, risks revealing investigative methodology by showing what *was* removed—the agency need not bother with line-by-line extraction. This creates a high barrier: unless non-exempt information exists in discrete blocks (like a separate cover letter or an unrelated attachment), it remains locked behind redactions if it is entangled with exempt data.

Doctrinally, this approach significantly narrows the practical reach of FOIA in counterterrorism cases where files are voluminous and complex. While Exemption 7(A) itself requires a showing that disclosure would harm enforcement proceedings—a hurdle some lower courts have struggled with when investigations stall—the segregability requirement usually serves as Congress's safety valve to ensure maximum disclosure even when exemptions apply. *Farahi* suggests that when an agency submits detailed ex parte declarations explaining why specific categories are inextricably intertwined with exempt material, courts will accept those assertions at face value rather than demanding independent verification or sampling. This deference transforms segregability from an active duty to mine for public information into a passive defense against fragmentation claims by plaintiffs relying on statistical probability rather than specific examples of redactable text.

The downstream effect is clear: agencies holding massive investigative dossiers now possess greater latitude to withhold entire files under Exemption 7(A) without fear that plaintiffs can force partial releases based on theoretical separability. For litigants like Farahi, who seek accountability through transparency, this ruling implies that once an investigation is deemed "ongoing" (even years after its inception), the protective shield around those records becomes nearly impenetrable unless they contain clearly distinct pockets of non-sensitive data. While *Farahi* does not overturn existing precedent regarding what constitutes reasonable segregation, its application here signals a judicial preference for preserving investigative integrity over forcing granular disclosure in cases where national security stakes are perceived as highest. As long as agencies can credibly argue that redacting lines would compromise context or reveal investigatory techniques via negative inference, *Farahi* ensures that "information" remains largely out of reach for those caught in federal net-wide dragnets.
