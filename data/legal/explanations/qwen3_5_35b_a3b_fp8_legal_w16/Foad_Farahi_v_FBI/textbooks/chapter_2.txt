Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Legal Framework and Standard of Review

## Overview of FOIA Disclosure Obligations and Statutory Exemptions
The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a foundational presumption of openness within the federal government, mandating that agencies make records available to the public upon request. Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3) and (b), an agency must comply with a request unless the records fall within one of ten specific statutory exemptions designed to protect particular governmental interests, such as national defense or personal privacy. While the default rule favors disclosure, the statute explicitly permits agencies to withhold information when a valid exemption applies. Furthermore, even when an exemption justifies withholding certain parts of a record, the law imposes a duty of "reasonable segregation." Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii), agencies must redact only the exempt portions and release any remaining nonexempt information that can be reasonably separated from the protected content. In *Farahi*, this framework sets the stage for determining whether the FBI's massive collection of files—comprising thousands of pages and CDs—must be released or if they qualify for complete withholding under a specific exception.

## Elements of Exemption 7(A): Law Enforcement Purpose and Interference Risk
At the heart of *Farahi* lies Exemption 7(A), codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). This exemption allows an agency to deny access to records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their production "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The Supreme Court and lower courts have interpreted this language strictly; both prongs must be satisfied for the exemption to apply. First, the records must have been created or assembled specifically to assist in law enforcement activities, rather than being merely incidental to such work. Second, there must be a causal link between disclosure and potential harm; releasing the documents must create a foreseeable risk that actual enforcement efforts would be compromised. In *Farahi*, the FBI argued that its file contained intelligence gathered during investigations into terrorism and national security threats, satisfying both elements by linking the compilation purpose directly to active investigations and arguing that revealing these methods would tip off subjects or otherwise hinder ongoing operations.

## Definition of 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Proceedings
A critical limitation on Exemption 7(A) is that interference protections apply only when enforcement proceedings are either "pending" or "reasonably anticipated." This requirement prevents agencies from indefinitely shielding old investigative files once all action has ceased or where no future action is feasible. A proceeding does not necessarily mean a formal trial; ongoing criminal investigations often qualify as "proceedings" because they represent an active phase where evidence is being gathered for potential litigation or administrative action. The D.C. Circuit in *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*) clarified that vague assertions are insufficient; however, an agency need not prove beyond peradventure that prosecution will occur, only that proceedings are sufficiently concrete and imminent enough that early disclosure would cause tangible harm. In *Farahi*, this definition became pivotal when the district court initially questioned whether nearly two decades since Farahi's entry into removal proceedings meant any threat was now purely historical rather than current. The appellate court emphasized that as long as an investigation continues gathering evidence for a possible future case—and premature release would jeopardize it—the "pending" threshold remains met regardless of how long the process takes compared to typical civil timelines.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Analysis in FOIA Cases
On appeal from a grant of summary judgment in a FOIA case like *Farahi*, the reviewing court conducts a de novo analysis under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56 principles adapted for statutory interpretation questions involving agency discretion regarding secrecy. Unlike some areas where factual findings receive clear error deference, appellate judges independently assess whether the government has met its burden under FOIA statutes without deferring automatically to lower court conclusions on evidentiary sufficiency regarding exemptions themselves, though they respect district courts' credibility determinations made during hearings if applicable here via affidavits.
