Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of Law Enforcement Purpose Nexus

## "Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes": The Rational Nexus Test

To qualify for protection under Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), an agency must first demonstrate that the requested records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." While courts generally afford deference to a law enforcement agency like the FBI regarding its internal determinations, this deference is not absolute. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, specifically *Campbell v. Department of Justice*, the agency's declarations must establish two critical elements: a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's core law enforcement duties, and a connection between the specific individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. Without this dual foundation, an agency cannot successfully claim that its files fall within the scope of Exemption 7(A). The court does not merely accept an agency's label but looks to see if the factual assertions in the administrative record logically connect the inquiry to legitimate policing objectives, such as preventing crime or investigating threats to national security.

## Role of Public vs. Sealed (Ex Parte) Declarations in Establishing Context

In FOIA litigation involving sensitive national security matters, agencies often submit both public declarations and sealed *ex parte* submissions to satisfy their burden of proof. A public declaration serves as the baseline justification available for judicial review and public scrutiny, outlining the general purpose of the investigation and invoking broad statutory authority. However, when specific details regarding sources, methods, or intelligence relationships are involved, a public declaration alone may lack sufficient granularity to meet the "rational nexus" test described in *Pratt v. Webster*. Consequently, agencies frequently supplement these filings with *ex parte* declarations submitted under seal. These confidential documents provide the detailed factual matrix—such as specific leads, intelligence assessments, or operational constraints—that bridges the gap between a generic investigation and a concrete threat to federal law. Courts evaluate these materials together; while they may not publish the specifics of an *ex parte* affidavit due to privilege concerns, they rely on them to verify that the public representations are grounded in reality rather than being conclusory statements designed solely to evade disclosure statutes.

## Fact Application: Connection Between Farahi's File and Counterterrorism Duties

In *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the appellate court affirmed that Farahi's FBI file was compiled for lawful enforcement purposes based on a robust combination of declaratory evidence. The primary public declaration from David Hardy explicitly stated that records were gathered "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism," linking Farahi directly to individuals associated with such violations. While this public statement established a theoretical link between Farahi and federal laws concerning terrorism, it was bolstered significantly by Hardy's accompanying *ex parte* filing, which offered considerable detail regarding the nature and scope of the specific investigation into Farahi's activities. Furthermore, subsequent updates from Joseph Bender confirmed that these investigations remained active at least through March 2023. By combining Hardy's initial articulation of terror-related grounds with Bender's corroboration that investigative efforts continued unimpeded by time delays, the court found ample evidence satisfying both prongs of the *Campbell* test: a clear tie to law enforcement duties (counterterrorism) and a specific connection between Farahi and potential federal violations (ties to terrorist organizations).

## Precedent Application: Concessions Made by Plaintiff Regarding Terror Ties

Perhaps even more persuasive than the government's own affidavits was Foad Farahi's own concession made during prior litigation regarding his status before immigration authorities. In opposing earlier motions in district court regarding his deportation proceedings (*Farahi v. U.S.'t Gen.*), Farahi admitted that he had been investigated for ties to organizations designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist groups and acknowledged associations with individuals subsequently charged as enemies combatants or convicted terrorists. Specifically, he conceded links to figures such as Jose Padilla and involvement with charities financing terrorism like the Holy Land Foundation. This admission served as powerful corroborative evidence supporting the FBI's assertions in its FOIA response; because Farahi himself recognized his own status as a target of counterterrorism inquiries in separate legal forums, he effectively waived any argument that his FBI file lacked a nexus to law enforcement functions. The court noted that given these concessions alongside public allegations regarding his role as a recruiter for groups like Al-Qaeda affiliates mentioned in immigration hearings, there was no doubt that the FBI had met its burden under Exemption 7(A). Thus, plaintiff conduct can sometimes supply missing evidentiary gaps left by official declarations alone.
