Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Procedural History and Background Facts

To understand the D.C. Circuit's affirmation of the FBI's withholding of records in *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, one must first grasp the complex intersection of national security, immigration enforcement, and the procedural demands of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The case arises from a multi-year legal battle initiated by Foad Farahi, an Iranian national residing in the United States since 1993, following his placement in removal proceedings by the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) in 2002 due to a visa overstay. During these extensive immigration proceedings—which included applications for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture—the government opposed Farahi's requests for relief using evidence gathered by the FBI. An FBI agent testified that Farahi maintained "connections to high-level terrorists." These underlying facts set the stage for Farahi's subsequent attempt to access his own file through a FOIA request, triggering a litigation process that hinged on whether the passage of time rendered outdated the FBI's claim that releasing these records would interfere with active law enforcement efforts.

**Plaintiff Profile: Immigration Proceedings and Terrorism Allegations**

The factual backdrop involves serious allegations concerning Farahi's associations. While living in the U.S., Farahi faced deportation proceedings initiated because he overstayed his visa. Throughout this period, he sought various forms of humanitarian relief, yet DHS consistently argued against granting such relief by presenting documentary evidence and witness testimony linking Farahi to international terror networks. Specifically, government officials described him as having been associated with high-level terrorists. Later judicial opinions referenced in this case noted that Farahi was accused by public authorities of being a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to figures like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and Al Qaeda members, and linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists, including Jose Padilla. Furthermore, he allegedly supported charities financing terrorism. Although some aspects of these accusations were detailed in ex parte submissions or later appellate briefs regarding separate immigration appeals (*Farahi v. U.S. Att'y Gen.*), they provide crucial context for understanding why the FBI deemed its files sensitive enough to warrant continued secrecy even years after the initial FOIA filing.

**The FOIA Request: Volume of Records and Initial Withholding**

Responding to these tensions, Farahi submitted a formal request under FOIA for his entire FBI file. The sheer scale of this repository initially posed logistical challenges; the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 compact discs containing potentially responsive information. Rather than immediately releasing everything or denying everything outright, the agency engaged in a lengthy review process spanning several years. During this time, they disclosed certain portions but ultimately withheld most records based on statutory exemptions available under FOIA Exemption 7(A), which protects law enforcement records whose release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. After missing its statutory deadline for response—an administrative error that prompted immediate litigation—Farahi sued once the agency completed its internal processing and refused further release beyond what had already been provided. By October 2019, believing it had fulfilled its obligations under FOIA despite delays caused by voluminous data collection and classification reviews, the FBI moved for summary judgment in federal district court.

**First Summary Judgment Motion: District Court's Order to Update Declarations**

At the outset of the litigation before the district court in October 2019, Deputy Director David Hardy submitted a sworn declaration representing that all withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings supporting those assertions with specific details about ongoing investigations into national security threats and international terrorism involving Farahi. Crucially recognizing potential staleness issues inherent in delayed responses often seen in large-scale FOIA disputes involving classified materials or extensive dossiers, the district court took notice that significant time had passed since Hardy made his original representations regarding current investigative status. # Legal Framework and Standard of Review

## Overview of FOIA Disclosure Obligations and Statutory Exemptions
The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a foundational presumption of openness within the federal government, mandating that agencies make records available to the public upon request. Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3) and (b), an agency must comply with a request unless the records fall within one of ten specific statutory exemptions designed to protect particular governmental interests, such as national defense or personal privacy. While the default rule favors disclosure, the statute explicitly permits agencies to withhold information when a valid exemption applies. Furthermore, even when an exemption justifies withholding certain parts of a record, the law imposes a duty of "reasonable segregation." Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii), agencies must redact only the exempt portions and release any remaining nonexempt information that can be reasonably separated from the protected content. In *Farahi*, this framework sets the stage for determining whether the FBI's massive collection of files—comprising thousands of pages and CDs—must be released or if they qualify for complete withholding under a specific exception.

## Elements of Exemption 7(A): Law Enforcement Purpose and Interference Risk
At the heart of *Farahi* lies Exemption 7(A), codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). This exemption allows an agency to deny access to records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their production "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The Supreme Court and lower courts have interpreted this language strictly; both prongs must be satisfied for the exemption to apply. First, the records must have been created or assembled specifically to assist in law enforcement activities, rather than being merely incidental to such work. Second, there must be a causal link between disclosure and potential harm; releasing the documents must create a foreseeable risk that actual enforcement efforts would be compromised. In *Farahi*, the FBI argued that its file contained intelligence gathered during investigations into terrorism and national security threats, satisfying both elements by linking the compilation purpose directly to active investigations and arguing that revealing these methods would tip off subjects or otherwise hinder ongoing operations.

## Definition of 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Proceedings
A critical limitation on Exemption 7(A) is that interference protections apply only when enforcement proceedings are either "pending" or "reasonably anticipated." This requirement prevents agencies from indefinitely shielding old investigative files once all action has ceased or where no future action is feasible. A proceeding does not necessarily mean a formal trial; ongoing criminal investigations often qualify as "proceedings" because they represent an active phase where evidence is being gathered for potential litigation or administrative action. The D.C. Circuit in *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*) clarified that vague assertions are insufficient; however, an agency need not prove beyond peradventure that prosecution will occur, only that proceedings are sufficiently concrete and imminent enough that early disclosure would cause tangible harm. In *Farahi*, this definition became pivotal when the district court initially questioned whether nearly two decades since Farahi's entry into removal proceedings meant any threat was now purely historical rather than current. The appellate court emphasized that as long as an investigation continues gathering evidence for a possible future case—and premature release would jeopardize it—the "pending" threshold remains met regardless of how long the process takes compared to typical civil timelines.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Analysis in FOIA Cases
On appeal from a grant of summary judgment in a FOIA case like *Farahi*, the reviewing court conducts a de novo analysis under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56 principles adapted for statutory interpretation questions involving agency discretion regarding secrecy. Unlike some areas where factual findings receive clear error deference, appellate judges independently assess whether the government has met its burden under FOIA statutes without deferring automatically to lower court conclusions on evidentiary sufficiency regarding exemptions themselves, though they respect district courts' credibility determinations made during hearings if applicable here via affidavits. # Analysis of Law Enforcement Purpose Nexus

## "Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes": The Rational Nexus Test

To qualify for protection under Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), an agency must first demonstrate that the requested records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." While courts generally afford deference to a law enforcement agency like the FBI regarding its internal determinations, this deference is not absolute. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, specifically *Campbell v. Department of Justice*, the agency's declarations must establish two critical elements: a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's core law enforcement duties, and a connection between the specific individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. Without this dual foundation, an agency cannot successfully claim that its files fall within the scope of Exemption 7(A). The court does not merely accept an agency's label but looks to see if the factual assertions in the administrative record logically connect the inquiry to legitimate policing objectives, such as preventing crime or investigating threats to national security.

## Role of Public vs. Sealed (Ex Parte) Declarations in Establishing Context

In FOIA litigation involving sensitive national security matters, agencies often submit both public declarations and sealed *ex parte* submissions to satisfy their burden of proof. A public declaration serves as the baseline justification available for judicial review and public scrutiny, outlining the general purpose of the investigation and invoking broad statutory authority. However, when specific details regarding sources, methods, or intelligence relationships are involved, a public declaration alone may lack sufficient granularity to meet the "rational nexus" test described in *Pratt v. Webster*. Consequently, agencies frequently supplement these filings with *ex parte* declarations submitted under seal. These confidential documents provide the detailed factual matrix—such as specific leads, intelligence assessments, or operational constraints—that bridges the gap between a generic investigation and a concrete threat to federal law. Courts evaluate these materials together; while they may not publish the specifics of an *ex parte* affidavit due to privilege concerns, they rely on them to verify that the public representations are grounded in reality rather than being conclusory statements designed solely to evade disclosure statutes.

## Fact Application: Connection Between Farahi's File and Counterterrorism Duties

In *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the appellate court affirmed that Farahi's FBI file was compiled for lawful enforcement purposes based on a robust combination of declaratory evidence. The primary public declaration from David Hardy explicitly stated that records were gathered "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism," linking Farahi directly to individuals associated with such violations. While this public statement established a theoretical link between Farahi and federal laws concerning terrorism, it was bolstered significantly by Hardy's accompanying *ex parte* filing, which offered considerable detail regarding the nature and scope of the specific investigation into Farahi's activities. Furthermore, subsequent updates from Joseph Bender confirmed that these investigations remained active at least through March 2023. By combining Hardy's initial articulation of terror-related grounds with Bender's corroboration that investigative efforts continued unimpeded by time delays, the court found ample evidence satisfying both prongs of the *Campbell* test: a clear tie to law enforcement duties (counterterrorism) and a specific connection between Farahi and potential federal violations (ties to terrorist organizations).

## Precedent Application: Concessions Made by Plaintiff Regarding Terror Ties

Perhaps even more persuasive than the government's own affidavits was Foad Farahi's own concession made during prior litigation regarding his status before immigration authorities. In opposing earlier motions in district court regarding his deportation proceedings (*Farahi v. U.S.'t Gen.*), Farahi admitted that he had been investigated for ties to organizations designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist groups and acknowledged associations with individuals subsequently charged as enemies combatants or convicted terrorists. Specifically, he conceded links to figures such as Jose Padilla and involvement with charities financing terrorism like the Holy Land Foundation. This admission served as powerful corroborative evidence supporting the FBI's assertions in its FOIA response; because Farahi himself recognized his own status as a target of counterterrorism inquiries in separate legal forums, he effectively waived any argument that his FBI file lacked a nexus to law enforcement functions. The court noted that given these concessions alongside public allegations regarding his role as a recruiter for groups like Al-Qaeda affiliates mentioned in immigration hearings, there was no doubt that the FBI had met its burden under Exemption 7(A). Thus, plaintiff conduct can sometimes supply missing evidentiary gaps left by official declarations alone.
