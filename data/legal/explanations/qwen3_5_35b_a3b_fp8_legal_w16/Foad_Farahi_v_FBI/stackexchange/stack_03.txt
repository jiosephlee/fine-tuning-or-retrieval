Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Can a defendant effectively waive their right to challenge ex parte/sealed submissions by failing to explicitly oppose their admission into the record?
Question:
In paragraph V, the court notes that Farahi cannot complain about the district court relying heavily on Bender's ex parte declaration for segregability analysis because 'Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations.' This seems like a major procedural trap. If I am representing a FOIA requester and I suspect my opponent will submit sensitive classified info via ex parte affidavit, do I need to file a specific motion objecting to *any* potential reliance on sealed materials, even if I don't know exactly what they contain yet? Or does silence equal waiver for all future challenges regarding those specific sealed submissions?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Under *Farahi*, a FOIA plaintiff effectively waives the right to challenge the district court's reliance on *ex parte* or sealed submissions by failing to explicitly object to their admission into the record. Silence operates as a forfeiture of that specific procedural objection, regardless of whether the plaintiff knew the exact contents of the sealed material at the time.

**Legal Analysis**

The D.C. Circuit's decision in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* establishes that while *ex parte* declarations are "inherent" and sometimes necessary in national security FOIA cases, a litigant cannot remain silent regarding their introduction and then appeal based on the weight given to them later.

In **Section V**, the Court addresses Farahi's complaint that the district court relied "too much" on Agent Bender's *ex parte* declaration for its segregability analysis. The Court rejects this argument on two grounds:
1.  **Procedural Waiver:** The Court states plainly: *"For one thing, Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."* This language indicates that the failure to object to the *acceptance* of the sealed filing precludes challenging its subsequent *use*.
2.  **Necessity of Sealed Review:** The Court notes that while such filings disadvantage plaintiffs, they are often unavoidable when disclosure would reveal the very secrets being protected (*Perioperative Servs.*). Consequently, once a party fails to contest the entry of these materials, they accept the procedural reality that they will be part of the evidentiary record available to the judge for review.

**Practical Implication for Counsel**

You absolutely must file a specific objection if you wish to preserve this issue for appeal. You cannot argue on appeal that an undisclosed affidavit was improperly weighed if you never told the district court you objected to its inclusion in the first place.

As a matter of strategy, you do not necessarily need to know every word in a proposed sealed declaration before opposing it if you believe it violates discovery rules or exceeds statutory authority. A timely motion stating that you object to any consideration of unredacted, unopposed sealed materials—arguing either that they violate due process principles or that they should not be admitted without notice—is sufficient to avoid waiver under *Farahi*. By contrast, doing nothing signals consent ("did not oppose"), which binds you under appellate precedent.
