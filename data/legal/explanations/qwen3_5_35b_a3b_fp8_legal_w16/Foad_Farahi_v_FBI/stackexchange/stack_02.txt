Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the practical application of 'inextricably intertwined' vs. 'random interspersion' in FOIA segregability analysis for large volumes of data?
Question:
Section V discusses segregability. The court rejects Farahi's argument that there must be some releasable info just because there are thousands of pages, citing *Mead*. They state that if non-exempt material is 'interspersed line-by-line,' it isn't reasonably segregable. But my confusion lies in the definition of 'meaningful information.' If I have 10,000 pages where 99% is redacted under 7(A), and the remaining 1% consists of isolated names or dates scattered throughout, why is extracting those specific fragments legally impossible or unreasonable? Is the test purely about whether the extracted text conveys independent meaning without revealing the exempt context?
Answer:
**Answer:**

The distinction between "inextricably intertwined" and "random interspersion" in *Farahi* turns entirely on **informational utility**, not merely the physical possibility of extraction. Under the D.C. Circuit's precedent cited in *Farahi*, specifically *Mead Data Cent.*, the core inquiry is whether the non-exempt fragments convey **"meaningful information"** independently of the exempt context.

If you have 10,000 pages where 99% is redacted and the remaining 1% consists of isolated names or dates, the court holds that extracting those fragments is legally unreasonable because they lack independent semantic value. As the opinion explains, "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." (*Mead*, quoted in *Farahi*). 

Here is how the doctrine applies to your hypothetical:

1.  **Random Interspersion = Not Reasonably Segregable:** The court explicitly states that if non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line," it is not reasonably segregable. In your scenario, isolated names or dates scattered throughout a file dominated by sensitive investigative details constitute "random interspersion." Even if a clerk could technically copy-paste those few words into a new document, doing so yields no "meaningful information" because the context required to understand their significance (and thus their relevance to a requester) has been excised along with the exempt material.
2.  **No Requirement for Volume-Based Disclosure:** The court rejects Farahi's argument that a massive volume of records (10,750 pages) automatically necessitates some disclosure simply because statistically some non-exempt text must exist. The holding clarifies that quantity does not equal quality; if every scrap of releasable text is so fragmented that it cannot stand alone as useful information, the agency need not release it.
3.  **The Practical Standard:** Therefore, the test is not whether you can physically separate the ink from the paper (which you can), but whether what remains constitutes **"information."** If extracting those specific fragments results in a list of unconnected proper nouns or dates that reveal nothing about Farahi's status or rights without revealing the redacted investigative context, they are deemed "inextricably intertwined" with the exempt portions for legal purposes.

In short, *Farahi* establishes that agencies are not required to perform "needle-in-a-haystack" operations where the resulting needles provide no usable intelligence. If the extracted text offers no independent informational value because it relies entirely on the redacted context to make sense, it falls under *Mead*'s exception and need not be released.
