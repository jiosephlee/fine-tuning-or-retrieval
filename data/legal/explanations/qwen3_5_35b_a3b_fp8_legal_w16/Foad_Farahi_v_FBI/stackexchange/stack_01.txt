Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the D.C. Circuit distinguish between a 'standalone' declaration and an 'update' when determining if Exemption 7(A) pendency requirements are met?
Question:
The court affirms the FBI's use of a brief 'Bender declaration' to confirm that an investigation remains ongoing, despite Farahi arguing it was conclusory. The court accepts this because it views the Bender declaration as merely updating the earlier, detailed 'Hardy declarations.' I am confused about the evidentiary threshold here: Does this mean that once an agency establishes pendency with a robust affidavit, subsequent updates can be significantly thinner? How far can an agency push this logic before a plaintiff can successfully argue that the agency has failed to meet its burden of proof regarding current pendency?
Answer:
In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit distinguishes between "standalone" and "update" declarations by analyzing the **contextual relationship** between the new affidavit and prior, detailed submissions, rather than evaluating the new affidavit in a vacuum. The court holds that an agency need not submit a fresh, comprehensive declaration every time it seeks to extend a claim of pendency if it has previously established a robust factual foundation for that claim.

The distinction turns on whether the new declaration serves as the primary vehicle for establishing pendent status or merely confirms that existing conditions persist. Here, the court reasoned that David Hardy's original declarations—both public and ex parte—had already satisfied the burden of proving that an investigation existed and detailing its nature. Consequently, Joseph Bender's subsequent "brief" declaration was treated not as a standalone attempt to prove pendency from scratch, but as a procedural mechanism to **"confirm[] that nothing had changed"** during the litigation gap. The court explicitly stated: *"The government submitted the Bender declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed... So, the Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations."*

This approach allows agencies to rely on "thin" updates once they have laid a sufficient evidentiary groundwork. However, this logic is not unlimited; it relies heavily on two factors present in *Farahi*:
1.  **Prior Specificity:** There must be an earlier record (like Hardy's) containing "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation."
2.  **Judicial Context:** The lower court must have already acknowledged that the agency had "seemingly" met its initial burden before ordering an update.

An agency pushes this logic too far if it attempts to use a short update declaration where no prior detailed affidavit exists, or where the passage of time suggests a fundamental change in circumstances that requires re-establishment of facts rather than mere confirmation. As noted in *CREW*, vague assertions alone are insufficient (*Farahi* cites *CREW* for this proposition). Therefore, while *Farahi* permits abbreviated updates, it does so only because those updates are tethered to a substantive historical record that validates their accuracy. If an agency lacks that anchor—or if the plaintiff presents evidence suggesting the investigation has actually terminated—the "update" theory collapses into an impermissible conclusory assertion.
