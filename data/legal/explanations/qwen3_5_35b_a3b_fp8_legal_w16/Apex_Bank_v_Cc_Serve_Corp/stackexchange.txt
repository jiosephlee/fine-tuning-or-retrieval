Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How can the Board find 'legal identity' between credit card services and general banking services when the parties explicitly state they do not compete directly?
Question:
I am struggling with the Board's analysis of the second DuPont factor regarding the similarity of goods/services. The opinion states that CC Serve partners with banks to issue cards but doesn't offer banking services itself, while Apex is a bank offering loans and deposits but *no* credit cards. Yet, the Board concluded their services are 'legally identical, in part' simply because dictionary definitions of 'banking' encompass issuing credit cards. How does the court reconcile this broad dictionary definition approach with the practical reality that these are distinct business models? Is this a new trend where functional overlaps in definitions trump actual market competition?
Answer:
The Board reconciled the apparent disconnect between the parties' distinct business models and the "legal identity" of their services by applying a **functional definition** of the services rather than a strict operational one. Under the second *DuPont* factor, the standard is not whether the parties operate in direct competition or share the exact same revenue streams, but whether the services are "related in some manner" such that consumers might mistakenly believe they emanate from the same source (*Coach Servs.*).

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit affirmed that the Board correctly relied on dictionary definitions to bridge this gap. Although CC Serve does not hold a charter to offer traditional banking (loans/deposits) and Apex does not issue credit cards, both entities engage in activities that fall within the broad semantic umbrella of "banking." Specifically, the Board noted that dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." Because Apex's application covered "[b]anking and financing services," and CC Serve provided credit card services (which dictionaries define as a subset of banking/financing), the Board found their services were **"legally identical, in part."**

This approach confirms that for trademark purposes, overlapping functional capabilities trump structural differences in how a company delivers those capabilities. The court rejected Apex's argument that its lack of physical branches or specific product lines (like loans) distinguished it from CC Serve. Instead, because both parties offered financial services involving credit extension—a core component of banking—the risk of consumer confusion remained significant enough to satisfy the second factor. The court clarified that this is not a new trend but a consistent application of trademark law: if a dictionary definition links two service categories, and one party's service falls squarely within that definition relative to another's, they are sufficiently similar regardless of whether they are direct competitors in practice.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the Sixth DuPont Factor analysis based on a definitional inconsistency rather than just reviewing for substantial evidence?
Question:
In section III.B, the court vacates the Board's finding on the sixth factor (third-party marks). My confusion lies in why this wasn't just a 'substantial evidence' issue. The Board found only 9 similar marks for 'credit card services,' which wasn't enough to prove weakness. However, Apex wanted to include 42 other marks involving broader financial services. The court says restricting the universe was a legal error because it contradicted their own finding in Factor Two (that services are highly similar). Why does defining 'similar goods' differently across different DuPont factors constitute reversible legal error? Shouldn't the Board have discretion to define the relevant market narrowly for each specific factor?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's Sixth DuPont Factor analysis not because of a failure to apply "substantial evidence" to the *count* of marks, but because the Board committed a **legal error** by applying an inconsistent definition of "similarity" across different factors. While factual findings within a factor are reviewed for substantial evidence, the legal framework governing how those facts are gathered and compared is reviewed *de novo*.

Here is why the definitional inconsistency constituted reversible legal error:

**1. The Legal Standard Requires Consistency in Defining "Similar Goods"**
Under the Second DuPont Factor, the Board found that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex's "banking and financing services" were **"highly similar,"** even noting they were "legally identical, in part." This was a binding factual finding regarding the scope of similarity between the parties' goods.

However, when addressing the Sixth Factor (which looks at third-party marks on *similar* goods), the Board narrowed its scope to exclude all third-party marks that did not strictly say "credit card services," despite acknowledging that other marks covered broader financial services. The Federal Circuit held that once a court or board determines goods are highly similar under Factor Two, it cannot arbitrarily redefine "similar goods" to be much narrower under Factor Six without justification. As stated in Section III.B: *"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."*

**2. Why This Was Not Just a Substantial Evidence Issue**
You correctly noted that if there were merely insufficient evidence of 9 marks versus 51 marks, this would be a substantial evidence challenge. However, the problem here was procedural and logical, evidentiary sufficiency notwithstanding.

The Board created a contradiction:
*   **Step A (Factor 2):** Defined the universe of goods as broad (banking/financing = credit card services).
*   **Step B (Factor 6):** Defined the universe of goods as narrow (credit card services only).

By excluding 42 relevant third-party marks simply because they fell outside its self-imposed narrow definition for Factor 6—despite having already ruled they were legally similar under Factor 2—the Board failed to apply the correct legal standard for what constitutes "similar goods" in a likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The Sixth Factor explicitly requires considering marks on **"similar"** goods (*DuPont*, 476 F.2d at 1361), not necessarily *identical* goods (*Juice Generation*). By imposing a stricter standard than it applied to itself in Factor Two, the Board misapplied the law.

**Conclusion**
The court vacated because you cannot have it both ways: you cannot claim two sets of services are so closely related that confusion is likely (Factor Two) while simultaneously claiming they are distinct enough to ignore vast swaths of third-party precedent covering those very same related fields (Factor Six). The error was not that there weren't enough *facts* about nine marks; it was that the Board applied an incorrect *legal rule* by failing to maintain consistency in its definition of similarity across factors.

### What is the procedural mechanism for remanding a trademark case when one factor's re-evaluation logically necessitates changing another unrelated factor?
Question:
The court vacated both Factor Six and Factor One (similarity of marks). I understand why Factor Six needed a redo if we expand the pool of third-party marks (as per my previous question), but why did that automatically force a re-examination of Factor One? The opinion states: 'reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength... and affect the overall commercial impression.' Doesn't commercial strength usually feed into distinctiveness or fame, whereas commercial impression is about visual/phonetic similarity? I'm trying to understand if there is an inherent link between 'market crowding' (Factor Six) and how consumers perceive two specific words ('Aspire' vs 'Aspire Bank') that makes them inseparable on remand.
Answer:
The court's decision to vacate **Factor One** (similarity of marks) alongside **Factor Six** (third-party marks) rests on the interdependence between "commercial strength" and "commercial impression." While these concepts often appear distinct, *Apex Bank* clarifies that a change in the evidentiary basis for commercial strength can fundamentally alter the perceived similarity of the marks themselves.

Under the **first DuPont factor**, the inquiry is not merely a mechanical comparison of spelling or pronunciation ("appearance, sound, connotation"). Instead, it focuses on the **"overall commercial impression"** left on the consuming public (*In re I.AM.Symbolic*). The court explicitly links this impression to the mark's **commercial strength**.

The procedural necessity arises from how Factor Six functions as a proxy for commercial strength. As noted in Section III.B of the opinion, evidence of third-party use (crowding) demonstrates that a mark is "weak" and entitled to only a "narrow scope of protection." Conversely, a lack of such evidence suggests a stronger mark entitled to broader protection.

In *Apex*, the Board committed reversible error by artificially narrowing the scope of third-party marks considered in Factor Six. By excluding valid third-party marks outside the strict "credit card services" category, the Board incorrectly assessed CC Serve's mark as having only "normal scope" rather than potentially being weaker due to market crowding.

Because commercial strength dictates how much leeway consumers allow before assuming confusion, expanding the universe of third-party marks could reveal that CC Serve's mark is weak. If CC Serve's mark is weak, its "scope of protection" narrows significantly. Consequently, even minor differences between "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" might become sufficient to avoid confusion because consumers are accustomed to seeing variations of "Aspire" across many entities. Alternatively, if the expanded evidence shows *less* crowding than previously thought, the mark might be stronger, making even slight similarities more dangerous.

Therefore, you cannot determine whether "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" create a confusingly similar **commercial impression** without first knowing where those marks fall on the spectrum of **strength**. Since vacating Factor Six forces a re-evaluation of whether CC Serve's mark is strong or weak based on a corrected dataset, Factor One must be recalculated to reflect how that new assessment changes the overall commercial reality for consumers. The factors are not isolated; they operate within a unified framework where market context (Factor Six) directly shapes perceptual analysis (Factor One).

### Does this ruling imply that once courts find services are 'highly similar,' they must treat all subsequent DuPont factors as having an expanded scope automatically?
Question:
The core holding seems to hinge on consistency: since Factor Two said services are highly similar, Factor Six couldn't limit its search to just credit cards. Does this create a rigid rule where finding similarity in Factor Two locks in a broad interpretation for all other factors? If so, how does this interact with cases where courts typically analyze each DuPont factor somewhat independently based on its specific evidentiary record? Am I missing nuance here regarding whether this is about logical consistency or something deeper about how trademark rights scale with service breadth?
Answer:
No, *Apex Bank* does not establish a rigid rule that a "highly similar" finding under DuPont Factor Two automatically expands the scope of *all* subsequent factors. Instead, the court's reasoning is limited to ensuring **logical consistency** within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, specifically regarding the definition of "similar goods and services."

Here is the breakdown of the legal principle derived from the opinion:

**1. Consistency in Defining "Similarity"**
The core holding addresses a definitional inconsistency rather than an automatic expansion of rights. The Board had found under Factor Two that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services were "highly similar" (even "legally identical, in part"). However, when applying Factor Six (market crowding/third-party marks), the Board arbitrarily narrowed its search to *only* credit card services, ignoring other banking/financing services despite its own finding that those categories were sufficiently similar.

The Federal Circuit clarified that if a court finds two types of services are "highly similar" for the purpose of determining confusion potential (Factor Two), it cannot subsequently define "similar goods" for third-party mark analysis (Factor Six) as being narrower than that initial finding. As stated in Section III.B: *"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."*

**2. Not an Automatic Expansion of All Factors**
The opinion explicitly rejects imposing a "different and more stringent legal standard for similarity" only in this context. It does not suggest that every factor must now be interpreted broadly regardless of its specific evidentiary record. For instance:
*   **Factor One (Mark Similarity):** The court vacated this only because a broader look at market crowding (Factor Six) might alter the assessment of commercial strength and commercial impression. It did not say Factor One is automatically broadened; it required reconsideration *in light of* the corrected scope.
*   **Other Factors:** The text notes that courts generally analyze factors based on their specific records (*M2 Software*). The ruling simply prevents a contradiction where a court says Services A and B are alike for one purpose but distinct for another within the same case logic.

**3. Nuance: Logical Flow vs. Rights Scaling**
You are correct to suspect this is about logical consistency rather than trademark rights scaling with service breadth per se. The court emphasized that *"the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones"* (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*). By defining similarity narrowly under Factor Six while acknowledging broad similarity under Factor Two, the Board created an internal conflict that skewed the analysis against Apex without justification.

**Conclusion**
The ruling implies that once a trier of fact determines goods/services are highly similar under Factor Two, they must apply that **same definition of similarity** when identifying relevant third-party marks under Factor Six (and potentially affecting Factor One). It does not create a blanket rule expanding all factors; rather, it mandates that courts do not engage in "cherry-picking" definitions to manipulate outcomes across different factors within a single likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.

### If Apex didn't actually offer credit cards yet (intent-to-use), does that change how we evaluate likelihood of confusion compared to actual concurrent use?
Question:
The opinion mentions Apex filed an intent-to-use application and planned to launch later, while CC Serve had active registrations since 1996. Usually, likelihood of confusion looks at current market conditions. Since Apex hadn't started selling yet during most of this timeline, does that weaken or strengthen their argument against confusion? Or does it matter at all given that Lanham Act Section 2(d) prohibits registration even before use if confusion is likely upon launch? I'm confused about how temporal proximity plays into these specific du Pont analyses when one party hasn't entered the market.
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
No, Apex's status as an "intent-to-use" applicant does not automatically weaken its likelihood-of-confusion defense, nor does it fundamentally alter the *DuPont* analysis once the Board finds the services are similar. Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a refusal can occur based on a *future* likelihood of confusion if the applicant's proposed use would likely cause confusion upon launch. However, the *temporal gap* between the parties' actual use is a critical factual component of **Factor Six** (market crowding/weakness), which determines how much protection a senior mark deserves.

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit clarified that while Apex had not yet launched, the Board committed legal error by defining "similar goods" narrowly for Factor Six (looking only at credit cards) while defining them broadly for Factor Two (banking and financing). By restricting the search for third-party marks to only those currently offering credit cards, the Board ignored potential evidence that "Apire" is common across broader banking services—a sector where Apex intends to operate.

Here is the detailed breakdown of how this works under the opinion:

### 1. Intent-to-Use Does Not Preclude Confusion Analysis
The core misconception in your query is whether a lack of actual sales negates a Section 2(d) claim. The opinion explicitly rejects this idea by citing *Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, which establishes that Section 2(d) prohibits registration when confusion is likely due to **"concurrent use."**

While Apex had not yet commenced use at the time of filing (August 2019), the statute contemplates future concurrent use. The Board correctly applied this standard: if Apex launches its "ASPIRE BANK" service and CC Serve continues using "ASPIRE" for credit cards, consumers may believe they originate from the same source. Therefore, Apex's lack of current market presence does not immunize it from opposition; rather, it shifts the inquiry to whether confusion is likely *upon launch*.

### 2. How Temporal Proximity Affects Factor Six (Market Crowding)
You asked specifically about how temporal proximity plays into *DuPont* Factor Six ("number and nature of similar marks"). This factor asks whether there are many similar marks in existence, suggesting consumers are accustomed to distinguishing between them (making new marks weaker).

The opinion highlights that **the scope of "similar goods" dictates what counts toward Factor Six.**
*   **The Board's Error:** The Board found that credit card services and banking services were "highly similar" (Factor Two). However, when calculating Factor Six, it artificially narrowed its search to *only* existing marks registered strictly for "credit card services," ignoring marks used for general banking or financing where Apex intended to enter.
*   **The Court's Correction:** The Federal Circuit held that if you define goods as similar in Factor Two, you must maintain that same breadth in Factor Six. If Apex intends to sell banking services (which include issuing credit cards), then third-party marks used on *other* banking services count toward showing market crowding.

Because Apex was an intent-to-use applicant planning to enter the broader banking market immediately upon launch, excluding non-credit-card-specific third-party marks distorted the analysis of whether CC Serve's mark was strong or weak in that specific marketplace. If there are dozens of "Aspire" marks across various banking sectors (where Apex plans to compete), CC Serve gets narrower protection regardless of whether Apex has physically printed a single credit card yet.

### Conclusion
Apex's intent-to-use status does not change *how* we evaluate confusion legally; it simply means we project forward to when both parties will be operating concurrently. However, it significantly impacts **Fact-Finding**. Because Apex planned immediate entry into a broad range of financial services, the Board was required to look at third-party usage across that entire spectrum when assessing market crowding (Factor Six). By failing to do so—and focusing only on existing credit card users—the Board improperly weighted CC Serve's commercial strength against an applicant who was poised to enter a wider field than just credit cards alone.
