Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Vacatur Regarding DuPont Factor Six – Market Crowding and Scope Protection

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed a critical procedural and analytical flaw within the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) application of the *DuPont* likelihood-of-confusion test. Specifically, the court examined how the Board analyzed the sixth *DuPont* factor, which concerns "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." The central holding of this chapter is that a failure to maintain consistency in defining "similar goods" across different *DuPont* factors can lead to an artificial inflation of a mark's commercial strength, thereby distorting the market-crowding analysis. By restricting its search for third-party marks to a narrower category than the one established under the second factor, the Board erroneously concluded that CC Serve's mark was entitled to normal protection rather than a narrowed scope due to market saturation. The Federal Circuit vacated this portion of the decision, emphasizing that once a court or board finds services to be highly similar under one factor, that definition must govern subsequent analyses involving those same services.

To understand why the Board's error mattered, one must first grasp the function of Factor Six within trademark law. Unlike many other factors that focus on specific attributes like similarity of sight or sound, Factor Six serves as a check against over-protection of common terms. The logic follows that if a field is crowded with similar marks (e.g., multiple companies using "Aspire" in financial contexts), consumers become accustomed to distinguishing between them. Consequently, even if two new marks are somewhat alike, they are less likely to cause confusion because consumers are trained to look past minor differences in such a saturated environment. Conversely, if a mark is unique in its field, it enjoys a broad scope of protection where even slight similarities might trigger confusion. Therefore, accurately identifying the universe of "similar goods" is paramount; narrowing this universe artificially reduces the perceived density of competing marks, making an existing mark appear stronger than it truly is relative to the actual marketplace reality.

The conflict in *Apex Bank* arose from an inconsistency between how the Board defined "similar services" in its analysis of Factor Two versus its analysis of Factor Six. Under Factor Two, which assesses the similarity of goods and services themselves, the Board had determined that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex Bank's "banking and financing services" were legally identical in part and highly similar generally. The Board reasoned that both sectors involved issuing credit cards or extending credit through various financial instruments. Having established this high degree of similarity under Factor Two—a finding supported by substantial evidence—the Board then turned to Factor Six to determine if third-party usage weakened CC Serve's claim. However, instead of applying its own finding from Factor Two, the Board arbitrarily restricted its inquiry under Factor Six exclusively to third-party marks registered strictly for "credit card services." It explicitly excluded forty-two other third-party registrations using "Aspire" for broader banking and financing services, deeming them "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit identified this exclusion as a fundamental legal error rooted in logical inconsistency. The court noted that while exact identity is not required for Factor Six—only similarity—the scope of what constitutes "similar goods" cannot shift arbitrarily between factors without justification. Since the Board had already factually found that banking/financing services were sufficiently similar to credit card services to weigh heavily toward confusion under Factor Two, it logically followed that these same banking/financing services constituted valid comparisons for determining market crowding under Factor Six. By ignoring third-party users operating in these broader yet legally recognized service categories, the Board failed to capture the true extent of competition facing CC Serve's mark. If Apex Bank operates in banking/financing (which includes credit cards), then competitors offering those same broader services are legitimate sources of potential consumer confusion; ignoring them paints an inaccurate picture of how crowded or clear the market actually is for consumers seeking these combined financial solutions.

Consequently, because this definitional error skewed the assessment of commercial strength, it necessitated further action beyond simple reversal. The appellate court could not simply rule on whether there were enough third-party marks without knowing how broadly those marks should be counted based on consistent legal standards regarding service similarity. Furthermore, this error rippled into Factor One (similarity of marks). Commercial impression often depends on context; if a mark appears weak due to widespread use across a broad range of banking activities (as would be revealed by considering all relevant third-party marks), its visual or phonetic distinctiveness carries less weight in preventing confusion than if it appeared strong amidst sparse competition. Thus, because recalculating market crowding under Factor Six could alter perceptions regarding commercial strength—which directly informs whether Apex Bank's proposed "ASPIRE BANK" mark creates a likelihood of confusion—the court vacated both Factors Five and One (though primarily focusing on Four/Six logic) and remanded for reconsideration consistent with its finding on service similarity.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* stands as a cautionary tale for administrative bodies conducting trademark oppositions: consistency is not merely stylistic but substantive when applying multi-factor tests like *DuPont*. A tribunal cannot define markets narrowly for one purpose (to deny protection) while widening them for another (or vice versa) depending on which side benefits from that definition unless there is distinct evidentiary support justifying such divergence across different analytical stages. In this case, by failing to align its definition of "similar goods" between Factors Two and Six, the TTAB created an illusion where CC Serve's mark seemed uniquely dominant when it was actually subject to significant market saturation across related financial fields. The resulting vacatur ensured that upon remand, any determination regarding market crowding would reflect a realistic view of consumer behavior within the full scope of overlapping banking and credit card industries previously acknowledged by the board itself.
