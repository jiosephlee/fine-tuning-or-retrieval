# Chapter 1: Procedural Background and Factual Landscape

To understand the appellate court's decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, one must first navigate the specific factual matrix and procedural history that gave rise to the dispute. At its core, this case involves a conflict over the right to use the term "ASPIRE" within the financial sector, pitting a credit card service provider against a traditional retail bank. The litigation began not in federal district court, but before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB), an administrative body within the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) tasked with resolving disputes over trademark registration. Understanding how these parties interacted, what services they actually provide, and the chronological sequence of their filings is essential to grasping why the Federal Circuit later had to intervene to correct errors in the TTAB's analysis of similarity.

The primary respondent in this proceeding was CC Serve Corporation ("CC Serve"), a company specializing in credit card services rather than direct banking operations. CC Serve holds Registration No. 2126948 for the standard character word mark "ASPIRE." This registration is significant because it carries an effective priority date of October 17, 1996, making it a senior mark relative to Apex's application. CC Serve's business model involves partnering with various banks; while its partner banks issue branded credit cards, CC Serve handles the servicing of accounts, manages transactions, provides monitoring alerts, and offers access to credit scores for customers holding ASPIRE-branded cards. Thus, despite not issuing the physical plastic cards itself, CC Serve operates as a critical infrastructure provider for credit card services under its registered mark. In contrast, the appellant, Apex Bank, is a retail bank chartered in Tennessee with eighteen physical branch locations. Unlike CC Serve, Apex does not currently offer credit card products; instead, it focuses on personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and consumer loans. However, Apex planned to launch an online banking platform under new marks comprising both words and designs: "ASPIRE BANK."

The procedural journey toward this appeal began when Apex sought federal registration for its proposed marks. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the USPTO seeking registration for "ASPIRE BANK" covering "banking and financing services." During the examination phase of these applications, CC Serve took notice of Apex's filing and submitted a letter of protest asserting that Apex's proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own established "ASPIRE" mark. Despite this objection from CC Serve, the USPTO examining attorney initially approved Apex's marks for publication on December 17, 2019. This approval triggered a statutory window during which interested parties could challenge the registration. Seizing this opportunity in February 2020, CC Serve formally initiated an opposition proceeding before the TTAB under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1052(d)). This section prohibits registration if a mark so resembles another previously registered mark that it is likely to cause confusion among consumers regarding the source of goods or services.

The central issue before the TTAB was whether consumers would likely believe that Apex's "ASPIRE BANK" services originated from or were affiliated with CC Serve's "ASPIRE" credit card services. # Chapter 2: Standard of Review and Legal Framework

When a party appeals a decision from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the court operates within a specific statutory grant of authority and applies distinct standards for evaluating the lower tribunal's work. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit began its analysis by establishing its jurisdictional footing before dissecting the legal mechanics governing trademark disputes. Understanding these foundational principles is essential for grasping why the court affirmed some aspects of the TTAB's decision while vacating others. The court's power to hear this case stems from 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the TTAB in inter partes proceedings, such as trademark oppositions. This statute ensures that challenges to trademark registration refusals or opposition rulings are centralized in the Federal Circuit, providing uniformity in federal trademark law interpretation.

Once jurisdiction is established, the court must determine how deferential it should be toward the TTAB's ruling. The opinion clarifies that appellate review is bifurcated depending on whether the issue involves questions of law or questions of fact. Legal conclusions are reviewed *de novo*, meaning the appellate court gives no deference to the Board's legal interpretations and decides the matter anew based on its own judgment. Conversely, findings of fact are reviewed for "substantial evidence." This standard asks whether there is "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." In practice, this means that even if an appellate judge might have weighed the evidence differently, they cannot overturn a factual finding unless no reasonable person could have reached that conclusion based on the record before the Board. This distinction is critical because it preserves the TTAB's role as the primary fact-finder while allowing the appellate court to correct errors in legal application or statutory construction.

The substantive legal framework guiding this review is rooted in Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). This provision prohibits federal registration of a trademark if its use would likely cause confusion with a previously registered mark owned by another party who has rights to use that mark concurrently in commerce. The core inquiry under Section 2(d) is whether consumer confusion is likely due to this concurrent use on related goods or services. While Section 2(d) sets out the statutory prohibition, it does not provide a checklist for determining what constitutes "likelihood of confusion." To fill this gap, courts rely on precedent established in *In re E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The *DuPont* decision articulated thirteen non-exclusive factors designed to guide tribunals in assessing whether confusion is probable between two marks. These factors include considerations such as the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks themselves, their commercial impressions, and relatedness of goods; channels of trade; classes of purchasers; conditions under which buyers make purchasing decisions; fame of either mark; actual instances of confusion; and other relevant market dynamics like third-party usage patterns.

In applying these factors, courts recognize that likelihood of confusion is ultimately a question of law derived from underlying factual findings made under each *DuPont* factor. However, neither party nor the Board is required to analyze all thirteen factors in every instance. The Federal Circuit emphasizes that a tribunal need only consider those factors that are both relevant to the specific dispute and present in the evidentiary record (*M2 Software*). For example, if there is no evidence suggesting actual confusion occurred historically, that factor may be disregarded entirely without prejudicing either side's position on appeal. Similarly, if certain third-party registrations exist but bear little resemblance to either party's service offerings relative to other factors, they may fall outside the scope of relevance required for analysis. This flexibility allows litigants and adjudicators to focus their resources on issues where disagreement actually exists rather than engaging in rote recitation of every possible variable in every case. Ultimately, this structured approach balances judicial efficiency with thoroughness in resolving complex trademark conflicts like those presented between Apex Bank and CC Serve Corporation regarding their competing uses of "ASPIRE" variants across financial services markets.

# Chapter 3: Analysis of DuPont Factor Two – Similarity of Goods/Services

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed the foundational inquiry of trademark infringement: whether the parties' goods or services are so similar that consumers would likely mistake one for the other. Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a refusal to register occurs if "confusion is likely." To determine this likelihood, courts apply the thirteen factors established in *Application of E. I. Du Pont de Nemours & Co.* (the *DuPont* factors). While all thirteen factors inform the ultimate legal conclusion, each individual factor represents a specific question of fact reviewed for substantial evidence. This chapter focuses on the second *DuPont* factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and/or services. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) finding that Apex Bank's proposed "banking and financing services" were highly similar to CC Serve's registered "credit card services," rejecting Apex's contention that the services were unrelated simply because they were not direct competitors.

### Defining 'Credit Card Services': Interpreting Post-ID Manual Changes via Dictionary Definitions

The initial hurdle in applying Factor Two arose from a shift in administrative classification within the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). At the time of CC Serve's original registration and subsequent opposition proceedings, the Trademark Identification (ID) Manual listed "credit card services" as a specific category. However, during litigation, this entry was deleted from the manual. Consequently, before comparing CC Serve's services to Apex's, the TTAB had to define what "credit card services" actually entailed without relying on a rigid manual entry. The Board engaged in a textual analysis, looking beyond mere labels to examine the functional reality of the service provided. It determined that "credit card services" encompasses a broad spectrum of activities: issuing credit cards for purchase financing, counseling on debt management, processing payments and transactions, authorizing transactions, monitoring accounts, and providing access to credit scores.

To reach this expansive definition, particularly regarding functions like account servicing and credit score provision which go beyond simple card issuance, the Board relied on dictionary definitions of terms central to banking operations. The TTAB reasoned that standard dictionary meanings for words like "banking," "finance," and "extend credit" naturally include activities involving credit cards. By anchoring its interpretation in common linguistic usage rather than an outdated list item, the Board established that CC Serve's mark covered a comprehensive suite of financial management tools associated with credit lines. This definitional work was critical; it prevented CC Serve from being limited to a narrow slice of activity merely because it partnered with banks for actual funding while handling administration itself. Instead, the Board viewed CC Serve as offering integrated financial solutions where credit card mechanics are central.

### Comparative Analysis: Credit Card Servicing vs. General Banking Services

Once both sets of services were defined functionally rather than formally by trade names alone, they were placed side-by-side for comparison against Apex Bank's application for "[b]anking and financing services." Apex argued that its position as a retail bank offering checking accounts, savings accounts, mortgages, and loans meant it did not compete with CC Serve, which manages credit card programs but does not issue checks or hold traditional deposit accounts itself. Apex contended that because CC Serve acts as an agent joining with other banks rather than acting as a bank per se, its services were distinct from Apex's core offerings.

The Federal Circuit found this distinction unpersuasive upon reviewing whether substantial evidence supported the TTAB's conclusion that the services were related enough to cause confusion. The court noted that under established precedent (*Coach Servs., Inc.*), goods or services need not be identical or even directly competitive; they need only be related such that circumstances suggest they might originate from a single source or be affiliated entities. The analysis turned on overlapping functions rather than exclusive market niches. Since both parties offered financial instruments tied to consumer lending—specifically credit extension—their services occupied adjacent spaces in the marketplace where consumers reasonably expect interconnectivity. A consumer seeing "ASPIRE" attached to a new online banking platform operated by Apex might easily assume it was an expansion of their existing relationship with CC Serve's ASPIRE credit program given their shared focus on managing consumer debt and funds through digital interfaces. The court upheld the Board's determination that this functional overlap created sufficient similarity under Factor Two to warrant scrutiny under later stages of confusion analysis.

The TTAB utilized these registrations to demonstrate that it is common practice in financial markets for companies to bundle various banking functions under one brand name without causing market chaos due to confusion among sophisticated consumers or ordinary buyers alike regarding affiliation.

# Chapter 4: Vacatur Regarding DuPont Factor Six – Market Crowding and Scope Protection

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed a critical procedural and analytical flaw within the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) application of the *DuPont* likelihood-of-confusion test. Specifically, the court examined how the Board analyzed the sixth *DuPont* factor, which concerns "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." The central holding of this chapter is that a failure to maintain consistency in defining "similar goods" across different *DuPont* factors can lead to an artificial inflation of a mark's commercial strength, thereby distorting the market-crowding analysis. By restricting its search for third-party marks to a narrower category than the one established under the second factor, the Board erroneously concluded that CC Serve's mark was entitled to normal protection rather than a narrowed scope due to market saturation. The Federal Circuit vacated this portion of the decision, emphasizing that once a court or board finds services to be highly similar under one factor, that definition must govern subsequent analyses involving those same services.

To understand why the Board's error mattered, one must first grasp the function of Factor Six within trademark law. Unlike many other factors that focus on specific attributes like similarity of sight or sound, Factor Six serves as a check against over-protection of common terms. The logic follows that if a field is crowded with similar marks (e.g., multiple companies using "Aspire" in financial contexts), consumers become accustomed to distinguishing between them. Consequently, even if two new marks are somewhat alike, they are less likely to cause confusion because consumers are trained to look past minor differences in such a saturated environment. Conversely, if a mark is unique in its field, it enjoys a broad scope of protection where even slight similarities might trigger confusion. Therefore, accurately identifying the universe of "similar goods" is paramount; narrowing this universe artificially reduces the perceived density of competing marks, making an existing mark appear stronger than it truly is relative to the actual marketplace reality.

The conflict in *Apex Bank* arose from an inconsistency between how the Board defined "similar services" in its analysis of Factor Two versus its analysis of Factor Six. Under Factor Two, which assesses the similarity of goods and services themselves, the Board had determined that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex Bank's "banking and financing services" were legally identical in part and highly similar generally. The Board reasoned that both sectors involved issuing credit cards or extending credit through various financial instruments. Having established this high degree of similarity under Factor Two—a finding supported by substantial evidence—the Board then turned to Factor Six to determine if third-party usage weakened CC Serve's claim. However, instead of applying its own finding from Factor Two, the Board arbitrarily restricted its inquiry under Factor Six exclusively to third-party marks registered strictly for "credit card services." It explicitly excluded forty-two other third-party registrations using "Aspire" for broader banking and financing services, deeming them "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit identified this exclusion as a fundamental legal error rooted in logical inconsistency. The court noted that while exact identity is not required for Factor Six—only similarity—the scope of what constitutes "similar goods" cannot shift arbitrarily between factors without justification. Since the Board had already factually found that banking/financing services were sufficiently similar to credit card services to weigh heavily toward confusion under Factor Two, it logically followed that these same banking/financing services constituted valid comparisons for determining market crowding under Factor Six. By ignoring third-party users operating in these broader yet legally recognized service categories, the Board failed to capture the true extent of competition facing CC Serve's mark. If Apex Bank operates in banking/financing (which includes credit cards), then competitors offering those same broader services are legitimate sources of potential consumer confusion; ignoring them paints an inaccurate picture of how crowded or clear the market actually is for consumers seeking these combined financial solutions.

Consequently, because this definitional error skewed the assessment of commercial strength, it necessitated further action beyond simple reversal. The appellate court could not simply rule on whether there were enough third-party marks without knowing how broadly those marks should be counted based on consistent legal standards regarding service similarity. Furthermore, this error rippled into Factor One (similarity of marks). Commercial impression often depends on context; if a mark appears weak due to widespread use across a broad range of banking activities (as would be revealed by considering all relevant third-party marks), its visual or phonetic distinctiveness carries less weight in preventing confusion than if it appeared strong amidst sparse competition. Thus, because recalculating market crowding under Factor Six could alter perceptions regarding commercial strength—which directly informs whether Apex Bank's proposed "ASPIRE BANK" mark creates a likelihood of confusion—the court vacated both Factors Five and One (though primarily focusing on Four/Six logic) and remanded for reconsideration consistent with its finding on service similarity.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* stands as a cautionary tale for administrative bodies conducting trademark oppositions: consistency is not merely stylistic but substantive when applying multi-factor tests like *DuPont*. A tribunal cannot define markets narrowly for one purpose (to deny protection) while widening them for another (or vice versa) depending on which side benefits from that definition unless there is distinct evidentiary support justifying such divergence across different analytical stages. In this case, by failing to align its definition of "similar goods" between Factors Two and Six, the TTAB created an illusion where CC Serve's mark seemed uniquely dominant when it was actually subject to significant market saturation across related financial fields. The resulting vacatur ensured that upon remand, any determination regarding market crowding would reflect a realistic view of consumer behavior within the full scope of overlapping banking and credit card industries previously acknowledged by the board itself.
