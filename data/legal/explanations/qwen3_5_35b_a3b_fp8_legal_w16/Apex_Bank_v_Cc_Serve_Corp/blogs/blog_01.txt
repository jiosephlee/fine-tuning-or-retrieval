Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Defining "Similar Goods" Across Factors: How *Apex Bank* Resolves the Scope Problem in Trademark Likelihood-of-Confusion Analysis

In the complex calculus of trademark law, few procedural tangles are as frustrating—or as consequential—as the inconsistency with which courts define the scope of "goods and services." The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* cuts through this ambiguity with a straightforward, logic-driven mandate: once a tribunal determines that two sets of services are sufficiently similar to trigger a likelihood of confusion under one factor, it cannot arbitrarily shrink that definition when analyzing market crowding under another. By vacating and remanding the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) analysis on grounds of internal contradiction, *Apex Bank* reinforces a critical doctrinal principle: the definition of similarity must remain consistent across all relevant *DuPont* factors to preserve the integrity of the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.

The dispute arose from a classic clash between established players and new entrants in the financial sector. CC Serve holds a long-standing registration for "ASPIRE" covering credit card services, while Apex Bank sought to register "ASPIRE BANK" for general banking and financing services. Although Apex does not currently issue credit cards, it planned to launch an online banking platform that would compete in the broader ecosystem of consumer finance. The TTAB initially sustained CC Serve's opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion primarily because it viewed credit card services and general banking as "legally identical, in part," thereby weighing heavily against Apex under the second *DuPont* factor (similarity of goods/services). However, when the Board turned to the sixth factor—which asks whether many third parties use similar marks on similar goods to prove that the plaintiff's mark is weak—it abruptly narrowed its lens. The Board ignored dozens of third-party marks using "Aspire" for general banking and lending, restricting its analysis exclusively to those tied strictly to credit card management.

This bifurcation is precisely what doomed the TTAB's ruling before Judge Hughes and her colleagues on appeal. Under traditional practice, courts often treat "similarity" differently depending on which *DuPont* factor is at play; they might apply a broad definition for Factor Two but demand near-identical goods for Factor Six to avoid diluting a strong mark. The Federal Circuit rejected this approach entirely. The court reasoned that if the Board has already made a factual finding under Factor Two that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services are highly similar—even legally identical—there is no logical basis for suddenly declaring those same services distinct enough to exclude vast swaths of third-party evidence under Factor Six. As the court noted, Factor Six does not require identical goods, only similar ones; therefore, applying a stricter standard here than in Factor Two creates an artificial barrier that undermines the evidentiary purpose of showing market saturation.

The implications of *Apex Bank* extend far beyond this specific financial dispute; they signal a necessary correction to how administrative tribunals conduct their fact-finding missions in trademark cases. For years, practitioners have navigated a murky landscape where opposing counsel could argue for expansive definitions of goods when claiming confusion but restrictive definitions when arguing about market crowding. This strategic maneuvering allowed boards to cherry-pick evidence that supported their desired outcome rather than adhering to a coherent narrative about the marketplace reality. By mandating that the scope of similarity remain fixed once established in one section of the analysis, *Apex Bank* forces litigants and adjudicators alike to confront the full breadth of third-party usage immediately upon establishing service similarity. If you admit your competitor sells something close enough to yours to cause confusion today, you cannot later claim their competitors aren't doing so just because you want your client's mark treated as stronger than it actually is relative to those others.

Furthermore, this ruling necessitates a domino effect throughout future proceedings involving Factors One through Thirteen. Since commercial impression (Factor One) is intrinsically linked to market context (Factor Six), vacating Factor Six logically requires revisiting Factor One as well—a step explicitly taken by the Federal Circuit in *Apex*. This interconnectedness ensures that no single factor operates in isolation; instead, they form a holistic picture where shifting one variable without adjusting others leads to legal error. While this may increase the complexity of initial briefs—requiring applicants like Apex to marshal all available third-party data upfront regardless of how narrowly or broadly their services are defined—it ultimately promotes greater transparency and fairness in trademark registration decisions.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank* serves as a potent reminder that trademark law is not merely a checklist exercise but an integrated assessment of commercial reality. The Federal Circuit has drawn a clear line: consistency is king. When defining what constitutes "similar goods," courts must choose one lane and stick with it across all relevant factors unless new evidence fundamentally alters that foundational premise. For attorneys navigating Section 2(d) refusals or oppositions, this decision means abandoning tactical inconsistencies in favor of rigorous, uniform definitions early in litigation strategy. As lower courts and potentially even state tribunals grapple with these nuances moving forward, *Apex Bank* stands as a clarifying beacon against fragmented analyses that risk distorting the very concept of consumer confusion it seeks to prevent.
