Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How can the Board find 'legal identity' between credit card services and general banking services when the parties explicitly state they do not compete directly?
Question:
I am struggling with the Board's analysis of the second DuPont factor regarding the similarity of goods/services. The opinion states that CC Serve partners with banks to issue cards but doesn't offer banking services itself, while Apex is a bank offering loans and deposits but *no* credit cards. Yet, the Board concluded their services are 'legally identical, in part' simply because dictionary definitions of 'banking' encompass issuing credit cards. How does the court reconcile this broad dictionary definition approach with the practical reality that these are distinct business models? Is this a new trend where functional overlaps in definitions trump actual market competition?
Answer:
The Board reconciled the apparent disconnect between the parties' distinct business models and the "legal identity" of their services by applying a **functional definition** of the services rather than a strict operational one. Under the second *DuPont* factor, the standard is not whether the parties operate in direct competition or share the exact same revenue streams, but whether the services are "related in some manner" such that consumers might mistakenly believe they emanate from the same source (*Coach Servs.*).

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit affirmed that the Board correctly relied on dictionary definitions to bridge this gap. Although CC Serve does not hold a charter to offer traditional banking (loans/deposits) and Apex does not issue credit cards, both entities engage in activities that fall within the broad semantic umbrella of "banking." Specifically, the Board noted that dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." Because Apex's application covered "[b]anking and financing services," and CC Serve provided credit card services (which dictionaries define as a subset of banking/financing), the Board found their services were **"legally identical, in part."**

This approach confirms that for trademark purposes, overlapping functional capabilities trump structural differences in how a company delivers those capabilities. The court rejected Apex's argument that its lack of physical branches or specific product lines (like loans) distinguished it from CC Serve. Instead, because both parties offered financial services involving credit extension—a core component of banking—the risk of consumer confusion remained significant enough to satisfy the second factor. The court clarified that this is not a new trend but a consistent application of trademark law: if a dictionary definition links two service categories, and one party's service falls squarely within that definition relative to another's, they are sufficiently similar regardless of whether they are direct competitors in practice.
