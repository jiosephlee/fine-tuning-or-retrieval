Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What is the procedural mechanism for remanding a trademark case when one factor's re-evaluation logically necessitates changing another unrelated factor?
Question:
The court vacated both Factor Six and Factor One (similarity of marks). I understand why Factor Six needed a redo if we expand the pool of third-party marks (as per my previous question), but why did that automatically force a re-examination of Factor One? The opinion states: 'reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength... and affect the overall commercial impression.' Doesn't commercial strength usually feed into distinctiveness or fame, whereas commercial impression is about visual/phonetic similarity? I'm trying to understand if there is an inherent link between 'market crowding' (Factor Six) and how consumers perceive two specific words ('Aspire' vs 'Aspire Bank') that makes them inseparable on remand.
Answer:
The court's decision to vacate **Factor One** (similarity of marks) alongside **Factor Six** (third-party marks) rests on the interdependence between "commercial strength" and "commercial impression." While these concepts often appear distinct, *Apex Bank* clarifies that a change in the evidentiary basis for commercial strength can fundamentally alter the perceived similarity of the marks themselves.

Under the **first DuPont factor**, the inquiry is not merely a mechanical comparison of spelling or pronunciation ("appearance, sound, connotation"). Instead, it focuses on the **"overall commercial impression"** left on the consuming public (*In re I.AM.Symbolic*). The court explicitly links this impression to the mark's **commercial strength**.

The procedural necessity arises from how Factor Six functions as a proxy for commercial strength. As noted in Section III.B of the opinion, evidence of third-party use (crowding) demonstrates that a mark is "weak" and entitled to only a "narrow scope of protection." Conversely, a lack of such evidence suggests a stronger mark entitled to broader protection.

In *Apex*, the Board committed reversible error by artificially narrowing the scope of third-party marks considered in Factor Six. By excluding valid third-party marks outside the strict "credit card services" category, the Board incorrectly assessed CC Serve's mark as having only "normal scope" rather than potentially being weaker due to market crowding.

Because commercial strength dictates how much leeway consumers allow before assuming confusion, expanding the universe of third-party marks could reveal that CC Serve's mark is weak. If CC Serve's mark is weak, its "scope of protection" narrows significantly. Consequently, even minor differences between "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" might become sufficient to avoid confusion because consumers are accustomed to seeing variations of "Aspire" across many entities. Alternatively, if the expanded evidence shows *less* crowding than previously thought, the mark might be stronger, making even slight similarities more dangerous.

Therefore, you cannot determine whether "ASPIRE" and "ASPIRE BANK" create a confusingly similar **commercial impression** without first knowing where those marks fall on the spectrum of **strength**. Since vacating Factor Six forces a re-evaluation of whether CC Serve's mark is strong or weak based on a corrected dataset, Factor One must be recalculated to reflect how that new assessment changes the overall commercial reality for consumers. The factors are not isolated; they operate within a unified framework where market context (Factor Six) directly shapes perceptual analysis (Factor One).
