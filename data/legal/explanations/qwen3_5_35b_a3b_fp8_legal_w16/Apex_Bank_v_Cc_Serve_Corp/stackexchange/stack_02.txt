Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the Sixth DuPont Factor analysis based on a definitional inconsistency rather than just reviewing for substantial evidence?
Question:
In section III.B, the court vacates the Board's finding on the sixth factor (third-party marks). My confusion lies in why this wasn't just a 'substantial evidence' issue. The Board found only 9 similar marks for 'credit card services,' which wasn't enough to prove weakness. However, Apex wanted to include 42 other marks involving broader financial services. The court says restricting the universe was a legal error because it contradicted their own finding in Factor Two (that services are highly similar). Why does defining 'similar goods' differently across different DuPont factors constitute reversible legal error? Shouldn't the Board have discretion to define the relevant market narrowly for each specific factor?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's Sixth DuPont Factor analysis not because of a failure to apply "substantial evidence" to the *count* of marks, but because the Board committed a **legal error** by applying an inconsistent definition of "similarity" across different factors. While factual findings within a factor are reviewed for substantial evidence, the legal framework governing how those facts are gathered and compared is reviewed *de novo*.

Here is why the definitional inconsistency constituted reversible legal error:

**1. The Legal Standard Requires Consistency in Defining "Similar Goods"**
Under the Second DuPont Factor, the Board found that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex's "banking and financing services" were **"highly similar,"** even noting they were "legally identical, in part." This was a binding factual finding regarding the scope of similarity between the parties' goods.

However, when addressing the Sixth Factor (which looks at third-party marks on *similar* goods), the Board narrowed its scope to exclude all third-party marks that did not strictly say "credit card services," despite acknowledging that other marks covered broader financial services. The Federal Circuit held that once a court or board determines goods are highly similar under Factor Two, it cannot arbitrarily redefine "similar goods" to be much narrower under Factor Six without justification. As stated in Section III.B: *"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."*

**2. Why This Was Not Just a Substantial Evidence Issue**
You correctly noted that if there were merely insufficient evidence of 9 marks versus 51 marks, this would be a substantial evidence challenge. However, the problem here was procedural and logical, evidentiary sufficiency notwithstanding.

The Board created a contradiction:
*   **Step A (Factor 2):** Defined the universe of goods as broad (banking/financing = credit card services).
*   **Step B (Factor 6):** Defined the universe of goods as narrow (credit card services only).

By excluding 42 relevant third-party marks simply because they fell outside its self-imposed narrow definition for Factor 6—despite having already ruled they were legally similar under Factor 2—the Board failed to apply the correct legal standard for what constitutes "similar goods" in a likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The Sixth Factor explicitly requires considering marks on **"similar"** goods (*DuPont*, 476 F.2d at 1361), not necessarily *identical* goods (*Juice Generation*). By imposing a stricter standard than it applied to itself in Factor Two, the Board misapplied the law.

**Conclusion**
The court vacated because you cannot have it both ways: you cannot claim two sets of services are so closely related that confusion is likely (Factor Two) while simultaneously claiming they are distinct enough to ignore vast swaths of third-party precedent covering those very same related fields (Factor Six). The error was not that there weren't enough *facts* about nine marks; it was that the Board applied an incorrect *legal rule* by failing to maintain consistency in its definition of similarity across factors.
