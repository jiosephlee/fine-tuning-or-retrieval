Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Does this ruling imply that once courts find services are 'highly similar,' they must treat all subsequent DuPont factors as having an expanded scope automatically?
Question:
The core holding seems to hinge on consistency: since Factor Two said services are highly similar, Factor Six couldn't limit its search to just credit cards. Does this create a rigid rule where finding similarity in Factor Two locks in a broad interpretation for all other factors? If so, how does this interact with cases where courts typically analyze each DuPont factor somewhat independently based on its specific evidentiary record? Am I missing nuance here regarding whether this is about logical consistency or something deeper about how trademark rights scale with service breadth?
Answer:
No, *Apex Bank* does not establish a rigid rule that a "highly similar" finding under DuPont Factor Two automatically expands the scope of *all* subsequent factors. Instead, the court's reasoning is limited to ensuring **logical consistency** within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, specifically regarding the definition of "similar goods and services."

Here is the breakdown of the legal principle derived from the opinion:

**1. Consistency in Defining "Similarity"**
The core holding addresses a definitional inconsistency rather than an automatic expansion of rights. The Board had found under Factor Two that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services were "highly similar" (even "legally identical, in part"). However, when applying Factor Six (market crowding/third-party marks), the Board arbitrarily narrowed its search to *only* credit card services, ignoring other banking/financing services despite its own finding that those categories were sufficiently similar.

The Federal Circuit clarified that if a court finds two types of services are "highly similar" for the purpose of determining confusion potential (Factor Two), it cannot subsequently define "similar goods" for third-party mark analysis (Factor Six) as being narrower than that initial finding. As stated in Section III.B: *"When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."*

**2. Not an Automatic Expansion of All Factors**
The opinion explicitly rejects imposing a "different and more stringent legal standard for similarity" only in this context. It does not suggest that every factor must now be interpreted broadly regardless of its specific evidentiary record. For instance:
*   **Factor One (Mark Similarity):** The court vacated this only because a broader look at market crowding (Factor Six) might alter the assessment of commercial strength and commercial impression. It did not say Factor One is automatically broadened; it required reconsideration *in light of* the corrected scope.
*   **Other Factors:** The text notes that courts generally analyze factors based on their specific records (*M2 Software*). The ruling simply prevents a contradiction where a court says Services A and B are alike for one purpose but distinct for another within the same case logic.

**3. Nuance: Logical Flow vs. Rights Scaling**
You are correct to suspect this is about logical consistency rather than trademark rights scaling with service breadth per se. The court emphasized that *"the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones"* (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*). By defining similarity narrowly under Factor Six while acknowledging broad similarity under Factor Two, the Board created an internal conflict that skewed the analysis against Apex without justification.

**Conclusion**
The ruling implies that once a trier of fact determines goods/services are highly similar under Factor Two, they must apply that **same definition of similarity** when identifying relevant third-party marks under Factor Six (and potentially affecting Factor One). It does not create a blanket rule expanding all factors; rather, it mandates that courts do not engage in "cherry-picking" definitions to manipulate outcomes across different factors within a single likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.
