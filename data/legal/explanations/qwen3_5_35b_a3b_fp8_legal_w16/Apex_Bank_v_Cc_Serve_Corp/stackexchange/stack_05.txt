Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### If Apex didn't actually offer credit cards yet (intent-to-use), does that change how we evaluate likelihood of confusion compared to actual concurrent use?
Question:
The opinion mentions Apex filed an intent-to-use application and planned to launch later, while CC Serve had active registrations since 1996. Usually, likelihood of confusion looks at current market conditions. Since Apex hadn't started selling yet during most of this timeline, does that weaken or strengthen their argument against confusion? Or does it matter at all given that Lanham Act Section 2(d) prohibits registration even before use if confusion is likely upon launch? I'm confused about how temporal proximity plays into these specific du Pont analyses when one party hasn't entered the market.
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
No, Apex's status as an "intent-to-use" applicant does not automatically weaken its likelihood-of-confusion defense, nor does it fundamentally alter the *DuPont* analysis once the Board finds the services are similar. Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a refusal can occur based on a *future* likelihood of confusion if the applicant's proposed use would likely cause confusion upon launch. However, the *temporal gap* between the parties' actual use is a critical factual component of **Factor Six** (market crowding/weakness), which determines how much protection a senior mark deserves.

In *Apex Bank*, the Federal Circuit clarified that while Apex had not yet launched, the Board committed legal error by defining "similar goods" narrowly for Factor Six (looking only at credit cards) while defining them broadly for Factor Two (banking and financing). By restricting the search for third-party marks to only those currently offering credit cards, the Board ignored potential evidence that "Apire" is common across broader banking services—a sector where Apex intends to operate.

Here is the detailed breakdown of how this works under the opinion:

### 1. Intent-to-Use Does Not Preclude Confusion Analysis
The core misconception in your query is whether a lack of actual sales negates a Section 2(d) claim. The opinion explicitly rejects this idea by citing *Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, which establishes that Section 2(d) prohibits registration when confusion is likely due to **"concurrent use."**

While Apex had not yet commenced use at the time of filing (August 2019), the statute contemplates future concurrent use. The Board correctly applied this standard: if Apex launches its "ASPIRE BANK" service and CC Serve continues using "ASPIRE" for credit cards, consumers may believe they originate from the same source. Therefore, Apex's lack of current market presence does not immunize it from opposition; rather, it shifts the inquiry to whether confusion is likely *upon launch*.

### 2. How Temporal Proximity Affects Factor Six (Market Crowding)
You asked specifically about how temporal proximity plays into *DuPont* Factor Six ("number and nature of similar marks"). This factor asks whether there are many similar marks in existence, suggesting consumers are accustomed to distinguishing between them (making new marks weaker).

The opinion highlights that **the scope of "similar goods" dictates what counts toward Factor Six.**
*   **The Board's Error:** The Board found that credit card services and banking services were "highly similar" (Factor Two). However, when calculating Factor Six, it artificially narrowed its search to *only* existing marks registered strictly for "credit card services," ignoring marks used for general banking or financing where Apex intended to enter.
*   **The Court's Correction:** The Federal Circuit held that if you define goods as similar in Factor Two, you must maintain that same breadth in Factor Six. If Apex intends to sell banking services (which include issuing credit cards), then third-party marks used on *other* banking services count toward showing market crowding.

Because Apex was an intent-to-use applicant planning to enter the broader banking market immediately upon launch, excluding non-credit-card-specific third-party marks distorted the analysis of whether CC Serve's mark was strong or weak in that specific marketplace. If there are dozens of "Aspire" marks across various banking sectors (where Apex plans to compete), CC Serve gets narrower protection regardless of whether Apex has physically printed a single credit card yet.

### Conclusion
Apex's intent-to-use status does not change *how* we evaluate confusion legally; it simply means we project forward to when both parties will be operating concurrently. However, it significantly impacts **Fact-Finding**. Because Apex planned immediate entry into a broad range of financial services, the Board was required to look at third-party usage across that entire spectrum when assessing market crowding (Factor Six). By failing to do so—and focusing only on existing credit card users—the Board improperly weighted CC Serve's commercial strength against an applicant who was poised to enter a wider field than just credit cards alone.
