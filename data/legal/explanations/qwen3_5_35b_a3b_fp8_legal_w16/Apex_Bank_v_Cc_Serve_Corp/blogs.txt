Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Defining "Similar Goods" Across Factors: How *Apex Bank* Resolves the Scope Problem in Trademark Likelihood-of-Confusion Analysis

In the complex calculus of trademark law, few procedural tangles are as frustrating—or as consequential—as the inconsistency with which courts define the scope of "goods and services." The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* cuts through this ambiguity with a straightforward, logic-driven mandate: once a tribunal determines that two sets of services are sufficiently similar to trigger a likelihood of confusion under one factor, it cannot arbitrarily shrink that definition when analyzing market crowding under another. By vacating and remanding the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (TTAB) analysis on grounds of internal contradiction, *Apex Bank* reinforces a critical doctrinal principle: the definition of similarity must remain consistent across all relevant *DuPont* factors to preserve the integrity of the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.

The dispute arose from a classic clash between established players and new entrants in the financial sector. CC Serve holds a long-standing registration for "ASPIRE" covering credit card services, while Apex Bank sought to register "ASPIRE BANK" for general banking and financing services. Although Apex does not currently issue credit cards, it planned to launch an online banking platform that would compete in the broader ecosystem of consumer finance. The TTAB initially sustained CC Serve's opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion primarily because it viewed credit card services and general banking as "legally identical, in part," thereby weighing heavily against Apex under the second *DuPont* factor (similarity of goods/services). However, when the Board turned to the sixth factor—which asks whether many third parties use similar marks on similar goods to prove that the plaintiff's mark is weak—it abruptly narrowed its lens. The Board ignored dozens of third-party marks using "Aspire" for general banking and lending, restricting its analysis exclusively to those tied strictly to credit card management.

This bifurcation is precisely what doomed the TTAB's ruling before Judge Hughes and her colleagues on appeal. Under traditional practice, courts often treat "similarity" differently depending on which *DuPont* factor is at play; they might apply a broad definition for Factor Two but demand near-identical goods for Factor Six to avoid diluting a strong mark. The Federal Circuit rejected this approach entirely. The court reasoned that if the Board has already made a factual finding under Factor Two that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services are highly similar—even legally identical—there is no logical basis for suddenly declaring those same services distinct enough to exclude vast swaths of third-party evidence under Factor Six. As the court noted, Factor Six does not require identical goods, only similar ones; therefore, applying a stricter standard here than in Factor Two creates an artificial barrier that undermines the evidentiary purpose of showing market saturation.

The implications of *Apex Bank* extend far beyond this specific financial dispute; they signal a necessary correction to how administrative tribunals conduct their fact-finding missions in trademark cases. For years, practitioners have navigated a murky landscape where opposing counsel could argue for expansive definitions of goods when claiming confusion but restrictive definitions when arguing about market crowding. This strategic maneuvering allowed boards to cherry-pick evidence that supported their desired outcome rather than adhering to a coherent narrative about the marketplace reality. By mandating that the scope of similarity remain fixed once established in one section of the analysis, *Apex Bank* forces litigants and adjudicators alike to confront the full breadth of third-party usage immediately upon establishing service similarity. If you admit your competitor sells something close enough to yours to cause confusion today, you cannot later claim their competitors aren't doing so just because you want your client's mark treated as stronger than it actually is relative to those others.

Furthermore, this ruling necessitates a domino effect throughout future proceedings involving Factors One through Thirteen. Since commercial impression (Factor One) is intrinsically linked to market context (Factor Six), vacating Factor Six logically requires revisiting Factor One as well—a step explicitly taken by the Federal Circuit in *Apex*. This interconnectedness ensures that no single factor operates in isolation; instead, they form a holistic picture where shifting one variable without adjusting others leads to legal error. While this may increase the complexity of initial briefs—requiring applicants like Apex to marshal all available third-party data upfront regardless of how narrowly or broadly their services are defined—it ultimately promotes greater transparency and fairness in trademark registration decisions.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank* serves as a potent reminder that trademark law is not merely a checklist exercise but an integrated assessment of commercial reality. The Federal Circuit has drawn a clear line: consistency is king. When defining what constitutes "similar goods," courts must choose one lane and stick with it across all relevant factors unless new evidence fundamentally alters that foundational premise. For attorneys navigating Section 2(d) refusals or oppositions, this decision means abandoning tactical inconsistencies in favor of rigorous, uniform definitions early in litigation strategy. As lower courts and potentially even state tribunals grapple with these nuances moving forward, *Apex Bank* stands as a clarifying beacon against fragmented analyses that risk distorting the very concept of consumer confusion it seeks to prevent.



# The Ripple Effect of Market Crowding: Why *Apex Bank* Forces Re-evaluation of Commercial Strength and First-Factor Impressions

In the intricate machinery of trademark law, the Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* serves as a stark reminder that the thirteen *DuPont* factors are not isolated silos but a tightly interwoven web of analysis. While the Board correctly identified that credit card services and general banking services are sufficiently similar to trigger a likelihood of confusion, it stumbled significantly in its assessment of "market crowding." By artificially narrowing the scope of third-party marks considered under the sixth *DuPont* factor, the Board inadvertently skewed its evaluation of commercial strength, a flaw so fundamental that it necessitated vacating even the initial determination regarding the marks' commercial impressions under the first factor. For practitioners navigating Section 2(d) oppositions, *Apex Bank* clarifies that consistency in defining "similar goods" across all relevant factors is not merely a matter of preference—it is a prerequisite for a valid likelihood-of-confusion ruling.

The core tension in *Apex Bank* arose from how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) defined the universe of competing marks. Under the second *DuPont* factor, which assesses the similarity of goods and services, the Board found substantial evidence that CC Serve's "credit card services" and Apex Bank's "banking and financing services" were legally identical in part. Having established this broad equivalence to justify weighing heavily against Apex, the Board pivoted to the sixth factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—to determine if CC Serve's mark had acquired strong commercial protection or if it was weak due to market saturation. Here, however, the Board committed a critical logical error: it restricted its search for third-party "Aspire" marks exclusively to those registered for credit card services. It dismissed over forty other third-party registrations covering broader banking and financing services as "essentially irrelevant," despite having previously ruled that these fields were functionally equivalent.

This inconsistency is precisely where the Federal Circuit intervened. Writing for a unanimous panel, Circuit Judge Hughes noted that while exact identity isn't required for similarity under any factor, once a court or board finds two categories of service to be highly similar under one lens (Factor Two), it cannot arbitrarily shrink that definition under another (Factor Six). The logic is straightforward: if consumers perceive banking services and credit card management as emanating from potentially overlapping sources—a premise accepted by both parties and supported by third-party registrations—they are equally capable of perceiving multiple brands offering variations within that ecosystem. By ignoring forty-two additional marks operating in this broader financial space, the TTAB failed to capture what might be significant evidence that "Aspire" is actually quite common in this sector. If those forty-two marks are admitted into evidence, they could transform CC Serve's mark from one entitled to normal protection into one entitled only to narrow protection due to market crowding.

The most instructive aspect of *Apex Bank*, however, lies in its procedural consequence: why did an error under Factor Six force a remand on Factor One? The first *DuPont* factor examines whether there is confusion based on the similarity of marks themselves—appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Crucially, courts have long recognized that this analysis is not static; it is dynamic depending on context. A mark like "Aspire" might appear distinctive enough to cause confusion when viewed against a backdrop where only nine competitors exist (as suggested by the Board's limited search). However, if viewed against a backdrop where dozens of entities use "Aspire" across various financial niches (the corrected reality), consumers become acclimated to seeing variations within that term family. They develop a heightened ability to distinguish between them. Consequently, what looks like a striking similarity between "ASPIRE BANK" and "ASPIRE" might fade into insignificance once the full breadth of third-party usage is acknowledged. Because Factor Six informs commercial strength—and commercial strength dictates how much weight consumers place on minor differences—the Federal Circuit correctly reasoned that you cannot fix Factor Six without re-evaluating Factor One.

For counsel drafting future briefs or appealing TTAB decisions, *Apex Bank* establishes a clear doctrine: definitional consistency is paramount. An opponent seeking to prove likelihood of confusion must ensure their definition of "similar goods" remains robust throughout their entire argument; conversely, an applicant challenging an opposition must argue that any expansion or contraction of service categories applies holistically across all factors. The danger lies in cherry-picking definitions—arguing broadly for similarity when necessary to establish competition under Factor Two, only to argue narrowly when assessing market saturation under Factor Six simply because it favors one side's narrative at different stages. The Federal Circuit rejected this approach in *Apex*, signaling that such piecemeal analysis undermines the integrity of the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry entirely.

Ultimately, while CCP Serve retains its victory on appeal regarding the similarity of services (Factor Two), its path forward remains uncertain pending remand proceedings where hundreds more third-party registrations may enter the fray. If those marks indeed dilute CC Serve's claim to exclusivity in ways not currently apparent on record, Apex Bank may yet secure registration for ASPIRE BANK upon retrial before an open-minded Board applying consistent standards across all thirteen factors. Until then, litigants would do well to heed Judge Hughes' warning: you cannot redefine your playing field halfway through the game without inviting judicial correction at every turn.



# Beyond Credit Cards: The Expanding Universe of Financial Services in Modern Trademark Disputes

In the rapidly evolving landscape of financial technology, the line between "pure" credit card processors and full-service banks is blurring. Yet, for decades, trademark practitioners relied on these technical distinctions to argue against likelihood of confusion. That strategy took a significant hit last week in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, where the Federal Circuit affirmed a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) decision finding similarity between banking services and credit card services but critically vacated the Board's analysis of market crowding. While the outcome leaves the ultimate fate of Apex's "ASPIRE BANK" application undecided, the court's reasoning sends a clear signal: in modern trademark disputes involving financial services, courts are unlikely to tolerate artificially narrow definitions of "similar goods" simply to shield a registrant from a crowded marketplace defense.

The dispute arose when CC Serve opposed Apex Bank's intent-to-use application for "ASPIRE BANK," relying on its own senior registration for "ASPIRE" covering credit card services. The core tension lay in the nature of their businesses: CC Serve partners with banks to manage credit card programs but does not issue cards itself, whereas Apex operates as a traditional brick-and-mortar bank offering loans and deposits but currently no credit cards. Despite these operational differences, the TTAB found under the second *DuPont* factor that the services were sufficiently related—indeed, partially legally identical—to create a risk of confusion. The Board reasoned that both entities involve extending credit and managing financial accounts, noting that third-party registrations often straddle both categories. The Federal Circuit agreed with this assessment, upholding the finding that the services are highly similar enough to trigger Lanham Act protections.

However, where the Federal Circuit truly departed from precedent—and where *Apex Bank* will likely reshape future litigation—is in its treatment of the sixth *DuPont* factor regarding third-party use. Under this factor, a plaintiff can defeat an opposition by showing that many others are using similar marks on similar goods, thereby rendering their own mark weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The logic follows that if consumers are accustomed to seeing variations of "Aspire" across a crowded field, they are less likely to assume two specific users share a common source.

Here lies the critical error committed by the TTAB and corrected by the appellate court. When evaluating which third-party marks counted toward proving crowding, the Board limited its search strictly to marks registered specifically for "credit card services." It excluded dozens of other marks containing "Aspire" used for broader banking and financing activities. The Federal Circuit rejected this compartmentalization as legally flawed. If, as established under Factor Two, banking and credit card services are functionally similar or even identical in this context, then excluding third-party marks from adjacent financial sectors creates an arbitrary artificial boundary. The court held that once similarity is established generally under Factor Two, Factor Six cannot restrict its scope further; otherwise, it would allow parties to game the system by defining their niche so narrowly that it appears uncrowded when viewed in isolation from its functional reality.

This ruling carries profound implications for strategic planning within the fintech sector and legacy banking alike. For years, companies may have attempted to carve out defensive moats around specific service descriptions—arguing that while they process payments and another entity offers mortgages or general lending, those are distinct markets warranting separate trademark zones. *Apex Bank* suggests such arguments will carry less weight if those markets are economically intertwined or serve overlapping consumer needs regarding credit extension and account management. Applicants can no longer rely on technical distinctions in service descriptions alone to insulate themselves from claims based on crowded markets if those markets are functionally related in practice.

Furthermore, this decision underscores a shift toward a holistic view of commercial reality over semantic precision in trademark classification systems like Nice Classification or USPTO identification manuals. By forcing consistency between Factors Two and Six, the Federal Circuit ensures that the evidentiary basis for determining market saturation reflects how consumers actually perceive these industries rather than how regulators categorize them on paper. For banks entering new digital spaces or fintechs expanding into broader advisory roles, this means due diligence must expand beyond direct competitors to include all entities operating within their broader ecosystem of financial trust-building activities.

Ultimately, while *Apex Bank* returns the matter to the TTAB without resolving whether Apex's mark is ultimately registrable today's ruling acts as a cautionary tale about fragmentation defenses in trademark law. It establishes that when goods or services are sufficiently linked in commerce, courts will demand a comprehensive look at third-party usage across those linked fields before granting broad protection rights—or denying them based on market saturation). As financial products continue to converge digitally and operationally, *Apex Bank* serves as an early indicator that American trademark doctrine is adapting to ensure that legal boundaries keep pace with economic realities rather than lagging behind them in rigid silos defined by outdated service classifications.
