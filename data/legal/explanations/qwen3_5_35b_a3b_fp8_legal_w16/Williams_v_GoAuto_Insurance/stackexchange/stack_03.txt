Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### What constitutes a valid 'private carrier' in the context of transmitting insurance cancellation notices between entities?
Question:
While affirming on different grounds regarding electronic transmission, the district court also argued that an APAC employee delivering physical documents fit the definition of a 'private carrier.' The appellate court explicitly declines to rule on this because they found emails sufficient. Given that federal courts rarely define state common law terms like 'private carrier,' why did the lower court feel comfortable making such an analogy (citing *Huntley*), and what are the risks for insurers relying on internal employees versus traditional carriers for statutory notice delivery?
Answer:
The district court's reliance on the "private carrier" analogy was likely driven by a practical attempt to satisfy the statutory requirement of "receipt" under Louisiana law when physical delivery occurred via an APAC employee rather than a traditional third-party logistics provider. While the Fifth Circuit declined to endorse this reasoning—stating the district court's definition was "imaginative and unconventional"—the lower court's logic appears rooted in *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, 452 F. App'x 453 (5th Cir. 2011), where the Fifth Circuit interpreted "private carrier" broadly enough to include a railroad acting as a conduit for goods. By citing *Huntley*, the district court attempted to bridge the gap between a statute designed for mailed or couriered documents and an internal employee delivering paper files, arguing that if an independent contractor could qualify as a private carrier, perhaps an employee of the sender could too.

However, relying on such analogies carries significant risk for insurers and premium finance companies due to the doctrine of **strict adherence** required by Louisiana law. As noted in *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, cited extensively in *Williams*, strict adherence is mandatory because defects render cancellations ineffective. The primary risk lies in the ambiguity of defining "private carrier" under state common law when applied to internal actors. Unlike traditional carriers (e.g., UPS, FedEx) or even railroads recognized in *Huntley*, an employee of the entity sending the notice creates potential conflicts regarding agency and independence. If a future Louisiana court determines that an internal employee does not constitute a neutral "carrier," any cancellation predicated on that specific mode of delivery could be deemed invalid, exposing the insurer to liability for wrongful termination of coverage despite having acted in good faith.

Fortunately, *Williams* mitigates this specific risk by clarifying that **electronic transmission** alone satisfies the statutory mandate for "sending... with a private carrier" or via email. The Fifth Circuit affirmed that once GoAuto's automated system received the email notification, it had constructive "receipt" sufficient to trigger cancellation rights under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Consequently, insurers should prioritize robust electronic communication channels over physical hand-delivery mechanisms involving internal staff. Relying on an internal employee as a "private carrier" invites litigation based on technicalities regarding who qualifies as a carrier, whereas utilizing authorized electronic methods aligns directly with modern statutory interpretation and avoids the uncertainty of extending common-law definitions to corporate employees.
