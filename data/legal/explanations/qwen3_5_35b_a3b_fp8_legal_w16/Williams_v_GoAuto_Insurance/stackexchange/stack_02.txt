Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### When does a computer system constitute 'receipt' of a cancellation notice under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(b)?
Question:
The plaintiffs argue that 'receipt' implies actual human possession of the document by GoAuto employees before cancellation can legally happen. The court rejects this, accepting Black's Law Dictionary definitions but applying them to a modern digital context where an email triggers an automated API call to cancel the policy instantly. Is this a novel expansion of 'receipt' that ignores the legislative intent behind providing cure periods? Specifically, if the system cancels at 8:00 AM based on an email, but no human sees it until noon, does the insured technically lose their coverage rights before they could possibly react?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
Under *Williams v. GoAuto*, a computer system constitutes "receipt" of a cancellation notice when the insurer's automated systems successfully receive and process the data transmitted by the premium finance company, regardless of whether a human employee has manually reviewed or physically possessed the document at that moment. The court rejected the argument that "receipt" requires actual human possession, reasoning that modern statutes must be interpreted in light of contemporary technological realities where automated APIs function as the insurer's agent for receiving notice.

**Legal Analysis**
The core dispute turns on the interpretation of **La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(b)**, which permits an insurer to cancel a policy "upon receipt of such notice of cancellation... from the premium finance company." Plaintiffs argued that "receipt" necessitates actual human possession, citing *Black's Law Dictionary* definitions emphasizing "taking physical possession."

However, the Fifth Circuit affirmed that while *Black's* definitions regarding physical possession are accurate in isolation, they cannot override the operational reality of modern business practices governed by a "modern statute set in current times." The court reasoned that:

1.  **Automated Systems as Agents:** The court accepted testimony describing how APAC's computer system makes an API call (a "web service") to GoAuto's system. Once GoAuto's system receives this call and executes the cancellation command, the statutory condition of "receipt" is met.
2.  **Rejection of Human-Centric Formalism:** The court explicitly noted that under GoAuto's procedure, no person is involved until hours after the automated cancellation occurs (specifically, when an APAC employee later applies a signature stamp to printed documents). The court held that strict adherence to the statute does not require adding procedural hurdles—such as mandating human review—that are not present in the text of La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(b).
3.  **Legislative Intent vs. Technological Evolution:** While acknowledging that strict adherence serves to fix the time/date of cancellation and provide cure periods (*Britten*), the court concluded that allowing automated systems to trigger cancellations upon successful data transmission satisfies these goals just as effectively as manual delivery. To require human intervention would ignore how insurance transactions currently operate digitally.

**Conclusion on Your Scenario**
To directly answer your hypothetical: Yes, under *Williams*, if GoAuto's system processes an email or API call at 8:00 AM and automatically cancels the policy at that moment, coverage terminates at 8:00 AM—even if no human sees the notification until noon. The court found this compliant with Louisiana law because the insurer took "possession" or "custody" of the request via its automated infrastructure immediately upon successful transmission, satisfying the statutory requirement of "receipt."
