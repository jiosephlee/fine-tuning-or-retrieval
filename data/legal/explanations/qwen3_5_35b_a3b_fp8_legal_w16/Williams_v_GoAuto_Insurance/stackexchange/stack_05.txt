Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How does an implied power of attorney in a premium finance agreement interact with statutory notice requirements?
Question:
The factual background notes that APAC holds a power of attorney granting it authority to cancel policies upon default. The statute allows a finance company to request cancellation by sending specific certifications. If APAC acts as an agent via POA, does their automated communication count as acting within that agency scope even without manual intervention? Furthermore, since the statute lists four specific items that must be certified (valid POA, default status, notice sent to insured, notice sent to lienholders), how critical is it that every single element is explicitly stated in one document versus distributed across multiple systems?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
Under *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, an implied power of attorney in a premium finance agreement permits the finance company to act as an agent for cancellation even when the communication is fully automated, provided the statute's substantive certification requirements are met. The court held that "strict adherence" to the Louisiana statute does not mandate a human signature or manual review at the moment of cancellation; rather, it requires that the insurer receives a notice containing the four specific statutory certifications. These certifications may be generated electronically and distributed across systems (e.g., an email triggering an automatic cancellation) without violating the statute, as long as the final output sent to the insurer contains all required elements and represents a valid "receipt" by the insurer's system.

**Detailed Analysis:**

**1. Agency Scope and Automated Communication**
The core issue regarding agency is whether APAC acted within the scope of its Power of Attorney (POA) when its computer system automatically transmitted a cancellation request without immediate human intervention. The Fifth Circuit affirmed that **yes**, this falls within the authorized scope.

The court rejected the plaintiffs' argument that a human must manually sign or approve the request before cancellation occurs. While Louisiana law requires "strict adherence" to statutory procedures (*Delatte v. Lemotte*), strict adherence applies to the *content* and *delivery* of the notice, not necessarily to manual human processing steps unless explicitly mandated by text. The court reasoned that:
*   The statute designates "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity effecting cancellation by "certifying" information, implying corporate action rather than individual action (*Benitez*).
*   In modern contexts, corporate actions include those performed by automated systems ("company computers do much that individuals once did").
*   Therefore, APAC's automated email, which triggered GoAuto's system to cancel the policy immediately upon receipt, constituted a valid exercise of agency authority under the POA. The subsequent affixing of a signature stamp by an APAC employee was deemed irrelevant to the validity of the initial automated cancellation request.

**2. Distribution of Statutory Certifications**
Regarding your second question on whether all four statutory elements must appear in a single document or can be distributed across systems: The court indicated that **all elements must be present in the communication received by the insurer**, but they need not be manually compiled if they are generated automatically as part of a unified transmission.

The Louisiana statute (La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)) mandates that a statement certifying four specific items accompany the notice:
1.  Validity of POA;
2.  Default status;
3.  Notice sent to insured;
4.  Notice sent to interested parties (lienholders).

In *Williams*, APAC's automated system generated an email containing exactly these four points on APAC letterhead: *"APAC certifies that... (1)... (2)... (3)... (4)..."*. Although GoAuto's computer system processed this data instantly without human eyes reading it first, and although proof of mailing for item #3 was noted as "available upon request" rather than physically attached in every instance described in this specific snippet (though enclosed copies were mentioned), the Fifth Circuit focused on whether GoAuto received a notice containing these certifications.

The court emphasized that because GoAuto’s system accepted this electronic transmission as satisfying "receipt," and because that transmission contained all four requisite certifications, strict adherence was met. The decision implies that splitting these certifications across disparate documents or failing to ensure they are collectively present in one transmission would likely violate strict adherence, but **automated generation and distribution within a single functional transaction is permissible**.

**Conclusion**
*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* establishes that statutory compliance for premium finance cancellations turns on whether the insurer receives a notice containing all four mandatory certifications from an authorized agent (via POA). Whether that notice is drafted by a lawyer or generated by code is immaterial so long as:
1.  The sender acts under valid agency authority;
2.  The content includes all four statutory certifications; and
3.  The insurer takes possession ("receipt") of said notice via its own systems before cancelling coverage.
