Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does 'strict adherence' to Louisiana's insurance cancellation statute require a human signature on the certification notice?
Question:
The court emphasizes that Louisiana law requires 'strict adherence' to statutory cancellation procedures, noting that defects render a cancellation ineffective. However, the specific dispute turns on whether the word 'certifying' implicitly demands a handwritten or stamped signature from an identifiable employee *before* the automated cancellation occurs. The court concludes it doesn't, citing the statute's focus on the entity ('premium finance company') rather than an individual. How does this ruling reconcile with cases like *Benitez* which criticized unsigned notices? Does 'strict adherence' ever allow for corporate automation to substitute for personal authentication in this context?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit reconciles the "strict adherence" doctrine with corporate automation by distinguishing between **substantive compliance** (ensuring the statutory conditions are met) and **formalistic rituals** (requiring specific signatures or manual steps not explicitly mandated by the text).

The court holds that "strict adherence" does not require a human signature or pre-cancellation authentication because the Louisiana statute focuses on the **entity's certification**, not an individual's personal attestation. The relevant provision, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), mandates that the "insurance premium finance company" send a notice containing a statement "certifying" four specific facts regarding the power of attorney, default status, and prior notifications. The court reasoned that since the statute imposes the duty on the company rather than a specific employee, and since no Louisiana precedent explicitly requires a signature for this purpose, adding such a requirement would exceed the bounds of statutory interpretation.

Regarding your concern about *Benitez v. Elsayed*, which criticized unsigned notices for failing to identify an employee or purport to "certify," *Williams* distinguishes those cases based on content rather than form. In *Benitez*, the notice was defective because it failed to actually "certify" anything; it merely listed facts without asserting them as true certifications. By contrast, in *Williams*, the automated email explicitly stated: *"APAC certifies that..."* followed by the required statutory declarations. The Fifth Circuit concluded that as long as the document contains this affirmative certification of fact, the absence of a handwritten signature or a named preparer does not constitute a fatal defect under "strict adherence."

Furthermore, regarding timing, the court rejected the argument that "receipt" requires human possession before cancellation. Acknowledging modern business practices, the court interpreted "receipt" functionally: once GoAuto's computer system accepted and processed APAC's automated email notification (which constituted valid delivery under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)), GoAuto had satisfied its obligation to receive notice. The subsequent application of a signature stamp by an APAC employee occurred post-cancellation but was deemed irrelevant because the statutory trigger—the certified notice—was already delivered electronically at the moment of cancellation.

Therefore, *Williams* clarifies that "strict adherence" ensures all substantive statutory prerequisites are fulfilled (e.g., accurate data transmission and explicit certification language) but does not mandate archaic formalities like manual signatures when those formalities are neither specified in the statute nor essential to achieving its dual purposes: fixing the exact time of cancellation and providing a cure period for defaults.
