Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Under what circumstances will a federal circuit court refuse to certify a question of state law despite significant ambiguity?
Question:
The plaintiffs urged certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court because there is no definitive precedent defining whether signatures or specific definitions of 'receipt' are required under La. R.S. 9:3550(G). The Fifth Circuit declined, citing *Jefferson*, stating that mere lack of definition isn't enough and that overuse of certification threatens its utility. What is the threshold for a 'compelling reason' to certify in Louisiana-specific statutory interpretation cases? Did the court's ability to rely on general dictionary definitions and existing case law paraphrases satisfy this burden sufficiently?
Answer:
Based on the Fifth Circuit's decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, a federal circuit court will refuse to certify a question of state law—even in the face of significant ambiguity—when the ambiguity can be resolved using **general principles of statutory construction**, **reliable secondary sources** (such as Black's Law Dictionary), and **existing case law paraphrases**, rather than leaving the issue entirely open-ended.

In *Williams*, the Fifth Circuit established that the threshold for a "compelling reason" to certify is high and is not met merely because a state supreme court has never explicitly defined a specific term (e.g., "signature" or "receipt") within a statute. The court reiterated the principle from *Jefferson v. Lead Industries Association* that certification is a limited resource that should not be overused. Specifically, the court reasoned that **"mere lack of definition isn't enough"** to trigger certification.

The court determined it could resolve the ambiguity regarding whether a signature was required under La. R.S. 9:3550(G) by relying on two key factors:
1.  **Statutory Text:** The statute imposes the duty on the "insurance premium finance company" to "certify," not on an individual employee. The court concluded that adding a signature requirement would improperly expand statutory obligations beyond what is written ("strict adherence... is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures").
2.  **Secondary Sources and Paraphrasing:** Regarding the undefined term "receipt," the court relied on standard definitions from *Black's Law Dictionary*. Furthermore, it noted that while no case had directly ruled on signatures, several lower appellate courts had previously paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing only "a statement certifying compliance."

Consequently, the Fifth Circuit held that where a federal judge can reasonably interpret ambiguous terms using dictionary definitions and analogies drawn from existing lower-court interpretations, there is no "compelling reason" to pause proceedings for certification. The inability of state appellate courts to provide definitive precedent does not absolve the federal court of its duty to interpret the law based on available tools; certification becomes unnecessary unless those tools fail to yield a determinate answer.
