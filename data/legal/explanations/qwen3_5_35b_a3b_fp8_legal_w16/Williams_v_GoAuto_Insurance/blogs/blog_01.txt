Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Digital Delivery and 'Receipt': How the Fifth Circuit Navigates Automated Insurance Cancellations Under Strict State Statutes

**By [Your Name]**  
*October 4, 2025*

In the high-stakes world of insurance regulation, few doctrines are as unforgiving as the requirement of "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures. In *Williams v. GoAuto*, decided last week, the Fifth Circuit was tasked with determining whether a fully automated, digital cancellation process could satisfy Louisiana’s rigid statutory framework designed to protect consumers from sudden coverage lapses. The court affirmed the district court's dismissal of a class action, ruling that an insurer's computer system accepting an email trigger constitutes valid "receipt" under state law, even before a human ever touches the file. While the outcome preserves operational efficiency for insurers and premium finance companies, the decision highlights a growing judicial willingness to interpret archaic statutory terms in light of modern technological realities—a move that balances consumer protection mandates with the inevitable digitization of commerce.

The factual backdrop involves a standard arrangement where policyholders finance their auto insurance premiums through a third-party lender, APAC. When these borrowers defaulted on payments, APAC exercised powers of attorney embedded in their contracts to cancel coverage with GoAuto Insurance. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto violated Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G), which governs cancellations initiated by premium finance companies. Specifically, they pointed to two procedural failures: first, that the cancellation notice lacked a handwritten signature from a specific employee at the moment of transmission; and second, that GoAuto's computer system canceled policies immediately upon receiving an automated email notification from APAC, long before an APAC employee physically delivered paper copies to a GoAuto filing cabinet or stamped them as "received."

Under Louisiana precedent, courts have historically demanded strict compliance with cancellation statutes. As noted in cases like *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, any defect in the process renders the cancellation ineffective. This strictness serves vital purposes: it fixes the precise moment coverage ends and ensures defaulters have a meaningful window to cure their delinquency before losing protection. However, applying this traditional lens to modern automation created friction for the Fifth Circuit. The plaintiffs contended that "certifying" required a human signature and that "receipt" necessitated physical possession by an insurer representative before any cancellation could occur. They viewed GoAuto's workflow—where an algorithm triggers immediate termination based on an email input—as a circumvention of these safeguards.

Judge Southwick's opinion for the panel navigated this tension by refusing to read extra-statutory requirements into Louisiana law regarding signatures or manual processing. On the issue of signatures, Judge Southwick observed that while some lower Louisiana courts had criticized unsigned notices for failing to identify responsible employees, no binding authority explicitly mandated a wet signature for every certification under § 9:3550(G). The statute speaks of "the insurance premium finance company" certifying information; it does not specify *how* that certification must be manifested beyond providing a written statement containing specific facts. The court reasoned that allowing corporate entities to certify information via authorized electronic systems does not violate the principle of strict adherence so long as the substantive requirements—the existence of power of attorney, proof of default, and notice to interested parties—are met. To demand otherwise would be for courts to add conditions Congress or the state legislature did not impose simply because technology has evolved since those laws were drafted.

The more consequential aspect of *Williams* concerns the definition of "receipt." Here, Judge Southwick embraced a functionalist approach rather than a formalistic one. The statute requires the insurer to have "receipt" of notice before proceeding with cancellation; it does not define what constitutes receipt in an era dominated by API integrations and cloud computing. Relying on Black's Law Dictionary definitions focusing on taking possession or coming into custody from an outside source, Judge Southwick concluded that GoAuto's computer system had indeed taken possession when it accepted APAC's automated signal via web service interface. The fact that human staff members only interacted with physical files hours later was immaterial because the critical act—the insurer acknowledging and acting upon notice—had already occurred electronically.

This reasoning represents a significant shift in how federal courts apply state regulatory strictures to automated business models. By equating machine-to-machine communication with traditional mail delivery or private carrier transport (a theory briefly raised by the district court but ultimately unnecessary here), Judge Southwick validated millions of dollars worth of daily transactions across industries reliant on real-time data exchange. If *Williams* stands alone as merely confirming that emails count as mailings under old statutes, its impact might be limited; however, its affirmation that *immediate* machine-triggered action counts as valid performance after receipt suggests that regulators cannot rely on lag times between digital submission and physical filing to challenge cancellations retroactively unless they can prove fraud or bad faith independent of timing mechanics.

Doctrinally, this decision underscores a reluctance among appellate courts to certify questions back to state supreme courts when existing interpretations can reasonably accommodate new technologies without violating legislative intent. Although counsel urged certification due to ambiguity surrounding "signature" and "receipt," Judge Southwick declined, citing judicial economy and noting that undefined statutory terms do not automatically mandate referral if reasonable construction is possible within current precedent (*Jefferson v Lead Indus.*).
