Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## The Tripartite Relationship: Insureds, APAC (Finance Company), and GoAuto (Insurer)

To understand *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, one must first grasp the unique financial arrangement that created the dispute. The case involves three distinct entities interacting in a chain of dependency: the insured consumers, the premium finance company, and the insurance carrier. In this instance, plaintiffs Kimberly Williams, Felita Wright, and Nicholas Jenkins were insureds under policies issued by GoAuto Insurance Company ("GoAuto"). However, these insureds did not pay their premiums directly to GoAuto. Instead, they entered into financing agreements with a third-party entity known as American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"). Under this structure, APAC paid GoAuto the full premium amount upfront. In exchange, the insureds made monthly installment payments to APAC rather than to the insurer. This tripartite relationship is critical because it separates the entity holding the insurance risk (GoAuto) from the entity managing the cash flow and collection efforts regarding those premiums (APAC). The litigation arose specifically because of tensions within this structure when payments ceased, triggering a dispute over who had the authority to terminate coverage and whether that termination was legally effective.

## The Power of Attorney Mechanism: How APAC is Authorized to Cancel Policies

Central to the factual background is the legal instrument that empowered APAC to act on behalf of both GoAuto and the insureds regarding policy cancellation: a power of attorney. Each financing agreement executed by Williams, Wright, and Jenkins explicitly granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel their respective insurance policies in the event of non-payment. This delegation of authority meant that APAC did not merely suggest cancellation; it possessed the contractual right to effectuate it directly. When an insured failed to meet their financial obligations under the financing plan, APAC could exercise this power without needing further permission from either GoAuto or the individual insureds at that moment. Consequently, when non-payment occurred in these cases, APAC initiated cancellation proceedings based on this pre-existing authorization. The legal efficacy of these cancellations hinged entirely on whether APAC exercised this power in strict accordance with Louisiana statutes governing such transactions.

## The Trigger Event: Non-Payment Leading to Automated Cancellation Procedures

The specific trigger for this litigation was a failure by the insureds to make timely installment payments to APAC. Once a default occurred and remained uncured—specifically after a ten-day grace period passed following written notice—the automated machinery of premium finance began its operation. Under GoAuto's procedures, which were adopted for efficiency in handling high volumes of accounts receivable, an unpaid balance triggered an immediate sequence of events mediated by computer systems rather than manual human intervention. First, APAC sent a notice to the insured warning that cancellation would occur if payment was not received within ten days. If no payment arrived by midnight on that tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically generated an email containing a copy of the notice of cancellation and a formal request for cancellation directed at GoAuto's web services. This digital transmission served as the operative step initiating termination. Only after this automated process completed—and only after policy cancellation had technically occurred—did human employees become involved later in the morning to affix signature stamps or file physical copies of documents into shared cabinets. This timing discrepancy between automated execution and manual documentation formed a key factual contention in the subsequent appeal.

## Litigation History: State Court Filing, Removal to Federal Court, and Grounds for Dismissal

Following these cancellations, Williams and Jenkins filed a class-action lawsuit against both GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court. Their claims alleged that GoAuto had failed to effectively cancel their policies according to state law and had acted in bad faith by allowing coverage lapses while continuing billing practices inconsistent with actual status. Wright subsequently joined as an additional plaintiff via an amended complaint. Recognizing diversity jurisdiction due to parties residing in different states involving more than $75 million in controversy at stake (implied by class action context), GoAuto removed the case from state court to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Once in federal court, both sides moved for summary judgment on narrow grounds focused exclusively on whether GoAuto’s procedural methods satisfied Louisiana law regarding policy cancellations by premium finance companies. The district court analyzed these motions de novo but ultimately concluded that GoAuto’s procedures were compliant with statutory mandates regardless of how they interacted with automated systems or signature timing. Based on this determination that no statutory violation existed as a matter of law, the district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto and dismissed all claims against them with prejudice against all remaining defendants implicitly included in that ruling scope relative to liability findings on cancellation validity.

# Chapter 2: Governing Law and Statutory Requirements for Cancellation

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit undertook a rigorous analysis of Louisiana's statutory framework governing the cancellation of auto insurance policies financed through third-party premium finance companies. The core of the dispute hinged on whether GoAuto Insurance Company, acting on behalf of its financing partner American Premium Assistance Company ("APAC"), strictly adhered to the specific mandates imposed by La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3). This chapter examines the statutory provisions at issue, the four mandatory elements required for a valid cancellation request, and the judicially created doctrine of "strict adherence" that dictates the consequences of even minor procedural deviations. Understanding these mechanics is essential to grasping why the appellate court affirmed the dismissal of the plaintiffs' claims despite their assertions regarding automated systems and missing signatures.

## Statutory Framework: La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)

The litigation turned entirely on the text and application of La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3), specifically subsection (G)(3)(a). Under Louisiana law, a premium finance company does not possess an inherent right to cancel an insured's policy; rather, they exercise this power only upon an uncured default by sending a specific type of notice to the insurer. The statute outlines a precise mechanism for this communication, allowing delivery via mail, private carrier, or electronic mail. However, mere notification is insufficient; accompanying this transmission must be a written statement that "certifies" four distinct factual predicates regarding the status of the insurance relationship and the rights of various stakeholders.

The Fifth Circuit emphasized that this statutory language is not merely directory but prescriptive. The statute explicitly lists four conditions that must be certified in writing by the premium finance company before an insurer may proceed with cancellation. These conditions serve as gatekeepers designed to protect consumers from premature or unauthorized termination of coverage while ensuring that insurers have a reliable basis for ending their liability exposure. In *Williams*, the court noted that while modern technology allows for automated email transmissions between corporate entities, these digital interactions must still satisfy every textual requirement laid out in Section 9:3550(G)(3). The statute acts as a rigid checklist; failure to meet any single item creates a legal vulnerability for the cancellation attempt, regardless of whether the underlying debt was actually unpaid or whether notice was eventually sent to interested parties through alternative means.

## Mandatory Elements of a Valid Cancellation Request

To effectuate a lawful cancellation under La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3), the premium finance company's certification must affirmatively establish four specific elements simultaneously. First, it must certify that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney granting them authority to cancel upon default. Second, it must confirm that the agreement is currently in default and that this default has not been timely cured by the insured within any grace period provided by law or contract. Third, and crucially for due process concerns, it must verify that a notice of cancellation was previously sent directly to the insured in accordance with state regulations, specifying exactly when that notice was dispatched to allow for cure attempts. Finally, fourthly, it must certify that copies of this notice were sent to all other persons who have an insurable interest in the vehicle or property covered by the policy—such as lienholders or government agencies—and list those recipients by name and address.

In *Williams*, GoAuto's computer-generated emails attempted to satisfy these requirements by reciting each element verbatim within an automated message sent from APAC's system to GoAuto's web services platform immediately after a ten-day cure period expired without payment. The plaintiffs argued that because these emails lacked human intervention at the moment of generation—specifically lacking a handwritten signature—they failed to constitute valid certifications under subsection (a). However, reviewing the text of Section 9:3550(G)(3), neither it nor prevailing Louisiana case law explicitly mandates a physical signature or personal identification for every certification made by a corporation acting through its authorized agents or automated systems authorized under its power of attorney agreements. The court observed that while one lower court decision (*Benitez*) criticized unsigned notices for failing to identify preparing employees or purporting to certify statements based on actual knowledge, other precedents accepted cancellations based on receipts from finance companies without demanding individual signatories so long as the entity itself stood behind the representation in writing via letterhead or official electronic transmission. Thus, GoAuto's system generated messages containing all four mandatory substantive declarations required by statute: confirmation of power of attorney existence, declaration of uncured default, verification of prior notice to insureds with dates included (or proof thereof available), and attestation regarding notifications to interested parties including lienholders and government bodies where applicable.
