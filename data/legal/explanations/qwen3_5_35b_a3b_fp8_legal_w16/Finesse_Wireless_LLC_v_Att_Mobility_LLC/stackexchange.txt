Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How can an expert's 'confusing' or 'self-contradictory' testimony ever constitute substantial evidence to survive a JMOL motion?
Question:
In the '134 patent section, the court reverses the jury's infringement verdict because Dr. Wells gave inconsistent testimony regarding whether the 'modeled PIM path' or the variables 'x1/x2' constituted the 'interference generating signals.' He initially mapped them one way, then pivoted without clarifying, leading the court to say his testimony wasn't clear enough. My confusion is about the evidentiary standard: If I understand correctly, at JMOL we view evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict. How does a judge decide that an expert's ambiguity means *no* reasonable jury could find infringement, rather than just weighing conflicting expert opinions? Is there a threshold where contradiction equals total lack of evidence?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
An expert's "confusing" or "self-contradictory" testimony fails to constitute substantial evidence when the contradictions go to the heart of the element the expert is trying to prove, leaving the jury with no rational basis to accept one version of events over another. In *Finesse Wireless*, the Federal Circuit held that when a party bears the burden of proof and relies solely on an expert who explicitly maps a technical component to a claim term, then immediately pivots to a different theory without clarifying that pivot, and finally refuses to definitively link his new theory back to the claim terms, the resulting testimony is too ambiguous for a reasonable jury to resolve. The court does not simply weigh conflicting opinions; it determines that **no reasonable jury could fill in the gaps** created by the expert's own uncertainty.

**Detailed Analysis:**

To survive a Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL), evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict. However, this standard has a floor: there must be *some* evidence upon which a reasonable jury could rely. As stated in *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* (cited by the court), if an expert offers contradictory testimony on a critical factual finding, and no other evidence supports that finding, "no reasonable jury could have found" in favor of the prevailing party based on that testimony alone.

In *Finesse Wireless*, this principle applied specifically to Dr. Wells' testimony regarding the '134 patent's requirement that the radio sample "interference generating signals."

1.  **The Initial Mapping:** Dr. Wells initially testified clearly that he mapped the "modeled PIM path" (generated downstream of the sampler) to the claim term "interference generating signals."
2.  **The Logical Flaw:** Cross-examination exposed that this mapping was impossible because physical signals cannot be sampled before they exist (i.e., you cannot sample something generated after sampling occurs).
3.  **The Pivot Without Clarification:** Instead of correcting his initial error or explaining why his initial mapping was wrong despite its logical impossibility, Dr. Wells pivoted, stating there were "two signals... x1 and x2."
4.  **The Fatal Ambiguity:** Crucially, Dr. Wells never actually testified that **x1** corresponded to "signals of interest" and **x2** corresponded to "interference generating signals." He merely said x1 and x2 were inputs represented by signals on a diagram labeled differently elsewhere in his testimony ("downlink TX reference and modeled PIM path").

The court reasoned that requiring a jury to piece together these disparate statements across ninety pages of transcript—to infer what Dr. Wells likely meant—is not permissible under the substantial evidence standard when the expert himself refused to clarify his position during direct examination or immediate cross-examination. The contradiction was not merely between two experts (which courts often allow juries to weigh); it was within a single witness's account where one half invalidated itself physically, and the other half lacked any explicit connection to the claim language required by law.

As Chief Judge Moore wrote: *"When the party with the burden of proof... rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony... we may conclude the evidence is insufficient."* The threshold where contradiction equals a lack of evidence is reached when the contradictions render it impossible for a reasonable juror to determine which version of facts constitutes proof of infringement without engaging in speculation or guesswork unsupported by clear testimony. Here, because Dr. Wells never definitively linked x1/x2 to the specific claim limitations, his testimony provided no reliable foundation for infringement, making reversal appropriate regardless of how much weight one might otherwise give an expert's opinion.

### Does amending a patent claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' always constitute a disclaimer of scope for devices using only two unique inputs?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, Finesse amended their claims during prosecution to specify 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' instead of 'two or three,' apparently to distinguish from prior art that used two inputs. However, the Federal Circuit held this didn't amount to a 'clear and unambiguous disavowal' of scope for products using only two unique input signals (like x1 and x2), provided they were 'separately identifiable.' Why isn't adding a specific number ('three') usually interpreted as excluding anything less? What distinguishes this case from typical prosecution history estoppel scenarios where narrowing amendments restrict claim scope?
Answer:
**Answer:**

No, amending a claim from "two or three" to "three" does not *always* constitute a disclaimer of scope for devices using fewer than three unique inputs. As the Federal Circuit clarified in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the determination depends entirely on whether the prosecution history reflects a **"clear and unambiguous"** surrender of subject matter.

In *Finesse*, the patentee amended the claim to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3" specifically to distinguish from prior art (*Filipovic*) that utilized only two inputs. While this narrowing amendment triggered an inquiry into prosecution history estoppel, the Court held that the amendment did not disclaim devices using only two unique signals because the context of the amendment revealed a different limiting purpose. The record showed that Finesse distinguished its invention primarily on the basis that *Filipovic* addressed **active** intermodulation products, whereas Finesse's invention targeted **passive** intermodulation products (PIM). Consequently, the addition of "three signals" was interpreted as requiring **three separately identifiable signals**, rather than three *unique* input signals.

Under Federal Circuit precedent (specifically citing *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*), a disclaimer must be explicit and unambiguous to narrow claim scope via prosecution history estoppel. Here, the Court reasoned that requiring three "separately identifiable" signals allows for a scenario where two unique physical inputs ($x_1$ and $x_2$) can satisfy the limitation if one of them is treated as a separate entity (e.g., via copying or processing) to create three distinct mathematical entities ($S_1, S_2, S_3$). Because Finesse did not explicitly state during prosecution that it was abandoning all embodiments involving fewer than three unique physical inputs—and because the primary distinction drawn against the prior art was functional (active vs. passive PIM)—the Court refused to read a broad exclusionary limitation into the claim language.

Therefore, unlike typical scenarios where a narrowing amendment creates an irrebuttable presumption of surrender regarding literal equivalents or broader ranges, this case illustrates that courts will look beyond the literal text of an amendment to determine if the patentee actually intended to disavow specific configurations based on the rationale provided to secure allowance. If the amendment serves to distinguish based on function or type rather than quantity alone, it may not preclude infringement by devices utilizing fewer unique inputs but satisfying the structural requirement of "identifiability."

### Can an appellate court consider new mappings or theories of infringement raised for the first time on appeal when overturning a jury verdict?
Question:
On appeal regarding the '775 patent, Finesse tried to explain how their expert's identification of three distinct multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications by citing new arguments and interpretations of Nokia documents that weren't fully developed at trial. The court explicitly stated that Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping on appeal but noted that this evidence was never presented to the jury. Since JMOL is reviewed based on whether *substantial evidence* existed at trial, why does it matter if Finesse offers a better theoretical explanation later? Does raising new theories on appeal automatically invalidate a jury verdict even if some thread of logic existed at trial?
Answer:
**No, an appellate court cannot consider new mappings or theories of infringement raised for the first time on appeal to sustain a jury verdict.**

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) precisely because Finesse attempted to cure evidentiary deficiencies with new arguments and interpretations that were not presented to the jury. While JMOL is indeed reviewed based on whether "substantial evidence" existed at trial, that standard applies strictly to the **record developed during litigation**, not to post-verdict theorizing.

Here is how the opinion clarifies this principle:

### 1. The Record Must Support the Verdict at Trial
The court emphasized that a jury verdict stands only if a reasonable jury could have reached that conclusion based on the evidence presented *at trial*. When Finesse's expert, Dr. Wells, testified at trial regarding the '775 patent, he identified only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia technical documents and admitted he could not explain how those three mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications required by the patent claims.

At JMOL and on appeal, Finesse attempted to bridge this gap by offering new explanations: arguing that x1 and x2 map onto specific signals (like complex conjugates or moduli) and suggesting subsets of ten total multiplications accounted for the seven claimed ones. The Federal Circuit explicitly rejected this approach, stating: **"Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury."**

Because these clarifications were absent from the trial record, no reasonable jury could have found infringement based solely on Dr. Wells' original testimony. The appellate court cannot act as a fact-finder to supply missing links in the chain of logic after the fact.

### 2. New Arguments Do Not Cure Defective Evidence
You asked whether raising new theories invalidates a verdict even if "some thread of logic" existed at trial. The opinion suggests that if that "thread" relies on a logical leap requiring facts or mappings not introduced to the trier of fact, it fails the substantial evidence test.

Regarding both patents in *Finesse*, the court noted that:
*   **'134 Patent:** Dr. Wells gave self-contradictory testimony regarding signal paths. He refused to clarify his position despite cross-examination, leaving it to Finesse's attorneys years later to piece together fragments of testimony across 90 pages of transcript to construct an argument that satisfied them but was never clearly articulated to the jury. The court held this "confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict."
*   **'775 Patent:** The expert failed to map three observed multiplications to seven claimed ones at trial. On appeal, Finesse proposed complex mathematical mappings involving complex conjugates ($x_2'$) and moduli ($|x_2|$). However, because Dr. Wells never testified about these specific relationships during his direct examination or cross-examination, these new theories constitute **new evidence**. An appellate court reviewing JMOL cannot import new evidence into the record; it must evaluate what was actually before the jury.

### Conclusion
Raising new theories on appeal does **not** automatically validate a verdict simply because a plausible theory exists in hindsight. To survive JMOL review, every element necessary for infringement must be supported by substantial evidence presented *during trial*. If an expert's testimony leaves gaps—such as failing to map specific technical steps—and those gaps are filled only by arguments advanced for the first time on appeal (or at JMOL without having been tested before a jury), those arguments cannot serve as substantial evidence supporting a guilty/infringed finding. Consequently, *Finesse* reversed because Finesse rested its case on an expert's unclear testimony and failed to present a coherent mapping theory until after losing at trial and moving for JMOL denial was already challenged on appeal.

### What is the precise relationship between reviewing JMOL denials under regional circuit law versus federal patent law standards?
Question:
The opinion states that for non-infringement JMOL denials, they review under Fifth Circuit law (de novo), looking for evidence pointing so strongly one way that no reasonable juror could disagree. Yet they also mention reviewing factual findings for infringement for 'substantial evidence.' Are these actually different standards in practice? How does the Federal Circuit reconcile its own precedent (which often emphasizes claim construction de novo) with the requirement to defer heavily to state/circuit jury verdicts when applying local civil procedure rules like Rule 50?
Answer:
In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit clarifies that while the **standard of review** for denying a Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) is governed by regional circuit law, the **application** of that standard remains strictly bound by federal patent substantive requirements. The apparent tension between "regional circuit law" and "federal patent law" is resolved by understanding that the regional standard dictates the *degree* of deference, but federal law defines what constitutes *insufficient* evidence in a patent context.

Under the Fifth Circuit's procedural rule, which applies here via 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), a court reverses a denial of JMOL only if the evidence points "so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that... reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." However, the Federal Circuit explicitly notes that this regional standard operates in tandem with the **"substantial evidence"** test applied to jury findings. As stated in *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*, a finding is supported by substantial evidence if "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party."

The distinction lies in how these standards function procedurally versus substantively:
1.  **Procedural Deference:** The Fifth Circuit standard prevents appellate courts from reweighing credibility or substituting their judgment for the jury's simply because they might have decided differently. It sets a very high bar for reversal, requiring evidence to be so weak that no reasonable juror could accept it.
2.  **Substantive Application:** Despite this deference, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court because Finesse failed to present *any* substantial evidence satisfying specific claim limitations. In both cases discussed (*'134* and *'775*), the expert testimony was either self-contradictory or entirely absent regarding critical elements (e.g., mapping specific signals to claim terms or identifying seven distinct multiplications).

Chief Judge Moore's opinion demonstrates that "substantial evidence" does not mean "some evidence," nor does it allow a verdict to stand on speculation or post-hoc rationalization. In the *'134* case, despite Dr. Wells' confused testimony shifting between mappings, the Federal Circuit held that no reasonable jury could rely on such contradictory statements to meet the burden of proof for infringement. Similarly, for the *'775* patent, because Dr. Wells failed to map three distinct multiplications to seven claimed ones, and no other evidence existed, the verdict lacked substantial support regardless of how deferential the review must be under Fifth Circuit law.

Therefore, there is no conflict: The Federal Circuit uses Fifth Circuit law to determine *if* a jury could reasonably reach a verdict (the threshold), but applies federal patent principles to ensure that verdict rests on actual proof of every claim element rather than ambiguity or contradiction. If an expert's testimony fails to bridge a gap in claim construction due to internal inconsistency or lack of data, it fails the "substantial evidence" test automatically, rendering it impossible for any reasonable juror to find infringement under any interpretation of regional procedural rules.

### If infringement is reversed via JMOL due to lack of substantial evidence, does that automatically mandate vacatur of damages regardless of how strong the damages theory was?
Question:
The court reverses both patents on non-infringement grounds and consequently vacates the entire $166M damages award. I want to confirm my understanding: Even though Appellants also appealed specifically on damages/new trial grounds (arguing perhaps there was no valid royalty base), did they need to win those separate arguments? Or does establishing non-infringement via JMOL act as a complete bar to recovery immediately upon reversal? Is it procedurally possible for a court to affirm non-infringement but remand solely on damages if there was some other theory of liability not addressed by JMOL?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Once the Federal Circuit reverses the denial of JMOL of non-infringement because the jury's verdict lacks substantial evidence, the damages award must be vacated automatically. You do not need to win a separate appeal on damages, nor can the court affirm non-infringement while remanding for damages on an "other" theory if that theory was not tried or supported by evidence.

Here is the breakdown based strictly on *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 WL 2024-1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025):

### 1. The "Complete Bar" Effect of Non-Infringement Reversal
In patent law, liability for damages is predicated entirely on a finding of infringement. If a finding of infringement is reversed via JMOL due to a lack of substantial evidence, there is no legal basis left to sustain any monetary award.

As stated explicitly in **Section II (Damages)** of the opinion:
> "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims... we vacate the damages award."

The court did not engage in a separate analysis of whether the damages theory itself was sound (e.g., whether the royalty base was proper). The logic is binary: if there is no infringement, there are no damages. Therefore, even though Appellants raised separate arguments regarding a new trial on damages (arguing perhaps about the validity of the royalty base), those arguments became moot once the court determined that *no reasonable jury* could have found infringement in the first place (*Johns Hopkins Univ.* standard applied to Section I).

### 2. Why Separate Damages Arguments Were Unnecessary
You asked if Appellants needed to win their separate appeals on damages/new trial grounds. Based on this opinion, **they did not**.

The procedural posture shows that Appellants appealed both:
1.  Denial of JMOL on non-infringement; and
2.  Denial of a new trial on damages.

However, once Section I concludes that the infringement verdicts were unsupported by substantial evidence ("no reasonable jury could have found"), Section II treats the damages question as resolved by necessity. The court vacated the entire $166M award without reaching into the merits of Appellants' specific arguments regarding royalty bases or new trials on damages. The reversal on infringement acts as a "complete bar" to recovery; any discussion of what *would* happen if infringement were proven becomes irrelevant speculation when infringement is legally impossible under current standards.

### 3. Can a Court Affirm Non-Infringement but Remand Solely on Damages?
You asked if it is procedurally possible to affirm non-infringement but remand for damages based on an "unaddressed theory." The answer under this opinion is **no**, particularly when the reversal stems from a lack of substantial evidence at trial regarding *all* theories presented to the jury.

The Federal Circuit emphasized that Finesse's burden was to prove infringement based on specific elements (sampling specific signals and performing specific multiplications) using evidence actually presented at trial (Dr. Wells' testimony and Nokia documents). Because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence linking those elements to the accused devices:
*   There was no valid jury finding upon which to rest a remand for re-calculating damages under a different theory unless that new theory had been properly litigated and submitted to the jury originally (which it wasn't).
*   The court noted Finesse attempted to clarify mappings *on appeal* ("Finesse attempts to clarify... but that evidence was never presented to the jury").

Consequently, you cannot have a scenario where:
1.  The appellate court finds no substantial evidence for Infringement Theory A (tried at trial).
2.  Then remands for a new trial solely on Damages based on Infringement Theory B (never tried/never supported by evidence).

If Infringence Theory A fails due to evidentiary insufficiency, and Theory B was never tried or submitted as an alternative ground for liability with sufficient evidence, there is simply no foundation for an award, let alone a remand for calculation purposes absent further litigation history showing Theory B was viable at trial.

### Conclusion
Under *Finesse Wireless*, establishing non-infringement via JMOL due to lack of substantial evidence mandates immediate vacatur of all associated damages awards ($166M in this case). The appellee does not need to prevail on independent damage arguments because those arguments presuppose liability that has now been erased by law and fact findings regarding sufficiency of proof at trial.
