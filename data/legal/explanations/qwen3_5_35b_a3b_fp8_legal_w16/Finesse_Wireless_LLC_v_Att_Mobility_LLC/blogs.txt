# When Expert Testimony Collapses: The Fatal Flaw in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*

In the high-stakes theater of patent litigation, the difference between a hundred-million-dollar verdict and a complete loss often hinges on the clarity of an expert witness's testimony. That distinction became starkly evident last week in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, where the Federal Circuit reversed a jury's infringement verdict and vacated a $166 million damages award. While the case involved two patents concerning passive intermodulation (PIM) cancellation technology, the core lesson lies in how Chief Judge Moore and her colleagues applied the "substantial evidence" standard to an expert who appeared to contradict himself on fundamental technical mappings. The decision serves as a potent reminder that in patent cases, particularly those involving complex digital signal processing, an expert's inability to draw a straight line from claim language to accused hardware is fatal to the plaintiff's case.

The dispute centered on Finesse's assertion that AT&T and Nokia infringed patents related to methods for mitigating intermodulation products—unwanted frequencies created when strong signals mix within radio equipment. To prove infringement of the '134 patent, Finesse relied heavily on Dr. Jonathan Wells, whose testimony hinged on interpreting a specific diagram from Nokia's technical documentation. The claim required the accused device to sample both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells initially mapped these concepts directly onto the diagram's "downlink transmit (DL-TX) reference" and "modeled PIM path," respectively. However, under pressure from cross-examination regarding the physical reality of signal generation versus sampling, his testimony unraveled. He admitted that if his initial mapping were correct, infringement was impossible because the "modeled PIM path"—which generates interference downstream—could not exist at the moment of sampling upstream.

Herein lay the fatal flaw identified by the Federal Circuit: Dr. Wells attempted to pivot without ever clarifying his position for the jury or providing a coherent alternative theory that satisfied the claim limitations. When pressed further, he muddied the waters by suggesting there were two signals on a red path ("x1" and "x2") but refused to explicitly map x1 or x2 back to "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals." Instead, he continued to insist that his original labels were correct despite acknowledging their logical impossibility regarding timing. The district court had allowed this confusion to stand, seemingly hoping a jury could piece together Dr. Wells' fragmented statements into a viable theory of infringement. The Federal Circuit had none of it. Citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, Chief Judge Moore noted that when a party bearing the burden of proof relies on self-contradictory testimony that forces a jury to speculate rather than apply logic, there is no substantial evidence to support a verdict. The court emphasized that piecing together disparate fragments of testimony separated by dozens of pages does not constitute clear proof; it constitutes confusion that cannot sustain a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL).

The analysis deepened when looking at the '775 patent, though here the collapse was driven less by internal contradiction and more by evidentiary insufficiency regarding specific mathematical steps. The claim required seven distinct third-order multiplications of three signals ($S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$). Despite Finesse arguing during prosecution that it narrowed its scope specifically to exclude two-signal solutions, they managed to survive arguments regarding surrender of scope under estoppel principles—a nuanced victory for them legally speaking—but they lost ground on what actually happened inside Nokia's chip. Dr. Wells pointed to three distinct multiplication types listed in Nokia documents but offered no evidence showing how those three covered all seven required combinations mandated by the claim text ("$S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$", "$S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$", etc.). On appeal, Finesse tried retroactively mapping ten total multiplications found in other parts of Nokia's docs to fit their theory, but this evidence was never presented to or understood by the jury at trial. Without a direct link established during trial between the accused device's actual operations and every single step claimed by Finesse, no reasonable jury could find infringement as a matter of fact.

This ruling carries significant doctrinal weight for future patent disputes involving software-implemented inventions or complex engineering systems where experts rely heavily on technical schematics and flowcharts rather than physical inspection alone (*i.e.*, where you can't just open up a phone and see if it works). First, it reinforces that courts will not tolerate "jigsaw puzzle" litigation strategies where plaintiffs ask juries to assemble scattered testimonial shards into a cohesive theory of infringement that fails under basic scrutiny if laid bare in one place. Second, it underscores that appellate courts will rigorously examine whether an expert has clearly connected every element of every independent claim limitation to an element in the accused device before allowing a case to go forward against JMOL standards set by regional circuits like the Fifth Circuit here. If an expert changes their story mid-stream without offering clear explanations for why previous statements were wrong or how new ones align with physical constraints (like causality in signal processing), they risk destroying their own credibility entirely before reaching closing arguments—and certainly before reaching an appellate court reviewing substantial evidence sufficiency.

The downstream effect of *Finesse Wireless* will likely be stricter gatekeeping at summary judgment and early stages of trial preparation for plaintiffs asserting similar digital signal processing patents against major telecommunications providers who possess robust internal documentation defenses alike (such as Nokia). Plaintiffs can no longer assume that vague assertions backed by plausible-sounding diagrams will suffice; they must ensure their experts can articulate exactly how each numbered step maps onto existing hardware architecture without requiring temporal magic or speculative leaps across conflicting definitions provided within their own reports or transcripts themselves included earlier discussions about claim construction history too often overlooked unless challenged properly pre-verdict proceedings now seem critical again post-*Finesse*. Ultimately, this case stands as another chapter confirming what seasoned practitioners already knew: In patent law, precision isn't just preferred—it is mandatory when lives depend upon accurate interpretation? # The Double Bind of Digital Multiplication: How *Finesse* Untangles Prosecution History from Evidence Sufficiency

The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided September 24, 2025, serves as a stark reminder that winning a jury verdict on infringement is merely the first hurdle; surviving a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) often hinges on the precision of expert testimony and the strict boundaries set by claim construction. While the case involved two patents concerning passive intermodulation (PIM) cancellation in mobile radios, the most legally significant portion of Chief Judge Moore's opinion lies in its nuanced handling of the '775 patent. Here, the court navigates a difficult intersection between prosecution history estoppel and the evidentiary requirements for proving literal infringement. The result is a reversal that rejects a broad surrender argument while simultaneously dismantling the patentee's theory of infringement based on a failure to map specific mathematical operations—a double-edged sword that highlights the fragility of relying on "substantial evidence" when an expert's testimony fails to bridge the gap between claim limitations and accused technology.

The core dispute regarding the '775 patent centered on a specific claim limitation requiring the generation of Intermodulation Product (IMP) cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals ($S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$) in seven specific third-order combinations. AT&T argued that Finesse had surrendered claim scope covering devices using only two unique input signals during prosecution. Specifically, AT&T pointed to an amendment where Finesse changed its claims from "two or three signals" to specifically "three signals" to distinguish its invention from prior art (*Filipovic*). Under traditional principles of prosecution history estoppel, such amendments often trigger a presumption that the applicant has surrendered everything between the original and amended language. However, Chief Judge Moore firmly rejected this characterization. She reasoned that Finesse's amendment was driven by a distinction between *active* IMPs (addressed by *Filipovic*) and *passive* IMPs (which Finesse covered), rather than a clear disavowal of devices utilizing two unique inputs. The court clarified that requiring "three separately identifiable signals" does not necessarily mandate three *unique* input sources; one signal could theoretically be reused or copied to satisfy the count without triggering estoppel against two-input architectures. This holding preserves a degree of flexibility in claim construction but sets a high bar for what constitutes "clear and unambiguous" disavowal necessary to invoke estoppel fully.

Despite saving Finesse from total forfeiture via prosecution history arguments, the court delivered a fatal blow to the infringement theory itself on evidentiary grounds. The '775 patent explicitly requires seven distinct multiplications involving permutations of three signals ($S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, etc.). At trial, Finesse's expert, Dr. Wells, relied on Nokia documentation showing only three distinct multiplications performed by AT&T/Nokia radios: $x_1|x_1||x_1|$, $x_1 x_1 x_2'$, and $x_1 |x_2||x_2|$. Crucially, Dr. Wells offered no testimony mapping these three observed operations onto the seven claimed operations, nor did he explain how repeating certain calculations could account for all seven steps required by the claim language. The Federal Circuit noted that while Finesse later attempted to argue during oral argument that Nokia actually performed ten multiplications (not seven), this theory was never presented to or tested by the jury. Without evidence establishing that every single one of the seven claimed multiplications occurred—or explaining how fewer distinct operations satisfied the literal requirement—the jury's verdict lacked substantial evidence support.

This dichotomy creates an instructive precedent for future patent litigation involving complex digital signal processing algorithms. The court effectively drew a line between interpreting what words mean (claim construction/prosecution history) versus proving what happened in reality (fact-finding/evidence sufficiency). By refusing to read "three signals" as excluding two-input systems solely based on amendment history, the Federal Circuit prevented parties from easily narrowing scope through procedural maneuvers unless they were explicit about it. Conversely, by vacating damages due to Dr. Wells' failure to map specific algorithmic steps, the court reinforced that experts cannot simply assert infringement exists; they must provide a concrete roadmap connecting every element of a claim to physical or logical features in the accused device. If an expert relies on documents showing fewer operations than claimed without explaining how those operations cumulatively satisfy all limitations, JMOL becomes inevitable regardless of how broadly one might construe similar terms elsewhere in the file wrapper.

The downstream implications for software-heavy telecommunications cases are significant. Litigants can no longer assume that satisfying a numerical limitation like "multiplying three signals" allows for some interpretive leeway regarding unique inputs versus repeated usage if that interpretation contradicts explicit claim language or lacks evidentiary backing at trial. Furthermore, *Finesse* underscores that appellate courts will not allow litigants to retrofit theories after losing at trial; arguments raised for the first time during oral argument regarding additional hidden multiplications cannot cure defects in jury instructions or expert testimony presented months earlier. Ultimately, while Finesse avoided an admission that it surrendered scope over two-signal devices, it lost entirely because it failed to prove those devices actually performed every step required by its carefully drafted claims—a loss earned less by poor drafting than by insufficient proof at trial.



# Finesse Wireless: When Infringement Evidence Collapses, So Does the Billions

In the high-stakes theater of patent litigation, the damage award often becomes the headline long before the question of liability is fully settled. However, *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided by the Federal Circuit on September 24, 2025, serves as a stark reminder of a fundamental procedural reality: if there is no infringement, there is no damages. The court's decision to reverse the denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on non-infringement grounds and immediately vacate a staggering $166 million jury verdict offers a masterclass in judicial economy and doctrinal clarity. By cutting off the damages inquiry entirely upon finding a total absence of substantial evidence for infringement, the Federal Circuit reinforced that speculative theories cannot sustain massive financial liabilities.

The case centered on two patents owned by Finesse Wireless concerning methods for mitigating "passive intermodulation" (PIM) interference in wireless radios—a critical issue for carriers like AT&T and manufacturers like Nokia. Following a jury trial that returned a guilty verdict against AT&T/Nokia and awarded nearly $166 million, the defendants sought JMOL arguing that Finesse had failed to prove essential elements of its claims. The district court denied this motion, allowing the massive verdict to stand. On appeal, Chief Judge Moore's unanimous panel dismantled Finesse's entire case on both patents, concluding that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the evidence presented.

The collapse began with Finesse's reliance on an expert witness whose testimony was fundamentally at odds with itself regarding claim construction. For the '134 patent, which required sampling both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," Finesse's expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, attempted to map these limitations onto a Nokia technical diagram. The problem was structural impossibility: Dr. Wells initially identified a "modeled PIM path" as the interference generating signal. Yet, as cross-examination revealed, this modeled path existed *downstream* of the radio's Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). Since signals cannot be sampled before they exist, the very element Dr. Wells identified as infringing could not physically be sampled by the accused device. When pressed further, Dr. Wells pivoted to suggest that variables labeled "x1" and "x2" were the relevant signals but failed to explicitly link them to his earlier definitions or explain how they satisfied the claim language in a way that avoided his initial contradiction.

Chief Judge Moore did not mince words regarding this evidentiary failure. Citing *Johns Hopkins Univ.*, she noted that when a party with the burden of proof relies on an expert who offers self-contradictory testimony without clarification sufficient to resolve those contradictions for a reasonable juror, substantial evidence is absent. The court found that piecing together disparate fragments of testimony spanning dozens of pages did not constitute a coherent factual basis for infringement. Without evidence that the accused radios actually sampled both types of signals simultaneously—as required by claim 1—the foundation of liability crumbled instantly.

The '775 patent faced an equally fatal evidentiary hurdle regarding claim construction and mathematical precision. This patent claimed a method involving seven specific digital multiplications derived from three signals (S1, S2, S3). While Finesse argued during prosecution that it narrowed its scope to three signals specifically to distinguish prior art teaching two-signal systems—a move intended to secure allowance—it now tried to argue that using only two unique input signals still met its own definition of "three separately identifiable signals." The Federal Circuit correctly rejected this attempt at re-litigating prosecution history estoppel principles via backdoor interpretation; however, even assuming such flexibility existed in theory, it collapsed in practice due to lack of proof on performance metrics.

At trial, Dr. Wells pointed to a Nokia document listing only three distinct multiplication operations but asserted these covered all seven claimed steps required by the patent claims. He offered no mapping explaining how three physical operations could equalize seven distinct mathematical requirements defined in the specification. Furthermore, he provided no testimony on how complex conjugates or modulus operations shown in the documentation related to the specific linear combinations mandated by claim 1(d). Attempting to fix this gap post-verdict by claiming ten total multiplications occurred somewhere else in system logs did not cure the defect; appellate courts cannot consider new evidence introduced for the first time on appeal or theoretical mappings never presented to a jury under oath under Rule 50 standards. Consequently, no reasonable jury could find that seven specific multiplications were performed based on Dr. Wells' equivocal testimony and incomplete documentary analysis.
