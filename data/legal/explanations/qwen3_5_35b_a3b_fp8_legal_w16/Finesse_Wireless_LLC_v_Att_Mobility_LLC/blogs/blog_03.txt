# Finesse Wireless v. AT&T: When Three Operations Don't Make Seven

**By [Your Name]**

In the high-stakes world of digital signal processing litigation, the difference between infringement and non-infringement often comes down to a single line of code—or in this case, a missing set of mathematical operations. Yesterday's Federal Circuit decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that plaintiffs cannot bridge the gap between theory and reality with speculation alone. While the case involved two patents regarding Passive Intermodulation Product (PIM) cancellation, the reversal of the infringement verdict hinged on a critical failure of proof: showing that an accused device actually performs seven specific multiplications when the claim language demands it, rather than just three. This ruling reinforces a vital principle for software and algorithm-heavy patents: courts will not allow juries to infer claim limitations from incomplete technical documentation or partial operational data.

The core of the dispute centered on U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 ('775 patent), which covers a method for canceling interference by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific combinations to generate cancellation signals. The claim explicitly lists seven distinct third-order multiplications involving variables S1, S2, and S3. During trial, Finesse's expert witness relied heavily on a Nokia technical document to demonstrate infringement. That document depicted the accused radios performing three distinct mathematical operations involving input signals x1 and x2. However, Finesse attempted to stretch this evidence to cover all seven multiplications required by the patent claims without providing any direct evidence or logical mapping connecting the three observed operations to the seven mandated ones.

The Federal Circuit's analysis exposes the fatal flaw in this approach. As Chief Judge Moore wrote for the court, identifying three distinct multiplications is not equivalent to demonstrating that seven occur. The technical manual showed operations like $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$ and similar variations, but crucially lacked any indication that these operations were repeated or combined in ways that satisfied every term of claim 1(d). Furthermore, Finesse tried to bolster its position on appeal by arguing that Nokia's documents actually listed ten multiplications total. Yet, neither Finesse nor its expert ever explained which subset of those ten matched the seven claimed steps, nor did they provide a mechanism for how those ten reduced down to the specific three observed at trial that supposedly covered all seven requirements.

This evidentiary disconnect led directly to reversal under the "substantial evidence" standard applicable at judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Court emphasized that when a plaintiff relies on an expert's testimony to fill gaps in technical mapping, that testimony must be consistent and clear enough for a reasonable jury to reach the conclusion demanded by the claim terms. Here, Dr. Wells' attempt to equate three observed operations with seven claimed ones was unsupported by any explanation within his testimony or the documentary record he cited. The Court noted that Finesse's proposed mapping required forcing signals into roles they didn't occupy—such as treating a complex conjugate or modulus operation as one of the raw signals S1-S3—which fundamentally altered what was actually happening in the accused device compared to what was patented.

Doctrinally, *Finesse* sends a clear message about scrutiny applied to algorithmic claims where "generating" elements are central to validity and infringement determinations. Courts have long warned against allowing plaintiffs to rely on functional definitions where structural details matter; this decision tightens that screw further regarding quantitative limitations within algorithms. If a claim requires "seven" instances of an action derived from "three" inputs via specific permutations, simply showing three actions occurring is insufficient unless there is proof that those actions encompass all permutations or that additional hidden iterations exist within the hardware logic described in admissible evidence. By rejecting arguments based on extrapolation from partial data sets (three vs. seven), *Finesse* ensures that patent owners must prove exactly what their inventions do inside black-box chips or software modules before seeking damages from industry giants like AT&T and Nokia.

The implications extend beyond this specific technology sector to any field involving complex computational processes—from machine learning architectures to cryptographic protocols where specific iterative steps define novelty and infringement risk. Litigants can no longer assume that because an algorithm *could* theoretically produce more outputs than are visibly documented in marketing materials or white papers, it *does* so in practice without explicit evidence tying those theoretical possibilities to actual device behavior during trial proceedings. The Federal Circuit has effectively drawn a line: if you need seven apples and you only bring four oranges labeled "fruit," you haven't proven you brought apples unless you can show precisely how those oranges transform into apples in real-time operation—a transformation Dr. Wells failed to demonstrate here.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T* underscores that innovation protection requires rigorous proof matching invention disclosure with commercial implementation alike. The Court reversed not because it doubted Finesse's intellectual property rights existed or were valuable—in fact, it acknowledged Finesse had likely amended its claims specifically to distinguish its technology from prior art—but because Finesse failed to carry its burden of proving those rights were violated by AT&T/Nokia's specific configuration as alleged at trial without speculative leaps over missing data points.
