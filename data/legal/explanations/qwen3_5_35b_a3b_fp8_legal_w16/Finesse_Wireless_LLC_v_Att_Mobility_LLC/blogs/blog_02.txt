Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Beyond Claim Construction: Why Prosecution History Did Not Save Scope in *Finesse Wireless*

In the high-stakes world of patent litigation, particularly within the telecommunications sector, the line between a clever workaround and literal infringement often hinges on a single word in a specification or a nuance in the file wrapper. The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided on September 24, 2025, serves as a stark reminder that procedural victories at trial do not guarantee survival on appeal when the evidentiary foundation is shaky. More significantly, *Finesse Wireless* offers a nuanced look at how the court applies prosecution history estoppel, specifically rejecting the notion that narrowing an amendment from "two or three" to "three" signals constitutes an absolute bar against devices utilizing only two inputs. While the case ultimately turned on sufficiency of evidence rather than pure claim construction, the court's treatment of the prosecution history highlights the difficulty plaintiffs face when attempting to stretch claim language beyond its explicit boundaries without making clear disclaimers.

The dispute centered on patents related to mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference—a critical issue for mobile network operators like AT&T and hardware manufacturers like Nokia. Finesse had secured jury verdicts of infringement and damages totaling over $166 million, predicated largely on expert testimony interpreting Nokia's internal documentation. However, upon review, Chief Judge Moore's opinion dismantled both the infringement theory for the '134 patent and the '775 patent. The reversal underscores a fundamental principle: an expert's ability to reconcile contradictory testimony or infer mappings that are not explicitly supported by the evidence cannot sustain a verdict against overwhelming logical inconsistencies. In both instances, Finesse's expert witness failed to bridge gaps between the claimed limitations and the accused technology with clarity that would allow a reasonable jury to reach a finding of infringement.

The crux of my analysis lies in Section II.B regarding the '775 patent and its treatment of prosecution history. Here, Finesse attempted to defend its position by arguing that it had not surrendered scope over devices using two signals when it amended its claims during prosecution to require "three signals." During examination, Finesse faced an obviousness rejection based on prior art teaching two-input systems (specifically *Filipovic*). To overcome this rejection—which concerned active IMPs—the applicant distinguished their invention as addressing passive IMPs and simultaneously narrowed their claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals S1, S2 and S3."

Under strict applications of prosecution history estoppel, such an amendment often triggers a presumption of surrender regarding anything falling outside the new scope. The Federal Circuit has long held that if an applicant narrows a claim for reasons relating to patentability, they cannot later recapture what was surrendered unless they can show that they did not intend to give up ground covered by prior art or equivalent subject matter. In *Finesse*, however, Chief Judge Moore applied this doctrine with precision but leniency toward Finesse's interpretation. She noted that while Finesse amended to specify "three," this did not amount to a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of using only two unique input signals if those signals could be generated in ways that allowed for three separate identifiers (such as using copies or variations).

This distinction is vital for practitioners navigating post-*Continental Circuits* jurisprudence. The court reasoned that Finesle's amendment was primarily driven by a desire to distinguish passive vs. active interference mechanisms from *Filipovic*, rather than an intent to exclude all dual-input architectures entirely. By framing the amendment as clarifying "identifiability" rather than strictly limiting physical inputs to three distinct wires or channels, Finesse managed to keep its claim scope somewhat flexible despite the textual narrowing. The court effectively ruled that adding "three" was not enough to trigger total disqualification of two-signal systems unless Finesse explicitly stated they were abandoning them in response to specific prior art concerns regarding two-signal configurations themselves.

However, it is crucial not to conflate this favorable reading of prosecution history with success in proving infringement itself. Even though Finesse successfully argued that it hadn't fully surrendered scope over two-input devices via estoppel arguments alone, it still lost on substantive grounds because no reasonable jury could find that Nokia's radios performed seven specific multiplications involving three signals based on the evidence presented at trial. The expert testimony linking Nokia's actual architecture—where only three distinct multiplications occurred—to seven claimed mathematical operations was speculative at best and contradicted by technical documents showing complex conjugate operations instead of direct multiplication. This disconnect illustrates that while prosecution history might save you from losing your claim scope entirely due to estoppel doctrines, it cannot rescue you from failing to prove literal infringement where the math simply doesn't add up in light of substantial evidence standards.

The downstream implications of *Finesse Wireless* suggest a tightening grip on expert-driven infringement theories where mapping becomes purely inferential rather than demonstrable via documentary evidence or admissions within technical schematics. Future litigants should expect courts to scrutinize whether experts are merely stitching together disparate pieces of testimony into a coherent narrative or if there is actually substantial evidence supporting every element of their maps against competing technical realities. Furthermore, while *Finesse* leaves room for defendants who narrow claims without explicitly disavowing equivalents based on input counts rather than functional outputs, it reinforces that "three" means exactly what it says when paired with specific mathematical steps required by independent claims—if you don't perform those exact steps with those exact inputs/outputs as described in your own documents and admitted during discovery, you likely won't survive JMOL regardless of how you framed your amendment strategy years ago.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* stands as another cautionary tale for patent owners who rely too heavily on broad interpretations of claim language bolstered by equivocal expert testimony. The Federal Circuit did not need to overturn Finses' entire strategy based solely on claim construction; instead, it found fatal flaws in how those constructions were applied to facts at trial and how prosecution history was interpreted regarding surrender rules combined with actual performance data. For lawyers drafting responses or preparing witnesses for future trials involving digital signal processing algorithms, this opinion suggests that clarity is paramount—not just in defining terms like "signal," but in ensuring that every step of an algorithmic process can be traced directly back to intrinsic evidence without requiring jurors to fill in significant gaps through speculation about what an expert might have meant when they said something ambiguous thirty pages earlier in deposition transcripts.
