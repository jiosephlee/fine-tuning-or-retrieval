# Chapter 1: Case Background and Procedural History

## Overview of Passive Intermodulation (PIM) Technology

To understand the technological stakes in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first grasp the phenomenon of intermodulation products (IMPs). In radio communications, signals are typically kept separate by operating on distinct frequencies. However, when radio signals encounter physical obstacles such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors, the nonlinear properties of these materials cause transmit signals to mix. This mixing creates new, unwanted frequencies known as "intermodulation products." When these products fall within the frequency range of the intended signals ("inband"), they create significant interference that degrades communication quality.

Specifically, the patents at issue address "passive intermodulation" (PIM), which arises from these physical obstacles rather than electronic components within the radio itself. While some PIM is internal to the radio hardware, the invention covered by Finesse's patents focuses on mitigating interference caused by passive external sources. The core innovation involves detecting these interference-generating signals and mathematically estimating the resulting IMPs to cancel them out digitally before they disrupt the desired transmission. Understanding this mechanism is crucial because the entire validity and infringement analysis turns on whether the accused devices actually implement this specific mathematical isolation and cancellation process described in the patent claims.

## Identification of Patents '134 and '775 and Their Subject Matter

The litigation centers on two United States patents owned by Finesse Wireless LLC: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the "'134 patent") and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "'775 patent"). Both patents describe methods for reducing IMP interference in receivers, but they approach the problem through slightly different claim structures involving digital signal processing techniques.

The '134 patent claims a method focused on isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals" within a passband of received data. Once isolated via decimating filters, the system computes an estimate of the intermodulation products generated by those interference signals and cancels them out using a closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustment that includes sub-sample phase shifts. Essentially, this patent covers a workflow where incoming signals are oversampled to capture both good and bad data streams simultaneously for subsequent processing.

The '775 patent claims a related but distinct method that relies on generating "Intermodulation Product Cancellation Signals" (ICSs) through digital multiplication. Specifically, Claim 1 requires taking three input signals—designated S1, S2, and S3—and performing seven specific third-order multiplications (such as $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$) to create cancellation signals that neutralize passive IMPs generated downstream of a high-powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter. The crux of this claim lies in the requirement for seven distinct multiplicative operations derived from three specifically identified input signals to achieve near real-time cancellation. These technical specifics define the boundaries of what constitutes infringement in this case.

## Parties: Finesse Wireless LLC vs. AT&T/Nokia

The primary litigant against whom enforcement was sought was AT&T Mobility LLC ("AT&T"). However, Nokia of America Corporation ("Nokia") intervened directly in the lawsuit because it manufactured the mobile phones containing at least one component—the Passband Intermodulation Cancellation (PIM-C) feature—that Finesse alleged was infringing its patents. Consequently, Nokia became a named party alongside AT&T as Appellants on appeal after losing in district court; they are often referred to collectively as "Appellants" throughout this text because their interests were aligned regarding both infringement liability and damages exposure. Finesse Wireless LLC acted as the Plaintiff/Appellee below and now stands as the respondent defending its jury victory on appeal before the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (CAFC). The procedural dynamic shifted significantly once Nokia joined, as it brought its own technical expertise regarding how its radios function internally to challenge Finesse's expert testimony regarding signal flow and sampling timing.

## Procedural Posture: Jury Verdict ($166M Award) and Denial of Post-Trial Motions

Following discovery and trial proceedings in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, a jury rendered a verdict finding all asserted claims across both patents valid and infringed by AT&T's use of Nokia's PIM-C feature in its network infrastructure or handsets depending on implementation details not fully elaborated in this excerpt but implied by their joint defense strategy. Based on these findings, particularly regarding market share or licensing rates applicable to millions of units sold over several years, the jury awarded Finesse $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages covering the remaining life of both patents.

Unwilling to accept this outcome without further legal recourse, Appellants filed post-trial motions challenging both liability and monetary relief specifically under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(b). # Chapter 2: Standard of Review for Noninfringement JMOL

In patent litigation, the decision to grant a Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) represents a significant judicial intervention into the jury's fact-finding role. When an appellate court reviews a district court's denial of a JMOL motion asserting noninfringement, it must apply a precise standard of review that respects the jury's verdict unless the evidence is legally insufficient. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit applied these standards to reverse a jury's finding of infringement against Nokia and AT&T. Understanding the hierarchy of review—distinguishing between claim construction, factual findings, and the ultimate question of sufficiency—is essential for analyzing whether a verdict stands or falls.

## Review Standards: De Novo for Construction vs. Substantial Evidence for Facts

The Federal Circuit employs a tiered approach when reviewing patent cases on appeal. First, the court reviews claim construction de novo, meaning it examines the interpretation of patent terms without deference to the district court's ruling. However, any underlying factual findings that support that construction are reviewed for "clear error." This distinction is critical because while legal interpretations are revisited from scratch, factual determinations regarding what occurred in the accused device receive significant deference. Once claim construction is settled, the analysis turns to whether there is substantial evidence to support the jury's finding that the accused device meets every limitation of the properly construed claims. In *Finesse*, this framework guided the court's assessment of whether Nokia's radios actually performed the specific steps claimed in U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775.

## The 'Light Most Favorable' Standard for JMOL Denials in Fifth Circuit Law

When determining whether to overturn a jury verdict on appeal via JMOL, the Federal Circuit looks to the law of the regional circuit where the district court sits; in this instance, that is the Fifth Circuit. The standard is exceptionally rigorous for parties seeking reversal. A denial of JMOL is reversed only if "the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that... reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This "light most favorable" standard requires viewing all evidence not just as potentially supporting one side, but as compellingly pointing to one outcome so definitively that no fair-minded juror could disagree. Consequently, mere ambiguities or conflicting interpretations are insufficient to warrant granting JMOL; the evidence must leave no room for a reasonable dispute regarding infringement. In *Finesse*, despite Finesse presenting an expert witness who opined on infringement, the Federal Circuit ultimately found that no reasonable jury could reach its conclusion given specific evidentiary flaws highlighted by Appellants' arguments regarding timing and calculation counts.

## Defining 'Substantial Evidence' in Patent Infringement Cases

While JMOL asks whether reasonable jurors *could* differ, substantial evidence focuses on whether they *did* have enough proof to decide in favor of the prevailing party at trial. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." This definition acts as a shield for jury verdicts; it does not require absolute certainty or undisputed facts, but rather a foundation strong enough to sustain a rational conclusion. If an expert provides an opinion that creates a logical bridge between technology and claim limitations—even if imperfect—it often survives this threshold unless completely contradicted by unrefuted physical reality or logic within that specific record. However, as seen in *Finesse*, when expert testimony relies on internal inconsistencies or fails to map technical realities directly onto claim language without clarification from opposing counsel during trial (such as failing to correct an expert mid-testimony), that testimony may fail to constitute substantial evidence under this standard.

## The Burden of Proof Placed on the Patent Owner (Plaintiff)

In infringement suits involving patented technologies like those challenged by AT&T and Nokia, the burden always remains with the patent owner—the plaintiff—to prove infringement by preponderance of the evidence. This burden dictates that if an expert's testimony contains fatal gaps or contradictions regarding essential claim elements (limitations), and no other independent evidence fills those gaps, infringement cannot be sustained as a matter of law. In *Finesse*, Finesse bore this burden specifically regarding two technical hurdles: first, ensuring its accused devices sampled both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" simultaneously; and second, demonstrating seven specific digital multiplications were performed internally by Nokia hardware versus merely listing three distinct types of calculations on paper diagrams provided by Nokia itself because Finesse failed to present additional data showing repetition or mapping during trial? # Chapter 3: Analysis of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 - Sampling Limitations

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding infringement of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 ('134 patent). The central failure lay not in the complexity of the technology itself, but in the plaintiff's inability to present substantial evidence satisfying a specific structural limitation of Claim 1(a). The '134 patent describes a method for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference by isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," computing an estimate of the resulting IMPs, and canceling them out. To establish literal infringement, Finesse had to prove that the accused Nokia radios performed every step of Claim 1, including step [a]: "over-sampling... a passband of received signals... wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." The appellate court determined that Finesse failed to demonstrate that the accused radios actually sampled both types of signals simultaneously within the required passband. This failure stemmed from a fundamental logical flaw in the plaintiff's expert testimony: attempting to map a signal type that is mathematically generated *after* the sampling event occurred.

Claim 1(a) imposes a strict temporal and functional requirement on the accused device's Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). The claim language dictates that the device must first receive a passband containing both desired information ("signals of interest") and potential noise sources ("interference generating signals"), and only then perform an over-sampling operation on that combined passband to create a bit stream. The critical distinction in this patent architecture is identifying what constitutes an "interference generating signal." In this context, these are signals capable of interacting with other signals within physical obstacles (like rusted connectors) to create unwanted intermodulation products. However, for digital cancellation systems like those described in the '134 patent, there is often a distinction between actual incoming radio waves and internal models or calculations used by software to predict interference. If an element labeled as an "interference generating signal" exists only as a computational output generated downstream in the processing chain, it cannot logically be present in the raw signal stream entering the ADC for initial sampling.

The pivotal moment in *Finesse*'s case hinged on Dr. Jonathan Wells' expert testimony regarding how he mapped these claim terms to Nokia's technical documentation. Dr. Wells attempted to satisfy limitation [a] by pointing to a diagram in a Nokia document where two paths were highlighted with red dashed lines: one representing the Downlink Transmit Reference (DL[TX]) and another representing a "Modeled PIM Path." He testified consistently throughout direct examination that these two red-path elements corresponded directly to Claim 1(a)'s requirements: specifically, that DL[TX] was the "signal[s] of interest" and the "Model PIM path" was the "interference generating signal[s]." Under Dr. Wells' theory, both these signals entered the RF ADC together for sampling. However, this mapping created an insurmountable logical contradiction once subjected to cross-examination and scrutiny during post-trial briefing.

The defense successfully demonstrated that Dr. Wells' primary mapping relied on an impossible temporal sequence. Technical evidence established that the "Modeled PIM path" represents a calculation derived from data processed *after* sampling has already occurred by higher-level logic circuits (specifically mentioned as occurring within a GROOT FPGA). In other words, the model is generated downstream; it does not exist as an independent input signal available at the front end of the receiver prior to digitization. Consequently, it is physically impossible for an ADC to sample a passband containing both genuine incoming radio waves and a mathematical model generated later in time from those very same waves. One cannot sample what has not yet been generated; you cannot capture future data or algorithmic outputs from inputs that have not yet existed at that specific moment in time. By defining his own expert opinion based on this flawed premise—that two distinct entities existing at different stages of processing were simultaneously present at the input—the foundation for his infringement conclusion collapsed immediately upon admission that modeling occurs post-sampling.

# Chapter 4: Analysis of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 - Multiplication Requirements

## '775 Patent Claim Structure: Seven Third-Order Multiplications Methodology

The *Finesse Wireless* litigation centers heavily on the specific mathematical requirements imposed by independent Claim 1 of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 ('775 patent). Unlike the '134 patent, which focused on signal isolation and sampling timing, the '775 patent claims a method for performing interference cancellation in a receiver containing both a transmitter and receiver co-located within the same device. The core innovation involves generating "intermodulation product" (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) by mathematically modeling the nonlinear processes that create interference. Specifically, Claim 1(d) requires that the generation of these ICSs be based on a power series description of the non-linear process and includes generating an *n*-th order ICS by performing seven specific digital multiplications involving three signals, designated S1, S2, and S3.

The claim language explicitly enumerates these seven multiplications: $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$, $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_3$, $S_2 \times S_2 \times S_3$, $S_1 \times S_3 \times S_3$, and $S_2 \times S_3 \times S_3$. These terms represent various combinations of three distinct input signals raised to powers and multiplied together to approximate the third-order intermodulation products that degrade radio performance. To prove literal infringement under this claim structure, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the accused device actually performs these exact seven calculations using three defined input signals. In *Finesse*, the district jury found that AT&T and Nokia infringed this claim because their PIM-C feature allegedly utilized copies of transmitter signals to perform these cancellations. However, on appeal, the Federal Circuit scrutinized whether the evidence supported the conclusion that the accused devices performed all seven specific mathematical operations mandated by the claim text.

## Claim Construction Debate: 'Separately Identifiable' Signals vs. 'Unique' Inputs

A threshold issue in analyzing whether three signals exist in the accused device is determining what "three signals" means legally. During proceedings leading up to trial, including summary judgment motions, Finesse argued that "three signals" implied three unique physical inputs (i.e., three completely different radio waves). However, AT&T contended that Finesse had surrendered this scope during prosecution to distinguish their invention from prior art. The district court resolved this dispute by construing "three signals" to mean "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." Under this construction, a system could theoretically satisfy the claim using fewer than three physically unique input streams if those streams were processed as separate entities—for example, using two unique physical signals where one is copied to serve as a second identifier ($x$ and $x'$).

The appellate court affirmed this construction but applied it strictly against Finesse's evidentiary showing. While agreeing that Finesse did not surrender rights regarding systems utilizing only two unique input signals via prosecution history estoppel—a critical point discussed further below—the court noted that even under the broadest interpretation ("separately identifiable"), the accused device still failed to meet the numerical requirement of performing seven multiplications. The debate over uniqueness was significant because if "unique" were required, AT&T's defense would have been simpler: they only used two distinct physical inputs ($x$ and $y$), thus failing limitation 1(c) immediately. By accepting "separately identifiable," Finesse attempted to bridge this gap by arguing that their software treated internal signal variations as distinct inputs even if derived from fewer physical sources. Nevertheless, this concession did not save Finesse's case because it shifted the battleground entirely to whether those identified inputs resulted in all seven required multiplicative steps.

## Prosecution History Estoppel Did Not Disclaim Two-Signal Embodiments

Finesse faced a significant hurdle regarding prosecution history estoppel, an equitable doctrine preventing patentees from recapturing subject matter they surrendered during patent examination to secure issuance or maintain validity against prior art rejections. AT&T argued that Finesse had explicitly narrowed their claims from covering "two or three signals" to specifically requiring "three signals" solely to overcome an obviousness rejection based on a reference named *Filipovic*. *Filipovic* taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs; therefore, AT&T reasoned that Finesse's amendment constituted a clear disavowal of any embodiment using only two unique input signals. If true, this would bar Finesse from asserting infringement against AT&T's two-signal architecture regardless of how many multiplications were performed.

The Federal Circuit rejected AT&T's application of prosecution history estoppel in this instance for two primary reasons rooted in the record of allowance notices and examiner correspondence. First, Finesle distinguished *Filipovic* not merely on quantity but on quality; they argued *Filipovic* addressed active intermodulation products arising from active electronic components within amplifiers (active IMPs), whereas their claimed invention targeted passive intermodulation products (PIMs) generated by passive obstacles like rusty connectors or corroded cables outside the active electronics stack. Second, although Finesne amended the claim text to specify "three signals," they provided no explicit statement indicating they intended to abandon all embodiments utilizing only two unique inputs if those inputs could be treated as separately identifiable for calculation purposes. Citing *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, which sets a high bar for establishing disavowal through amendments alone (requiring "clear and unambiguous" intent), the court held that amending from "two or three" to "three" did not amount to such a clear abandonment of two-input scenarios when combined with arguments distinguishing types of interference rather than purely numerical limits. Consequently, Finesne remained free to argue its theory regarding separately identifiable inputs derived from fewer than three unique physical sources without being barred by estoppel for having narrowed its claims previously.

## Evidence Gap: Nokia Documents Show Three Distinct Operations Not Seven

Despite successfully navigating potential estoppel barriers regarding signal countability, Finsess ultimately lost its battle over whether AT&T's hardware performed enough calculations to satisfy Claim 1(d). The crux of infringement lay in Dr. Wells' testimony linking Nokia's technical documentation directly to the seven claimed multiplications at trial. Dr. Wells relied heavily on internal Nokia diagrams which illustrated how their PIM-C feature operated digitally within field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs). He testified that these diagrams showed three distinct multiplication operations occurring sequentially or simultaneously within the processor logic flow corresponding to his expert model of how PATENTED technology functions in practice. Specifically, he identified three equations represented in Nokia materials: $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x'_2$, and $x_1 \cdot |x'_2| \cdot |x'_2|$ (where primes denote complex conjugates). Crucially, none of these equations matched any single term listed in Claim 1(d)'s mandatory list of seven products exactly as written; nor did Dr. Wells provide evidence showing repetition or sequencing sufficient to aggregate these into all seven required forms ($S_i^a S_j^b$ combinations).

The Federal Circuit found no substantial evidence supporting a reasonable jury verdict on this point because Dr. Wells failed to establish a causal link between Nokia's documented operations and every element of Claim 1(d). While Nokia documents indicated ten possible polynomial coefficients were calculated (from $p(n)=a(0)$ through $p(n)=a(9)$), neither Dr. Wells nor Finsess demonstrated which specific subset comprised exactly those seven permutations mandated by Claim 1(d). Furthermore, there was absolutely no testimony or documentary proof suggesting any operation was repeated multiple times within a single cycle or across cycles such that three base computations could somehow generate four additional results absent direct instruction from code execution traces or firmware analysis presented at trial. # Chapter 5: Damages Award Vacatur

In patent litigation, the assessment of monetary relief is entirely contingent upon a foundational finding of liability. Once a court determines that no infringement has occurred, the legal basis for any compensatory award evaporates. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit faced this precise scenario after reversing the district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement. Having concluded that the jury's verdicts of infringement lacked substantial evidence for both the '134 and '775 patents, the appellate court addressed the status of the $166 million damages award granted by the jury. The court's analysis underscores a fundamental principle of patent remedies: damages cannot stand independently of a valid infringement determination. Consequently, having reversed the liability findings, the Federal Circuit vacated the damages award without needing to engage in a substantive re-evaluation of the dollar figure itself.

## Relationship Between Infringement Finding and Damage Awards

The logical architecture of a patent infringement suit dictates that damages are a remedy for harm caused by unauthorized use. If there is no unauthorized use—because the accused product does not infringe—the premise for calculating compensation disappears. In *Finesse*, this relationship proved decisive. The district court had denied Appellants' motions for JMOL on damages and for a new trial, effectively accepting that a valid infringement existed and leaving open only questions regarding the quantum of recovery or potential procedural errors in how that recovery was calculated. However, on appeal, the Federal Circuit determined that Finesse failed to meet its burden of proof on every single asserted claim. Without an infringement finding for claims 1–3 of the '134 patent or claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the '775 patent, there was no legal injury to compensate. The court reasoned that once liability is extinguished, any attempt to uphold or adjust an award becomes legally irrelevant. Therefore, rather than remanding for a recalculation or debating whether the expert testimony on value was sound, the appellate court simply removed the entire financial penalty because it rested on a void foundation.

## Standard for New Trial Motions on Damages

While the ultimate outcome turned on liability rather than calculation methodology, *Finesse* provides important context regarding how appellate courts review challenges to damage awards via motions for a new trial. To properly situate this case within broader civil procedure principles, one must understand the standard applied by both federal circuits and specifically reviewed here: those courts apply local circuit law when reviewing denials of post-trial motions like new trials under Rule 59(e) or similar state-law equivalents incorporated by reference in diversity cases (though here it is applied via general appellate practice). Specifically, under Fifth Circuit law governing this region's procedures within this context (and referenced by Fed. Cir precedent cited in *Finesse*), an appellate court reviews such denials for "abuse of discretion." This standard grants significant deference to the district judge's firsthand observation of witness credibility and trial dynamics; reversal occurs only if that decision falls outside the range of reasonable choices available to lower courts based on evidence presented at trial.

However, in *Finesse*, reaching this deferential standard became unnecessary due to procedural hierarchy. The Federal Circuit noted that while it would normally scrutinize whether evidence supported the specific valuation figures or whether prejudice warranted a new trial under an abuse-of-discretion lens, such scrutiny presupposes that some form of liability exists to justify damages at all. Because Appellants successfully demonstrated total noninfringement—a question decided de novo or based on substantial evidence depending on whether it involved fact-finding—the "abuse" inquiry into damages was rendered moot. The court acknowledged Appellants' motion for JMOL on damages or alternatively a new trial but held that since they prevailed on their primary defense against liability entirely, they were entitled to relief from any financial obligation regardless of whether their arguments regarding damage calculation were themselves strong enough to trigger an abuse-of-discretion reversal alone.

## Effect of Total Reversal on Liability

The core mechanism driving this portion of *Finesse* is what happens when an appellant succeeds in overturning every ground upon which liability was predicated. In many appeals involving mixed results—where some claims are upheld and others overturned—the damages section often survives partially or requires allocation adjustments if multiple patents were found infringed differently across various counts. Here, however, Finesse lost completely: none of its asserted claims survived judicial scrutiny due to insufficient expert testimony linking Nokia's technology to specific claim limitations like "interference generating signals" or "seven claimed multiplications." When a patent owner fails to prove literal infringement across all asserted claims due to evidentiary gaps identified by experts who contradicted themselves or failed to map technical features accurately (as seen with Dr. Wells' confusion over signal paths), there is no residual basis left standing in either patent portfolio upon which money could be assessed against AT&T/Nokia. The effect is absolute: vacatur follows automatically because there is nothing left from which damages could logically flow; you cannot calculate interest rates on zero principal if zero principal represents zero debt owed under current facts found by higher authority. Thus, reversing denial means granting judgment immediately—not just theoretically but operationally removing any existing monetary judgments attached previously pending further proceedings unless
