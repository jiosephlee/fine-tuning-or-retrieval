# Case Background and Procedural Posture

The litigation *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* arises from a dispute concerning advanced digital signal processing techniques used in modern wireless communications. The central technological conflict involves "Passive Intermodulation" (PIM), a phenomenon that plagues radio frequency systems. While radios typically avoid interference by operating on separate frequencies, physical imperfections in hardware—such as rusty connectors, loose cables, or metal enclosures—can cause transmitted signals to mix nonlinearly. This mixing generates new, unwanted frequencies known as intermodulation products (IMPs). When these spurious signals fall within the operational bandwidth of the receiving radio, they degrade performance. To combat this, Finesse Wireless LLC developed technologies designed to detect and cancel these PIM effects internally.

In this case, Finesse holds ownership of two relevant patents: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the "'134 patent") and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "'775 patent"). Both patents cover methods for mitigating intermodulation interference in receivers by utilizing software-based algorithms to isolate useful signals ("signals of interest") from noise-generating signals and subsequently subtracting estimated interference from the data stream. Finesse initiated suit against AT&T Mobility LLC alleging that AT&T's network equipment infringed upon these patents specifically through the implementation of a PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature embedded in Nokia radios supplied to AT&T. Consequently, Nokia intervened in the action as a codefendant alongside AT&T. Together, AT&T and Nokia are referred to herein as the Appellants.

## Parties and Technology Overview: Passive Intermodulation Products (PIM)

To understand the stakes of this dispute, one must appreciate the nature of the technology at issue: passive intermodulation products (PIM). Unlike active electronics where semiconductors might generate harmonics due to nonlinearity, PIM originates from physical defects or material properties in otherwise passive components like antennas, connectors, and cabling. When high-power transmit signals encounter these irregularities—often described metaphorically as "loose metal contacts"—they interact to create sum and difference frequencies that were not present in the original transmission. These artifacts manifest as "noise floors" that obscure legitimate user data ("signals of interest"), necessitating sophisticated mitigation strategies in dense cellular networks where multiple carriers share spectrum resources efficiently but dangerously close together.

Finesse asserts that its patented inventions solve this problem by modeling the generation of these interference products mathematically within the radio's digital processor rather than attempting purely physical fixes upstream. By predicting what the interference looks like based on observed input signals and subtracting that prediction from the received bitstream, Finesse argues its system effectively cancels out PIM without requiring expensive hardware upgrades across an entire cell tower infrastructure. This background is critical because it frames why infringement hinges entirely on whether the accused devices actually perform these specific mathematical steps: oversampling passbands to capture both wanted signals and potential interferers simultaneously, calculating estimates of future interference based on those captured inputs, and applying precise phase/amplitude adjustments to neutralize them before demodulation occurs. Without understanding that PIM is generated *after* certain internal sampling stages in some architectures versus others becomes crucial for determining literal infringement later in this analysis.

## Patent Claims Asserted: Summary of '134 and '775 Patents

At trial, Finesse asserted several claims from both patents against Nokia's implementation of its PIM-C feature. From the **'134 patent**, Finesse pursued independent claim 1 along with dependent claims 2–3. Claim 1 describes a method involving six primary steps: first, **over-sampling** a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" to produce a raw bit stream; second and third steps involve **isolating** these respective signal types using decimation filters; fourth step computes an **estimate** of each intermodulation product derived directly from those isolated source signals; fifth step executes **cancellation** by subtracting said estimates; and finally sixth performs iterative **phase and amplitude adjustments** via sub-sample shifts within a feedback loop to refine accuracy throughout operation ([J.A.] 28:2–26). # Standard of Review and Legal Framework for JMOL

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit begins its analysis by establishing the precise lens through which it views the District Court's decision. While the Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over patent appeals nationwide, it does not apply a uniform federal standard for every procedural question arising in a patent case. Instead, when addressing post-trial motions such as Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL), the Federal Circuit looks to the law of the regional circuit where the district court sits. Here, because the litigation originated in the Eastern District of Texas—a part of the Fifth Circuit—the appellate court applies Fifth Circuit standards to determine whether the district court correctly refused to enter judgment against Finesse. This deference to local procedural law ensures consistency within the geographic region while allowing the Federal Circuit to focus its specialized expertise on substantive patent doctrine, such as claim construction and infringement analysis.

Under Fifth Circuit precedent, which governs this appeal, a denial of JMOL is reviewed *de novo*. This means the appellate judges examine the record independently without deferring to the lower court's ruling on sufficiency. However, "de novo" in this context carries a heavy weight toward affirmance rather than reversal. The standard is exceptionally stringent: a verdict may be overturned only if, when viewing all evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party (in this case, Finesse), "the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that... reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This high bar reflects a fundamental respect for the jury system; courts are reluctant to substitute their own judgment for that of twelve laypersons unless no rational person could disagree with one side's version of events based on what was presented at trial. Consequently, even if some evidence supports Appellants' theory of non-infringement, that alone is insufficient to warrant JMOL if conflicting evidence exists that allows a reasonable jury to find infringement.

To sustain a jury verdict against a challenge for lack of substantial evidence, there must be enough relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. In *Finesse*, this principle dictates how the court evaluates Dr. Wells' expert testimony regarding whether specific signals were present in Nokia's hardware. If Dr. Wells provided inconsistent statements—for instance, initially mapping certain paths as "interference generating signals" and later offering confused explanations about inputs labeled x1 and x2—the appellate court asks whether these contradictions render his testimony too weak to support any reasonable finding of fact. If an expert's testimony is so internally contradictory that no reasonable juror could rely upon it to establish an element of infringement, then substantial evidence is lacking. Conversely, if there is merely ambiguity or room for interpretation that favors Finesse's position under light-of-the-evidence scrutiny, the verdict stands despite Appellants' objections. The line drawn here separates mere errors or weaknesses in proof from total insufficiency; only when proof fails completely does JMOL become appropriate.

Beyond evaluating factual disputes regarding infringement elements like signal sampling or multiplication counts, appellate review distinguishes sharply between questions of fact and questions of law inherent in patent cases. Claim construction—the process by which terms in a patent are defined—is reviewed *de novo* by the Federal Circuit without deference to either district judge or jury interpretations. This makes sense because interpreting written instruments like patents is viewed primarily as a matter of legal meaning derived from intrinsic records (claims, specification, prosecution history). However, once terms are constructed legally correcty applied them to physical devices constitutes factual inquiry subject to "substantial evidence" review. In *Finesse*, while definitions like "three signals S1, S2 and S3" were determined anew by judges applying legal principles, determining whether Nokia actually multiplied three separate signals involved weighing witness credibility and documentary exhibits—a task reserved for juries unless those determinations lacked evidentiary support entirely. Thus, even if claim language seems broad enough theoretically on paper (*de novo*), actual application requires concrete proof meeting substantial thresholds (*jury*).

Finally, understanding why Finesse lost its battle hinges on recognizing where burdens rest throughout this framework. As plaintiffs asserting infringement liability under 35 U.S.C., Finesse carried the burden throughout trial—and consequently remained responsible at appeal—to prove every single limitation described in their claims existed within AT&T/Nokia equipment beyond doubt supported by admissible evidence. # '134 Patent Analysis: Insufficiency of Expert Testimony on Sampling Signals

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding non-infringement of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 ('134 patent). Central to this reversal was the insufficiency of the plaintiff's evidence to prove literal infringement of Claim 1, specifically limitation 1[a]. This provision requires a method that "over-samples... a passband of received signals... wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." To sustain a jury verdict, Finesse had to present substantial evidence showing that the accused Nokia radios actually sampled a single passband containing both types of signals simultaneously. While Finesse presented expert testimony from Dr. Jonathan Wells, the appellate court found his testimony internally inconsistent and logically impossible under the laws of physics governing digital signal processing. Consequently, no reasonable jury could have found that limitation 1[a] was satisfied based on Dr. Wells' account alone.

## '134 Patent Claim 1 Structure: Over-Sampling Requirement

To understand the evidentiary failure, one must first grasp the structural requirements of Claim 1. The '134 patent covers a method for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference by identifying unwanted noise ("interference generating signals") within a stream of useful data ("signals of interest"), estimating what that noise looks like, and subtracting it from the original stream. Limitation 1[a] sets forth the foundational step for this entire process: over-sampling a passband at a desired frequency to create a bit stream. Crucially, this initial act of sampling must capture both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals" within that same passband. If the system does not receive or sample both signal types at this upstream stage, subsequent steps involving isolation, computation, and cancellation cannot physically occur as claimed. Therefore, establishing that the accused hardware captures both signal categories in a single sampling event is a prerequisite for infringement liability under this claim element.

## Expert Witness Testimony Mapping Diagram Elements to Claim Terms

At trial, Finesse attempted to bridge the gap between its claims and Nokia's technology through Dr. Wells' testimony regarding diagrams provided in Nokia's internal technical documentation. These diagrams illustrated various signal paths within an RF Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). Dr. Wells interpreted these visual representations by mapping them directly to the language in Claim 1[a]. He testified that one portion of a specific red line on the diagram—the "downlink transmit (DL[TX]) reference"—corresponded to the claimed "signals of interest." Simultaneously, he mapped another component along that same red path—the "modeled PIM path"—to the claimed "interference generating signals." By doing so, Dr. Wells sought to demonstrate that when the RF ADC sampled this red path, it captured both requisite signal types in accordance with limitation 1[a]. His expert opinion formed the sole basis upon which Finesse rested its burden of proof for this specific claim element; without his testimony linking these diagram labels to statutory limitations, there was no direct evidence presented to suggest infringement occurred at Step 1[a].

## The Temporal Contradiction: Modeling Occurs Post-Sampling

The fatal flaw in Dr. Wells' analysis emerged when examining his understanding of *when* these signals existed relative to one another in time—a concept known as temporal causality in engineering systems. During cross-examination and further examination at JMOL proceedings, it became evident that Dr. Wells admitted the "modeled PIM path" represents an estimation generated mathematically by software running on field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) located downstream in the radio architecture. In essence, this modeled signal is calculated *after* the analog-to-digital conversion has already taken place; it is derived from previous measurements rather than being an independent incoming wave captured during sampling itself.

If infringement requires sampling both signals simultaneously within a single passband prior to any processing or generation logic occurring later in the chain, then a signal created via mathematical modeling *after* sampling cannot possibly be included in that initial sample stream. Physical reality dictates that one cannot sample something before it exists; conversely, one cannot sample a future calculation as though it were an existing input wave at an earlier timestamp. This distinction creates an insurmountable logical barrier: if Dr. Wells correctly identifies the "interference generating signals" as merely modeled outputs generated post-conversion, then limitation 1[a]'s requirement—that these signals be present *within* the passband being sampled—is inherently unmet because those signals simply do not yet exist at that precise moment in time when sampling occurs upstream at the RF ADC input port.

## Self-Contradictions in Expert Testimony Undermining Substantial Evidence

# '775 Patent Analysis: Failure to Prove Seven Distinct Multiplications

## '775 Patent Claim 1 Structure: Three Signals and Seven Multiplications

To understand the Federal Circuit's reversal in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first dissect the structural requirements of the asserted claims in U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 ('775 patent). Unlike many software or telecommunications patents that rely heavily on broad functional language, Claim 1 of the '775 patent imposes precise mathematical constraints designed to mitigate Passive Intermodulation Products (PIM) within radio receivers. The claim defines a method for performing interference cancellation that relies on a very specific algorithmic approach involving "seven multiplications."

Specifically, limitation 1(d) of Claim 1 mandates that generating Intermodulation Product Cancellation Signals (ICSs) must involve creating an $n$-th order ICS by digitally multiplying three signals—designated as $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$. Crucially, the claim explicitly enumerates exactly seven combinations of these three signals that must be processed. These seven mathematical operations are: $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$, $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_3$, $S_2 \times S_2 \times S_3$, $S_1 \times S_3 \times S_3$, and $S_2 \times S_3 \times S_3$. The court emphasized that meeting this limitation is not merely a matter of generating some cancellations; rather, the accused device must perform these specific seven digital multiplications to fall within the literal scope of the claim. Consequently, the burden rested squarely on Finesse to prove via substantial evidence that AT&T's accused Nokia radios executed this exact sequence of seven calculations. Without proof that every single one of these seven multiplications occurred in practice, infringement could not stand as a matter of law.

## Claim Construction Issue: Separately Identifiable vs. Unique Signals

Before addressing whether the accused devices actually performed the required math, the appellate court had to consider a threshold dispute regarding what constitutes "three signals" under the claim terms. During litigation, Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) contended that "three signals" implied three *unique* input signals—that is, three physically distinct waves entering the system simultaneously. They argued that if Finesse intended to allow duplicate inputs (such as using only two physical signals where one was copied), they had effectively narrowed their claim scope too far during prosecution history estoppel proceedings. However, the district court had already resolved this issue at summary judgment by construing "three signals" to mean "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." This construction allowed for scenarios where fewer than three unique physical inputs existed, provided they were treated as separate entities for processing purposes—for instance, taking Signal X and copying it to serve as Signal Y.

On appeal, Appellants pressed forward with their argument that Finesse surrendered rights to any system utilizing only two unique input signals when they amended their original claims during prosecution. Originally drafted to cover systems using "two or three signals," Finesse amended them to specifically recite "three signals" after facing an obviousness rejection based on prior art known as Filipovic. Appellants argued this amendment constituted a clear disclaimer against two-input systems. While this issue remained relevant for determining potential infringement theories based on input count alone, it became secondary once the court determined that even assuming Finesse's interpretation was correct (allowing two unique inputs), there was still no evidence the accused device satisfied the subsequent requirement of performing seven distinct multiplications. Nevertheless, understanding this distinction is vital because it highlights how courts scrutinize amendments made during patent prosecution; such amendments can trigger prosecution history estoppel if they constitute a clear surrender of subject matter to overcome prior art rejections.

## Prosecution History Estoppel Argument Dismissed

In evaluating whether Finesse surrendered claim scope over two-input systems through its amendment history, the Federal Circuit applied a strict standard derived from precedent like *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.* To establish prosecution history estoppel barring a particular embodiment from infringement liability, there must be a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope by the applicant during examination proceedings mere implication or ambiguity is insufficient; otherwise, applicants could inadvertently narrow their own patents without realizing it. Here, although Finesse changed its claims from covering "two or three" signals to strictly "three" signals to distinguish itself from Filipovic—which taught mitigation techniques using only two inputs—the court found this move did not amount to an explicit abandonment of all systems employing two unique input signals combined with internal duplication logic.

The Federal Circuit reasoned that Finesle's primary justification for amending away from "two or three" was not purely numerical but qualitative: it distinguished its invention by noting that Filipovic addressed *active* intermodulation products whereas its invention targeted *passive* ones caused by hardware imperfections like rusted connectors or loose cables. By framing the amendment around overcoming Filipovic's focus on active versus passive phenomena rather than simply limiting the arithmetic count of inputs per se, Finesse maintained flexibility in how those inputs might be sourced internally within the device architecture. Therefore, while Finsess conceded it needed three *identifiable* streams for calculation purposes (even if derived from fewer unique sources), it did not irrevocably promise never to assert claims against devices running on just two unique physical channels provided those channels could be logically separated into three tracks for computation. Because no clear disavowal occurred regarding two-unique-input architectures, Appellants' reliance on prosecution history estoppel failed as a standalone bar; however, this failure did not save Finses' case due to evidentiary deficiencies elsewhere in its theory of infringement regarding the actual performance of mathematical operations.

# Damages Award Vacatur

When an appellate court reverses a jury verdict on the ultimate question of liability—in this instance, patent infringement—the legal foundation for any associated monetary award collapses simultaneously. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit faced a situation where it had already determined that the district court erred in denying judgments as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding non-infringement for every single asserted claim of both the ’134 and ’775 patents. The panel found that Finesse failed to present substantial evidence to support the jury's conclusion that AT&T's devices practiced the patented methods. Once the court concludes that no reasonable jury could have found infringement, the existence of patent liability itself is negated. Consequently, the subsequent discussion focuses on why the appellate court chose to vacate the $166 million damages award rather than remanding the case back to the district court for a separate trial solely on damages or reinstating a reduced damage figure.

## Relationship Between Liability Determination and Damage Awards

The calculation of damages in patent litigation is entirely contingent upon a preceding finding of validity and infringement. Damages serve as a remedy for an established wrong; they quantify the harm suffered by the patentee resulting from another's unauthorized use of their invention. Without a valid finding that an act occurred which constitutes infringement, there is no "wrong" to compensate and thus no metric by which to calculate compensation. In *Finesse*, the district jury had awarded approximately $166 million based on its determination that AT&T infringed all asserted claims. However, because the Federal Circuit reversed this determination on substantive grounds—finding that Finesse's expert testimony was self-contradictory and insufficient to prove essential claim limitations like sampling "interference generating signals"—the premise of that financial award vanished. The relationship between liability and damages is hierarchical: liability is the predicate condition precedent to damages. If the predicate fails, the consequent financial obligation cannot stand, regardless of how meticulously that amount might have been calculated relative to hypothetical sales or licensing rates derived from an invalid theory of infringement.

Furthermore, allowing a damages award to survive when liability has been extinguished would create a logical impossibility within federal civil procedure. An award implies that money is owed because rights were violated; if no violation exists because no infringement occurred, ordering payment transforms innocent conduct into compensable wrongdoing retroactively. The Federal Circuit noted specifically that because it reversed *all* grounds for infringement across both patents involved in the suit, there remained zero legal basis upon which any portion of the $166 million could be justified. The district court's denial of JMOL on damages was therefore legally untenable once the underlying infringement verdict was set aside. To uphold any part of that sum would effectively endorse an infringement finding that this very court had explicitly declared unsupported by substantial evidence, thereby undermining judicial consistency and statutory requirements for patent enforcement.

## Mootness Principle Applied Here

The concept of mootness plays a critical role in determining whether an appellate court needs to address ancillary issues when primary errors are corrected at their root cause. Generally, courts prefer not to decide cases unless there is an actual controversy requiring resolution; if resolving one issue renders others irrelevant, those remaining issues become moot. In *Finesse*, Appellants appealed two distinct rulings: first, the denial of JMOL regarding non-infringement (liability), and second, the denial of a new trial concerning damages (remedy). Had Finesse prevailed on some claims but lost on others—for instance, if only half the claims were found validly infringed—the question of whether a new trial was necessary for damages would remain live and potentially subject to remand depending on how much liability survived. However, because Finesse lost completely on all asserted claims due to insufficiency of evidence, any dispute regarding whether a new trial should be granted specifically for damages became academic and unnecessary ("moot").

If there is no infringement liability whatsoever after reversal, asking whether a new trial is appropriate for calculating damages assumes facts in contradiction with reality—that there still remains something liable enough to warrant assessment against AT&T mobility LLC or Nokia Corporation under these patents. A request for a new trial presupposes potential future liability; yet here, future liability has been foreclosed by this decision's holding on sufficiency of evidence regarding claim elements like signal isolation and multiplication steps. Thus, addressing whether Mr./Ms., Judge denied correctly in refusing a new trial serves no practical purpose because even granting such relief would lead nowhere absent surviving infringement findings. By declaring everything moot post-reversal-of-liability-the-court avoids wasting resources debating procedural nuances around remedies when none exist anymore—a principle often summarized as "if you win big enough initially you don't need smaller fights later."
