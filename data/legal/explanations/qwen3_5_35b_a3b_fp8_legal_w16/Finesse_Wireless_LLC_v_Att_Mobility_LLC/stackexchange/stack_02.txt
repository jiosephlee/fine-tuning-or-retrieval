Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Does amending a patent claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' always constitute a disclaimer of scope for devices using only two unique inputs?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, Finesse amended their claims during prosecution to specify 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' instead of 'two or three,' apparently to distinguish from prior art that used two inputs. However, the Federal Circuit held this didn't amount to a 'clear and unambiguous disavowal' of scope for products using only two unique input signals (like x1 and x2), provided they were 'separately identifiable.' Why isn't adding a specific number ('three') usually interpreted as excluding anything less? What distinguishes this case from typical prosecution history estoppel scenarios where narrowing amendments restrict claim scope?
Answer:
**Answer:**

No, amending a claim from "two or three" to "three" does not *always* constitute a disclaimer of scope for devices using fewer than three unique inputs. As the Federal Circuit clarified in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the determination depends entirely on whether the prosecution history reflects a **"clear and unambiguous"** surrender of subject matter.

In *Finesse*, the patentee amended the claim to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3" specifically to distinguish from prior art (*Filipovic*) that utilized only two inputs. While this narrowing amendment triggered an inquiry into prosecution history estoppel, the Court held that the amendment did not disclaim devices using only two unique signals because the context of the amendment revealed a different limiting purpose. The record showed that Finesse distinguished its invention primarily on the basis that *Filipovic* addressed **active** intermodulation products, whereas Finesse's invention targeted **passive** intermodulation products (PIM). Consequently, the addition of "three signals" was interpreted as requiring **three separately identifiable signals**, rather than three *unique* input signals.

Under Federal Circuit precedent (specifically citing *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*), a disclaimer must be explicit and unambiguous to narrow claim scope via prosecution history estoppel. Here, the Court reasoned that requiring three "separately identifiable" signals allows for a scenario where two unique physical inputs ($x_1$ and $x_2$) can satisfy the limitation if one of them is treated as a separate entity (e.g., via copying or processing) to create three distinct mathematical entities ($S_1, S_2, S_3$). Because Finesse did not explicitly state during prosecution that it was abandoning all embodiments involving fewer than three unique physical inputs—and because the primary distinction drawn against the prior art was functional (active vs. passive PIM)—the Court refused to read a broad exclusionary limitation into the claim language.

Therefore, unlike typical scenarios where a narrowing amendment creates an irrebuttable presumption of surrender regarding literal equivalents or broader ranges, this case illustrates that courts will look beyond the literal text of an amendment to determine if the patentee actually intended to disavow specific configurations based on the rationale provided to secure allowance. If the amendment serves to distinguish based on function or type rather than quantity alone, it may not preclude infringement by devices utilizing fewer unique inputs but satisfying the structural requirement of "identifiability."
