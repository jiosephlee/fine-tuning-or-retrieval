Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Analysis of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 - Multiplication Requirements

## '775 Patent Claim Structure: Seven Third-Order Multiplications Methodology

The *Finesse Wireless* litigation centers heavily on the specific mathematical requirements imposed by independent Claim 1 of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 ('775 patent). Unlike the '134 patent, which focused on signal isolation and sampling timing, the '775 patent claims a method for performing interference cancellation in a receiver containing both a transmitter and receiver co-located within the same device. The core innovation involves generating "intermodulation product" (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) by mathematically modeling the nonlinear processes that create interference. Specifically, Claim 1(d) requires that the generation of these ICSs be based on a power series description of the non-linear process and includes generating an *n*-th order ICS by performing seven specific digital multiplications involving three signals, designated S1, S2, and S3.

The claim language explicitly enumerates these seven multiplications: $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$, $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_3$, $S_2 \times S_2 \times S_3$, $S_1 \times S_3 \times S_3$, and $S_2 \times S_3 \times S_3$. These terms represent various combinations of three distinct input signals raised to powers and multiplied together to approximate the third-order intermodulation products that degrade radio performance. To prove literal infringement under this claim structure, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the accused device actually performs these exact seven calculations using three defined input signals. In *Finesse*, the district jury found that AT&T and Nokia infringed this claim because their PIM-C feature allegedly utilized copies of transmitter signals to perform these cancellations. However, on appeal, the Federal Circuit scrutinized whether the evidence supported the conclusion that the accused devices performed all seven specific mathematical operations mandated by the claim text.

## Claim Construction Debate: 'Separately Identifiable' Signals vs. 'Unique' Inputs

A threshold issue in analyzing whether three signals exist in the accused device is determining what "three signals" means legally. During proceedings leading up to trial, including summary judgment motions, Finesse argued that "three signals" implied three unique physical inputs (i.e., three completely different radio waves). However, AT&T contended that Finesse had surrendered this scope during prosecution to distinguish their invention from prior art. The district court resolved this dispute by construing "three signals" to mean "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." Under this construction, a system could theoretically satisfy the claim using fewer than three physically unique input streams if those streams were processed as separate entities—for example, using two unique physical signals where one is copied to serve as a second identifier ($x$ and $x'$).

The appellate court affirmed this construction but applied it strictly against Finesse's evidentiary showing. While agreeing that Finesse did not surrender rights regarding systems utilizing only two unique input signals via prosecution history estoppel—a critical point discussed further below—the court noted that even under the broadest interpretation ("separately identifiable"), the accused device still failed to meet the numerical requirement of performing seven multiplications. The debate over uniqueness was significant because if "unique" were required, AT&T's defense would have been simpler: they only used two distinct physical inputs ($x$ and $y$), thus failing limitation 1(c) immediately. By accepting "separately identifiable," Finesse attempted to bridge this gap by arguing that their software treated internal signal variations as distinct inputs even if derived from fewer physical sources. Nevertheless, this concession did not save Finesse's case because it shifted the battleground entirely to whether those identified inputs resulted in all seven required multiplicative steps.

## Prosecution History Estoppel Did Not Disclaim Two-Signal Embodiments

Finesse faced a significant hurdle regarding prosecution history estoppel, an equitable doctrine preventing patentees from recapturing subject matter they surrendered during patent examination to secure issuance or maintain validity against prior art rejections. AT&T argued that Finesse had explicitly narrowed their claims from covering "two or three signals" to specifically requiring "three signals" solely to overcome an obviousness rejection based on a reference named *Filipovic*. *Filipovic* taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs; therefore, AT&T reasoned that Finesse's amendment constituted a clear disavowal of any embodiment using only two unique input signals. If true, this would bar Finesse from asserting infringement against AT&T's two-signal architecture regardless of how many multiplications were performed.

The Federal Circuit rejected AT&T's application of prosecution history estoppel in this instance for two primary reasons rooted in the record of allowance notices and examiner correspondence. First, Finesle distinguished *Filipovic* not merely on quantity but on quality; they argued *Filipovic* addressed active intermodulation products arising from active electronic components within amplifiers (active IMPs), whereas their claimed invention targeted passive intermodulation products (PIMs) generated by passive obstacles like rusty connectors or corroded cables outside the active electronics stack. Second, although Finesne amended the claim text to specify "three signals," they provided no explicit statement indicating they intended to abandon all embodiments utilizing only two unique inputs if those inputs could be treated as separately identifiable for calculation purposes. Citing *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, which sets a high bar for establishing disavowal through amendments alone (requiring "clear and unambiguous" intent), the court held that amending from "two or three" to "three" did not amount to such a clear abandonment of two-input scenarios when combined with arguments distinguishing types of interference rather than purely numerical limits. Consequently, Finesne remained free to argue its theory regarding separately identifiable inputs derived from fewer than three unique physical sources without being barred by estoppel for having narrowed its claims previously.

## Evidence Gap: Nokia Documents Show Three Distinct Operations Not Seven

Despite successfully navigating potential estoppel barriers regarding signal countability, Finsess ultimately lost its battle over whether AT&T's hardware performed enough calculations to satisfy Claim 1(d). The crux of infringement lay in Dr. Wells' testimony linking Nokia's technical documentation directly to the seven claimed multiplications at trial. Dr. Wells relied heavily on internal Nokia diagrams which illustrated how their PIM-C feature operated digitally within field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs). He testified that these diagrams showed three distinct multiplication operations occurring sequentially or simultaneously within the processor logic flow corresponding to his expert model of how PATENTED technology functions in practice. Specifically, he identified three equations represented in Nokia materials: $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x'_2$, and $x_1 \cdot |x'_2| \cdot |x'_2|$ (where primes denote complex conjugates). Crucially, none of these equations matched any single term listed in Claim 1(d)'s mandatory list of seven products exactly as written; nor did Dr. Wells provide evidence showing repetition or sequencing sufficient to aggregate these into all seven required forms ($S_i^a S_j^b$ combinations).

The Federal Circuit found no substantial evidence supporting a reasonable jury verdict on this point because Dr. Wells failed to establish a causal link between Nokia's documented operations and every element of Claim 1(d). While Nokia documents indicated ten possible polynomial coefficients were calculated (from $p(n)=a(0)$ through $p(n)=a(9)$), neither Dr. Wells nor Finsess demonstrated which specific subset comprised exactly those seven permutations mandated by Claim 1(d). Furthermore, there was absolutely no testimony or documentary proof suggesting any operation was repeated multiple times within a single cycle or across cycles such that three base computations could somehow generate four additional results absent direct instruction from code execution traces or firmware analysis presented at trial.
