# Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and the Emergency Stay Context

## Timeline of District Court Rulings: Feb/March 2025 Preliminary Injunctions

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* began with a rapid escalation in the Western District of Washington following the issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 on January 20, 2025. This executive order suspended entry into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) based on a presidential finding that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Simultaneously, a separate executive order led to the suspension of all funding for USRAP, including domestic resettlement services. Plaintiffs, comprising admitted refugees, approved applicants awaiting arrival, and non-profit organizations responsible for processing and resettling refugees, challenged these actions immediately. They alleged violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 regarding the suspension of admissions and violations of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) concerning the defunding of essential services.

The district court responded swiftly to these allegations. On February 25, 2025, acting on an oral motion, the district court issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement of specific sections of Executive Order No. 14163. Recognizing that funding was integral to the execution of refugee policy, the court clarified this injunction in writing on February 28, 2025 (District Court Docket Entry 45), explicitly stating that it also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. The conflict escalated when the Department of State terminated cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations shortly thereafter. In response to these terminations, which threatened immediate harm to already-admitted refugees dependent on support services, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, 2025 (District Court Docket Entry 79). This subsequent order specifically commanded the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after February 25. These dual rulings created an immediate directive for federal agencies to maintain both refugee admissions processes and domestic service funding despite the President's executive orders setting them aside.

## The Government's Appeal and Initial Motions for Stay

Upon receiving notice of these preliminary injunctions, the government moved aggressively to halt their enforcement while appealing to a higher court. Notices of appeal were filed on February 28 and March 25, 2025. Crucially, alongside filing their appeals—which typically pauses proceedings but does not automatically stop an injunction—the government filed emergency motions seeking a stay pending appeal. A stay pending appeal is a critical procedural mechanism that preserves the status quo by preventing a party from suffering irreparable harm during litigation while simultaneously allowing an appellate court to review whether a permanent injunction should have been issued in the first place. Without such a stay, defendants might be forced to comply with conflicting orders or suffer damages that cannot be undone even if they ultimately win on appeal; conversely, plaintiffs might suffer harm if an erroneous injunction remains in force too long before reversal.

The government argued that under *Trump v. Hawaii*, courts must afford significant deference to presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), suggesting they were likely to succeed on the merits regarding both admission suspensions and funding decisions. However, rather than waiting for full briefing on all issues, which can take months in complex immigration cases involving national security implications, the government sought immediate relief from specific portions of the district court's orders that they believed exceeded judicial authority over presidential prerogatives in immigration control. This set up a race against time where multiple judges across different levels were issuing conflicting directives regarding what laws applied to refugees and aid workers as early as spring 2025.

## The Ninth Circuit's Evolving Stays: From Partial Carve-outs to Full Vacatur

The Ninth Circuit initially addressed these emergency requests through its motions panel rather than waiting for a full panel opinion on the merits. On March 25, 2025, citing *Trump v.* *Hawaii*, motions panels granted part of the government's request for a stay but denied it entirely regarding individuals who had already received conditional approval for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This created a complex "partial stay" scenario: while most aspects of President Trump's orders were allowed to proceed without interference from lower courts during litigation (because they lacked merit), refugees who had cleared USCIS hurdles prior to Januarry remained protected by an exception known as a "carve-out." The appellate panel acknowledged that while broad deference existed generally ("exuding deference"), it did not extend fully enough at that moment to strip away rights already accrued by specific individuals who had completed rigorous vetting processes domestically or abroad before new restrictions took effect.

However, this procedural arrangement proved unstable and difficult to administer as litigation progressed through April and May 2025. The Ninth Circuit issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9 defining exactly how this limited carve-out operated in practice amidst confusion over which refugees fell under protection versus exclusion zones defined by dates alone. By July 14—a date significant because it marks another layer of complexity—the district court attempted to operationalize this carve-out by appointing a magistrate judge and establishing an enforcement framework specifically designed to manage exceptions for pre-approved refugees while enforcing other parts of its original order against general admissions bans and funding cuts. This created a fractured legal environment where different rules applied depending on individual application dates and specific categories of aid recipients within weeks of each other's rulings.

# Chapter 2: Factual Background and Statutory Framework

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* arises from a dual-pronged executive action taken in early 2025 that fundamentally altered the United States' approach to its refugee system. The factual landscape is defined by two distinct Executive Orders issued by President Trump shortly after his inauguration on January 20, 2025. These orders triggered a cascade of administrative actions and judicial interventions, creating a complex procedural history centered on the tension between presidential immigration authority and statutory obligations to support refugees already present in the country. Understanding the specific mechanics of these orders and the conflicting statutory regimes they implicate is essential to grasping the appellate court's eventual split holding regarding stays pending appeal.

## Executive Order No. 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions under § 1182(f)

The primary driver of the controversy was Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program," issued on January 20, 2025, and published in the Federal Register on January 30. This order relied explicitly on the authority granted to the President by Section 1182(f) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That statute provides that whenever the President finds that "the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States," he may suspend their entry by proclamation for such period as he deems necessary.

Under Executive Order No. 14163, § 3(a), President Trump made a formal finding that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Based on this finding, he directed that entry into the USRAP be suspended pending further findings. The text of Section 1182(f) is notably broad, allowing for restrictions on both immigrants and non-immigrants without explicit temporal limits or substantive criteria beyond the presidential determination of detriment to national interest. In subsequent proceedings, including oral arguments before this court, reliance was placed heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, where the Supreme Court emphasized that Section 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with ample power to impose entry restrictions beyond those otherwise enumerated in the INA. The government argued that because Section 1182(f) contains only a single prerequisite—that the President make a finding—the order did not exceed textual limits on presidential authority.

## Executive Order No. 14169: Defunding Domestic Resettlement Services

Concurrent with the suspension of admissions, a separate mechanism was employed to disrupt existing resettlement operations: Executive Order No. 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," issued on January 20, 2025. While this order ostensibly addressed foreign aid generally, its practical effect within days was significant for domestic refugees. On January 24, 2025, pursuant to this executive directive, the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP operations abroad and, crucially for this case, halted funding for domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States.

This defunding action targeted cooperative agreements used by non-profit organizations to provide reception and placement services domestically. Within weeks of these orders taking effect—specifically by February 26—the State Department terminated every cooperative agreement responsible for providing reception and placement services for newly arrived refugees within U.S.-based communities. Furthermore, nearly all cooperative agreements supporting overseas processing were terminated save for one exception. This abrupt cessation created an immediate crisis regarding how recently admitted refugees would receive essential support services like housing assistance and job training, setting the stage for allegations that these actions violated specific statutory mandates rather than merely exercising executive discretion over future admissions policies.

# Chapter 3: Analysis of Presidential Authority over Entry Restrictions (§ 1182(f))

This chapter examines the Ninth Circuit's analysis in *Pacito v. Trump* regarding the President's authority to suspend the entry of refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The central issue addressed by the appellate panel is whether the government demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits sufficient to justify staying the district court's preliminary injunction against Executive Order No. 14163. This analysis serves as a foundational lesson in how courts apply the standard for stays pending appeal and, more specifically, how they defer to presidential authority in matters of national security and immigration under precedent set by *Trump v. Hawaii*.

### The Standard for Review under *Nken v. Holder*: Success on Merits Factor One

To obtain a stay pending appeal, a party must satisfy four factors outlined in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009): (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm absent a stay; (3) substantial injury to opposing parties if a stay is granted; and (4) alignment with the public interest. The Ninth Circuit emphasized that while all four factors matter, "the first two—the showing of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are 'the most critical.'" In *Pacito*, the court focused primarily on the first factor because, without a strong showing that the government would ultimately win its defense of Executive Order No. 14163, there would be no need to weigh the remaining equities.

The court did not engage in a de novo review of the constitutional questions surrounding executive power but rather applied a highly deferential lens required by Supreme Court precedent. The question was not whether the court agreed with the President's policy choice, but whether that choice fell within the statutory boundaries set by Congress and respected by previous judicial interpretations. By framing the analysis around "likelihood of success," the court signaled that it was assessing whether any reasonable interpretation of § 1182(f) would support the Executive Order, rather than re-litigating the wisdom of suspending refugee admissions entirely.

### 'Exuding Deference': Applying *Trump v. Hawaii* to § 1182(f)*

The Ninth Circuit anchored its reasoning directly in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), noting that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." This language indicates that when Congress delegates authority to suspend entry based on a presidential finding, it intends for courts to grant significant weight to that determination. In *Pacito*, this principle meant that the judiciary's role was limited to ensuring procedural compliance with § 1182(f)'s text, rather than acting as a super-legislature evaluating the factual accuracy or political prudence of those findings.

The court rejected any notion that it could conduct a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Instead, it treated § 1182(f) as granting broad discretion: whenever the President finds that entering aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests, he may suspend their entry for whatever period he deems necessary or impose appropriate restrictions. Because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contained such a finding—that refugee entry via USRAP was detrimental—the court found no legal barrier preventing its enforcement under this statute alone. This approach underscores a key tenet of administrative law: when statutes delegate power broadly and conditionally upon an executive finding, courts generally accept those findings unless they are facially invalid or procedurally defective in ways unrelated to their substantive truthfulness.

# Chapter 4: The APA Challenge and Mandatory Duties under § 1522

## Irreparable Injury Without Continued Funding for Resettlement Agencies

In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit's analysis of the stay pending appeal hinges critically on the distinction between the President's broad authority to suspend future refugee admissions and the specific statutory mandates governing those already present or approved for entry. While the majority agrees that the government is likely to succeed on its defense of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of admissions based on the deferential standard applied in *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court identifies a sharp departure in its assessment of the government's liability regarding "reception and placement" services. The central procedural question becomes whether the termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations causes irreparable harm that outweighs the government's interest in defunding these programs during an administrative transition.

The district courts had previously issued preliminary injunctions not only stopping the suspension of admissions but also halting the termination of funding for domestic resettlement services. When reviewing these injunctions, the Ninth Circuit recognized that while a stay might be appropriate to pause enforcement against future applicants, maintaining a gap in services for current refugees presents a distinct legal problem. The court noted that despite assurances from the State Department that it was actively seeking new contractors to replace terminated agreements, there was no evidence at oral argument that such replacements were secured. This uncertainty created a situation where refugees admitted prior to January 20, 2025, faced immediate destitution without access to employment training, cash assistance, or medical care. In this context, "irreparable injury" is not merely speculative; it is imminent and concrete. The court reasoned that leaving hundreds of thousands of dollars in appropriated funds unused while simultaneously stripping essential services creates a crisis that cannot be remedied by money damages alone once they occur. Thus, even if the government ultimately prevails on its right to reduce funding eventually, the immediate cessation of operations violates statutory commands and inflicts injury severe enough to warrant judicial intervention via a stay modification.

## Success on Merits: Interpreting 'Shall' vs. 'Authorized' in § 1522(a)-(e)

The crux of the dispute regarding "success on merits" turns on statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, specifically whether its language imposes a mandatory duty to fund or merely authorizes discretionary action. The majority opinion adopts a textualist approach focused on imperative verbs found throughout the statute to conclude that Congress intended to bind the executive branch's hands regarding service delivery once appropriations are available. The text explicitly directs that "[t]o achieve economic self-sufficiency... [the Director] shall make available sufficient resources," "shall provide... opportunity," and "shall insure." For purposes of an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge, such mandatory language ("shall") typically signifies a non-discretionary command rather than a suggestion or an option open to executive whim.

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a competing interpretive framework rooted in legislative structure and purpose titles. He argues that because § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs..." and contains multiple instances where Congress states the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance (particularly in subsections (b) through (e)), the statute functions as a grant of power rather than an imposition of duty. Under this reading, "authorization" implies permission; if Congress wanted to mandate funding regardless of executive preference, it would have used more unequivocal language devoid of qualifying conditions like "to the extent of available appropriations." Judge Lee contends that subsection (a)'s use of "shall" applies only as a conditional constraint *if* funding occurs—it dictates how money must be spent if Congress appropriates it—but does not compel Congress or the President to appropriate it in any specific amount or manner. This disagreement highlights a fundamental tension in administrative law: does statutory silence on specific budget allocations leave room for executive discretion, or does specific performance language override general budgetary constraints? The majority resolves this by viewing § 1522 as creating an affirmative obligation tied directly to existing appropriations, rejecting the notion that funding cuts can legally nullify statutory service requirements without violating the APA's prohibition against arbitrary and capricious action by agencies failing to follow binding statutes.

## Available Appropriations: Does Reduced Funding Excuse Performance?

A pivotal element in determining whether there is success on merits involves interpreting the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" found within § 1522(a). The government argued implicitly—and sometimes explicitly—that because Congress reduced its appropriation for migration and refugee assistance by $800 million through subsequent legislation (Pub. L. No. 119-28), it lacked sufficient funds to fulfill all previous commitments under § 1522 without further congressional action. If interpreted broadly, this clause could allow an agency facing budget reductions to simply cease operations entirely until new funds are allocated by Congress, effectively treating "available appropriations" as a ceiling rather than a floor for operational capacity derived from existing funds.

However, the Ninth Circuit majority rejects this expansive reading in favor of one that respects congressional intent behind both appropriations acts and substantive mandates alike. The court points out that despite significant cuts, substantial funds remained ($3 billion minus $800 million), which were still legally designated for migration and refugee assistance under Pub. L. No. 119-4 (§§ 1101(a)(11)). Therefore, there were indeed "available appropriations" capable of supporting core reception and placement functions required by law. The reduction did not render total impossibility; rather, it necessitated difficult prioritization but did not absolve the government of its duty to utilize remaining lawful funds for statutory purposes like cash assistance and medical support for newly arrived refugees who had already been vetted and admitted under prior frameworks. By holding that reduced funding does not equate to zero availability when billions remain dedicated to similar objectives, the court establishes that agencies cannot invoke budget cuts as a blanket excuse for abandoning statutory mandates unless they demonstrate true insolvency or lack of any relevant funds whatsoever—a high bar rarely met when congressionally earmarked monies persistently exist alongside unfilled needs identified by statute itself.
