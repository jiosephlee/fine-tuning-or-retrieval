# Procedural History and the Evolution of Stays

The procedural history of *Pacito v. Trump* illustrates the dynamic tension between district courts exercising equitable powers to enjoin executive action and appellate courts managing the stability of those orders during litigation. The controversy began in early 2025 when President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, suspending entry into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), followed shortly by a separate order terminating funding for domestic resettlement services. Plaintiffs, comprising admitted refugees, approved applicants awaiting arrival, and service organizations, sued in the Western District of Washington. They argued that the suspension of admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of services violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and specific mandates in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 regarding reception and placement services.

The district court moved swiftly to address these claims through a series of escalating preliminary injunctions. On February 25, 2025, the district court issued an oral preliminary injunction halting enforcement of key sections of Executive Order No. 14163. Two days later, on February 28, the court issued a written order clarifying that this injunction extended beyond mere entry restrictions to prohibit the suspension of USRAP funding itself. By March 24, recognizing that the government had already terminated cooperative agreements with private organizations essential for refugee resettlement, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction specifically ordering the State Department to reinstate those terminated agreements. These rulings created an immediate legal crisis: while the executive branch claimed broad deference under immigration statutes like 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the district court found statutory duties under § 1522 required continued funding and service delivery regardless of presidential policy shifts.

The government responded by filing notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, seeking relief from what they viewed as overly broad judicial interference with presidential authority. Upon receiving these appeals, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit intervened with an initial stay order on March 25, effectively pausing much of the district court's work pending further review. However, this stay was not absolute; it contained a "limited carveout" preserving relief for individuals who had already received conditional approval for refugee status prior to January 20, 2025. This nuanced approach reflected a balance between respecting presidential deference—citing *Trump v. Hawaii* regarding Article II authority—and protecting specific reliance interests developed before the executive order took effect. The panel subsequently issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9 to refine exactly who fell within this protected carveout group, directing full briefing and scheduling expedited oral arguments for September to resolve the underlying merits.

As litigation progressed toward argument, procedural friction intensified regarding enforcement mechanisms rather than substantive law alone. On July 14, facing confusion over how to administer the limited carveout for pre-approved refugees without disrupting operations entirely, the district court issued an order establishing a detailed enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee compliance with its earlier rulings. This move prompted an emergency motion from the government on July 17 asking for clarification or amendment of stays to prevent potential harm from this new oversight structure. The Ninth Circuit responded by issuing an administrative stay on July 18, temporarily blocking enforcement of this new framework while it considered whether further intervention was necessary before hearing arguments later in September.

The current procedural posture emerged after oral arguments were heard on September 3 but before a final merits opinion could be rendered in time to guide lower courts during that interim period. Recognizing that lingering uncertainty could cause irreversible harm to either side or disrupt national security interests pending final adjudication, a majority panel issued an order vacating both its own administrative stay from July and its original stay from March (as amended). Instead of leaving cases in limbo or allowing conflicting directives from multiple levels of court to stand simultaneously, judges directed that all prior district court orders—from February through March—be stayed in their entirety moving forward as a single unitary directive pending final resolution of the appeal on merit grounds like § 1522 liability versus § 1182(f) authority.

This evolution underscores how stays function as provisional remedies designed to maintain *status quo* while appellate courts deliberate complex questions involving separation of powers and statutory interpretation involving immigration policy versus social welfare obligations for recent arrivals. # The Two-Pronged Challenge: Entry Suspension vs. Service Funding

The *Pacito* litigation presents a complex procedural landscape defined by a bifurcated legal challenge. While the case originated from a single political moment—the issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 on January 20, 2025—the legal disputes diverge sharply based on the specific rights asserted and the statutory authorities implicated. The Ninth Circuit's interim order distinguishes between the President's broad, deferential power to suspend the *admission* of refugees and the more constrained statutory duties regarding *services* for those already within or conditionally approved for entry. Understanding this split is essential to grasping why the appellate court vacated previous stays regarding funding while maintaining them regarding admissions, ultimately leading to a partial reinstatement of service contracts pending full briefing on the merits.

## Plaintiffs' Profile: Previously Admitted Refugees, Approved Applicants, and Service Provider Organizations

The standing and interests of the plaintiffs are central to understanding why the court treated the two prongs of the case differently. The complaint was brought by two distinct groups: individuals and organizations. The individual plaintiffs consist of refugees who had already been admitted to the United States prior to January 2025, alongside individuals who had received conditional approval for resettlement but remained outside the country at the time of the Executive Order's issuance. These individuals represent competing equities; those inside the country face potential loss of support services if funding is cut, while those awaiting admission face an absolute bar on entry.

Complementing these individual litigants are three non-profit organizations that serve as cooperative partners with the Department of State. These entities hold formal cooperative agreements designed to provide overseas processing for prospective refugees and manage reception and placement services for those once admitted domestically. Their involvement transforms what might otherwise be a purely humanitarian dispute into one involving contractual expectations and administrative continuity. By challenging both the suspension of admissions and the termination of these agreements, these organizations argued that they possessed a direct stake in preserving the operational framework required to execute refugee resettlement under existing statutes like 8 U.S.C. § 1522. This diverse plaintiff base allowed for arguments spanning constitutional immigration powers, statutory interpretation of welfare mandates, and administrative procedure regarding contract terminations.

## Executive Order No. 14163: Suspending Entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The first pillar of the government's action involved Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." Issued pursuant to Presidential authority found in 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), this order declared that entering refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) would be detrimental to U.S. interests and directed an immediate suspension of such entry pending further findings. This invocation relies on one of Congress's broadest delegations of power in immigration law, allowing the President to suspend entry whenever he deems it necessary for national interest without explicit judicial scrutiny of his underlying rationale beyond his own finding.

The Ninth Circuit addressed this prong with significant deference rooted in precedent, specifically *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). In its analysis, the court emphasized that Section 1182(f) vests "ample power" in the President and exudes deference to his determinations regarding national security and foreign relations challenges inherent in immigration control. The text requires only that the President "find" that entry would be detrimental; it does not require independent judicial verification that such detriment actually exists or that less restrictive means were available. Consequently, when evaluating whether a stay should be granted against this aspect of Executive Order No. 14163, the court concluded that because there is no textual limit on this authority beyond the President's finding itself, plaintiffs faced a low probability of success on this specific claim during an expedited review phase. # Presidential Deference in Immigration Control (The First Prong)

In addressing the government's motion for a stay pending appeal in *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit panel first turned to the threshold question of whether the government demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. This analysis focused exclusively on Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended entry into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) based on a determination that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court's reasoning in this portion of the order serves as a potent illustration of the extreme deference afforded to the President in matters of immigration control, relying heavily on precedent established in *Trump v. Hawaii*. By applying this standard, the Ninth Circuit concluded that the government was likely to prevail because Executive Order No. 14163 fell squarely within the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), rendering judicial review virtually nonexistent regarding the substantive justification for the suspension.

The core of the court's reasoning rests on the application of *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which dictates that statutes governing presidential immigration powers "exude[d] deference" to the Executive Branch. The panel emphasized that § 1182(f) vests "ample power" in the President to impose entry restrictions that go beyond those explicitly enumerated elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Specifically, the statute provides that whenever the President finds that "the entry of any aliens... would be detrimental to the interest[s] of the United States," he may suspend their entry for such period as he deems necessary. The Ninth Circuit treated this statutory scheme as granting broad discretion, noting that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear" and does not exceed textual limits on presidential authority. Consequently, when reviewing a challenge to such an exercise of power, courts are precluded from engaging in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Instead, judicial scrutiny is confined to verifying whether a formal finding was made; if such a finding exists, it is generally conclusive for purposes of establishing jurisdiction and authority.

This approach necessitates a strict reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) through a lens of textualism that prioritizes presidential autonomy over evidentiary requirements typically found in administrative law cases involving other agencies. Unlike many administrative actions subject to arbitrary-and-capricious review under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), where an agency must produce evidence supporting its factual predicates, § 1182(f) imposes only one explicit prerequisite: that the President "find[]" that entry would be detrimental. In *Pacito*, because President Trump had formally declared via Executive Order No. 14163 that refugee entry was indeed detrimental to U.S. interests, his action satisfied this sole statutory condition. The court declined to examine whether there was empirical data or specific intelligence backing this declaration during this interlocutory phase. The existence of a facially valid finding by an official acting within his designated capacity was deemed sufficient to insulate the order from immediate judicial invalidation, reflecting a doctrine where political questions regarding national security and border control are reserved almost entirely for political actors rather than judges.

Furthermore, the concept of "detrimental interests" operates as a self-executing trigger within this framework rather than an objective standard open to rigorous debate by litigants or lower courts. The Ninth Circuit noted that once a finding is made, courts lack standing or authority to second-guess whether those interests are actually being harmed or what constitutes harm enough to warrant suspension. This refusal to probe deeper ensures that executive judgments concerning foreign relations and national safety remain insulated from litigation tactics designed to delay or undermine policy shifts during periods of transition between administrations or crises in international affairs. By characterizing these determinations as matters falling within "broad discretion," the panel signaled that unless Congress has explicitly stripped away this power—a burden not met here—the President retains unilateral authority to halt classes of entrants based on his own assessment of national welfare.

# 'Should' vs. 'Authorized': Interpreting Mandatory Resettlement Duties (The Second Prong)

## Introduction to the Dispute Over Domestic Services
While *Pacito v. Trump* affirms the President's broad discretion to halt the admission of new refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the Ninth Circuit's partial stay order pivots sharply on the distinct legal question of what happens to refugees already present in the United States or conditionally approved prior to the suspension. This divergence forms the core of the "second prong" of the litigation: whether the federal government retains a statutory obligation to provide reception and placement services to these individuals despite a suspension of funding and cooperation agreements. The majority opinion establishes that while the President can stop new entries, he cannot legally abrogate existing statutory duties owed to those who have crossed the threshold or secured approval. Conversely, Judge Lee's partial concurrence and dissent argue that even if such duties exist, they are merely permissive authorizations rather than mandatory commands, allowing the executive branch complete discretion to cease operations. Understanding this conflict requires a close reading of how the court interprets specific statutory language within 8 U.S.C. § 1522, specifically distinguishing between phrases granting authority versus those imposing affirmative obligations.

## The 'Available Appropriations' Clause: Conditional Availability vs. Total Discretion
A primary battleground in interpreting 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is the clause stating that services must be provided "to the extent of available appropriations." The majority opinion treats this phrase as a limitation on *capacity*, not a grant of *discretion*. In other words, once Congress has appropriated funds—as evidenced by the $3.178 billion allocated for FY 2025, minus subsequent rescissions—the "available appropriations" constraint is satisfied, triggering an immediate duty to act. The court notes that Congress did not appropriate zero dollars; therefore, the government cannot claim a lack of funds as an excuse for non-performance unless it proves no money remains for these specific purposes.

Judge Lee challenges this interpretation by suggesting that "available appropriations" implies a continued executive judgment on how much money is actually necessary or usable for specific programs at any given time. However, the majority rejects this expansive reading in favor of a more rigid statutory construction: if Congress allocates money via legislation (such as Pub. L. No. 119-4), and then attempts to withhold it via executive action (like terminating cooperative agreements without a concurrent legislative repeal), the executive branch violates the statute by failing to utilize those congressionally mandated resources for their intended purpose. The distinction is crucial: "available appropriations" determines *if* and *how much* service can occur based on budget reality, but it does not determine *whether* service must begin if funds exist. By highlighting that Congress explicitly funded these programs and that those funds were not fully rescinded by law, the majority concludes that the government lacks standing to claim total discretion over whether to initiate services at all once funds are legally present.

## Parsing Mandatory Verbs: The Weight of 'Shall' in Subsections (a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)
The heart of the majority's reasoning rests on its analysis of imperative language found throughout § 1522, particularly verbs like "shall," which traditionally signal a mandatory command in statutory interpretation rather than mere suggestion or authorization. The court meticulously dissects three key subsections where this language appears:

First, subsection (a)(1)(A) directs that "the Director... shall... make available sufficient resources..." This directive covers essential components like employment training and English language acquisition aimed at achieving economic self-sufficiency quickly. The majority emphasizes that this "shall" creates an affirmative duty to allocate resources efficiently once funding is secured. Unlike terms like "may" or "authorized," which leave room for choice, "shall" compels action unless expressly excused by another provision—which § 1522 does not do regarding basic resettlement needs.

Second, subsection (d)(2)(B) addresses unaccompanied minor refugees during interim periods or transit before placement. It states that if necessary, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility... including financial responsibility." While Judge Lee argues this is qualified by necessity ("if necessary"), thereby preserving discretion, the majority views this as a mandatory trigger: whenever an unaccompanied minor enters an interim period where they lack care, the Director *must* step in legally and financially. There is no optionality in assuming responsibility once a child falls into this vulnerable category; the statute removes executive choice at that precise moment of need.
