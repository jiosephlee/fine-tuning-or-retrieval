Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# A Stay That Stays the Status Quo: The Ninth Circuit's Unusual Path in *Pacito v. Trump*

**By Legal Analyst**

The procedural posture of *Pacito v. Trump*, decided by the Ninth Circuit on September 12, 2025, presents a rare judicial paradox: a court ordering the reinstatement of a government program it has not yet definitively ruled is lawful, simply because it believes the government might lose on that specific point during a future merits hearing. While the bulk of the panel majority's reasoning leans heavily on precedent granting the President broad deference under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, the order creates a distinct exception for domestic resettlement services. By staying the district court's injunctions entirely except to force the restoration of cooperative agreements for receiving refugees already admitted, the Ninth Circuit has effectively prioritized the "irreparable injury" prong of the *Nken v. Holder* standard over its own hesitation to declare the underlying statute mandatory.

This approach marks a significant departure from typical appellate practice, where stays pending appeal usually maintain the status quo ante—leaving lower court injunctions in place until a final decision is rendered. Here, rather than waiting weeks or months for a full merits opinion on whether Congress compelled funding under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the panel issued an immediate directive: reinstate the contracts. The logic rests squarely on the fourth factor of *Nken*: where does the public interest lie? The judges reasoned that while they could not "engage in a searching inquiry" into the President's justification for halting new entries—a task foreclosed by *Trump v. Hawaii*'s deferential standard—they could not ignore what appeared to be an imminent collapse of domestic support networks for refugees already inside U.S. borders. The order essentially treats the cessation of these services not merely as an administrative dispute, but as an active crisis requiring immediate remedial action before a final judgment can be reached.

The crux of this interim relief lies in how the panel interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1522 versus § 1182(f). On one hand, citing *Hawaii*, the majority held that § 1182(f) vests "ample power" in the President to suspend entry whenever he finds it "detrimental," making it unlikely plaintiffs would succeed in challenging Executive Order No. 14163's ban on new admissions. However, when turning to domestic aid, specifically Section 1522's mandates for "reception and placement" services like cash assistance and medical support for recent arrivals, the calculus shifted. The panel noted that while Congress appropriated billions for these programs in Fiscal Year 2025, including funds reduced by subsequent rescission acts, Section 1523 requires strict reporting and accountability from HHS officials regarding these very activities.

Faced with evidence that State Department officials were unable to confirm whether they had secured replacement contractors to fulfill these statutory duties—a stark admission during oral argument—the court found that denying relief would cause irreparable harm to vulnerable populations dependent on these lifelines. The distinction drawn between "entry" (an area of plenary executive power) and "domestic support" (an area where statutory text appears more prescriptive) allowed the court to carve out a narrow window of protection without deciding whether Congress actually stripped HHS of its discretion entirely. It is a tactical maneuver that acknowledges statutory ambiguity while refusing to let that ambiguity result in human suffering while litigation drags on.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent illuminates exactly why this move feels legally precarious yet practically necessary. Lee argues that reading § 1522 as creating an absolute mandate ignores its structure; she contends that terms like "authorized" and phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations" signal permissive rather than obligatory intent for Congress. In her view, if Section 1522 is merely an authorization scheme allowing HHS to craft programs when funds exist, then courts lack jurisdiction to compel their existence absent explicit congressional command to spend regardless of executive preference or budgetary shifts. Her dissent suggests that by forcing reinstatement now, the majority may be usurping legislative intent by treating discretionary authority as binding obligation before fully parsing the text against historical practice and appropriation laws.

Doctrinally, this order sets a fascinating stage for what follows in any eventual merits opinion regarding administrative law and immigration statutes alike. If upheld upon final review, it reinforces a growing trend where appellate courts use stay proceedings not just as gatekeepers for temporary relief but as tools to manage complex administrative realities where delay itself constitutes injury—particularly involving basic subsistence needs like housing and food assistance for displaced persons. Conversely, should a future opinion reject Lee's textualist reading and affirm Section 1522 as purely discretionary or subject to broader executive control over foreign aid budgets (via Executive Order No. 14169), this interim reinstatement could appear as judicial overreach born of sympathy rather than statutory fidelity. Yet even if ultimately overturned on merit grounds after months of chaos for resettlement agencies and families alike, today's order serves as a powerful reminder: sometimes procedural speed is required precisely because constitutional deference cannot answer questions about human welfare fast enough for life-and-death circumstances to wait out an appellate schedule.
