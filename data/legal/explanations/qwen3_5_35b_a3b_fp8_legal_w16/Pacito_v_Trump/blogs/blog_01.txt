Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Deference vs. Duty: How *Pacito* Reaffirms Presidential Immigration Power While Carving Out Statutory Service Obligations

The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a masterclass in judicial compartmentalization, drawing a sharp line between the President’s plenary power to exclude aliens and the statutory duties Congress has imposed once those aliens enter. Issued on September 12, 2025, the panel order vacates previous stays and reinstates a limited stay pending appeal, creating a "split-rail" outcome that simultaneously validates the President's sweeping authority to halt refugee admissions while mandating the continuation of domestic resettlement services for those already admitted. The decision underscores a fundamental tension in modern immigration jurisprudence: while courts may grant near-total deference to executive determinations regarding border security under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), they retain the power—and perhaps the duty—to enforce congressional mandates regarding the treatment of individuals already within the nation's borders under statutes like 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

At its core, the majority's reasoning relies heavily on the precedent set in *Trump v. Hawaii*. When addressing Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended all entries into the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), the court applied a standard of extreme deference. Citing *Hawaii*, the panel noted that § 1182(f) vests the President with "ample power" to suspend entry whenever he finds it "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court explicitly declined to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the factual underpinnings of that finding, accepting at face value that the President had fulfilled his sole prerequisite under the statute. Consequently, the district court's preliminary injunction blocking the suspension of admissions was stayed entirely, preserving the government's ability to freeze USRAP operations indefinitely pending further findings or litigation on the merits. This aspect of *Pacito* serves as a potent reaffirmation of *Hawaii*'s legacy: unless there is evidence of bad faith beyond what can be gleaned from facial analysis, presidential proclamations restricting entry are insulated from rigorous judicial second-guessing during emergency proceedings.

However, where *Pacito* diverges sharply from pure deference is in its handling of Executive Order No. 14169 and related actions terminating funding for domestic resettlement services. Here, the court pivoted from constitutional and statutory interpretation of executive power to a strict reading of congressional commandments found in 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority reasoned that unlike § 1182(f), which grants open-ended discretion over entry, § 1522 imposes affirmative obligations on federal agencies to provide reception and placement services—including employment training, cash assistance, and medical care—to refugees who have already been admitted or are conditionally approved before a suspension takes effect. The court pointed out that Congress had appropriated billions for these purposes and that § 1522 specifically authorizes cooperation with private nonprofits to deliver these services via cooperative agreements. By noting that these funds were statutorily earmarked for specific outcomes rather than merely discretionary grants-in-aid, the Ninth Circuit concluded that terminating these agreements violated mandatory statutory directives regardless of broader executive policy goals regarding future admissions.

This distinction creates a legally precarious but logically coherent boundary between exclusion and inclusion. The majority recognized that while Congress may allow—or even encourage—the President to stop people at the door via § 1182(f), it did not intend for those same actors to abandon individuals once they cross it under § 1522. The court highlighted a critical gap in the government's execution: despite advising lower courts it was seeking new contractors for resettlement services, officials confessed at oral argument they had no concrete plan in place when they terminated existing agreements overnight. This uncertainty tipped the balance against an APA violation finding based on arbitrary and capricious action; if an agency cannot explain how it will fulfill its statutory mandate because it lacks contractors, it cannot simply walk away from its obligations under § 1522(a)(1). Thus, while staying injunctions against admission suspensions protects national security prerogatives, ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements ensures that humanitarian commitments made by Congress are not eviscerated by administrative fiat alone.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and dissent illuminates exactly where this doctrinal fault line runs deepest in American law today. Judge Lee argues that reading § 1522 as a mandatory funding scheme ignores basic principles of legislative drafting; she contends that terms like "authorized" throughout §§ 1522(b) through (e) signal permissibility rather than compulsion. From her perspective, phrases like "shall... make available" are conditioned strictly on "available appropriations," meaning Congress authorized programs but left their actual operation entirely within executive discretion if political winds shift or budgets tighten (even amidst apparent appropriation). If adopted fully, Judge Lee's view would align domestic resettlement with international aid: an optional exercise rather than an entitlement for refugees already inside U.S.-controlled territory. Her dissent suggests that allowing judges to mandate contract renewals effectively forces spending decisions upon an administration claiming budgetary constraints or policy shifts—a role she believes belongs solely to Congress or Article II powers when paired with broad immigration statutes like § 1583(a).

The downstream implications of *Pacito* could reshape how litigants challenge executive actions across multiple fronts involving conditional rights versus absolute exclusions. Future cases may see defendants attempting to decouple entry bans from subsequent welfare obligations more aggressively than ever before; conversely, plaintiffs will likely scrutinize every instance where an agency terminates service contracts mid-stream without identifying alternative mechanisms for compliance with § 1522 or similar statutes governing veterans' benefits or disaster relief where federal agencies hold fiduciary-like responsibilities toward beneficiaries already enrolled in systems created by statute rather than regulation alone.* Moreover, this split-rail approach might force agencies facing similar crises to maintain parallel operational structures: halting intake while keeping existing beneficiaries serviced through pre-existing vendor networks until new arrangements can be vetted—a logistical nightmare but potentially a legal necessity.* Ultimately, *Pacito* leaves us with a nuanced reality where presidential power remains formidable at borders but circumscribed by congressional intent once citizenship processes begin.*
