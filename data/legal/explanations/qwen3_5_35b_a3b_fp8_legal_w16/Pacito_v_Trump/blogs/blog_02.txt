Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Shall' vs. 'May': The Semantic Battle Over Mandatory Funding in *Pacito v. Trump*

The recent panel order in *Pacito v. Trump*, issued by the Ninth Circuit on September 12, 2025, may seem at first glance to be another procedural maneuver in a high-stakes immigration saga. The court vacated its previous stays and reinstated a stay pending appeal of district court injunctions blocking the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). However, buried within the majority's reasoning—and explicitly challenged in Judge Lee's sharp partial concurrence and dissent—is a fundamental clash over statutory construction that could redefine the limits of executive discretion in administering social welfare programs. At the heart of this dispute is 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Does the statute command the federal government to fund refugee resettlement services regardless of policy shifts, or does it merely authorize such funding subject to political whim?

The majority opinion treats the text "shall" as a rigid command that binds the executive branch to maintain the infrastructure of refugee resettlement, even when the President suspends new admissions via Executive Order No. 14163. Relying on specific subsections where Congress used imperative language—"shall make available," "shall develop," and "shall assume legal responsibility"—the majority concluded that § 1522 creates a mandatory duty to provide reception and placement services to those already admitted or conditionally approved. The logic follows that because Congress appropriated funds ($3.178 billion for FY 2025, albeit reduced) and enacted these mandates, the administration cannot simply terminate cooperative agreements with private nonprofits without violating the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) or overstepping its statutory authority. For the majority, the "shall" in § 1522(a) acts as a legislative straitjacket, preventing the government from hollowing out existing services while claiming broad Article II powers over entry restrictions.

Judge Lee's dissent cuts through this narrative with a starkly different reading of administrative law semantics. He argues that labeling § 1522 as an "Authorization" rather than a "Mandate" changes everything. In his view, every specific provision granting funds—whether for employment training or medical assistance—uses permissive language ("is authorized") coupled with conditions tied to "available appropriations." Lee posits that § 1522(a)'s use of "shall" does not create an independent obligation to spend money; rather, it establishes mandatory *standards* for how spending should occur *if* and *when* Congress appropriates funds and the executive chooses to run programs. By parsing the structure of the statute, Lee contends that Congress did not intend to strip the President of control over resource allocation during times of national security recalibration or budgetary realignment. To him, interpreting these provisions as binding mandates ignores the reality that without active appropriation requests or renewed contracts, there is no legal mechanism forcing execution of unrequested programs.

This semantic tug-of-war between "shall" and "authorized" carries profound doctrinal weight beyond refugee policy alone. If Judge Lee's reading prevails—or if future courts adopt his structural analysis—it significantly narrows the scope of judicial intervention in cases where agencies dissolve grant networks or terminate service providers based on shifting presidential priorities. Currently, litigants often rely on specific statutory verbs like "must" or "shall" to prove an agency has abdicated its duties unlawfully under the APA's arbitrary-and-capricious standard or through ultra vires claims. Lee's approach suggests that unless Congress explicitly ties funding mandates to unconditional obligations separate from discretionary appropriation mechanisms, agencies retain broad latitude to dismantle programs they once administered. This effectively elevates congressional silence (or ambiguous phrasing) into implicit permission for executive deregulation within complex regulatory frameworks.

Furthermore, this distinction highlights a growing friction between separation-of-powers principles in administrative law. The majority assumes that once Congress speaks in terms of mandatory service delivery, it locks in a level of entitlement for beneficiaries regardless of subsequent executive policy changes—a view that protects individual reliance interests against sudden bureaucratic pivots. Conversely, Lee's stance reinforces a more unitary-executive theory where programmatic continuity depends entirely on current political will and active fiscal management rather than static statutory commands. If adopted broadly, this interpretation could embolden administrations facing budget constraints or ideological opposition to quietly defund entire categories of federal assistance by terminating cooperative agreements and citing lack of active appropriations as justification for non-performance under seemingly mandatory statutes.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a microcosm for larger debates about how courts police executive overreach when statutes are drafted with mixed signals between authorization and compulsion. While we await a full opinion on whether Executive Order No. 14163 violates substantive immigration laws or constitutional rights, Judge Lee's dissent offers a crucial warning: semantic precision matters immensely when determining who holds the reins on America's safety net—the legislature drafting vague mandates or an executive wielding broad discretion over their implementation? Until higher courts resolve whether § 1522 is a floor beneath which funding cannot fall or merely a ceiling permitting optional generosity, lower courts will continue to navigate these treacherous waters with potentially divergent outcomes depending on how strictly they parse single words like "shall."
