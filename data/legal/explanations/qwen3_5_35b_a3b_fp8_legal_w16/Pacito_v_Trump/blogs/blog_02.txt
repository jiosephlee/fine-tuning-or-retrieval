Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The 'Shall' That Wasn't: Deconstructing Judge Lee's Dissent on Mandatory Appropriations in Refugee Resettlement

**By [Your Name/Persona]**  
*September 14, 2025*

The Supreme Court has long held that when the President acts under the broad grant of authority found in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), courts must afford him "ample power" to suspend the entry of aliens deemed detrimental to national interests. In *Pacito v. Trump*, decided today by a Ninth Circuit panel, that principle remains intact regarding the suspension of new refugee admissions. However, the more legally significant—and perhaps constitutionally fraught—battle occurred not over the border itself, but over the domestic machinery required to sustain those already inside. While the majority vacated stays on the admission moratorium, they reinstated a narrow but vital obligation: the government must resume funding cooperative agreements for reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Judge Steven Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent offers a sharp, textually driven counter-narrative that threatens to unravel this statutory floor entirely. His opinion serves as a stark reminder that in administrative law, the difference between an authorization and a mandate often turns on how strictly one reads the word "shall."

Judge Lee's dissent rests on a classic textualist premise: headers and overarching titles control statutory interpretation. He notes that § 1522 is explicitly titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement," and observes that subsections (b) through (e) repeatedly use the phrase "is authorized" when discussing specific funding streams for employment training, cash assistance, and medical support. For Lee, this linguistic pattern suggests Congress intended to give the executive branch discretion to create—or dismantle—these programs based on budgetary whims or policy shifts. He argues that even where subsection (a) uses "shall," it is merely setting conditions *if* the government chooses to act, rather than commanding action itself. Furthermore, he highlights the qualifier "to the extent of available appropriations," interpreting this not as a constraint on *whether* funds are spent, but as a limitation on *how much*. In his view, if Congress wanted to guarantee funding regardless of appropriation levels or political winds, it would have used stronger imperative language stripped of such qualifiers.

This reading creates a fascinating tension with the majority's approach. The majority leans heavily on specific operative verbs within subsections (d) and (e). Subsection (d)(2)(B) mandates that during an interim period for unaccompanied minors, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility... if necessary." Similarly, (e)(7)(A) directs that "the Secretary shall develop and implement alternative projects... as needed." The majority treats these commands as independent obligations triggered by circumstance ("if necessary," "as needed"), rather than preconditions dependent on prior legislative approval of a specific program structure. By contrast, Judge Lee views these clauses as embedded within a broader permissive scheme where the executive retains ultimate veto power over whether any program exists at all. If Lee's interpretation holds sway in future litigation or appeals to the Supreme Court, it implies that once Congress appropriates money for refugee services but fails to pass legislation mandating their distribution, those funds could theoretically sit idle without violating § 1522's terms.

The practical stakes of this debate extend far beyond semantic hair-splitting; they touch upon fundamental questions about separation of powers and humanitarian safety nets. If § 1522 is merely an authorization, as Judge Lee posits, then there is no statutory check against a scenario where an administration admits refugees under one pretext but refuses to fund their basic survival needs because it deems such expenditures unnecessary or politically inconvenient. Currently, Congress has appropriated billions for migration assistance in FY 2025; yet under Lee's logic, unless Congress passes another statute specifically ordering those funds be distributed via cooperative agreements with NGOs like those in *Pacito*, the executive branch could legally terminate all such agreements and leave newly arrived refugees without housing or medical care. This raises a profound question: Does statutory silence regarding *implementation* equate to statutory permission for abandonment?

Lee's dissent also exposes a vulnerability in relying solely on context-dependent commands like "as needed" or "if necessary." While these phrases suggest flexibility in execution, they presuppose an underlying duty to execute at all. A purely permissive reading risks turning humanitarian statutes into mere suggestions—a dangerous precedent in immigration law where life-and-death consequences hinge on bureaucratic compliance. However, one must concede that Lee correctly identifies ambiguity in how "available appropriations" interacts with mandatory duties. If appropriations are insufficient to meet every need described in § 1522(e), does the statute collapse entirely? Or does it simply scale back? The majority sidesteps this by focusing on recent history where adequate funds existed but were withheld due to policy choices; Lee argues that if those funds ever dry up again due to rescission acts or new laws (like Pub. L. No. 119-28 mentioned in the background), his reading would absolve the government of liability for failing to provide services even if they had previously committed to them via contract.

Ultimately, Judge Lee's dissent forces us to confront whether Congress has truly spoken clearly enough when it comes time to pay for human lives versus when it speaks about who may enter our borders. The majority sees § 1522 as a binding promise made by Congress that cannot be unilaterally revoked by executive fiat once conditions are met; Lee sees it as an open-ended invitation subject to executive discretion until formally constrained by subsequent legislation or explicit appropriation mandates tied directly to service delivery requirements without escape hatches like "authorized." As *Pacito* moves forward toward a merits opinion expected soon from Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson or her successor at SCOTUS following potential certiorari petitions next term, this divide will likely determine whether refugee resettlement remains a guaranteed right conditioned only on availability of funds—or becomes entirely optional depending on whose hand holds the pen today. Until then, refugees admitted under current policies remain caught between two competing visions of American statutory intent: one demanding continuity of care despite shifting tides of politics, and another permitting those tides to wash away existing commitments entirely unless explicitly forbidden by clearer text than currently exists in § 1522's headers and modifiers alone.
