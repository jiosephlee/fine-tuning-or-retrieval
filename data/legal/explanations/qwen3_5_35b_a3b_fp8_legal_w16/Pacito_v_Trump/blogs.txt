Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Deference vs. Duty: How *Pacito* Reaffirms Presidential Immigration Power While Carving Out Statutory Service Obligations

The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a masterclass in judicial compartmentalization, drawing a sharp line between the President’s plenary power to exclude aliens and the statutory duties Congress has imposed once those aliens enter. Issued on September 12, 2025, the panel order vacates previous stays and reinstates a limited stay pending appeal, creating a "split-rail" outcome that simultaneously validates the President's sweeping authority to halt refugee admissions while mandating the continuation of domestic resettlement services for those already admitted. The decision underscores a fundamental tension in modern immigration jurisprudence: while courts may grant near-total deference to executive determinations regarding border security under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), they retain the power—and perhaps the duty—to enforce congressional mandates regarding the treatment of individuals already within the nation's borders under statutes like 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

At its core, the majority's reasoning relies heavily on the precedent set in *Trump v. Hawaii*. When addressing Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended all entries into the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), the court applied a standard of extreme deference. Citing *Hawaii*, the panel noted that § 1182(f) vests the President with "ample power" to suspend entry whenever he finds it "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court explicitly declined to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the factual underpinnings of that finding, accepting at face value that the President had fulfilled his sole prerequisite under the statute. Consequently, the district court's preliminary injunction blocking the suspension of admissions was stayed entirely, preserving the government's ability to freeze USRAP operations indefinitely pending further findings or litigation on the merits. This aspect of *Pacito* serves as a potent reaffirmation of *Hawaii*'s legacy: unless there is evidence of bad faith beyond what can be gleaned from facial analysis, presidential proclamations restricting entry are insulated from rigorous judicial second-guessing during emergency proceedings.

However, where *Pacito* diverges sharply from pure deference is in its handling of Executive Order No. 14169 and related actions terminating funding for domestic resettlement services. Here, the court pivoted from constitutional and statutory interpretation of executive power to a strict reading of congressional commandments found in 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority reasoned that unlike § 1182(f), which grants open-ended discretion over entry, § 1522 imposes affirmative obligations on federal agencies to provide reception and placement services—including employment training, cash assistance, and medical care—to refugees who have already been admitted or are conditionally approved before a suspension takes effect. The court pointed out that Congress had appropriated billions for these purposes and that § 1522 specifically authorizes cooperation with private nonprofits to deliver these services via cooperative agreements. By noting that these funds were statutorily earmarked for specific outcomes rather than merely discretionary grants-in-aid, the Ninth Circuit concluded that terminating these agreements violated mandatory statutory directives regardless of broader executive policy goals regarding future admissions.

This distinction creates a legally precarious but logically coherent boundary between exclusion and inclusion. The majority recognized that while Congress may allow—or even encourage—the President to stop people at the door via § 1182(f), it did not intend for those same actors to abandon individuals once they cross it under § 1522. The court highlighted a critical gap in the government's execution: despite advising lower courts it was seeking new contractors for resettlement services, officials confessed at oral argument they had no concrete plan in place when they terminated existing agreements overnight. This uncertainty tipped the balance against an APA violation finding based on arbitrary and capricious action; if an agency cannot explain how it will fulfill its statutory mandate because it lacks contractors, it cannot simply walk away from its obligations under § 1522(a)(1). Thus, while staying injunctions against admission suspensions protects national security prerogatives, ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements ensures that humanitarian commitments made by Congress are not eviscerated by administrative fiat alone.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and dissent illuminates exactly where this doctrinal fault line runs deepest in American law today. Judge Lee argues that reading § 1522 as a mandatory funding scheme ignores basic principles of legislative drafting; she contends that terms like "authorized" throughout §§ 1522(b) through (e) signal permissibility rather than compulsion. From her perspective, phrases like "shall... make available" are conditioned strictly on "available appropriations," meaning Congress authorized programs but left their actual operation entirely within executive discretion if political winds shift or budgets tighten (even amidst apparent appropriation). If adopted fully, Judge Lee's view would align domestic resettlement with international aid: an optional exercise rather than an entitlement for refugees already inside U.S.-controlled territory. Her dissent suggests that allowing judges to mandate contract renewals effectively forces spending decisions upon an administration claiming budgetary constraints or policy shifts—a role she believes belongs solely to Congress or Article II powers when paired with broad immigration statutes like § 1583(a).

The downstream implications of *Pacito* could reshape how litigants challenge executive actions across multiple fronts involving conditional rights versus absolute exclusions. Future cases may see defendants attempting to decouple entry bans from subsequent welfare obligations more aggressively than ever before; conversely, plaintiffs will likely scrutinize every instance where an agency terminates service contracts mid-stream without identifying alternative mechanisms for compliance with § 1522 or similar statutes governing veterans' benefits or disaster relief where federal agencies hold fiduciary-like responsibilities toward beneficiaries already enrolled in systems created by statute rather than regulation alone.* Moreover, this split-rail approach might force agencies facing similar crises to maintain parallel operational structures: halting intake while keeping existing beneficiaries serviced through pre-existing vendor networks until new arrangements can be vetted—a logistical nightmare but potentially a legal necessity.* Ultimately, *Pacito* leaves us with a nuanced reality where presidential power remains formidable at borders but circumscribed by congressional intent once citizenship processes begin.*



# The 'Shall' That Wasn't: Deconstructing Judge Lee's Dissent on Mandatory Appropriations in Refugee Resettlement

**By [Your Name/Persona]**  
*September 14, 2025*

The Supreme Court has long held that when the President acts under the broad grant of authority found in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), courts must afford him "ample power" to suspend the entry of aliens deemed detrimental to national interests. In *Pacito v. Trump*, decided today by a Ninth Circuit panel, that principle remains intact regarding the suspension of new refugee admissions. However, the more legally significant—and perhaps constitutionally fraught—battle occurred not over the border itself, but over the domestic machinery required to sustain those already inside. While the majority vacated stays on the admission moratorium, they reinstated a narrow but vital obligation: the government must resume funding cooperative agreements for reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Judge Steven Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent offers a sharp, textually driven counter-narrative that threatens to unravel this statutory floor entirely. His opinion serves as a stark reminder that in administrative law, the difference between an authorization and a mandate often turns on how strictly one reads the word "shall."

Judge Lee's dissent rests on a classic textualist premise: headers and overarching titles control statutory interpretation. He notes that § 1522 is explicitly titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement," and observes that subsections (b) through (e) repeatedly use the phrase "is authorized" when discussing specific funding streams for employment training, cash assistance, and medical support. For Lee, this linguistic pattern suggests Congress intended to give the executive branch discretion to create—or dismantle—these programs based on budgetary whims or policy shifts. He argues that even where subsection (a) uses "shall," it is merely setting conditions *if* the government chooses to act, rather than commanding action itself. Furthermore, he highlights the qualifier "to the extent of available appropriations," interpreting this not as a constraint on *whether* funds are spent, but as a limitation on *how much*. In his view, if Congress wanted to guarantee funding regardless of appropriation levels or political winds, it would have used stronger imperative language stripped of such qualifiers.

This reading creates a fascinating tension with the majority's approach. The majority leans heavily on specific operative verbs within subsections (d) and (e). Subsection (d)(2)(B) mandates that during an interim period for unaccompanied minors, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility... if necessary." Similarly, (e)(7)(A) directs that "the Secretary shall develop and implement alternative projects... as needed." The majority treats these commands as independent obligations triggered by circumstance ("if necessary," "as needed"), rather than preconditions dependent on prior legislative approval of a specific program structure. By contrast, Judge Lee views these clauses as embedded within a broader permissive scheme where the executive retains ultimate veto power over whether any program exists at all. If Lee's interpretation holds sway in future litigation or appeals to the Supreme Court, it implies that once Congress appropriates money for refugee services but fails to pass legislation mandating their distribution, those funds could theoretically sit idle without violating § 1522's terms.

The practical stakes of this debate extend far beyond semantic hair-splitting; they touch upon fundamental questions about separation of powers and humanitarian safety nets. If § 1522 is merely an authorization, as Judge Lee posits, then there is no statutory check against a scenario where an administration admits refugees under one pretext but refuses to fund their basic survival needs because it deems such expenditures unnecessary or politically inconvenient. Currently, Congress has appropriated billions for migration assistance in FY 2025; yet under Lee's logic, unless Congress passes another statute specifically ordering those funds be distributed via cooperative agreements with NGOs like those in *Pacito*, the executive branch could legally terminate all such agreements and leave newly arrived refugees without housing or medical care. This raises a profound question: Does statutory silence regarding *implementation* equate to statutory permission for abandonment?

Lee's dissent also exposes a vulnerability in relying solely on context-dependent commands like "as needed" or "if necessary." While these phrases suggest flexibility in execution, they presuppose an underlying duty to execute at all. A purely permissive reading risks turning humanitarian statutes into mere suggestions—a dangerous precedent in immigration law where life-and-death consequences hinge on bureaucratic compliance. However, one must concede that Lee correctly identifies ambiguity in how "available appropriations" interacts with mandatory duties. If appropriations are insufficient to meet every need described in § 1522(e), does the statute collapse entirely? Or does it simply scale back? The majority sidesteps this by focusing on recent history where adequate funds existed but were withheld due to policy choices; Lee argues that if those funds ever dry up again due to rescission acts or new laws (like Pub. L. No. 119-28 mentioned in the background), his reading would absolve the government of liability for failing to provide services even if they had previously committed to them via contract.

Ultimately, Judge Lee's dissent forces us to confront whether Congress has truly spoken clearly enough when it comes time to pay for human lives versus when it speaks about who may enter our borders. The majority sees § 1522 as a binding promise made by Congress that cannot be unilaterally revoked by executive fiat once conditions are met; Lee sees it as an open-ended invitation subject to executive discretion until formally constrained by subsequent legislation or explicit appropriation mandates tied directly to service delivery requirements without escape hatches like "authorized." As *Pacito* moves forward toward a merits opinion expected soon from Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson or her successor at SCOTUS following potential certiorari petitions next term, this divide will likely determine whether refugee resettlement remains a guaranteed right conditioned only on availability of funds—or becomes entirely optional depending on whose hand holds the pen today. Until then, refugees admitted under current policies remain caught between two competing visions of American statutory intent: one demanding continuity of care despite shifting tides of politics, and another permitting those tides to wash away existing commitments entirely unless explicitly forbidden by clearer text than currently exists in § 1522's headers and modifiers alone.



# A Stay That Isn't a Stay: How the Ninth Circuit Forced a Policy Pivot in *Pacito*

**By [Your Name/Staff]**

The landscape of immigration litigation is often defined by the tension between presidential prerogative and statutory obligation, but the Ninth Circuit's recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* introduces a peculiar procedural hybrid that blurs the lines between a stay pending appeal and a final judgment on the merits. While the court ostensibly paused the entire machinery of the district court's preliminary injunctions, it simultaneously issued an order that functionally dismantles the core of the President's refugee policy shift regarding domestic services. By vacating its own prior stays while mandating the immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements, the panel has created a "stay" that operates more like an interlocutory decree, raising urgent questions about when appellate courts can—and should—intervene in complex administrative realities without waiting for full briefing on the underlying facts.

At first glance, *Pacito* appears to be a victory for executive authority. The majority explicitly reaffirmed the broad deference owed to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), citing *Trump v. Hawaii*. The court found that President Trump's determination that refugee entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States" satisfied the sole statutory prerequisite for suspending admissions under Executive Order No. 14163. Consequently, the Ninth Circuit stayed all district court injunctions preventing this suspension, signaling that on this fundamental question of national security and border control, the government is likely to succeed on appeal. This portion of the ruling aligns with decades of precedent treating immigration powers as largely immune from judicial second-guessing once a formal presidential finding is made.

However, the order fractures precisely where it attempts to address what happens to refugees already inside American borders or those whose funding streams have been severed. Here, the majority pivots sharply away from deference toward strict statutory construction of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court reasoned that while Congress may authorize executive action regarding admissions, it mandates specific reception and placement services for those admitted under existing cooperative agreements. Noting that State Department officials admitted at oral argument they were "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency," the panel concluded that halting these services would violate statutory duties regardless of appropriations reductions or executive preference. Thus, while staying the ban on new entries, the court directed an immediate reinstatement of pre-existing cooperative agreements to ensure continuity of care for current residents and incoming minors.

This creates a striking procedural paradox: The Ninth Circuit stayed its own prior stays—which had allowed some carveouts for conditionally approved applicants—but replaced them with a directive that effectively re-enforces parts of what it just said was being stayed in terms of broader policy impact. The result is an interim legal reality where new refugee flows are frozen by presidential fiat, yet existing infrastructure must be propped up by judicial command because agency leadership confessed ignorance about how to fulfill their duties otherwise. Judge Lee's partial dissent captures this friction perfectly; she argues that § 1522 is merely an authorization scheme rather than a mandatory command, suggesting that by forcing reinstatement based on "uncertainty," the majority has usurped executive discretion over resource allocation and program design before even hearing arguments on whether Congress actually compelled these specific expenditures in FY 2025 given recent rescissions acts.

The doctrinal implications of this maneuver are significant and potentially destabilizing for administrative law jurisprudence. Traditionally, appellate courts grant stays pending appeal to preserve the status quo until they can fully digest complex factual records and legal arguments presented in briefs spanning months or years. Yet here, relying heavily on statements made during oral argument regarding agency confusion rather than developed evidentiary records or comprehensive statutory history involving budgetary constraints, the Ninth Circuit imposed affirmative relief against an active administration mid-litigation. This risks establishing a precedent where appellate panels can short-circuit standard review processes simply by identifying perceived gaps in agency planning or confidence during oral proceedings. If future administrations face similar scrutiny over programmatic transitions during appeals, they may find themselves subject to ad hoc judicial management of their operations before their constitutional defenses have been fully aired on merit-based grounds.

Furthermore, this approach highlights a growing trend where courts intervene in executive administration not just to halt illegal actions but to actively manage bureaucratic continuity when political winds shift abruptly during litigation cycles. By tying relief directly to statements made at oral argument ("we advise... we are unaware"), the court blurred its role from neutral arbiter to operational overseer of federal resettlement logistics—a task ill-suited for emergency stay proceedings designed primarily for preserving rights against imminent harm rather than orchestrating agency compliance strategies amidst shifting funding landscapes. While protecting vulnerable refugees from sudden service cutoffs is undeniably compelling morally and legally under certain readings of § 1522(a), doing so via emergency stay mechanics sidesteps crucial questions about appropriation limits and congressional intent behind recent budget cuts resolved only through summary orders lacking full adversarial testing on these specific fiscal constraints.

Ultimately, *Pacito* stands as a cautionary tale about the dangers of mixing substantive statutory interpretation with emergency equitable relief when agencies are caught off guard by rapid policy shifts pending appeal. The Ninth Circuit succeeded in creating stability for currently present refugees while acknowledging presidential power over future admissions; yet in doing so via this novel hybrid mechanism, it may have inadvertently lowered barriers for judicial intrusion into day-to-day executive administration whenever litigation exposes administrative uncertainty or hesitation during transition periods
