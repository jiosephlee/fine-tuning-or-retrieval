# The Two-Tiered Stay: How *Pacito* Distinguishes Presidential Power Over Entry From Statutory Duties to Serve Refugees

The Ninth Circuit's September 12, 2025, order in *Pacito v. Trump* represents a fascinating procedural hybrid that splits the difference between extreme executive deference and rigid statutory adherence. While the court broadly affirmed the President's sweeping authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), citing the near-plenary power recognized in *Trump v. Hawaii*, it simultaneously pulled back the curtain on the administration's attempt to gut domestic refugee support networks. By staying the district court's injunctions regarding the suspension of admissions but refusing to stay orders forcing the reinstatement of funding for resettlement services, the panel created a "two-tiered" relief regime. This distinction is crucial: it suggests that while the President controls who enters the United States, he cannot easily override Congress's specific mandates for how those already inside are treated once they arrive.

The court's reasoning begins with a straightforward application of precedent regarding national sovereignty and immigration control. Relying heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel concluded that Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended entry into the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), falls squarely within the President's discretion under § 1182(f). The standard for judicial review here is minimal; the court noted it cannot engage in a "searching inquiry" into the President's justifications as long as he makes a finding that entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Given that this threshold finding appears to have been satisfied, and considering the "broad discretion" inherent in foreign affairs and border security, the government was deemed likely to succeed on this prong of its appeal. Consequently, the stay pending appeal covers this aspect entirely, halting any judicial interference with the suspension of new admissions.

However, the analysis takes a sharp turn when addressing the termination of cooperative agreements responsible for receiving and placing refugees already admitted or approved for resettlement domestically. Here, the court pivots from constitutional deference to statutory interpretation under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Unlike § 1182(f), which grants open-ended presidential power, § 1522 imposes affirmative duties on federal agencies to provide reception and placement services—including employment training, cash assistance, and medical support—to refugees upon their arrival. The Ninth Circuit observed that despite terminating these contracts in February 2025 without clear evidence of a viable replacement plan, neither Congress nor existing appropriations laws had explicitly repealed these statutory obligations. The government admitted during oral argument it was unsure if it could secure new providers soon enough to meet these deadlines. Because there appeared to be no "strong showing" that the government would prevail against claims that its actions violated these mandatory service requirements under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), a stay was inappropriate for this specific subset of injuries.

This bifurcated approach highlights a significant doctrinal boundary line in administrative law: deference ends where statutory command begins. While courts traditionally grant wide berth to executive decisions regarding admissibility—a domain deeply intertwined with national security—they are far more skeptical when an agency attempts to ignore congressionally mandated welfare provisions once an individual crosses that border. Judge Lee's partial concurrence and dissent underscores this tension; she argues that reading § 1522 as a mandatory funding stream ignores its titular designation as an "authorization" and conflates conditions for spending with obligations to spend regardless of appropriation status or availability. Yet, for now, nine justices have signaled that even if § 1522 contains elements of discretion, it does not grant an unfettered right to abandon statutory responsibilities overnight without notice-and-comment procedures or valid justification under APA standards.

The downstream effects of *Pacito* could reshape how litigation strategies are deployed against rapid-fire executive actions involving humanitarian aid versus border closure. Litigants may find themselves better positioned challenging terminations of social safety nets than suspensions of entry itself, precisely because statutes like § 1522 create clearer hooks for judicial review than vague proclamations of detriment to national interests. Furthermore, this order forces an immediate practical reckoning: while new refugees can be kept out based on presidential whim today, those currently processing or newly arrived retain a statutory right to assistance unless Congress acts otherwise or courts eventually rule differently on remand after reviewing APA compliance in detail. For now, however, *Pacito* ensures that while America may close its doors more tightly tomorrow morning via executive fiat, it cannot legally leave yesterday's arrivals hanging without a plan tomorrow night unless it clears higher hurdles than simply asserting national interest alone might suggest proving insufficient?



# Shall' vs. 'May': The Semantic Battle Over Mandatory Funding in *Pacito v. Trump*

The recent panel order in *Pacito v. Trump*, issued by the Ninth Circuit on September 12, 2025, may seem at first glance to be another procedural maneuver in a high-stakes immigration saga. The court vacated its previous stays and reinstated a stay pending appeal of district court injunctions blocking the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). However, buried within the majority's reasoning—and explicitly challenged in Judge Lee's sharp partial concurrence and dissent—is a fundamental clash over statutory construction that could redefine the limits of executive discretion in administering social welfare programs. At the heart of this dispute is 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Does the statute command the federal government to fund refugee resettlement services regardless of policy shifts, or does it merely authorize such funding subject to political whim?

The majority opinion treats the text "shall" as a rigid command that binds the executive branch to maintain the infrastructure of refugee resettlement, even when the President suspends new admissions via Executive Order No. 14163. Relying on specific subsections where Congress used imperative language—"shall make available," "shall develop," and "shall assume legal responsibility"—the majority concluded that § 1522 creates a mandatory duty to provide reception and placement services to those already admitted or conditionally approved. The logic follows that because Congress appropriated funds ($3.178 billion for FY 2025, albeit reduced) and enacted these mandates, the administration cannot simply terminate cooperative agreements with private nonprofits without violating the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) or overstepping its statutory authority. For the majority, the "shall" in § 1522(a) acts as a legislative straitjacket, preventing the government from hollowing out existing services while claiming broad Article II powers over entry restrictions.

Judge Lee's dissent cuts through this narrative with a starkly different reading of administrative law semantics. He argues that labeling § 1522 as an "Authorization" rather than a "Mandate" changes everything. In his view, every specific provision granting funds—whether for employment training or medical assistance—uses permissive language ("is authorized") coupled with conditions tied to "available appropriations." Lee posits that § 1522(a)'s use of "shall" does not create an independent obligation to spend money; rather, it establishes mandatory *standards* for how spending should occur *if* and *when* Congress appropriates funds and the executive chooses to run programs. By parsing the structure of the statute, Lee contends that Congress did not intend to strip the President of control over resource allocation during times of national security recalibration or budgetary realignment. To him, interpreting these provisions as binding mandates ignores the reality that without active appropriation requests or renewed contracts, there is no legal mechanism forcing execution of unrequested programs.

This semantic tug-of-war between "shall" and "authorized" carries profound doctrinal weight beyond refugee policy alone. If Judge Lee's reading prevails—or if future courts adopt his structural analysis—it significantly narrows the scope of judicial intervention in cases where agencies dissolve grant networks or terminate service providers based on shifting presidential priorities. Currently, litigants often rely on specific statutory verbs like "must" or "shall" to prove an agency has abdicated its duties unlawfully under the APA's arbitrary-and-capricious standard or through ultra vires claims. Lee's approach suggests that unless Congress explicitly ties funding mandates to unconditional obligations separate from discretionary appropriation mechanisms, agencies retain broad latitude to dismantle programs they once administered. This effectively elevates congressional silence (or ambiguous phrasing) into implicit permission for executive deregulation within complex regulatory frameworks.

Furthermore, this distinction highlights a growing friction between separation-of-powers principles in administrative law. The majority assumes that once Congress speaks in terms of mandatory service delivery, it locks in a level of entitlement for beneficiaries regardless of subsequent executive policy changes—a view that protects individual reliance interests against sudden bureaucratic pivots. Conversely, Lee's stance reinforces a more unitary-executive theory where programmatic continuity depends entirely on current political will and active fiscal management rather than static statutory commands. If adopted broadly, this interpretation could embolden administrations facing budget constraints or ideological opposition to quietly defund entire categories of federal assistance by terminating cooperative agreements and citing lack of active appropriations as justification for non-performance under seemingly mandatory statutes.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a microcosm for larger debates about how courts police executive overreach when statutes are drafted with mixed signals between authorization and compulsion. While we await a full opinion on whether Executive Order No. 14163 violates substantive immigration laws or constitutional rights, Judge Lee's dissent offers a crucial warning: semantic precision matters immensely when determining who holds the reins on America's safety net—the legislature drafting vague mandates or an executive wielding broad discretion over their implementation? Until higher courts resolve whether § 1522 is a floor beneath which funding cannot fall or merely a ceiling permitting optional generosity, lower courts will continue to navigate these treacherous waters with potentially divergent outcomes depending on how strictly they parse single words like "shall."



# A Stay That Stays the Status Quo: The Ninth Circuit's Unusual Path in *Pacito v. Trump*

**By Legal Analyst**

The procedural posture of *Pacito v. Trump*, decided by the Ninth Circuit on September 12, 2025, presents a rare judicial paradox: a court ordering the reinstatement of a government program it has not yet definitively ruled is lawful, simply because it believes the government might lose on that specific point during a future merits hearing. While the bulk of the panel majority's reasoning leans heavily on precedent granting the President broad deference under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, the order creates a distinct exception for domestic resettlement services. By staying the district court's injunctions entirely except to force the restoration of cooperative agreements for receiving refugees already admitted, the Ninth Circuit has effectively prioritized the "irreparable injury" prong of the *Nken v. Holder* standard over its own hesitation to declare the underlying statute mandatory.

This approach marks a significant departure from typical appellate practice, where stays pending appeal usually maintain the status quo ante—leaving lower court injunctions in place until a final decision is rendered. Here, rather than waiting weeks or months for a full merits opinion on whether Congress compelled funding under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the panel issued an immediate directive: reinstate the contracts. The logic rests squarely on the fourth factor of *Nken*: where does the public interest lie? The judges reasoned that while they could not "engage in a searching inquiry" into the President's justification for halting new entries—a task foreclosed by *Trump v. Hawaii*'s deferential standard—they could not ignore what appeared to be an imminent collapse of domestic support networks for refugees already inside U.S. borders. The order essentially treats the cessation of these services not merely as an administrative dispute, but as an active crisis requiring immediate remedial action before a final judgment can be reached.

The crux of this interim relief lies in how the panel interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1522 versus § 1182(f). On one hand, citing *Hawaii*, the majority held that § 1182(f) vests "ample power" in the President to suspend entry whenever he finds it "detrimental," making it unlikely plaintiffs would succeed in challenging Executive Order No. 14163's ban on new admissions. However, when turning to domestic aid, specifically Section 1522's mandates for "reception and placement" services like cash assistance and medical support for recent arrivals, the calculus shifted. The panel noted that while Congress appropriated billions for these programs in Fiscal Year 2025, including funds reduced by subsequent rescission acts, Section 1523 requires strict reporting and accountability from HHS officials regarding these very activities.

Faced with evidence that State Department officials were unable to confirm whether they had secured replacement contractors to fulfill these statutory duties—a stark admission during oral argument—the court found that denying relief would cause irreparable harm to vulnerable populations dependent on these lifelines. The distinction drawn between "entry" (an area of plenary executive power) and "domestic support" (an area where statutory text appears more prescriptive) allowed the court to carve out a narrow window of protection without deciding whether Congress actually stripped HHS of its discretion entirely. It is a tactical maneuver that acknowledges statutory ambiguity while refusing to let that ambiguity result in human suffering while litigation drags on.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent illuminates exactly why this move feels legally precarious yet practically necessary. Lee argues that reading § 1522 as creating an absolute mandate ignores its structure; she contends that terms like "authorized" and phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations" signal permissive rather than obligatory intent for Congress. In her view, if Section 1522 is merely an authorization scheme allowing HHS to craft programs when funds exist, then courts lack jurisdiction to compel their existence absent explicit congressional command to spend regardless of executive preference or budgetary shifts. Her dissent suggests that by forcing reinstatement now, the majority may be usurping legislative intent by treating discretionary authority as binding obligation before fully parsing the text against historical practice and appropriation laws.

Doctrinally, this order sets a fascinating stage for what follows in any eventual merits opinion regarding administrative law and immigration statutes alike. If upheld upon final review, it reinforces a growing trend where appellate courts use stay proceedings not just as gatekeepers for temporary relief but as tools to manage complex administrative realities where delay itself constitutes injury—particularly involving basic subsistence needs like housing and food assistance for displaced persons. Conversely, should a future opinion reject Lee's textualist reading and affirm Section 1522 as purely discretionary or subject to broader executive control over foreign aid budgets (via Executive Order No. 14169), this interim reinstatement could appear as judicial overreach born of sympathy rather than statutory fidelity. Yet even if ultimately overturned on merit grounds after months of chaos for resettlement agencies and families alike, today's order serves as a powerful reminder: sometimes procedural speed is required precisely because constitutional deference cannot answer questions about human welfare fast enough for life-and-death circumstances to wait out an appellate schedule.
