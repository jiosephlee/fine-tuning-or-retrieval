Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Analysis of Presidential Authority over Entry Restrictions (§ 1182(f))

This chapter examines the Ninth Circuit's analysis in *Pacito v. Trump* regarding the President's authority to suspend the entry of refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The central issue addressed by the appellate panel is whether the government demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits sufficient to justify staying the district court's preliminary injunction against Executive Order No. 14163. This analysis serves as a foundational lesson in how courts apply the standard for stays pending appeal and, more specifically, how they defer to presidential authority in matters of national security and immigration under precedent set by *Trump v. Hawaii*.

### The Standard for Review under *Nken v. Holder*: Success on Merits Factor One

To obtain a stay pending appeal, a party must satisfy four factors outlined in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009): (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm absent a stay; (3) substantial injury to opposing parties if a stay is granted; and (4) alignment with the public interest. The Ninth Circuit emphasized that while all four factors matter, "the first two—the showing of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are 'the most critical.'" In *Pacito*, the court focused primarily on the first factor because, without a strong showing that the government would ultimately win its defense of Executive Order No. 14163, there would be no need to weigh the remaining equities.

The court did not engage in a de novo review of the constitutional questions surrounding executive power but rather applied a highly deferential lens required by Supreme Court precedent. The question was not whether the court agreed with the President's policy choice, but whether that choice fell within the statutory boundaries set by Congress and respected by previous judicial interpretations. By framing the analysis around "likelihood of success," the court signaled that it was assessing whether any reasonable interpretation of § 1182(f) would support the Executive Order, rather than re-litigating the wisdom of suspending refugee admissions entirely.

### 'Exuding Deference': Applying *Trump v. Hawaii* to § 1182(f)*

The Ninth Circuit anchored its reasoning directly in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), noting that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." This language indicates that when Congress delegates authority to suspend entry based on a presidential finding, it intends for courts to grant significant weight to that determination. In *Pacito*, this principle meant that the judiciary's role was limited to ensuring procedural compliance with § 1182(f)'s text, rather than acting as a super-legislature evaluating the factual accuracy or political prudence of those findings.

The court rejected any notion that it could conduct a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Instead, it treated § 1182(f) as granting broad discretion: whenever the President finds that entering aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests, he may suspend their entry for whatever period he deems necessary or impose appropriate restrictions. Because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contained such a finding—that refugee entry via USRAP was detrimental—the court found no legal barrier preventing its enforcement under this statute alone. This approach underscores a key tenet of administrative law: when statutes delegate power broadly and conditionally upon an executive finding, courts generally accept those findings unless they are facially invalid or procedurally defective in ways unrelated to their substantive truthfulness.
