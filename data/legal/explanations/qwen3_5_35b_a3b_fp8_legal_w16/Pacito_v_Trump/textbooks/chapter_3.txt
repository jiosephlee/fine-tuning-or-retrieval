Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # Presidential Deference in Immigration Control (The First Prong)

In addressing the government's motion for a stay pending appeal in *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit panel first turned to the threshold question of whether the government demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. This analysis focused exclusively on Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended entry into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) based on a determination that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court's reasoning in this portion of the order serves as a potent illustration of the extreme deference afforded to the President in matters of immigration control, relying heavily on precedent established in *Trump v. Hawaii*. By applying this standard, the Ninth Circuit concluded that the government was likely to prevail because Executive Order No. 14163 fell squarely within the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), rendering judicial review virtually nonexistent regarding the substantive justification for the suspension.

The core of the court's reasoning rests on the application of *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which dictates that statutes governing presidential immigration powers "exude[d] deference" to the Executive Branch. The panel emphasized that § 1182(f) vests "ample power" in the President to impose entry restrictions that go beyond those explicitly enumerated elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Specifically, the statute provides that whenever the President finds that "the entry of any aliens... would be detrimental to the interest[s] of the United States," he may suspend their entry for such period as he deems necessary. The Ninth Circuit treated this statutory scheme as granting broad discretion, noting that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear" and does not exceed textual limits on presidential authority. Consequently, when reviewing a challenge to such an exercise of power, courts are precluded from engaging in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Instead, judicial scrutiny is confined to verifying whether a formal finding was made; if such a finding exists, it is generally conclusive for purposes of establishing jurisdiction and authority.

This approach necessitates a strict reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) through a lens of textualism that prioritizes presidential autonomy over evidentiary requirements typically found in administrative law cases involving other agencies. Unlike many administrative actions subject to arbitrary-and-capricious review under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), where an agency must produce evidence supporting its factual predicates, § 1182(f) imposes only one explicit prerequisite: that the President "find[]" that entry would be detrimental. In *Pacito*, because President Trump had formally declared via Executive Order No. 14163 that refugee entry was indeed detrimental to U.S. interests, his action satisfied this sole statutory condition. The court declined to examine whether there was empirical data or specific intelligence backing this declaration during this interlocutory phase. The existence of a facially valid finding by an official acting within his designated capacity was deemed sufficient to insulate the order from immediate judicial invalidation, reflecting a doctrine where political questions regarding national security and border control are reserved almost entirely for political actors rather than judges.

Furthermore, the concept of "detrimental interests" operates as a self-executing trigger within this framework rather than an objective standard open to rigorous debate by litigants or lower courts. The Ninth Circuit noted that once a finding is made, courts lack standing or authority to second-guess whether those interests are actually being harmed or what constitutes harm enough to warrant suspension. This refusal to probe deeper ensures that executive judgments concerning foreign relations and national safety remain insulated from litigation tactics designed to delay or undermine policy shifts during periods of transition between administrations or crises in international affairs. By characterizing these determinations as matters falling within "broad discretion," the panel signaled that unless Congress has explicitly stripped away this power—a burden not met here—the President retains unilateral authority to halt classes of entrants based on his own assessment of national welfare.
