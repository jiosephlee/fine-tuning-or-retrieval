Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Factual Background and Statutory Framework

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* arises from a dual-pronged executive action taken in early 2025 that fundamentally altered the United States' approach to its refugee system. The factual landscape is defined by two distinct Executive Orders issued by President Trump shortly after his inauguration on January 20, 2025. These orders triggered a cascade of administrative actions and judicial interventions, creating a complex procedural history centered on the tension between presidential immigration authority and statutory obligations to support refugees already present in the country. Understanding the specific mechanics of these orders and the conflicting statutory regimes they implicate is essential to grasping the appellate court's eventual split holding regarding stays pending appeal.

## Executive Order No. 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions under § 1182(f)

The primary driver of the controversy was Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program," issued on January 20, 2025, and published in the Federal Register on January 30. This order relied explicitly on the authority granted to the President by Section 1182(f) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That statute provides that whenever the President finds that "the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States," he may suspend their entry by proclamation for such period as he deems necessary.

Under Executive Order No. 14163, § 3(a), President Trump made a formal finding that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Based on this finding, he directed that entry into the USRAP be suspended pending further findings. The text of Section 1182(f) is notably broad, allowing for restrictions on both immigrants and non-immigrants without explicit temporal limits or substantive criteria beyond the presidential determination of detriment to national interest. In subsequent proceedings, including oral arguments before this court, reliance was placed heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, where the Supreme Court emphasized that Section 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with ample power to impose entry restrictions beyond those otherwise enumerated in the INA. The government argued that because Section 1182(f) contains only a single prerequisite—that the President make a finding—the order did not exceed textual limits on presidential authority.

## Executive Order No. 14169: Defunding Domestic Resettlement Services

Concurrent with the suspension of admissions, a separate mechanism was employed to disrupt existing resettlement operations: Executive Order No. 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," issued on January 20, 2025. While this order ostensibly addressed foreign aid generally, its practical effect within days was significant for domestic refugees. On January 24, 2025, pursuant to this executive directive, the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP operations abroad and, crucially for this case, halted funding for domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States.

This defunding action targeted cooperative agreements used by non-profit organizations to provide reception and placement services domestically. Within weeks of these orders taking effect—specifically by February 26—the State Department terminated every cooperative agreement responsible for providing reception and placement services for newly arrived refugees within U.S.-based communities. Furthermore, nearly all cooperative agreements supporting overseas processing were terminated save for one exception. This abrupt cessation created an immediate crisis regarding how recently admitted refugees would receive essential support services like housing assistance and job training, setting the stage for allegations that these actions violated specific statutory mandates rather than merely exercising executive discretion over future admissions policies.
