Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 1: # Procedural History and the Evolution of Stays

The procedural history of *Pacito v. Trump* illustrates the dynamic tension between district courts exercising equitable powers to enjoin executive action and appellate courts managing the stability of those orders during litigation. The controversy began in early 2025 when President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, suspending entry into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), followed shortly by a separate order terminating funding for domestic resettlement services. Plaintiffs, comprising admitted refugees, approved applicants awaiting arrival, and service organizations, sued in the Western District of Washington. They argued that the suspension of admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of services violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and specific mandates in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 regarding reception and placement services.

The district court moved swiftly to address these claims through a series of escalating preliminary injunctions. On February 25, 2025, the district court issued an oral preliminary injunction halting enforcement of key sections of Executive Order No. 14163. Two days later, on February 28, the court issued a written order clarifying that this injunction extended beyond mere entry restrictions to prohibit the suspension of USRAP funding itself. By March 24, recognizing that the government had already terminated cooperative agreements with private organizations essential for refugee resettlement, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction specifically ordering the State Department to reinstate those terminated agreements. These rulings created an immediate legal crisis: while the executive branch claimed broad deference under immigration statutes like 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the district court found statutory duties under § 1522 required continued funding and service delivery regardless of presidential policy shifts.

The government responded by filing notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, seeking relief from what they viewed as overly broad judicial interference with presidential authority. Upon receiving these appeals, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit intervened with an initial stay order on March 25, effectively pausing much of the district court's work pending further review. However, this stay was not absolute; it contained a "limited carveout" preserving relief for individuals who had already received conditional approval for refugee status prior to January 20, 2025. This nuanced approach reflected a balance between respecting presidential deference—citing *Trump v. Hawaii* regarding Article II authority—and protecting specific reliance interests developed before the executive order took effect. The panel subsequently issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9 to refine exactly who fell within this protected carveout group, directing full briefing and scheduling expedited oral arguments for September to resolve the underlying merits.

As litigation progressed toward argument, procedural friction intensified regarding enforcement mechanisms rather than substantive law alone. On July 14, facing confusion over how to administer the limited carveout for pre-approved refugees without disrupting operations entirely, the district court issued an order establishing a detailed enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee compliance with its earlier rulings. This move prompted an emergency motion from the government on July 17 asking for clarification or amendment of stays to prevent potential harm from this new oversight structure. The Ninth Circuit responded by issuing an administrative stay on July 18, temporarily blocking enforcement of this new framework while it considered whether further intervention was necessary before hearing arguments later in September.

The current procedural posture emerged after oral arguments were heard on September 3 but before a final merits opinion could be rendered in time to guide lower courts during that interim period. Recognizing that lingering uncertainty could cause irreversible harm to either side or disrupt national security interests pending final adjudication, a majority panel issued an order vacating both its own administrative stay from July and its original stay from March (as amended). Instead of leaving cases in limbo or allowing conflicting directives from multiple levels of court to stand simultaneously, judges directed that all prior district court orders—from February through March—be stayed in their entirety moving forward as a single unitary directive pending final resolution of the appeal on merit grounds like § 1522 liability versus § 1182(f) authority.

This evolution underscores how stays function as provisional remedies designed to maintain *status quo* while appellate courts deliberate complex questions involving separation of powers and statutory interpretation involving immigration policy versus social welfare obligations for recent arrivals.
