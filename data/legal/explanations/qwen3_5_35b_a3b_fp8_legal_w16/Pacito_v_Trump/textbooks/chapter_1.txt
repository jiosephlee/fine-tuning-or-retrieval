Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and the Emergency Stay Context

## Timeline of District Court Rulings: Feb/March 2025 Preliminary Injunctions

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* began with a rapid escalation in the Western District of Washington following the issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 on January 20, 2025. This executive order suspended entry into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) based on a presidential finding that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Simultaneously, a separate executive order led to the suspension of all funding for USRAP, including domestic resettlement services. Plaintiffs, comprising admitted refugees, approved applicants awaiting arrival, and non-profit organizations responsible for processing and resettling refugees, challenged these actions immediately. They alleged violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 regarding the suspension of admissions and violations of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) concerning the defunding of essential services.

The district court responded swiftly to these allegations. On February 25, 2025, acting on an oral motion, the district court issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement of specific sections of Executive Order No. 14163. Recognizing that funding was integral to the execution of refugee policy, the court clarified this injunction in writing on February 28, 2025 (District Court Docket Entry 45), explicitly stating that it also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. The conflict escalated when the Department of State terminated cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations shortly thereafter. In response to these terminations, which threatened immediate harm to already-admitted refugees dependent on support services, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, 2025 (District Court Docket Entry 79). This subsequent order specifically commanded the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after February 25. These dual rulings created an immediate directive for federal agencies to maintain both refugee admissions processes and domestic service funding despite the President's executive orders setting them aside.

## The Government's Appeal and Initial Motions for Stay

Upon receiving notice of these preliminary injunctions, the government moved aggressively to halt their enforcement while appealing to a higher court. Notices of appeal were filed on February 28 and March 25, 2025. Crucially, alongside filing their appeals—which typically pauses proceedings but does not automatically stop an injunction—the government filed emergency motions seeking a stay pending appeal. A stay pending appeal is a critical procedural mechanism that preserves the status quo by preventing a party from suffering irreparable harm during litigation while simultaneously allowing an appellate court to review whether a permanent injunction should have been issued in the first place. Without such a stay, defendants might be forced to comply with conflicting orders or suffer damages that cannot be undone even if they ultimately win on appeal; conversely, plaintiffs might suffer harm if an erroneous injunction remains in force too long before reversal.

The government argued that under *Trump v. Hawaii*, courts must afford significant deference to presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), suggesting they were likely to succeed on the merits regarding both admission suspensions and funding decisions. However, rather than waiting for full briefing on all issues, which can take months in complex immigration cases involving national security implications, the government sought immediate relief from specific portions of the district court's orders that they believed exceeded judicial authority over presidential prerogatives in immigration control. This set up a race against time where multiple judges across different levels were issuing conflicting directives regarding what laws applied to refugees and aid workers as early as spring 2025.

## The Ninth Circuit's Evolving Stays: From Partial Carve-outs to Full Vacatur

The Ninth Circuit initially addressed these emergency requests through its motions panel rather than waiting for a full panel opinion on the merits. On March 25, 2025, citing *Trump v.* *Hawaii*, motions panels granted part of the government's request for a stay but denied it entirely regarding individuals who had already received conditional approval for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This created a complex "partial stay" scenario: while most aspects of President Trump's orders were allowed to proceed without interference from lower courts during litigation (because they lacked merit), refugees who had cleared USCIS hurdles prior to Januarry remained protected by an exception known as a "carve-out." The appellate panel acknowledged that while broad deference existed generally ("exuding deference"), it did not extend fully enough at that moment to strip away rights already accrued by specific individuals who had completed rigorous vetting processes domestically or abroad before new restrictions took effect.

However, this procedural arrangement proved unstable and difficult to administer as litigation progressed through April and May 2025. The Ninth Circuit issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9 defining exactly how this limited carve-out operated in practice amidst confusion over which refugees fell under protection versus exclusion zones defined by dates alone. By July 14—a date significant because it marks another layer of complexity—the district court attempted to operationalize this carve-out by appointing a magistrate judge and establishing an enforcement framework specifically designed to manage exceptions for pre-approved refugees while enforcing other parts of its original order against general admissions bans and funding cuts. This created a fractured legal environment where different rules applied depending on individual application dates and specific categories of aid recipients within weeks of each other's rulings.
