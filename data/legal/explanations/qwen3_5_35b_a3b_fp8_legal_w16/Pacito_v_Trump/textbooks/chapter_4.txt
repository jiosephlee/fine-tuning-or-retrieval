Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: The APA Challenge and Mandatory Duties under § 1522

## Irreparable Injury Without Continued Funding for Resettlement Agencies

In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit's analysis of the stay pending appeal hinges critically on the distinction between the President's broad authority to suspend future refugee admissions and the specific statutory mandates governing those already present or approved for entry. While the majority agrees that the government is likely to succeed on its defense of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of admissions based on the deferential standard applied in *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court identifies a sharp departure in its assessment of the government's liability regarding "reception and placement" services. The central procedural question becomes whether the termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations causes irreparable harm that outweighs the government's interest in defunding these programs during an administrative transition.

The district courts had previously issued preliminary injunctions not only stopping the suspension of admissions but also halting the termination of funding for domestic resettlement services. When reviewing these injunctions, the Ninth Circuit recognized that while a stay might be appropriate to pause enforcement against future applicants, maintaining a gap in services for current refugees presents a distinct legal problem. The court noted that despite assurances from the State Department that it was actively seeking new contractors to replace terminated agreements, there was no evidence at oral argument that such replacements were secured. This uncertainty created a situation where refugees admitted prior to January 20, 2025, faced immediate destitution without access to employment training, cash assistance, or medical care. In this context, "irreparable injury" is not merely speculative; it is imminent and concrete. The court reasoned that leaving hundreds of thousands of dollars in appropriated funds unused while simultaneously stripping essential services creates a crisis that cannot be remedied by money damages alone once they occur. Thus, even if the government ultimately prevails on its right to reduce funding eventually, the immediate cessation of operations violates statutory commands and inflicts injury severe enough to warrant judicial intervention via a stay modification.

## Success on Merits: Interpreting 'Shall' vs. 'Authorized' in § 1522(a)-(e)

The crux of the dispute regarding "success on merits" turns on statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, specifically whether its language imposes a mandatory duty to fund or merely authorizes discretionary action. The majority opinion adopts a textualist approach focused on imperative verbs found throughout the statute to conclude that Congress intended to bind the executive branch's hands regarding service delivery once appropriations are available. The text explicitly directs that "[t]o achieve economic self-sufficiency... [the Director] shall make available sufficient resources," "shall provide... opportunity," and "shall insure." For purposes of an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge, such mandatory language ("shall") typically signifies a non-discretionary command rather than a suggestion or an option open to executive whim.

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a competing interpretive framework rooted in legislative structure and purpose titles. He argues that because § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs..." and contains multiple instances where Congress states the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance (particularly in subsections (b) through (e)), the statute functions as a grant of power rather than an imposition of duty. Under this reading, "authorization" implies permission; if Congress wanted to mandate funding regardless of executive preference, it would have used more unequivocal language devoid of qualifying conditions like "to the extent of available appropriations." Judge Lee contends that subsection (a)'s use of "shall" applies only as a conditional constraint *if* funding occurs—it dictates how money must be spent if Congress appropriates it—but does not compel Congress or the President to appropriate it in any specific amount or manner. This disagreement highlights a fundamental tension in administrative law: does statutory silence on specific budget allocations leave room for executive discretion, or does specific performance language override general budgetary constraints? The majority resolves this by viewing § 1522 as creating an affirmative obligation tied directly to existing appropriations, rejecting the notion that funding cuts can legally nullify statutory service requirements without violating the APA's prohibition against arbitrary and capricious action by agencies failing to follow binding statutes.

## Available Appropriations: Does Reduced Funding Excuse Performance?

A pivotal element in determining whether there is success on merits involves interpreting the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" found within § 1522(a). The government argued implicitly—and sometimes explicitly—that because Congress reduced its appropriation for migration and refugee assistance by $800 million through subsequent legislation (Pub. L. No. 119-28), it lacked sufficient funds to fulfill all previous commitments under § 1522 without further congressional action. If interpreted broadly, this clause could allow an agency facing budget reductions to simply cease operations entirely until new funds are allocated by Congress, effectively treating "available appropriations" as a ceiling rather than a floor for operational capacity derived from existing funds.

However, the Ninth Circuit majority rejects this expansive reading in favor of one that respects congressional intent behind both appropriations acts and substantive mandates alike. The court points out that despite significant cuts, substantial funds remained ($3 billion minus $800 million), which were still legally designated for migration and refugee assistance under Pub. L. No. 119-4 (§§ 1101(a)(11)). Therefore, there were indeed "available appropriations" capable of supporting core reception and placement functions required by law. The reduction did not render total impossibility; rather, it necessitated difficult prioritization but did not absolve the government of its duty to utilize remaining lawful funds for statutory purposes like cash assistance and medical support for newly arrived refugees who had already been vetted and admitted under prior frameworks. By holding that reduced funding does not equate to zero availability when billions remain dedicated to similar objectives, the court establishes that agencies cannot invoke budget cuts as a blanket excuse for abandoning statutory mandates unless they demonstrate true insolvency or lack of any relevant funds whatsoever—a high bar rarely met when congressionally earmarked monies persistently exist alongside unfilled needs identified by statute itself.
