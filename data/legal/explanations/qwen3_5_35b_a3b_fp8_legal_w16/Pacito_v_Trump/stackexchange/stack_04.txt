Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What constitutes 'irreparable injury' in this context when comparing individual refugees waiting outside vs. those already inside receiving services?
Question:
The Nken test requires showing irreparable injury without a stay. The court seems to find that stopping admissions causes injury (implied by denying relief there?), but stops short of finding irreparable harm for those inside who lose funding. Conversely, reinstating funding prevents injury to current residents but harms national sovereignty arguments? I don't understand how the court balances these injuries when deciding whether to lift or keep stays on specific parts of the injunctions.
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on the *Pacito* order, the court distinguishes between "irreparable injury" based on the nature of the plaintiff's relationship to the government and the statutory mandates involved. The analysis hinges entirely on **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which governs post-admission services.

**1. Refugees Outside the U.S.: No Irreparable Injury Found for Stay Purposes**
Regarding refugees waiting outside the country (those denied entry under Executive Order 14163), the court found that the government is **likely to succeed on the merits**. Relying heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court held that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) vests the President with broad discretion to suspend entry if deemed "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Because the President has already made this requisite finding, and because judicial review of such determinations is highly deferential ("we cannot engage in a searching inquiry"), the court concluded that staying the injunction against suspending admissions does not cause irreparable injury to these applicants that outweighs the government's interest in national security and sovereign control. Essentially, while they suffer an injury by being kept out, that injury is not "irreparable" in a way that compels a stay when weighed against a high probability of presidential victory on this specific legal ground.

**2. Refugees Inside the U.S.: Irreparable Injury Exists Due to Statutory Mandates**
Conversely, for refugees already admitted to the United States who face loss of funding for reception and placement services, the court identified a distinct source of irreparable injury: **statutory obligation under 8 U.S.C. § 1522**.

Unlike Article II powers regarding entry, § 1522 creates affirmative duties for the government once a refugee is present. The opinion details that:
*   The statute requires the Director of HHS to provide resources for employment training, English language acquisition, cash assistance, and medical services "to achieve economic self-sufficiency."
*   Specifically for unaccompanied minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) mandates that during an interim period, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility... and is authorized to make necessary decisions."
*   For recent arrivals (<36 months), § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires alternative projects providing interim support and case management "as needed."

The court reasoned that terminating cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations leaves currently admitted refugees without these statutorily guaranteed services. This creates an immediate risk of destitution or lack of care for vulnerable populations (particularly unaccompanied minors) while awaiting a permanent solution—a classic definition of irreparable harm where money damages are insufficient or unavailable to remedy homelessness or lack of medical care mid-stream.

**3. Balancing Against Sovereignty Arguments**
The court balanced these competing interests by separating **admission** from **resettlement**:
*   **Admission:** The government's sovereignty interest in controlling borders (via § 1182(f)) prevailed because it was supported by a strong likelihood of success on appeal. Thus, no stay was lifted regarding new entrants; they remain barred until final resolution or further order.
*   **Resettlement:** The government's failure to fulfill its statutory duty (§ 1522) created an irreparable injury that tipped the balance *in favor* of lifting stays for current residents specifically regarding funding reinstatement. The court noted its own uncertainty about whether new agencies could secure funds quickly enough to fill the void left by terminated contracts ("government advised... it was unaware whether any progress had been made").

Therefore, under *Nken*, while suspending entry causes no reversible error given presidential deference (favoring a stay), cutting off funding for existing residents violates specific statutory commands causing irreparable harm (disfavoring a stay). Consequently, the order vacated previous stays *only* regarding those inside receiving services (directing reinstatement of agreements) while maintaining stays on those outside entering (upholding suspension).

***Note:** Judge Lee's partial dissent argues that § 1522 is merely permissive ("authorization") rather than mandatory ("shall"), contending that Congress did not compel funding execution if appropriations exist but programs are not run.*
