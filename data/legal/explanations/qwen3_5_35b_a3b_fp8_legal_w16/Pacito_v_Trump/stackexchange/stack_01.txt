Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How can the court grant a 'stay pending appeal' while simultaneously 'vacating' previous stays? What is the practical effect on the lower court's injunctions?
Question:
I am completely lost on the procedural posture here. The order starts by saying it is issuing a 'stay pending appeal,' yet it explicitly says it is 'vacating' the administrative stay from July 18 and the earlier stay from March 25. If the previous stays are gone, why isn't the district court's original injunction fully back in force? Does 'vacating' mean those prior orders never existed, or just that they are replaced by this new order? And how does staying the district court's Feb/March orders differ from leaving them in place?
Answer:
**Answer**

The confusion stems from conflating the *status* of the previous appellate orders with the *scope* of the underlying district court injunctions. Procedurally, "vacating" a prior stay order simply means that specific order is dissolved and ceases to have any legal force. However, vacating a stay does not automatically reactivate the underlying injunction unless the court explicitly directs it.

Here is how the mechanics work in *Pacito v. Trump*:

**1. Vacatur vs. Stay: The Hierarchy of Orders**
When the Ninth Circuit "vacated" its administrative stay (July 18) and its prior stay (March 25), it erased those specific interim rulings. Those orders had previously modified or clarified what parts of the district court's injunction were paused. By removing them, those specific modifications vanish, returning the situation to whatever state existed *before* those specific orders were issued—but subject to the new order being issued simultaneously.

Crucially, the court did not leave the district court's injunctions in limbo. Immediately after vacating the old stays, the court issued a **new** stay pending appeal covering "the district court's orders... in their entirety." This is a distinct act:
*   **Vacatur:** Removes the old appellate intervention (the July 18 and March 25 orders).
*   **New Stay:** Imposes fresh appellate intervention on the district court's February 28 and March 24 injunctions.

If the court had merely vacated without issuing a new stay, yes, you are correct that the district court's injunctions would likely spring back into full force (subject to any existing stays). But because this opinion acts as a comprehensive disposition replacing all prior interim relief, it ensures that **nothing** from the district court is enforced until further notice, except for one specific carve-out found in the "Discussion" section.

**2. The Practical Effect: A Hybrid Remedy**
The practical effect is that the government gets a blanket pause on its primary goal (suspending refugee admissions) but loses its pause on secondary goals (funding/resettlement services).

Look closely at what remains effective despite the broad "stayed in their entirety" language:
> *"In light of the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide... services... directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide... services..."*

This directive overrides the general stay for one specific subset of issues. While the March 24 order (ordering reinstatement of agreements) was technically "stayed," this new paragraph functions as an affirmative command from this Court that effectively nullifies that stay regarding service providers alone. The result is:
*   **Suspension of Admissions:** The President's ban on entry remains blocked by this Court's finding that he will likely win on merits (*Nken* factor analysis).
*   **Restoration of Funding/Services:** The Court finds a high likelihood of success for plaintiffs under § 1522 (APA violation). Consequently, even though it stayed everything else, it carved out an exception requiring immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted or in transit.

**3. Why Not Just Leave Them In Place?**
You asked how staying differs from leaving them in place. If they were left in place without modification:
*   The government could not stop suspending entries (violating its strong case on § 1182(f)).
*   The government might continue cutting off funds indefinitely while waiting for a final opinion on appeal (potentially violating § 1522).

By issuing this hybrid order, the Ninth Circuit balances *irreparable harm*. It prevents irreparable harm to national security interests by staying entry bans (Factor 1 & 2), but prevents irreparable harm to refugees currently in America by mandating they receive services Congress deemed mandatory (§ 1522), acknowledging that money cannot be easily recovered once spent or withheld during an emergency (*Nken*).

**Conclusion**
The vacatur clears away conflicting procedural history so this single order can serve as both a shield against entry bans and a sword forcing service restoration. The district court's injunctions are temporarily frozen everywhere except where this Court explicitly commands performance based on statutory mandates (§ 1522).
