Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does *Trump v. Hawaii* apply equally to both the suspension of admissions AND the termination of domestic service funding, given they rely on different statutes?
Question:
The majority cites *Hawaii* extensively to show high deference to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) regarding suspending refugee *admissions*. However, they then pivot to say the government might lose on APA grounds regarding *domestic* services funded under § 1522. Since *Hawaii* deals with entry restrictions based on national security/foreign policy findings, does that level of deference extend to an executive decision to stop paying contractors for food/shelter inside the US? Or is § 1522 subject to stricter judicial review because it involves statutory mandates for welfare rather than border control?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No. While *Trump v. Hawaii* affords the President near-total deference regarding the suspension of **admissions** under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), that deference does not extend to the termination of **domestic service funding** under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The Ninth Circuit explicitly distinguished between the two statutory regimes, holding that while the President has broad discretion to bar entry, § 1522 contains a mandatory statutory command to provide reception and placement services once refugees are admitted, which overrides executive discretion when funds are available.

### Legal Analysis

The distinction turns on the difference between a **discretionary grant of authority** to exclude aliens versus a **mandatory statutory obligation** to assist those already present.

#### 1. Deference Applies Only to Entry Restrictions (§ 1182(f))
Regarding the suspension of admissions via Executive Order No. 14163, the court applied *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. The court found that because the statute requires only that the President "find[]" entry detrimental to U.S. interests—a finding clearly satisfied here—the judiciary cannot conduct a "searching inquiry" into the justification for barring entry. In this context, the President's determination is effectively unreviewable regarding the *decision* to suspend admissions entirely.

#### 2. Mandatory Duty Governs Domestic Services (§ 1522)
However, for domestic services (food, shelter, training) provided under § 1522, the court rejected the application of *Hawaii*-style deference. The opinion highlights several key differences in § 1522 compared to § 1182(f):
*   **Statutory Language:** Unlike § 1182(f)'s open-ended delegation, § 1522 uses imperative language ("shall") directing the Director of Refugee Resettlement to provide specific resources (employment training, cash assistance, etc.) "to the extent of available appropriations."
*   **Nature of Obligation:** The court reasoned that once refugees are admitted (or conditionally approved), Congress has imposed a duty on the executive branch to facilitate their resettlement. This is not merely an authorization for aid but a mandate for how aid must be administered if appropriated funds exist (noted as $3.178 billion minus rescissions).
*   **Judicial Review Standard:** Because § 1522 creates a statutory right for refugees and a corresponding duty for agencies, the government's failure to fulfill this duty constitutes a potential violation of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The court held that where there is "uncertainty regarding its ability to provide... statutorily mandated" services, it cannot simply terminate contracts without violating its legal obligations under Congress's explicit instructions.

#### Conclusion
The court's order reflects a bifurcated approach:
*   **Admissions:** High deference (*Hawaii*) prevails; courts cannot second-guess national security findings required by § 1182(f).
*   **Domestic Funding:** Strict adherence to statutory text prevails; courts can enjoin executive actions that violate clear congressional mandates like those in § 1522(a) and related subsections regarding reception and placement services.

Judge Lee's concurrence/dissent reinforces this distinction by arguing that even if one views § 1522 as permissive ("authorization"), it certainly does not carry the same constitutional weight as foreign policy judgments protected by *Hawaii*. However, the majority treats it as a binding statutory constraint on executive discretion once appropriations are made available.
