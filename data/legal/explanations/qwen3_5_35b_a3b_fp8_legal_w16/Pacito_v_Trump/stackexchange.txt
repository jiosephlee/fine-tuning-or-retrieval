Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How can the court grant a 'stay pending appeal' while simultaneously 'vacating' previous stays? What is the practical effect on the lower court's injunctions?
Question:
I am completely lost on the procedural posture here. The order starts by saying it is issuing a 'stay pending appeal,' yet it explicitly says it is 'vacating' the administrative stay from July 18 and the earlier stay from March 25. If the previous stays are gone, why isn't the district court's original injunction fully back in force? Does 'vacating' mean those prior orders never existed, or just that they are replaced by this new order? And how does staying the district court's Feb/March orders differ from leaving them in place?
Answer:
**Answer**

The confusion stems from conflating the *status* of the previous appellate orders with the *scope* of the underlying district court injunctions. Procedurally, "vacating" a prior stay order simply means that specific order is dissolved and ceases to have any legal force. However, vacating a stay does not automatically reactivate the underlying injunction unless the court explicitly directs it.

Here is how the mechanics work in *Pacito v. Trump*:

**1. Vacatur vs. Stay: The Hierarchy of Orders**
When the Ninth Circuit "vacated" its administrative stay (July 18) and its prior stay (March 25), it erased those specific interim rulings. Those orders had previously modified or clarified what parts of the district court's injunction were paused. By removing them, those specific modifications vanish, returning the situation to whatever state existed *before* those specific orders were issued—but subject to the new order being issued simultaneously.

Crucially, the court did not leave the district court's injunctions in limbo. Immediately after vacating the old stays, the court issued a **new** stay pending appeal covering "the district court's orders... in their entirety." This is a distinct act:
*   **Vacatur:** Removes the old appellate intervention (the July 18 and March 25 orders).
*   **New Stay:** Imposes fresh appellate intervention on the district court's February 28 and March 24 injunctions.

If the court had merely vacated without issuing a new stay, yes, you are correct that the district court's injunctions would likely spring back into full force (subject to any existing stays). But because this opinion acts as a comprehensive disposition replacing all prior interim relief, it ensures that **nothing** from the district court is enforced until further notice, except for one specific carve-out found in the "Discussion" section.

**2. The Practical Effect: A Hybrid Remedy**
The practical effect is that the government gets a blanket pause on its primary goal (suspending refugee admissions) but loses its pause on secondary goals (funding/resettlement services).

Look closely at what remains effective despite the broad "stayed in their entirety" language:
> *"In light of the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide... services... directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide... services..."*

This directive overrides the general stay for one specific subset of issues. While the March 24 order (ordering reinstatement of agreements) was technically "stayed," this new paragraph functions as an affirmative command from this Court that effectively nullifies that stay regarding service providers alone. The result is:
*   **Suspension of Admissions:** The President's ban on entry remains blocked by this Court's finding that he will likely win on merits (*Nken* factor analysis).
*   **Restoration of Funding/Services:** The Court finds a high likelihood of success for plaintiffs under § 1522 (APA violation). Consequently, even though it stayed everything else, it carved out an exception requiring immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted or in transit.

**3. Why Not Just Leave Them In Place?**
You asked how staying differs from leaving them in place. If they were left in place without modification:
*   The government could not stop suspending entries (violating its strong case on § 1182(f)).
*   The government might continue cutting off funds indefinitely while waiting for a final opinion on appeal (potentially violating § 1522).

By issuing this hybrid order, the Ninth Circuit balances *irreparable harm*. It prevents irreparable harm to national security interests by staying entry bans (Factor 1 & 2), but prevents irreparable harm to refugees currently in America by mandating they receive services Congress deemed mandatory (§ 1522), acknowledging that money cannot be easily recovered once spent or withheld during an emergency (*Nken*).

**Conclusion**
The vacatur clears away conflicting procedural history so this single order can serve as both a shield against entry bans and a sword forcing service restoration. The district court's injunctions are temporarily frozen everywhere except where this Court explicitly commands performance based on statutory mandates (§ 1522).

### Is the distinction between 'authorization' and 'mandate' in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 purely semantic, or does it hinge on the phrase 'to the extent of available appropriations'?
Question:
Judge Lee argues that because § 1522 uses words like 'authorized' and mentions 'available appropriations,' it creates discretion rather than a duty. However, the majority cites specific subsections using 'shall' to argue for a mandate. I'm confused: does the presence of 'available appropriations' automatically convert a mandatory command ('shall') into a discretionary one ('may'), or is there a deeper structural difference between Title II of the INA (Immigration) and Title IV (Refugee Assistance) that dictates this interpretation? Why doesn't Congress's appropriation of $3.178 billion imply an obligation to spend it?
Answer:
**Answer**

The distinction between "authorization" and "mandate" in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is **not purely semantic**, nor does the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" automatically convert a mandatory command into a discretionary one. Instead, the dispute hinges on how the court interprets the statutory structure: specifically, whether the "shall" commands in § 1522(a) create independent duties to act, or merely conditions that apply *only* if Congress has already exercised its discretion to fund the program.

Based on the *Pacito v. Trump* panel order and Judge Lee's concurrence/dissent, here is the breakdown of the competing interpretations:

### 1. The Majority View: Structural Mandate with Appropriation Limits
The majority treats § 1522 as creating a substantive statutory duty to provide reception and placement services once appropriations are made. Their reasoning rests on two pillars found in the text:

*   **Mandatory Language ("Shall"):** The majority points to specific subsections using imperative verbs. For instance, § 1522(a)(1)(A) directs that the Director **"shall... make available"** resources for employment training and cash assistance. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary **"shall develop and implement"** alternative projects for recent arrivals.
*   **The Role of Appropriations:** The phrase "**to the extent of available appropriations**" functions as a limitation on *how much* can be spent or what specific scope can be achieved, not as a grant of discretion to refuse acting entirely. The majority notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for FY 2025 (even after reductions). Because funds exist, the statutory condition triggering the "shall" obligation is met. Therefore, the government cannot simply terminate cooperative agreements and leave a vacuum; they must utilize existing funds to fulfill the statutory mandate unless they explicitly repeal or modify the underlying statute via new legislation or valid rescission procedures that affect these specific allocations.

In short, under this view, "authorization" refers to the mechanism (using funds/grants), but "mandate" refers to the obligation to deploy those mechanisms once money is allocated by Congress.

### 2. Judge Lee's View: Authorization Disguised as Condition
Judge Lee argues that despite the use of "shall," the statute remains fundamentally an **authorization** rather than a mandate because of its title and context:

*   **Statutory Title:** He emphasizes that § 1522 is titled "**Authorization** for programs..." implying permissiveness rather than compulsion.
*   **Contextual Reading:** Lee reads § 1522(a)'s "shall" requirements not as independent commands to initiate services, but as **"conditions and considerations"** that govern *if* and *when* services are provided under subsections (b) through (e), which explicitly state officials are "**authorized**."
    *   *Lee's Logic:* If you choose to run a program (discretionary), then you must follow these rules (mandatory). But if you choose not to run a program at all (because you lack funds or policy alignment), you aren't violating § 1522 because there was never an initial trigger to begin operations.
*   **Discretionary Triggers:** He highlights phrases like "if necessary" (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and "as needed" (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)), arguing these vest broad executive discretion in determining whether any service is required at all, rendering any apparent mandate illusory without further congressional direction.

### Conclusion: Is it Semantic?
No, it is not merely semantic; it represents a fundamental disagreement over **statutory construction principles**:

1.  **Textualism vs. Contextualism:** The majority focuses on isolated operative clauses containing "shall," treating them as binding directives conditioned only by funding availability. Judge Lee looks at the statute holistically (title + surrounding sections), concluding that without an explicit command to *spend*, Congress only authorized *potential* spending subject to executive judgment.
2.  **The Appropriation Factor:** The presence of appropriations ($3.178 billion) does not automatically force spending under either theory; however, it shifts the burden differently:
    *   Under the **Majority**, appropriations remove the barrier preventing compliance with a pre-existing duty ("You have money; therefore you must act").
    *   Under **Lee**, appropriations merely provide capacity ("You have money; therefore you *can* act"), leaving the decision to exercise that capacity entirely within executive discretion unless Congress explicitly says otherwise in subsequent legislation or rescission acts affecting those specific dollars differently than general policy shifts allow.

Therefore, Congress's appropriation implies an obligation only if one accepts that § 1522 creates a standing duty triggered by funding availability (Majority). If one accepts Lee's reading that § 1522 merely authorizes programs subject to executive prioritization regardless of funding levels (unless funds are specifically withheld), then appropriation alone does not compel expenditure against executive will.

### Does *Trump v. Hawaii* apply equally to both the suspension of admissions AND the termination of domestic service funding, given they rely on different statutes?
Question:
The majority cites *Hawaii* extensively to show high deference to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) regarding suspending refugee *admissions*. However, they then pivot to say the government might lose on APA grounds regarding *domestic* services funded under § 1522. Since *Hawaii* deals with entry restrictions based on national security/foreign policy findings, does that level of deference extend to an executive decision to stop paying contractors for food/shelter inside the US? Or is § 1522 subject to stricter judicial review because it involves statutory mandates for welfare rather than border control?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No. While *Trump v. Hawaii* affords the President near-total deference regarding the suspension of **admissions** under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), that deference does not extend to the termination of **domestic service funding** under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The Ninth Circuit explicitly distinguished between the two statutory regimes, holding that while the President has broad discretion to bar entry, § 1522 contains a mandatory statutory command to provide reception and placement services once refugees are admitted, which overrides executive discretion when funds are available.

### Legal Analysis

The distinction turns on the difference between a **discretionary grant of authority** to exclude aliens versus a **mandatory statutory obligation** to assist those already present.

#### 1. Deference Applies Only to Entry Restrictions (§ 1182(f))
Regarding the suspension of admissions via Executive Order No. 14163, the court applied *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. The court found that because the statute requires only that the President "find[]" entry detrimental to U.S. interests—a finding clearly satisfied here—the judiciary cannot conduct a "searching inquiry" into the justification for barring entry. In this context, the President's determination is effectively unreviewable regarding the *decision* to suspend admissions entirely.

#### 2. Mandatory Duty Governs Domestic Services (§ 1522)
However, for domestic services (food, shelter, training) provided under § 1522, the court rejected the application of *Hawaii*-style deference. The opinion highlights several key differences in § 1522 compared to § 1182(f):
*   **Statutory Language:** Unlike § 1182(f)'s open-ended delegation, § 1522 uses imperative language ("shall") directing the Director of Refugee Resettlement to provide specific resources (employment training, cash assistance, etc.) "to the extent of available appropriations."
*   **Nature of Obligation:** The court reasoned that once refugees are admitted (or conditionally approved), Congress has imposed a duty on the executive branch to facilitate their resettlement. This is not merely an authorization for aid but a mandate for how aid must be administered if appropriated funds exist (noted as $3.178 billion minus rescissions).
*   **Judicial Review Standard:** Because § 1522 creates a statutory right for refugees and a corresponding duty for agencies, the government's failure to fulfill this duty constitutes a potential violation of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The court held that where there is "uncertainty regarding its ability to provide... statutorily mandated" services, it cannot simply terminate contracts without violating its legal obligations under Congress's explicit instructions.

#### Conclusion
The court's order reflects a bifurcated approach:
*   **Admissions:** High deference (*Hawaii*) prevails; courts cannot second-guess national security findings required by § 1182(f).
*   **Domestic Funding:** Strict adherence to statutory text prevails; courts can enjoin executive actions that violate clear congressional mandates like those in § 1522(a) and related subsections regarding reception and placement services.

Judge Lee's concurrence/dissent reinforces this distinction by arguing that even if one views § 1522 as permissive ("authorization"), it certainly does not carry the same constitutional weight as foreign policy judgments protected by *Hawaii*. However, the majority treats it as a binding statutory constraint on executive discretion once appropriations are made available.

### What constitutes 'irreparable injury' in this context when comparing individual refugees waiting outside vs. those already inside receiving services?
Question:
The Nken test requires showing irreparable injury without a stay. The court seems to find that stopping admissions causes injury (implied by denying relief there?), but stops short of finding irreparable harm for those inside who lose funding. Conversely, reinstating funding prevents injury to current residents but harms national sovereignty arguments? I don't understand how the court balances these injuries when deciding whether to lift or keep stays on specific parts of the injunctions.
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on the *Pacito* order, the court distinguishes between "irreparable injury" based on the nature of the plaintiff's relationship to the government and the statutory mandates involved. The analysis hinges entirely on **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which governs post-admission services.

**1. Refugees Outside the U.S.: No Irreparable Injury Found for Stay Purposes**
Regarding refugees waiting outside the country (those denied entry under Executive Order 14163), the court found that the government is **likely to succeed on the merits**. Relying heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court held that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) vests the President with broad discretion to suspend entry if deemed "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Because the President has already made this requisite finding, and because judicial review of such determinations is highly deferential ("we cannot engage in a searching inquiry"), the court concluded that staying the injunction against suspending admissions does not cause irreparable injury to these applicants that outweighs the government's interest in national security and sovereign control. Essentially, while they suffer an injury by being kept out, that injury is not "irreparable" in a way that compels a stay when weighed against a high probability of presidential victory on this specific legal ground.

**2. Refugees Inside the U.S.: Irreparable Injury Exists Due to Statutory Mandates**
Conversely, for refugees already admitted to the United States who face loss of funding for reception and placement services, the court identified a distinct source of irreparable injury: **statutory obligation under 8 U.S.C. § 1522**.

Unlike Article II powers regarding entry, § 1522 creates affirmative duties for the government once a refugee is present. The opinion details that:
*   The statute requires the Director of HHS to provide resources for employment training, English language acquisition, cash assistance, and medical services "to achieve economic self-sufficiency."
*   Specifically for unaccompanied minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) mandates that during an interim period, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility... and is authorized to make necessary decisions."
*   For recent arrivals (<36 months), § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires alternative projects providing interim support and case management "as needed."

The court reasoned that terminating cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations leaves currently admitted refugees without these statutorily guaranteed services. This creates an immediate risk of destitution or lack of care for vulnerable populations (particularly unaccompanied minors) while awaiting a permanent solution—a classic definition of irreparable harm where money damages are insufficient or unavailable to remedy homelessness or lack of medical care mid-stream.

**3. Balancing Against Sovereignty Arguments**
The court balanced these competing interests by separating **admission** from **resettlement**:
*   **Admission:** The government's sovereignty interest in controlling borders (via § 1182(f)) prevailed because it was supported by a strong likelihood of success on appeal. Thus, no stay was lifted regarding new entrants; they remain barred until final resolution or further order.
*   **Resettlement:** The government's failure to fulfill its statutory duty (§ 1522) created an irreparable injury that tipped the balance *in favor* of lifting stays for current residents specifically regarding funding reinstatement. The court noted its own uncertainty about whether new agencies could secure funds quickly enough to fill the void left by terminated contracts ("government advised... it was unaware whether any progress had been made").

Therefore, under *Nken*, while suspending entry causes no reversible error given presidential deference (favoring a stay), cutting off funding for existing residents violates specific statutory commands causing irreparable harm (disfavoring a stay). Consequently, the order vacated previous stays *only* regarding those inside receiving services (directing reinstatement of agreements) while maintaining stays on those outside entering (upholding suspension).

***Note:** Judge Lee's partial dissent argues that § 1522 is merely permissive ("authorization") rather than mandatory ("shall"), contending that Congress did not compel funding execution if appropriations exist but programs are not run.*

### If § 1522 is interpreted as merely permissive ('can provide'), does terminating cooperative agreements violate any other statutory requirements besides potential APA violations?
Question:
Judge Lee suggests that if § 1522 is just an authorization, then ending contracts isn't illegal unless it violates another law. But if it's not illegal under § 1522 itself, what exactly was wrong with terminating all cooperative agreements? Was it solely an arbitrary and capricious violation under the APA because they didn't follow proper procedure, or was there an independent statutory duty I'm missing? How does this relate to their failure to secure new agencies despite promising to try?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
No. If § 1522 is interpreted as merely permissive (an "authorization" rather than a "mandate"), terminating cooperative agreements does **not** independently violate § 1522 itself. Under the majority's reasoning, the illegality arises exclusively from the **Administrative Procedure Act (APA)** because the government failed to follow required procedures when terminating funds it had already committed to providing. The termination was unlawful not because § 1522 forbids ending contracts, but because the government acted arbitrarily and capriciously by cutting off existing services without a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made, particularly given their own representations that they were seeking new agencies.

### Detailed Analysis

**1. The Statutory Standard: Authorization vs. Mandate**
The core dispute turns on the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Judge Lee's concurrence argues that the statute is permissive, noting that its title refers to "Authorization" and that specific subsections grant directors the power to "make available" resources or "authorize" programs. Under this reading, Congress permits the executive branch to run these programs but does not compel them to do so once funding levels change or policies shift. If this view were adopted, the government could terminate contracts at will without violating § 1522, provided they did not violate other laws (such as appropriation statutes).

However, the **majority opinion rejects this permissive reading**. The majority interprets § 1522 as imposing a statutory obligation to provide specific reception and placement services ("shall... make available," "shall develop and implement"). Because Congress has mandated these services, the executive branch cannot simply eliminate them via contract termination without addressing how those statutory duties will be fulfilled.

**2. Why Termination Violated Other Requirements (The APA)**
Since the majority holds that § 1522 creates a duty to provide services, terminating cooperative agreements—which are the primary mechanism for delivering those services—constitutes a substantive change in policy or practice that triggers APA scrutiny under *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm Mut.* standards (arbitrary and capricious review).

The court identified two fatal flaws in the government's actions that rendered them illegal under the APA:
*   **Failure to Follow Procedure:** By terminating *all* cooperative agreements simultaneously without ensuring continuity of service, the government failed to provide a reasoned analysis explaining why it could abandon its statutory duty under § 1522.
*   **Contradictory Representations:** The government had told the district court it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." Yet, at oral argument, they admitted being unaware if any progress had been made toward securing a replacement agency. This disconnect demonstrates that the termination was not based on a factual assessment of whether services could continue elsewhere; rather, it was an abrupt cessation of statutorily mandated aid without a plan in place.

Under these circumstances, terminating agreements violates the APA because it leaves refugees without the very services Congress required (§ 1522) and fails to explain how this outcome aligns with current capabilities or future plans. The court explicitly stated that because of this uncertainty regarding their ability to fulfill statutory mandates, they were directed to reinstate agreements necessary to provide those services immediately.

**3. Relation to Failure to Secure New Agencies**
The failure to secure new agencies is central to finding an APA violation but is distinct from a direct statutory violation of § 1522 *if* one accepts Judge Lee's premise that § 1522 is optional. However, since the majority treats § 1522 as mandatory:
*   The inability or refusal to secure new agencies confirms that no alternative mechanism exists for fulfilling the statutory duty post-termination.
*   Therefore, terminating old agreements while failing to replace them renders the action **"arbitrary"** (lacking a rational basis) and **"capricious"** (ignoring important aspects of the problem).

In summary, if you accept Judge Lee's view that § 1522 is purely permissive, there would be no independent statutory duty violated by ending contracts *unless* another law (like an appropriations act) prohibited it. But because the Ninth Circuit majority held that § 1522 *is* mandatory in effect (requiring specific services regardless of funding source), terminating contracts became illegal primarily because it constituted an arbitrary abandonment of those duties under the APA, exacerbated by the lack of a viable transition plan for new agencies.

### Why did the Ninth Circuit choose to issue a partial stay/injunction mix rather than remanding everything back to the district court immediately?
Question:
Usually, when there's ambiguity about whether funds are mandatory versus discretionary, courts remand for clarification or summary judgment on those specific points. Instead, this panel issued an order directing reinstatement of agreements while acknowledging they haven't written their final opinion yet. Is this considered an equitable remedy based on urgency (preventing starvation/homelessness during winter), or did they essentially decide the merits prematurely in their interim order? What binds them to reinstate these agreements if they admit an opinion explaining why follows later?
Answer:
The Ninth Circuit issued this hybrid order—a partial stay of the district court's injunction coupled with a mandatory directive to reinstate cooperative agreements—because it applied the *Nken* four-factor test for stays pending appeal and found that the balance of equities tipped sharply against leaving the government without a mechanism to fulfill its statutory duties. While the panel explicitly stated that an opinion on the ultimate merits would follow, the interim order functions as a "partial" stay because the court identified a specific, high-risk gap between the President's broad authority to suspend *admissions* (where the government is likely to succeed) and its alleged obligation to fund *services* for already-admitted refugees (where the government's likelihood of success is doubtful).

Under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), a stay applicant must show a strong likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm. The court concluded that regarding Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of new entries, the government meets this threshold based on the deferential standard established in *Trump v. Hawaii*. However, regarding the termination of cooperative agreements under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the court reasoned that "for reasons to be explained in full," the government is **not** likely to prevail on its interpretation that it can simply cease funding these services.

Because § 1522 uses mandatory language ("shall") requiring reception and placement services for refugees admitted prior to the suspension, and because Congress has appropriated funds for these purposes ($3.178 billion), leaving these agreements terminated creates a risk of irreparable injury to refugees who rely on these statutory mandates for housing, food, and medical care during winter. The court determined that maintaining the status quo (no funding) while litigation continues would cause severe hardship to third-party beneficiaries (refugees) without serving a clear governmental interest, given that § 1522 appears to mandate service delivery regardless of admission suspensions.

Consequently, rather than remanding immediately—which would leave a void in service delivery while waiting for lower-court proceedings—or staying everything entirely (which would ignore potential statutory violations), the panel crafted an equitable compromise: they stayed the injunctions preventing entry (protecting presidential authority) but lifted those same injunctions specifically regarding service delivery. This forces an immediate reinstatement of agreements to satisfy what appears to be a mandatory statutory duty (§ 1522) until a final opinion can resolve whether that duty actually exists or if it was properly waived by appropriations constraints. As Judge Lee notes in his dissent, this approach treats § 1522 as a mandate rather than mere authorization; by doing so in an interim order, the majority effectively pre-judged this specific factual/legal issue enough to dictate relief, acknowledging that "irreparable injury" is imminent if they wait for a final merits opinion before acting on this narrow point.
