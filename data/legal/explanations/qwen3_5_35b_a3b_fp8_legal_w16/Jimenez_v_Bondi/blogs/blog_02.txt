Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The High Bar for 'Past Persecution': Why Threats and Emotional Distress Alone Fail to Clear the Hurdle

**By [Your Name/Contributor]** | October 10, 2025

Immigration litigation often hinges on a deceptively simple question: When does harassment cross the line into persecution? For families fleeing violence in conflict zones, the distinction can feel academic, yet it is frequently the difference between remaining in a war zone or seeking refuge in the United States. Last week, the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals issued a stark reminder of this reality in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, denying asylum to a Colombian family whose ordeal included death threats, a colleague's murder, and direct intimidation of their children. While the court acknowledged the credibility of the petitioners' testimony, it firmly reinforced a doctrinal ceiling: without physical injury or harm beyond "restrictions or threats," even severe psychological trauma fails to constitute "past persecution" under federal law.

The *Jimenez* opinion serves as a rigorous application of the Tenth Circuit's established precedent regarding the threshold for asylum eligibility. The petitioners—a husband-and-wife team who campaigned for opposition candidates in Colombia and who intervened legally against a narco-trafficker known as "Don Rafa"—faced escalating dangers. These ranged from weekly threatening phone calls and a pamphlet declaring "death to snitches" to armed men holding Jimenez at gunpoint and harassing his young son at school. Despite these harrowing experiences, which resulted in anxiety and panic attacks among family members, the Tenth Circuit upheld the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) conclusion that these acts did not rise to the statutory level of persecution.

The crux of the court's reasoning lies in its interpretation of what constitutes "offensive" harm sufficient to trigger refugee status. The panel reiterated that while threats are relevant to an asylum claim, they are insufficient on their own. Citing *Karki v. Holder* and *Brandy v. Holder*, Judge Tymkovich emphasized that persecution requires more than sporadic threats or psychological stress; it demands suffering that goes beyond mere restrictions on liberty or life. The court explicitly rejected the argument that cumulative emotional distress—such as insomnia or panic attacks resulting from fear—could independently satisfy this element. This stance aligns with prior decisions like *Chhetri v. Rosen*, where even multiple threats and robbery attempts were deemed insufficient absent actual physical harm or severe deprivation of liberty. By maintaining this high bar, the Tenth Circuit signals that subjective fear, however rational, cannot substitute for objective evidence of severe mistreatment unless accompanied by physical consequences or analogous gravity.

This strict construction creates significant tension with broader humanitarian goals inherent in international refugee law, where psychological torture is increasingly recognized as a form of persecution comparable to physical abuse. While other circuits may occasionally weigh cumulative non-physical harms more liberally depending on specific context, the Tenth Circuit's approach prioritizes evidentiary certainty over subjective experience in borderline cases. In *Jimenez*, the court noted that previous rulings have rejected claims involving more egregious fact patterns than those presented here, suggesting that unless an applicant suffers direct physical injury or long-term confinement, courts will likely remain hesitant to find past persecution based solely on terror-inducing behavior. This doctrinal rigidity places an immense burden on applicants who face genuine danger but lack medical records documenting physical scars or injuries sustained during violent encounters.

Beyond the definition of persecution itself, *Jimenez* offers critical insights into how courts analyze causation ("nexus") and state protection when political activity intersects with criminal enterprise. The petitioners argued they were targeted for their political views supporting Federico Gutierrez against President Gustavo Petro's allies. However, the court found substantial evidence that their primary motivator was Jimenez's professional interference with Don Rafa's drug operations—not his political affiliation. The timeline was pivotal: escalation occurred only after Jimuez investigated Don Rafa's property as a lawyer and prosecutor intern, leading authorities to conclude his political identity was merely tangential to his role as a legal adversary threatening narco-revenue streams. This finding underscores a recurring difficulty in asylum cases where criminal actors target individuals for business reasons; even if those actors hold political power or affiliations, courts may sever the link between harm and protected grounds if commercial motives appear dominant.

Furthermore, *Jimenez* reinforces strict requirements regarding government protection inquiries when an applicant has not exhaustively utilized local remedies without clear justification for futility. Although Petitioners claimed police complicity due to narco-political alliances, they provided no evidence of systemic failure beyond isolated conversations with unhelpful officers who advised them to leave rather than intervene aggressively. The court highlighted that Jimenez himself had connections within Colombia's criminal justice system yet failed to file formal complaints until after fleeing abroad—a detail weighed heavily against their claim that state protection was unavailable without notice from them first. Additionally, the availability of internal relocation remained viable; since Petitioners survived temporarily at their mother's home in rural Bogotá and relatives remained safe elsewhere in Colombia despite ongoing hostilities near their workplace, the BIA correctly determined they could reasonably relocate within Colombia rather than seek asylum abroad entirely.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v.* Bondi stands as another chapter confirming that American immigration jurisprudence maintains narrow boundaries around what qualifies as "persecution." While critics might argue this standard overlooks modern realities where psychological warfare inflicts deep wounds equivalent to physical ones, judges operating within existing precedents must adhere strictly to textual definitions requiring tangible harm beyond fear-induced distress. For advocates representing clients facing similar scenarios—particularly those caught between political activism and organized crime—the lesson is clear: building an asylum case requires more than
