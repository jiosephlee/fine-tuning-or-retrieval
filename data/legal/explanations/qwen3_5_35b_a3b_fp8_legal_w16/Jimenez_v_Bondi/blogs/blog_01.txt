Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Beyond Political Labels: How the Tenth Circuit Distinguishes Criminal Motives from Protected Political Opinions in *Jimenez v. Bondi*

The intersection of political activism and organized crime creates some of the most complex scenarios in asylum law, testing the limits of what constitutes "persecution on account of" a protected ground. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the Tenth Circuit issued a sharp reminder that sharing a political label does not automatically immunize an immigrant from criminal retaliation arising from unrelated business or personal disputes. By affirming the denial of asylum for a Colombian family who actively campaigned against a rival presidential candidate yet faced violence rooted in a property dispute with a narco-leader, the court reinforced a critical doctrinal boundary: the nexus requirement demands that political opinion be a "central reason" for the harm, not merely a backdrop to private criminal grievances.

The factual tapestry in *Jimenez* is undeniably harrowing. Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family were vocal supporters of Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential election, engaging in door-to-door campaigning and enduring threats that included the kidnapping and murder of a colleague. However, the specific escalation leading to their flight occurred when Jimenez, acting as a provisional lawyer and intern for the National Prosecutor's office, intervened in a property dispute involving "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader aligned with President Gustavo Petro. When Jimez discovered that Don Rafa was using Jimenez's client's property as a front for migrant trafficking and prostitution, he attempted to mediate. The subsequent threats—gunpoint confrontations at his furniture factory and intimidation tactics aimed at his children—were explicitly linked by the perpetrators to Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's illicit operations and his perceived role as an informant for law enforcement.

The crux of the Tenth Circuit's decision rests on its rigorous application of the "nexus" element under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). While Petitioners argued that their persecution stemmed from their political opposition to Don Rafa's faction, Judge Tymkovich wrote that substantial evidence supported the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) conclusion that their political opinion was merely "tangential and subordinate." The court noted that Petitioners had faced no significant danger prior to Jimenez's direct confrontation with Don Rafa regarding the property; indeed, they continued their political work unabated even after learning of their colleague's death earlier in June 2022. It was only after Jimez threatened Don Rafa's revenue stream and security interests that violence erupted. The court emphasized that while persecutors often harbor mixed motives, asylum law requires proof that the protected ground is at least one "central reason" for the harm. Here, the record indicated Don Rafa acted primarily to protect his criminal enterprise from legal interference, not to punish Petitioners for their Liberal Party affiliations.

This distinction carries significant weight for future litigation involving individuals caught between state-aligned criminal enterprises and civil rights advocates or lawyers. The ruling underscores that an applicant cannot conflate general political hostility with specific retaliatory acts driven by economic or professional friction. If every encounter between an activist lawyer and a corrupt official resulted in asylum eligibility simply because both parties operated within polarized political spheres, courts would risk diluting the statutory definition of refugee status. The Tenth Circuit refused to allow Petitioners' broad political identity to absorb what was essentially a localized criminal dispute over land use and drug trafficking operations. By holding that the threat arose from Jimenez's professional actions rather than his ideology alone, the court preserved the integrity of the nexus test against attempts to stretch it into coverage for all forms of harassment occurring within politically charged environments.

Furthermore, *Jimenez* offers important insights into how appellate courts evaluate government protection claims when applicants have existing ties to law enforcement themselves. Petitioners argued they could not report crimes because police were complicit with Don Rafa given his proximity to President Petro's administration. However, Judge Tymkovich pointed out that Petitioners had minimal engagement with authorities beyond informal conversations where officers advised caution—a reaction described as individual apathy rather than systemic governmental refusal to act. Crucially, unlike many cases where applicants are barred from seeking help due to fear of reprisal, here Jimez worked for or near government entities but chose not to utilize those channels formally until it was arguably too late or futile given his own knowledge of police corruption locally but lack thereof nationally regarding this specific gang activity.
