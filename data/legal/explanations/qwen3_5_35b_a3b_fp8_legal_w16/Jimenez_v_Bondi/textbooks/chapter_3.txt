Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Analyzing Past Persecution – Severity and Cumulative Harm

## 'Threats Alone Are Insufficient': Precedent Distinguishing Verbal Threats from Actionable Persecution

In the realm of asylum law, the threshold for establishing "past persecution" serves as a critical gateway to eligibility. As outlined in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the definition of persecution remains fluid within the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), requiring courts to determine whether specific acts constitute "infliction of suffering or harm... regarded as offensive." However, the Tenth Circuit emphasizes that this standard demands more than mere restrictions on life or liberty; it requires a level of severity that transcends simple harassment. In *Jimenez*, the court confronted a factual scenario where the petitioners faced a litany of intimidating behaviors, ranging from weekly threatening phone calls to a menacing brochure stating "death to snitches," and culminating in armed confrontations where men held Jimenez at gunpoint. Despite the gravity of these interactions, the court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) conclusion that these incidents did not rise to the level of past persecution.

The pivotal distinction drawn by the court rests on the precedent set in *Karki v. Holder* and subsequent cases like *Brandy v. Holder*. These decisions establish a clear rule: threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution. The court reasoned that while threats are a significant component of an asylum claim and must be weighed when evaluating the totality of circumstances, they cannot stand as independent proof of persecution absent accompanying physical harm or severe consequences. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners explicitly conceded during their merits hearing that they suffered no physical harm and experienced no lasting physical injury resulting from these incidents. This concession was fatal to their claim regarding past persecution. The court noted that while physical injury is not strictly mandatory—a severe psychological assault could theoretically suffice—the harm must be "more than just restrictions or sporadic threats." The threats in this case, though frightening and indicative of danger, were viewed as preparatory or coercive measures rather than completed acts of persecution that met the statutory severity required by law.

Furthermore, the court addressed the petitioners' attempt to elevate their claim through assertions of emotional distress. Petitioners argued that their experiences caused lasting psychological trauma, citing examples such as Duarte's inability to sleep and her son's onset of panic attacks. The Tenth Circuit rejected this argument by referencing its own jurisprudence, which indicates that courts rarely grant asylum based solely on threats coupled with emotional or psychological harm derived from those threats. Citing *Chhetri v. Rosen* and *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, the opinion underscores that even fact patterns involving more extreme circumstances—such as repeated robbery attempts with weapons or prolonged detention followed by threats—have historically fallen short of the persecution threshold when physical violence was not actually inflicted. By adhering to this strict interpretation, the court clarified that subjective fear and stress, while understandable reactions to danger, do not automatically translate into legally cognizable persecution without objective evidence of severe suffering or tangible harm beyond intimidation.

## Cumulative Effect Analysis: Aggregating Incidents Without Crossing the Threshold

While individual incidents may appear insufficient in isolation, asylum applicants frequently rely on the doctrine of cumulative effect to argue that a series of lesser harms collectively amounts to persecution. The legal framework established in *Ritonga v. Holder* instructs courts not to view each incident separately but rather to consider them collectively, acknowledging that "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." In *Jimenez*, the petitioners presented a robust timeline of escalating hostility: years of political campaigning followed by targeted phone calls, a colleague's kidnapping and murder nearby, threatening literature delivered directly to their workplace, armed confrontations at gunpoint demanding they leave their neighborhood, an assault on a factory employee with explicit references to orders from "Don Rafa," and finally, a direct confrontation where a man grabbed Jimenez's son near his school before fleeing upon Jimenez's intervention.

Despite this extensive list of adverse events, applying them cumulatively did not bridge the gap between harassment and persecution in this instance. The Tenth Circuit engaged in an aggregation analysis but found that even when combining all reported events—including verbal threats, brandished weapons without discharge, and psychological terror—the total weight remained below the statutory threshold for persecution defined in prior precedents like *Wiransane*. The core deficiency identified by the court was not necessarily a lack of frequency or variety in mistreatment, but rather an absence of actual infliction of suffering that crossed into offensiveness sufficient for legal recognition. Because every incident relied upon was either purely verbal (threats/brochures) or involved intimidation without consummated violence (gunpoint displays where no shots were fired), adding them together did not transform them into acts equivalent to torture or severe bodily harm.

This analysis reinforces a crucial procedural reality for law students reading immigration opinions: courts apply substantial evidence review deferentially but rigorously when determining if facts meet legal definitions. In *Jimenez*, because there was no concession from petitioners regarding physical injury—and indeed they admitted none occurred—the court determined that no reasonable adjudicator could be compelled to find past persecution regardless of how many times they were threatened or intimidated over time. The cumulative effect doctrine is powerful; it allows isolated instances like minor assaults or brief detentions to coalesce into a valid claim if they create an environment intolerable enough for human dignity (*Ritonga*). However, in *Jimenez*, because every link in this chain lacked physical consequence or resulted only in transient fear rather than sustained suffering, the chain broke before reaching legal significance. Consequently, while the petitioners' story paints a picture of genuine peril and high anxiety characteristic of life under narco-trafficking influence in Colombia, under current Tenth Circuit precedent regarding past persecution specifically lacking physical injury, those facts remain legally categorized as less-than-persecutory harassment when viewed individually and collectively alike until actual violence occurs or is threatened with imminent execution leading beyond mere intimidation.
