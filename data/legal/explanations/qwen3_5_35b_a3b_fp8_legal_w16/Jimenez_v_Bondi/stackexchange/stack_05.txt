Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Is there a distinction between 'removal' and 'denial of asylum' when reviewing BIA credibility findings under 8 U.S.C. § 1252?
Question:
The background states Petitioners admitted removability but are appealing only on asylum/withholding grounds. Since the IJ found them credible ('IJ found Petitioners to be credible'), why did they lose on substantive grounds? I am confused about how credibility plays into these specific elements like 'nexus' or 'government protection.' If they are credible witnesses, shouldn't we accept every factual assertion they made? Where does the line fall between accepting testimony as true vs. applying legal standards (like what counts as persecution) to those accepted facts?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Credibility findings determine *what* facts the court accepts as true, whereas "denial of asylum" determines whether those accepted facts meet the legal definition of persecution, nexus, or government inability/unwillingness. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court explicitly accepted the Petitioners' testimony as credible but still denied relief because the accepted facts did not satisfy the statutory elements required for asylum.

**Detailed Explanation**

The confusion arises from conflating **factual credibility** with **legal sufficiency**. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, an appellate court reviews factual findings under a "substantial evidence" standard. If the IJ finds an applicant credible, the court must treat all material portions of that testimony as true. However, establishing that a set of facts is true does not automatically mean those facts constitute a valid legal claim for asylum. The court must then apply the relevant legal standards to those accepted facts to see if they fit the statutory definitions.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit illustrates this distinction clearly across three specific areas:

**1. Credibility Does Not Equal "Persecution"**
Even assuming every allegation made by Jimenez and his family is true (because they were found credible), the law distinguishes between "harm" and "persecution." The court notes that while threats can contribute to a finding of persecution, **"threats alone are insufficient."** Furthermore, while emotional distress is real and acknowledged by the court ("unfortunate circumstances"), it must be accompanied by harm that is "offensive" and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats.
*   **Application:** The Petitioners argued their threats caused panic attacks and insomnia. The IJ found them credible on these points, yet concluded that these specific harms, even cumulatively, did not rise to the level of "past persecution" as defined by *Wiransane* and *Karki*. Thus, credibility was established, but the legal threshold for persecution was not met.

**2. Credibility Does Not Establish "Nexus"**
A common misconception is that if you tell a story credibly about being targeted for your political views, a nexus exists. However, the law requires that political opinion be **"at least one central reason"** for the harm (*Rivera-Barrientos*). It cannot be incidental or tangential.
*   **Application:** The Petitioners testified credibly about their political campaigning and subsequent threats. However, upon analyzing those accepted facts against the timeline of events, the court found substantial evidence that the primary motive was Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's business interests (the property dispute), not his political affiliation with the Liberal Party. The court reasoned: *"Petitioners fail to establish that requirement... Predominant motive behind the threats was due to Jimenez's professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa rather than Petitioners' political opinion."* Here, accepting their testimony revealed that their politics were merely a side note to a criminal dispute over property rights; therefore, no statutory nexus existed despite their truthful testimony.

**3. Credibility Does Not Prove Government Unwillingness Without Reporting**
Finally, credibility regarding an applicant's fear does not prove government inability or unwillingness to protect if there is no record of seeking help or futility in doing so (*Singh*).
*   **Application:** While it was credible that Jimenez feared retaliation and spoke to police officers who advised him to leave, there was no substantial evidence that he formally reported specific instances of persecution to authorities or that such reporting would have been futile given prior successful police attempts to raid Don Rafa's property (as detailed in Jimenez's own testimony). The court held: *"Without such reporting... there is no way to know how police would react."* Consequently, even though his fear was genuine (credible), he failed to meet the legal element requiring proof that the state was unable or unwilling to intervene based on a lack of official engagement or systemic failure evidenced in the record.

**Conclusion**
The line falls precisely at this juncture: Once credibility is established via 8 U.S.C. § 1252 review standards, you must ask: **"Do these accepted facts legally constitute persecution on account of a protected ground?"** In *Jimenez*, they did not. The Petitioners lost not because they lied about what happened (credibility), but because what actually happened—a business dispute escalating into threats—did not legally qualify as asylum-qualifying persecution based on political opinion (*Nexus*), nor did it reach the severity threshold for *Persecution*, nor did it prove state complicity (*Government Protection*) absent proper reporting or systemic evidence of failure.
