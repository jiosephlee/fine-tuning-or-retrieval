# Chapter 1: Case Context and Procedural History

## Petitioners' Profile: Family Origins, Political Affiliation, and Professional Roles

The petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. Natives and citizens of Colombia, the family resided in Bogota prior to their departure for the United States in December 2022. Their eligibility for asylum hinges significantly on their background and public profile within their home country. Collectively, the family has been active members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012. This political affiliation placed them directly in opposition to the ruling party during the 2022 presidential election cycle. Specifically, they supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate running against President Gustavo Petro. Their activism was not passive; they engaged in door-to-door canvassing to solicit votes for Gutierrez in local neighborhoods. Beyond their political activities, Jimenez possessed a unique professional standing that intersected with his legal troubles. In 2022, he was a law student holding a provisional law license and simultaneously serving as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office in Colombia. In this dual capacity—as a legal advocate for victims of crime and a political operative—he was positioned to encounter significant risks from powerful criminal actors operating with impunity in Bogotá.

## Timeline of Alleged Harms: From Political Threats to Direct Confrontations

The narrative of alleged persecution begins in earnest around late May 2022, shortly after the peak of the election season. Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice a week specifically targeting his involvement with the Liberal Party. These threats were part of a broader pattern affecting other party leaders; notably, one colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite having reported similar threats earlier that summer. Despite this violence against peers, the petitioners remained undeterred and continued their campaign work until mid-June 2022. The nature of the harassment escalated dramatically following Jimenez's professional intervention in a property dispute unrelated to his political campaigns initially but ultimately fatal to his safety plan. Following this incident, the threats evolved from anonymous phone calls to direct physical intimidation. In July 2022, while working at his family's furniture factory at night, Jimenez was held at gunpoint by two men who identified him as a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and warned him to leave the neighborhood immediately. Two months later, these same men returned to assault an employee at the factory while demanding to know where Jimenez was hiding. The confrontation culminated days later when one of Petitioners' young sons was intercepted near his school by an associate who grabbed him and commanded him to "run to your dad." This series of escalating incidents forced the family into temporary displacement within Colombia before they fled to the United States without admission or parole three months after arriving on U.S. soil.

## The Property Dispute Incident: How Investigation Triggered Escalation

Central to understanding why violence targeted this specific family is Jimenez's professional engagement with Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros ("Don Rafa"). In June 2022, acting as a provisional lawyer representing a client in San Bernardo seeking restitution from an ex-husband refusing to vacate property transferred via divorce settlement, Jimenez conducted an on-site investigation at the disputed residence. There, he uncovered that Don Rafa—a leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas aligned with President Petro—was utilizing the property as a day-rental hub for migrants and a base for trafficking and prostitution. The premises had been linked to kidnappings and murders by local authorities who had attempted unsuccessful raids but were advised by police officers to stay away from "trouble." Upon discovering this connection between his client's property rights violation and high-level organized crime, Jimetz informed his client but also inadvertently drew attention from Don Rafa's network. The pivotal moment occurred when Don Rafa's associates realized Jimez had investigated their operations; they subsequently viewed him not merely as an activist but as a direct threat to their revenue streams and operational security because he had facilitated searches by prosecutors related to drug trafficking activities occurring on his turf. This realization transformed Don Rafa from a distant political figure into an immediate adversary willing to use lethal force against Jiminez personally due to his perceived role as an informant or "snitch" interfering with narco-trafficking interests.

## Procedural Posture: From Notice to Appeal

Upon entering the United States without inspection or parole three months after arrival (March 2023), Petitioners faced immediate immigration consequences when the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear charging them as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). By August 2023, Petitioners appeared before an Immigration Judge (IJ) where they admitted truthfully all allegations regarding removability but sought affirmative relief through applications for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). During these proceedings before the IJ, although credibility was established regarding their testimony concerning threats and fear of return, the IJ denied all forms of relief. # Chapter 2: Standards of Review and Legal Framework for Asylum

When a law student first encounters an immigration appeal like *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the sheer volume of factual detail regarding threats, property disputes, and family dynamics can obscure the specific legal questions the court must answer. Before diving into the facts of the Jimenez family's ordeal, it is essential to understand the "lens" through which the Tenth Circuit viewed their case. This chapter establishes the foundational legal framework governing asylum claims and explains the highly deferential standard of review that governs appellate decisions in this field. Understanding these mechanics is crucial because, as the court's opinion reveals, even sympathetic factual narratives can fail to meet strict statutory thresholds if they do not align with these specific legal definitions and evidentiary standards.

### Statutory Definition of Refugee
At the heart of every asylum claim lies a precise statutory definition found in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The court begins its analysis by grounding itself in 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), which defines a "refugee" as any person outside their country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of five specific grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. This list is exhaustive; an applicant cannot qualify for asylum simply because they are fleeing violence or poverty. The harm must be linked to one of these protected characteristics. In *Jimenez*, while the petitioners argued they faced harm due to their political affiliation with the Liberal Party and opposition to President Petro, the court scrutinized whether this "political opinion" was actually the driver of their persecution or merely incidental to other factors. This statutory gatekeeping mechanism ensures that asylum remains a remedy for specific types of discrimination rather than a general safety valve for all immigrants facing hardship abroad.

### Past Persecution vs. Well-Founded Fear
The legal pathway to asylum often bifurcates into two distinct but related concepts: proof of past persecution and proof of a well-founded fear of future persecution. The *Jimenez* opinion highlights how these concepts interact procedurally. An applicant may establish eligibility by demonstrating that they have already suffered "past persecution." If successful, this finding triggers a "rebuttable presumption" that they have a well-founded fear of future persecution, shifting the burden slightly onto the government to prove otherwise (such as by showing safe internal relocation). However, if an applicant fails to prove past persecution rises to the requisite level, they bear the heavier burden of independently proving a "well-founded fear" of future harm without any presumption in their favor. In *Jimenez*, because the petitioners failed to clear the threshold for past persecution—a hurdle explored in depth below—they were forced to rely entirely on their fear of future events, which ultimately collapsed under scrutiny regarding government protection and safe relocation options within Colombia.

### The Three Elements of Past Persecution
To succeed on a claim based on past persecution, an applicant must affirmatively prove three distinct elements simultaneously: severity (the act constitutes persecution), nexus (the act was on account of a protected ground), and state involvement (the government was unable or unwilling to control the perpetrator). First, regarding severity, the court notes that while physical injury is not strictly required, mere threats or restrictions on liberty are insufficient; the harm must be "offensive" and entail more than sporadic intimidation. Second, concerning nexus, even if severe harm occurred, it must be shown that one central reason for that harm was one of the five protected grounds listed in the statute; mixed motives are permissible only if at least one protected ground played a central role rather than being tangential or subordinate. Third, regarding state involvement in cases involving private actors like drug lords or gangs (as seen with Don Rafa), applicants must show that their home government is incapable or unwilling to control those persecutors. In *Jimenez*, the court systematically dismantled each element: finding no sufficient severity beyond threats alone; concluding that political opinion was subordinate to financial grievances over property; and determining there was insufficient evidence that the Colombian government could not protect them given prior attempts at law enforcement intervention at similar sites.

### Standard of Review
Perhaps most critical for understanding why *Jimenez* lost despite detailed testimony is the standard of review applied by federal appellate courts in immigration cases. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(B), courts do not retry cases de novo; instead, they apply a "substantial evidence" standard when reviewing factual findings made by Immigration Judges (IJ) or Board members (BIA). This standard is exceptionally deferential: BIA findings are conclusive unless any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude otherwise based solely on evidence contained in the administrative record created during proceedings before them. This means that even if an appellate judge might have weighed different pieces of evidence differently had they been sitting as an IJ initially, they cannot reverse unless no reasonable decision-maker could have reached that conclusion given what was presented below. In *Jimenez*, Circuit Judge Tymkovich repeatedly invoked this standard ("there is substantial evidence supporting..."), signaling that regardless of how tragic Petitioners' story appeared narratively absent from direct physical injury—the court upheld lower tribunal determinations because those findings were supported by enough evidence in the record to be legally defensible under this high bar for reversal.

# Chapter 3: Analyzing Past Persecution – Severity and Cumulative Harm

## 'Threats Alone Are Insufficient': Precedent Distinguishing Verbal Threats from Actionable Persecution

In the realm of asylum law, the threshold for establishing "past persecution" serves as a critical gateway to eligibility. As outlined in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the definition of persecution remains fluid within the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), requiring courts to determine whether specific acts constitute "infliction of suffering or harm... regarded as offensive." However, the Tenth Circuit emphasizes that this standard demands more than mere restrictions on life or liberty; it requires a level of severity that transcends simple harassment. In *Jimenez*, the court confronted a factual scenario where the petitioners faced a litany of intimidating behaviors, ranging from weekly threatening phone calls to a menacing brochure stating "death to snitches," and culminating in armed confrontations where men held Jimenez at gunpoint. Despite the gravity of these interactions, the court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) conclusion that these incidents did not rise to the level of past persecution.

The pivotal distinction drawn by the court rests on the precedent set in *Karki v. Holder* and subsequent cases like *Brandy v. Holder*. These decisions establish a clear rule: threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution. The court reasoned that while threats are a significant component of an asylum claim and must be weighed when evaluating the totality of circumstances, they cannot stand as independent proof of persecution absent accompanying physical harm or severe consequences. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners explicitly conceded during their merits hearing that they suffered no physical harm and experienced no lasting physical injury resulting from these incidents. This concession was fatal to their claim regarding past persecution. The court noted that while physical injury is not strictly mandatory—a severe psychological assault could theoretically suffice—the harm must be "more than just restrictions or sporadic threats." The threats in this case, though frightening and indicative of danger, were viewed as preparatory or coercive measures rather than completed acts of persecution that met the statutory severity required by law.

Furthermore, the court addressed the petitioners' attempt to elevate their claim through assertions of emotional distress. Petitioners argued that their experiences caused lasting psychological trauma, citing examples such as Duarte's inability to sleep and her son's onset of panic attacks. The Tenth Circuit rejected this argument by referencing its own jurisprudence, which indicates that courts rarely grant asylum based solely on threats coupled with emotional or psychological harm derived from those threats. Citing *Chhetri v. Rosen* and *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, the opinion underscores that even fact patterns involving more extreme circumstances—such as repeated robbery attempts with weapons or prolonged detention followed by threats—have historically fallen short of the persecution threshold when physical violence was not actually inflicted. By adhering to this strict interpretation, the court clarified that subjective fear and stress, while understandable reactions to danger, do not automatically translate into legally cognizable persecution without objective evidence of severe suffering or tangible harm beyond intimidation.

## Cumulative Effect Analysis: Aggregating Incidents Without Crossing the Threshold

While individual incidents may appear insufficient in isolation, asylum applicants frequently rely on the doctrine of cumulative effect to argue that a series of lesser harms collectively amounts to persecution. The legal framework established in *Ritonga v. Holder* instructs courts not to view each incident separately but rather to consider them collectively, acknowledging that "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." In *Jimenez*, the petitioners presented a robust timeline of escalating hostility: years of political campaigning followed by targeted phone calls, a colleague's kidnapping and murder nearby, threatening literature delivered directly to their workplace, armed confrontations at gunpoint demanding they leave their neighborhood, an assault on a factory employee with explicit references to orders from "Don Rafa," and finally, a direct confrontation where a man grabbed Jimenez's son near his school before fleeing upon Jimenez's intervention.

Despite this extensive list of adverse events, applying them cumulatively did not bridge the gap between harassment and persecution in this instance. The Tenth Circuit engaged in an aggregation analysis but found that even when combining all reported events—including verbal threats, brandished weapons without discharge, and psychological terror—the total weight remained below the statutory threshold for persecution defined in prior precedents like *Wiransane*. The core deficiency identified by the court was not necessarily a lack of frequency or variety in mistreatment, but rather an absence of actual infliction of suffering that crossed into offensiveness sufficient for legal recognition. Because every incident relied upon was either purely verbal (threats/brochures) or involved intimidation without consummated violence (gunpoint displays where no shots were fired), adding them together did not transform them into acts equivalent to torture or severe bodily harm.

This analysis reinforces a crucial procedural reality for law students reading immigration opinions: courts apply substantial evidence review deferentially but rigorously when determining if facts meet legal definitions. In *Jimenez*, because there was no concession from petitioners regarding physical injury—and indeed they admitted none occurred—the court determined that no reasonable adjudicator could be compelled to find past persecution regardless of how many times they were threatened or intimidated over time. The cumulative effect doctrine is powerful; it allows isolated instances like minor assaults or brief detentions to coalesce into a valid claim if they create an environment intolerable enough for human dignity (*Ritonga*). However, in *Jimenez*, because every link in this chain lacked physical consequence or resulted only in transient fear rather than sustained suffering, the chain broke before reaching legal significance. Consequently, while the petitioners' story paints a picture of genuine peril and high anxiety characteristic of life under narco-trafficking influence in Colombia, under current Tenth Circuit precedent regarding past persecution specifically lacking physical injury, those facts remain legally categorized as less-than-persecutory harassment when viewed individually and collectively alike until actual violence occurs or is threatened with imminent execution leading beyond mere intimidation.
