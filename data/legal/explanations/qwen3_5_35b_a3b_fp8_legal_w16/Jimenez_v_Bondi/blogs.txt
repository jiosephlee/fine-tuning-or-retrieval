# Report It or Lose It: The Fatal Flaw of Unreported Harm in Asylum Claims Regarding State Complicity

**By [Your Name]**

In the high-stakes arena of immigration appeals, the difference between survival and deportation often hinges on a single procedural act: Did the applicant ask for help? In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided last week by the Tenth Circuit, the court issued a stern reminder that in asylum cases alleging state complicity, silence is not golden—it is fatal. The panel's unanimous denial of relief for a Colombian family facing narco-guerrilla threats underscores a persistent doctrinal reality: without a demonstrable attempt to seek state protection—or a convincing explanation of why such an attempt would be futile—an asylum seeker cannot overcome the "unable or unwilling" prong of the refugee definition.

The case involves Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, who fled Colombia after escalating harassment linked to their political activism and a specific legal dispute with Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros ("Don Rafa"), a narco-trafficking leader aligned with President Gustavo Petro. The facts painted a harrowing picture: threatening phone calls, a colleague's execution, gunpoint confrontations at their factory, and a terrifying encounter where Jimenez's young son was grabbed and threatened at school. Despite this violence, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) conclusion that the family failed to establish that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

The crux of the court's reasoning lies in its rigorous application of the "notice" doctrine. As established in prior Tenth Circuit precedent like *Singh v. Bondi*, an agency's inability to protect an applicant is difficult to prove if the government never knew about the persecution in question. The court emphasized that while an applicant need not exhaust every possible avenue if reporting would be futile or dangerous, they must first create a record showing they attempted to engage authorities or explain why engagement was impossible. Here, the Jimenezes walked into this trap repeatedly.

Despite holding positions within Colombia's legal and prosecutorial systems—Jimenez as a provisional lawyer for the National Prosecutor's office and his father as another government employee—the family did not file formal police reports detailing these escalating threats until perhaps moments before departure, and even then, only verbally speaking to two officers who allegedly advised them to "leave." The court noted that this minimal effort stood in stark contrast to their ability to leverage their professional networks. By failing to report these crimes through official channels or utilizing their own insider knowledge of how the system functions, they deprived themselves of evidence needed to prove systemic government failure.

This brings us to the most contentious aspect of *Jimenez*: the allegation that Don Rafa is intimately connected to the current administration, rendering all state protection illusory. Petitioners argued that seeking help from police would be futile because their persecutors were effectively untouchable due to political ties. However, the Tenth Circuit rejected this speculative argument based on concrete counter-evidence in the record: law enforcement had actually attempted multiple searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property despite its use for illicit activities like migrant trafficking and prostitution. If police were willing enough to raid Narco-Rafa's compound—a known hub for serious crime—the inference that they would refuse protection simply because he is politically aligned was deemed unsubstantiated by anything other than conjecture.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Jimenez* reinforces a significant barrier for asylum seekers claiming persecution by non-state actors with alleged government ties. The ruling signals that courts will scrutinize an applicant's "futility" defense with extreme skepticism unless backed by robust evidence of systemic collapse or specific threats against those attempting aid. The court distinguished between isolated instances of police apathy—which might suggest individual corruption—and proof of governmental unwillingness as an institution. A single conversation with an unhelpful officer does not equate to state capitulation; conversely, active attempts by law enforcement against similar criminals suggest otherwise.

Furthermore, *Jimenez* highlights how factual nuances regarding motivation can derail entire asylum claims before reaching the protection analysis entirely. The court found that while political activity provided context for initial harassment, the primary driver for Don Rafa's wrath was Jimenez's interference in his business interests via property disputes and legal investigations—not purely ideological opposition. When persecution stems from economic or criminal retaliation rather than political opinion alone, it becomes harder to satisfy statutory requirements regardless of government responsiveness. This nexus failure meant that even if Jimenez had filed dozens of police reports, his claim might still have faltered on whether his harm was "on account" of his political opinion rather than his professional actions against traffickers.

The downstream implications for immigration practice are clear: counsel representing clients fleeing countries with complex power dynamics involving drug cartels or warlords must prioritize documentation of interaction with state authorities earlier in the timeline than ever before. Anecdotal testimony about fearing reprisal is increasingly insufficient when juxtaposed with evidence that law enforcement has acted against similar perpetrators elsewhere in the country. Applicants must articulate precisely why seeking help was impossible—perhaps citing specific threats made directly against them for intending to report crimes—rather than relying on generalized assumptions about national corruption levels tied to current leadership.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a cautionary tale about the gap between perceived danger and legal eligibility for asylum protections rooted in state failure. For families like Jimenez's whose lives were upended by genuine terror in Colombia, winning relief required more than surviving violence; it required navigating a bureaucratic labyrinth where silence was interpreted as consent or indifference rather than fear-induced paralysis. Until Congress clarifies these standards or higher courts shift their approach toward greater deference on futility defenses in highly polarized regimes, applicants will face steep odds proving they truly had nowhere else to turn but abroad—and even then, only if they can prove they tried everything else first inside their homeland's borders anyway?
