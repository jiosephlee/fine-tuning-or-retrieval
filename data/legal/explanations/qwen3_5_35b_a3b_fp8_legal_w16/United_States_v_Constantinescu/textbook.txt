# Chapter 1: Procedural Background and the District Court's Dismissal

## The Alleged Scheme: Social Media-Based 'Pump and Dump'

To understand the legal questions presented in *United States v. Constantinescu*, one must first grasp the factual mechanics of the alleged criminal enterprise described in the government's complaint. The case arises from a "pump and dump" scheme executed primarily through social media platforms. Eight defendants—including Edward Constantinescu, Perry "PJ" Matlock, and seven others—utilized their substantial online followings to manipulate stock prices. These individuals cultivated public personas as expert stock traders, frequently broadcasting their own trading activities to build credibility with their audiences.

The government's narrative describes a specific three-step process designed to generate illicit profits. First, the defendants would quietly purchase shares of a particular security at a low price. Second, they would engage in a "pump" phase by posting false and misleading information regarding that security on social media. These posts allegedly misrepresented both their own financial positions in the stock and the potential future price of the securities themselves. The objective of these misrepresentations was to induce unsuspecting followers to purchase the stock, thereby driving up demand and artificially inflating the share price. Finally, once the price reached an artificial peak, the defendants would execute a "dump," secretly selling their previously purchased shares for a significant profit before the market corrected itself. According to the indictment, this coordinated effort resulted in approximately $114 million in ill-gotten gains for the group between them. This factual backdrop is crucial because it frames the core conflict: whether deceiving investors via social media posts to induce them to buy stocks constitutes criminal fraud when no physical property changes hands initially, but rather capital flows from buyers to sellers based on misinformation.

## The Superseding Indictment: Conspiracy and Securities Fraud Counts

Following these allegations, federal prosecutors issued a superseding indictment charging all eight defendants with serious federal offenses. Specifically, the indictment charged each defendant with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple substantive counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Section 1348 makes it unlawful to knowingly execute a scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, or to obtain money or property by means of false pretenses related to such transactions. Section 2 addresses individual liability for aiding and abetting such crimes, while section 1349 covers conspiracies to violate federal laws involving fraud.

The procedural posture is further complicated by the fact that one defendant, Daniel Knight, subsequently entered a plea agreement and pleaded guilty to the charges mentioned above. However, seven co-defendants contested their guilt by filing motions to dismiss the indictment prior to trial. Their motion challenged not just specific facts within the indictment but its fundamental legal sufficiency—the claim that even if every allegation in the document were proven true as written, it still failed to constitute a crime under existing law. This set up a critical threshold issue for the district court: determining whether an indictment need only describe conduct that sounds like fraud or whether it must strictly satisfy specific statutory elements regarding what constitutes "property" or "harm" under modern judicial interpretations of fraud statutes.

## District Court Analysis: The Property Interest Requirement

The United States District Court granted the defendants' motions and dismissed the entire indictment sua sponte after reviewing its face alone—a standard procedure known as assessing whether an indictment states an offense upon which relief can be granted. In reaching this conclusion, the district court focused heavily on recent Supreme Court jurisprudence concerning what constitutes "property" for purposes of federal fraud statutes, specifically drawing parallels to *Ciminelli v. United States*.

The district court reasoned that while traditional fraud involves stealing tangible assets or cash directly from victims' pockets, this case involved a different dynamic: defendants allegedly sought only to deprive their social media followers of valuable economic information (specifically truthful data regarding trading positions) rather than seizing a traditional property interest from them immediately upon posting false statements. The court interpreted *Ciminelli* broadly enough to suggest that where a defendant schemes only to withhold information necessary for informed decision-making—without necessarily taking possession of funds at that exact moment—they have not infringed upon a protected property right recognized by federal law under theories akin to "right-to-control." Consequently, because the district court viewed the core grievance as an informational deprivation rather than a direct theft of money or property at its inception, it concluded that even accepting all factual allegations as true did not establish all elements of securities fraud as defined by current precedent. This ruling effectively created a barrier at Stage One of litigation: if an indictment relies on an informational deprivation theory rather than direct asset acquisition, it is legally insufficient from day one.

## The Disposition: Dismissal Based on Failure to State an Offense

Based on this analysis regarding property interests, the district court formally dismissed all counts against six of the remaining defendants (excluding Knight), holding that they had been denied due process rights because they were being prosecuted for conduct that did not amount to a crime under applicable statutes as interpreted by lower courts following *Ciminelli*. By finding that neither conspiracy nor substantive securities fraud could be sustained without proving interference with traditional property interests distinct from mere economic information control, the district court ruled there was no valid basis for proceeding further against them criminally at this time.

However, this dismissal triggered immediate appellate review by the United States Government via appeal pursuant to Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b). The government argued that its characterization of events differed fundamentally from what occurred; specifically, they contended that inducing investors via lies into purchasing stocks constituted obtaining money through fraudulent inducement—a classic form of property deprivation—not simply withholding information about rights or options available elsewhere in markets governed by free-flowing data streams (as seen in *Ciminelli*). Thus arose sharply contrasting views on whether social media manipulation falls outside federal reach due to abstract informational harms or remains firmly within it as direct financial predation disguised as investment advice preparation for market exit strategies ("pumping"). # Chapter 2: Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment, the trial court must determine whether the charging document legally supports the prosecution's case before proceeding to trial. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addressed the appropriate standard for evaluating such motions, establishing a rigorous framework for assessing whether an indictment is legally sufficient. This chapter examines the appellate standard of review, the specific constitutional thresholds an indictment must clear, and how these principles apply to complex fraud schemes involving securities markets. Understanding this standard is essential for recognizing why the district court erred in dismissing the "pump and dump" charges and how the Fifth Circuit reversed that decision based on a strict application of pleading requirements.

## De Novo Review of Indictment Sufficiency

The starting point for any challenge to an indictment's validity is the scope of appellate review. In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit emphasized that it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*. This means the appellate court does not defer to the district court's conclusion regarding whether the indictment states an offense; rather, it makes an independent determination based on the record below. Under this standard, as established in *United States v. Rafoi* and *United States v. Fontenot*, the court is required to accept every factual allegation contained within the indictment as true. The appellate judge does not weigh evidence or assess credibility but assumes the government's version of events is accurate for the purpose of determining if a crime has been alleged.

This presumption in favor of truthfulness serves a critical procedural function: it ensures that indictments are judged on their face rather than on what might emerge during discovery or trial. If taking all allegations as true still fails to describe conduct prohibited by law, then no amount of evidence presented later can save a defective charge. Conversely, if the allegations, assumed true, constitute a violation of federal statute, the motion to dismiss must fail regardless of whether those facts might eventually prove difficult to support at trial. In *Constantinescu*, applying this principle meant accepting the government's detailed narrative that defendants posted false information to inflate stock prices and sell their own shares at a profit, without needing to verify those facts immediately.

## The Three Requirements of a Valid Indictment

To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 7(c)(1) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure and satisfy constitutional due process concerns, an indictment must meet three distinct criteria outlined in *United States v. Gaytan*. First, it must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense. Second, it must fairly inform the defendant of the nature and cause of the accusations filed against him, ensuring he can prepare an adequate defense. Third, it must provide protection against double jeopardy by describing facts with enough specificity that they prevent future prosecutions for substantially similar conduct arising from different criminal intents or acts occurring after dismissal but related to same transactional history? No wait - third prong is providing double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." Essentially, this means identifying facts with enough particularity so that if acquitted or convicted today, one cannot be tried again tomorrow for essentially same thing unless new crimes committed later unrelated previous ones."

The core inquiry synthesized from these requirements is whether "each material element" of the offense has been alleged (*United States v. Guzman-Ocampo*). An indictment need not include evidentiary details—such as dates, times, locations beyond basic jurisdictional necessity, or witness names—but it must explicitly plead every constituent part of the statutory crime defined by Congress and interpreted by case law. # Chapter 3: Defining 'Scheme to Defraud' in Modern Securities Law

## 'Scheme to Defraud': Material Misrepresentation and Intent

To understand the holding in *United States v. Constantinescu*, one must first grasp the statutory framework governing the case. The defendants were charged under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the federal securities fraud statute. While § 1348 specifically prohibits schemes "to defraud any person in connection with... any security," the Fifth Circuit notes that there is limited circuit-specific caselaw directly interpreting this section. Consequently, courts look to established principles derived from the mail and wire fraud statutes, which serve as the conceptual backbone for modern fraud analysis. The court emphasizes that § 1348 borrows these key concepts, requiring prosecutors to prove two distinct elements: a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud."

The "scheme to defraud" element necessitates a material misrepresentation made with the specific purpose of deceiving another party to obtain something of value. This is not merely a claim of bad faith or poor judgment; it requires a tangible act of deception designed to extract value from a victim. Similarly, the "intent to defraud" element demands that the defendant possessed a dual intent: first, an intent to deceive, and second, an intent that harm result from that deception. However, as *Constantinescu* illustrates through its reliance on recent Supreme Court authority, the nature of that harm and what constitutes a protected property interest are critical distinctions that define whether conduct rises to the level of criminal fraud. The indictment in *Constantinescu* alleged that defendants purchased securities and then utilized social media posts containing false and misleading information to induce followers to buy those same securities, thereby artificially inflating prices before dumping them for profit. The core legal question became whether this conduct satisfied the statutory requirements or if it fell outside the scope of criminal liability due to deficiencies in how property interests were defined.

## 'Intent to Defraud': Deception and Harm Requirements

A central contention raised by the defendants was whether they possessed the requisite "intent to defraud" given their motive was personal enrichment rather than direct financial injury to their victims. Defendants argued that because they provided followers with market-value securities (even if acquired via manipulation), they did not intend to cause harm or deprive victims of property; they simply intended to profit from their own trading positions. This argument hinges on a misunderstanding of what constitutes fraud under current federal law, particularly following recent Supreme Court jurisprudence regarding economic loss.

The court rejects this distinction between seeking profit and causing harm, clarifying that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a victim suffers a net pecuniary loss or an economic downturn in their overall financial position. Drawing heavily on *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), the Fifth Circuit explains that liability attaches when a defendant schemes "to obtain" money or property through false pretenses, regardless of whether they simultaneously provide something of equal value in return or seek to leave the victim financially worse off than before the transaction occurred. In *Kousisis*, the Court clarified that providing services or goods in exchange for funds does not negate fraud if those exchanges were procured through deceptive means designed to induce a transfer of property rights. Therefore, in *Constantinescu*, the fact that defendants allegedly intended only to profit—and not necessarily to ruin their followers' portfolios—does not absolve them of criminal intent. The indictment successfully alleged an intent to defraud because it demonstrated an objective desire to obtain money (from investors purchasing stocks) through deception (false social media posts), satisfying both prongs of the intent test without needing proof of ultimate economic devastation for every follower involved.

## *Ciminelli* Revisited: The Limitations on Economic Information as Property

The district court initially dismissed the indictment based on a concern rooted in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023). In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court addressed whether withholding valuable economic information could constitute theft under wire fraud statutes if framed as depriving a victim of their "right-to-control" assets based on incomplete data arguments (*right-to-control theory*). The Court held firmly that while individuals have rights regarding how they manage their assets, those rights do not extend so far as making potentially valuable economic information itself a form of protected property interest under federal fraud laws. A defendant cannot be convicted merely for depriving someone else of useful information; there must be an interference with traditional property interests like money or physical goods.

Applying this principle is essential for understanding why *Constantinescu* survived dismissal despite involving complex financial instruments and social media influence where information asymmetry played a major role. If defendants had simply withheld trade secrets or manipulated data feeds without inducing an immediate transfer of cash or securities ownership based on lies about existing positions, they might have fallen within *Ciminelli*'s prohibition against punishing pure informational deprivation alone. However, the appellate court distinguishes *Constantinescu* by noting that neither side relied on the right-to-control theory here; instead, prosecutors focused entirely on direct transfers of capital induced by specific falsehoods regarding trading positions and stock values (*fraudulent-inducement*). By framing the scheme around obtaining actual funds from followers through active deception rather than merely controlling decision-making processes via hidden information, defendants' actions remained within constitutional bounds after *Ciminelli*. Thus, while *Ciminelli* closed one door—the prosecution's ability to sue over lost opportunities based solely on missing info—it left open another path where explicit lies drive direct financial transactions involving traditional property interests like currency and equity stakes in public companies (*securities*).

## Distinguishing Right-to-Control from Fraudulent Inducement

The final analytical step involves contrasting two theoretical frameworks often confused by litigants facing modern digital-era fraud charges: the right-to-control theory versus fraudulent inducement theory (*fraudulent-inducement theory*). Under right-to-control theories—which were effectively dismantled by *Ciminelli*—prosecutors attempt to argue that victims possess intangible rights over how third parties use internal knowledge affecting asset management decisions (*rights-of-discretion*). Such theories fail because they expand "property" beyond traditional boundaries into abstract concepts like autonomy over investment choices absent concrete transfers (*deprivation-of-information-only*). Conversely, fraudulent inducement operates differently; it focuses strictly upon using material misstatements ("lies") specifically crafted ("trickery") so victims voluntarily surrender hard assets ("money/property"). 

In *Constantinescu*, this distinction proved decisive because all four elements aligned perfectly with fraudulent inducement standards upheld post-*Kousisis*: First, defendants made false statements about trading positions (*misrepresentation*); Second, these statements caused followers part ways with capital (*induced purchase*); Third, money changed hands directly (*obtained-property-interest*); Fourth no need exists proving long-term portfolio damage since initial acquisition itself constituted injury once obtained via deceit (*complete-fraud-upon-transfer*). # Chapter 4: Applying *Kousisis* to Social Media Pump-and-Dump Schemes

## *Kousisis*: Profit Motive Does Not Negate Intent to Defraud

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit confronts a critical interpretive question arising from recent Supreme Court jurisprudence: whether a defendant's specific intent to profit, rather than specifically intend to cause financial harm to a victim, negates the "intent to defraud" required under federal fraud statutes. The district court had dismissed the indictment against the social media influencers, accepting their argument that they lacked criminal intent because their goal was self-enrichment, not the infliction of damage upon their followers. However, the appellate court reversed this dismissal by applying the logic established in *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court clarified that the core of fraud lies in the scheme to obtain another's money or property through deception, not necessarily in a subjective desire to leave that victim poorer than before.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* conceded that they made material misrepresentations but argued that these acts did not constitute fraud because they did not aim to injure their followers' "bottom lines." They contended that because they provided followers with securities that might hold market value, there was no intent to defraud absent an intent to cause net harm. The Fifth Circuit rejected this distinction as "without a difference." Drawing directly from *Kousisis*, the court emphasized that fraud statutes are satisfied when a defendant uses false pretenses to induce a victim to hand over money or property. The statute protects the integrity of transactions where consent is obtained through lies; it does not require proof that the transaction resulted in a net economic loss for the victim or that the perpetrator specifically desired such a loss. By focusing on whether the defendant had "money in mind"—that is, whether obtaining funds was an object of their conduct—the court found that the defendants' profit motive actually corroborated, rather than negated, their intent to defraud. Their scheme was designed precisely so they could obtain money through deception while providing something else in return.

## The Irrelevance of Net Pecuniary Loss in Fraud Statutes

A central pillar of the Fifth Circuit's analysis in *Constantinescu* is its application of Rule 1392 from *Kousisis*, which establishes that federal fraud laws do not mandate a "mirror image" relationship between what a defendant takes and what they give back. Under this framework, it is legally insufficient for a defendant to claim innocence simply because they delivered goods or services of perceived fair market value at the time of the transaction. In *Ciminelli v. United States*, previously decided by the Supreme Court, just eight months prior, Justice Barrett noted that while certain non-traditional interests like "information rights" are protected differently, traditional property interests remain robustly covered when obtained by deception. However, *Ciminelli* distinguished itself from cases involving tangible exchanges where property changes hands; it did not suggest that receiving equal value absolves one of liability if that exchange was procured through fraud.

The Fifth Circuit applied this nuance strictly within the context of social media trading schemes. The defendants argued that because their followers received securities worth what they paid (or perhaps more), no "injury" occurred in a traditional sense. The court countered by explaining that fraud is complete once money is transferred based on materially false pretenses, irrespective of subsequent market fluctuations or immediate value equivalence. As noted in *Kousisis*, a defendant may provide something of equal value in return and still be liable for fraud if that provision was merely part of a scheme to appropriate property through deceit. Therefore, in *Constantinescu*, the fact that followers purchased stocks at inflated prices due to misinformation did not negate the existence of an injury for legal purposes; rather, it highlighted exactly how much money was being extracted through artificial inflation caused by lies. The law focuses on the illegality of obtaining possession through trickery, not on balancing ledgers after the fact. This principle ensures that actors cannot immunize themselves from prosecution simply because they structured their scams so victims received nominal consideration at face value during peak manipulation moments.

## 'Obtaining Money or Property' as Sufficient Injury

To sustain an indictment for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and related conspiracy statutes, prosecutors must allege both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud involving an attempt to obtain money or property from another person using false pretenses (*Evans*; *Greenlaw*). In dismissing this case initially, the district court appeared confused about what constituted "property" or "injury" within these statutory definitions following early post-*Ciminelli* confusion regarding intangible rights versus tangible assets. However, upon closer inspection using guidance from both *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, it became clear that securities themselves—and crucially, cash—are indisputable forms of protected property interest under federal law when acquired via fraudulent inducement (*Ciminelli* distinguishes information-only schemes but affirms protection for monetary transfers).

The Fifth Circuit reasoned that when defendants posted false information about trading positions and price potentials with the specific purpose of inducing purchases ("pumping"), they were actively soliciting followers' capital (cash/property) based on fabricated premises ("false and misleading information"). This fits squarely within the "fraudulent-inducement theory" affirmed in *Kousisis*. Under this theory, liability attaches whenever a material misstatement tricks a victim into contracting for an asset transfer—specifically handing over money—in reliance on those falsehoods. The injury defined by statute is not limited to total ruin; it encompasses any unauthorized deprivation resulting from deception used as leverage for acquisition ($114 million alleged profit). Thus, even if followers eventually sold shares at break-even points later on (hypothetically), or if some shares retained intrinsic value independent of manipulation, those facts do not erase the initial act of securing funds through lies. The indictment successfully alleged this dynamic: defendants created artificial demand via deception specifically so others would surrender cash into accounts controlled by defendants or entities linked to them ("dumping"). Consequently, obtaining money via fraudulent inducement satisfies all elements required for criminal liability without needing additional proof of net loss per se at trial stage for pleading sufficiency purposes (*Fontenot*).

# Chapter 5: Holding and Remedy

## Summary of Legal Analysis

The Fifth Circuit's analysis culminates in a definitive rejection of the district court's narrow interpretation of the securities fraud statute, clarifying how modern fraud theories coexist with recent Supreme Court constraints on "right-to-control" claims. The appellate court applied a *de novo* standard of review, accepting all factual allegations within the superseding indictment as true to determine if the charged offenses were legally sufficient. The core legal dispute turned on two distinct elements derived from 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and interpreted through the lens of *Ciminelli v. United States* and *United States v. Kousisis*: whether the alleged conduct constituted a "scheme to defraud" involving a protected property interest, and whether it demonstrated an "intent to defraud."

Regarding the "scheme to defraud," the court distinguished the defendants' conduct from the specific right-to-control theory invalidated in *Ciminelli*. While *Ciminelli* barred convictions based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information necessary for discretionary decision-making, it left intact the "fraudulent-inducement theory." The Fifth Circuit found that the indictment successfully alleged this latter theory. By misrepresenting their trading positions and inducing followers to purchase securities, the defendants allegedly caused victims to part with tangible money or property. This direct deprivation of funds via material misrepresentation satisfied the requirement that fraud involve a traditional property interest, distinguishing these facts from a scenario where a victim merely loses access to confidential data without surrendering assets. Consequently, the court held that obtaining money through fraudulent inducement remains actionable even if the transaction involves complex digital assets or social media influence peddling.

On the issue of "intent to defraud," the court addressed the defendants' contention that their motive was self-enrichment rather than causing direct harm to victims, arguing that such an intent fell outside statutory requirements following *Kousisis*. Relying on *Kousisis*, which clarified that fraud statutes punish schemes designed to obtain money or property regardless of whether the victim suffers a net economic loss or receives goods/services of equal value, the Fifth Circuit ruled that actual injury is not a prerequisite for liability. The indictment's allegation that defendants engineered a "pump and dump"—buying low, artificially inflating prices through lies, and then selling high—demonstrated an intent to deceive followers into handing over capital. The court emphasized that once a defendant induces a victim to transfer money under false pretenses, the fraud is complete; it matters not whether the victim received securities worth what they paid or whether their primary grievance was lost opportunity rather than immediate cash loss. Thus, both essential elements of securities fraud were deemed sufficiently pled.

## Disposition: Reversal and Remand

Having concluded that the district court erred in dismissing the indictment for failing to state an offense upon which relief can be granted, the Fifth Circuit exercised its jurisdiction to reverse that judgment in full. The appellate panel determined that no error occurred in charging defendants with conspiracy and substantive securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1349, 1348, and 2 based on their alleged social media activities. Because an indictment serves three critical functions—enumerating elements of crime, informing defendants of charges, and providing notice for double jeopardy purposes—the appellate court found these functions were met despite its prior concerns regarding property definitions in digital contexts.

Accordingly, while affirming that strict adherence to traditional property interests limits certain abstract fraud theories like right-to-control claims post-*Ciminelli*, the court reversed specifically because this case relied on fraudulent inducement involving tangible monetary transfers. The disposition instructs lower courts on how to apply these distinctions: where an indictment alleges that false statements directly procured money or property from victims, it survives dismissal even if those transactions occur in nascent markets like crypto-assets or influencer-driven stocks. The judgment below is vacated, effectively restoring the case to its pre-dismissal status but now bound by this authoritative interpretation of § 1348's scope relative to Supreme Court precedent established earlier in 2025.

## Significance: Implications for Future Digital Asset Prosecutions

This holding carries substantial weight for prosecutors navigating enforcement actions in rapidly evolving digital financial landscapes. By rejecting a rigid reading that equates "property" strictly with physical currency while simultaneously narrowing protections against non-traditional fraud theories like right-to-control claims, *Constantinescu* creates a pragmatic middle ground for future litigation involving social media influencers and digital asset promoters. Specifically, it signals that prosecutors need not prove victims suffered catastrophic financial ruin or net losses beyond mere confusion; rather, proving that deceptive communications induced a transfer of value is sufficient to sustain criminal liability under federal fraud statutes.

Furthermore, this decision reinforces that technological medium does not alter fundamental fraud principles provided there is a clear causal link between deception and property transfer. In contexts where defendants utilize platforms like X (formerly Twitter), TikTok, or Discord to disseminate misinformation about token values or penny stocks (*securities*), courts will look first to whether those communications functioned as tools for fraudulent inducement rather than mere dissemination of information rights violations. This distinction protects First Amendment interests regarding free speech while ensuring that bad actors cannot hide behind technicalities regarding what constitutes "information" versus "property." For future cases involving algorithmic trading manipulation or influencer scams (*influencer-marketing-fraud*), this ruling establishes that as long as there is an allegation of obtaining money through lies about investment prospects, indictments will likely survive motions to dismiss unless they rely exclusively on deprivation-of-information arguments akin to those struck down in *Ciminelli*.
