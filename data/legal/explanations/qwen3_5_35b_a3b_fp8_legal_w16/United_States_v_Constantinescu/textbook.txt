Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Chapter I: Procedural History and Factual Allegations

## The Nature of the Scheme: Social Media 'Pump and Dump'

The factual backdrop of *United States v. Constantinescu* centers on a modern iteration of classic market manipulation known as a "pump and dump" scheme, executed through the expansive reach of social media. The government alleged that eight individuals—including Edward Constantinescu, Perry "PJ" Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—leveraged their significant online followings to execute this illicit plan. These defendants cultivated public personas as skilled stock traders, using various social media platforms to share their investment strategies and trading activities with thousands of followers. This digital platform served as the primary vehicle for disseminating the false information necessary to manipulate the market. By positioning themselves as trusted financial experts, the defendants created an environment where their statements carried significant weight with retail investors seeking guidance on where to place their capital.

## Specific Allegations: Misrepresentations, Artificial Price Inflation, and Secret Sales

The mechanics of the alleged scheme involved three distinct phases designed to generate artificial profits at the expense of unsuspecting investors. First, the defendants would quietly purchase shares of a specific security before announcing anything publicly. Second, they initiated the "pump" phase by posting false and misleading information regarding these securities on their social media channels. These posts contained material misrepresentations concerning both their own trading positions and the potential future price of the stock. The objective was to induce their followers to believe that these securities were undervalued or poised for rapid appreciation, thereby triggering a surge in buying pressure from retail investors who relied on the defendants' expertise. Third, once this artificial demand drove up the stock price, the defendants executed the "dump." Without warning their followers or disclosing their imminent exit strategy, they sold their previously acquired shares for substantial profits while leaving new investors holding devalued stock after the price inevitably collapsed. The indictment specifically alleges that this coordinated effort resulted in approximately $114 million in illegal profits for the group between them.

## Charges Filed: Conspiracy (18 U.S.C. § 1349) and Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1348)

Based on these factual allegations, federal prosecutors brought charges against seven of the eight defendants (Daniel Knight having already entered a guilty plea). The superseding indictment charged the remaining defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple substantive counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Section 1349 criminalizes conspiracies to violate federal laws related to securities fraud, requiring proof of an agreement between two or more persons to commit a crime against the United States alongside an overt act taken in furtherance of that conspiracy. Section 1348 is more direct, prohibiting anyone from engaging in a scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with any security or with obtaining money or property via false pretenses connected with the purchase or sale of any security. Together, these statutes target both the collective planning behind the manipulation and the execution of deceptive acts that disrupt fair markets and defraud individual investors.

## Procedural Posture: District Court Dismissal Based on Lack of Traditional Property Interest

Following service of the indictment, six co-defendants moved to dismiss it entirely rather than proceeding directly to trial or pleading guilty like Knight did. The district court granted this motion but did so on narrow grounds regarding what constitutes a valid "property interest" under federal fraud statutes at that time. Specifically, the lower court concluded that while defendants had indeed misled their followers regarding market data and trading intentions, they had not schemed to deprive those victims of a recognized property interest within the meaning of federal law. The district judge reasoned that because defendants allegedly provided followers with market-value securities upon purchase—even if those values later crashed due to manipulation—the core grievance was merely a deprivation of potentially valuable economic information rather than theft of money or tangible property itself. Consequently, believing that no traditional property interest had been violated by providing access to manipulated information alone, the district court found that failing states an offense under Sections 1348 and 2700/§ 2700 (as applicable) and dismissed all charges against these defendants prior to discovery or trial proceedings beginning against them individually beyond initial motions practice.

# Chapter II: Standard of Review and Elements of Securities Fraud

To evaluate the validity of the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit first established the legal lens through which the case would be viewed. The appellate court applies a **de novo** standard of review when assessing the sufficiency of an indictment. This standard mandates that the court must accept all factual allegations within the indictment as true and then determine whether those facts constitute a recognized offense under federal law. This approach ensures that at the pleading stage, the court does not weigh evidence or assess credibility but focuses strictly on whether the government has laid out a plausible criminal theory. The purpose of this rigorous textual analysis is to ensure that an indictment meets three specific constitutional requirements: it must enumerate every prima facie element of the charged offense; it must fairly inform the defendant of the nature of the accusations to allow for preparation; and it must provide adequate protection against double jeopardy by preventing future prosecutions for the same conduct. Ultimately, an indictment is sufficient if it alleges each material element of the crime charged.

Having defined the procedural standard, the court turned to substantive interpretation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the federal securities fraud statute. The text of § 1348 prohibits two primary actions: schemes to "defraud any person in connection with... any security" and schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses... any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale... of any security." Recognizing that there is limited precedent specifically construing § 1348 within the Fifth Circuit, judges look to analogous provisions in mail and wire fraud statutes for guidance. Consequently, courts apply similar interpretative frameworks to both sets of laws. Under this borrowed framework, establishing securities fraud requires proving two distinct components: a "scheme to defraud" enacted with an "intent to defraud." These elements function as interlocking gears; one cannot exist meaningfully without the other in a criminal prosecution.

The first core element, a "scheme to defraud," hinges on the presence of a **material misrepresentation**. A scheme is not established merely by making false statements; rather, those statements must be intended to deceive another party specifically to obtain something of value from them. This requirement distinguishes actionable fraud from mere business disputes or unfulfilled expectations. The Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis* clarified that obtaining money or property must be an explicit object—or goal—of the defendant's deceptive conduct. If deception occurs but lacks this objective financial target tied directly to a transaction involving securities, it falls outside statutory reach. Therefore, for an indictment to survive a motion to dismiss regarding this element, it must allege more than just misinformation; it must allege that defendants used lies as tools to extract money or property from victims in exchange for securities transactions they knew were based on falsehoods.

The second core element concerns **"intent to defraud,"** which carries two prongs: an intent to deceive and an intent that harm result from that deceit. In *Constantinescu*, defendants argued that their intent was merely to profit personally rather than actively harm their followers' wallets, suggesting this distinction negated criminal liability. However, recent Supreme Court jurisprudence rejects this dichotomy between profiting and harming victims in fraud cases. Under *Kousisis*, a defendant violates fraud statutes whenever they scheme "to obtain" money or property through deception, regardless of whether they simultaneously intend for the victim to suffer a net economic loss or receive goods/services of equal value in return. The statute protects against unauthorized transfers induced by lies; it does not require proof that the victim ended up financially worse off after receiving what they paid for under false pretenses. Thus, alleging that defendants sought personal enrichment through fraudulent inducement satisfies both prongs of intent: they intended to deceive (by posting false data) and intended for their victims' property (money) to move as a direct result of that deception (by purchasing overvalued stocks), completing the cycle required for liability under § 1348.

# Chapter III: Distinguishing Right-to-Control from Fraudulent-Inducement Theories

The district court dismissed the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu*, reasoning that the government failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a "traditional property interest." Instead, the district court concluded the defendants merely sought to withhold valuable economic information from their social media followers. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reversed this decision, anchoring its analysis in two recent Supreme Court decisions: *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*. These cases clarified the scope of federal fraud statutes, specifically distinguishing between impermissible theories based on intangible rights and permissible theories involving tangible property exchanges. To understand why the indictment survived, one must grasp how the court distinguished the "right-to-control" theory—which protects neither money nor property—from the "fraudulent-inducement" theory, which explicitly safeguards both.

## *Ciminelli* Limitations: Why Economic Information Alone Is Not Protected Property

The foundation of the defendants' argument rests on *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), a case that significantly narrowed the reach of federal wire and mail fraud statutes regarding intangible assets. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court addressed whether a defendant could be convicted of fraud simply for withholding competitive bidding information from a competitor, thereby preventing that competitor from making informed economic decisions. The Court held that while such conduct might be unethical or anticompetitive, it did not constitute criminal fraud under federal statutes because "potentially valuable economic information" is not a protected property interest within the meaning of those laws.

The Court reasoned that Congress drafted fraud statutes to protect traditional property interests—specifically money and physical things—and did not intend to create a broad federal crime for interfering with someone's ability to manage their own financial affairs through information asymmetry alone. Consequently, if a prosecutor relies solely on an allegation that a defendant deprived a victim of information necessary for decision-making, without alleging an exchange of money or property resulting from deception, the charge fails as a matter of law. This limitation creates a critical boundary for prosecutors: they cannot use fraud statutes as catch-all tools for regulating unfair business practices unless there is a clear transfer of tangible assets driven by deception.

## The Rejection of the Right-to-Control Theory in Federal Fraud Statutes

To understand why *Constantinescu* distinguishes itself from *Ciminelli*, it is essential to define what was rejected in *Ciminelli*: the "right-to-control" theory of fraud. Prior to this decision, some courts had entertained arguments that fraud occurred whenever a defendant deprived a victim of their right to control their own assets by hiding relevant facts or providing false ones. Under this theory, if a victim loses access to specific data required to decide how to spend their money, they have lost their "right to control," which was sometimes theorized as a property interest itself.

*Ciminelli* explicitly dismantled this theory. The Supreme Court declared that "[t]he right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction" because it attempts to expand statutory protection beyond traditional property interests. By rejecting this theory, the Court signaled that mere interference with discretion or informational autonomy is insufficient for liability under 18 U.S.C. § 1343 (wire fraud) or § 1348 (securities fraud). For a conviction to stand, there must be more than just an impairment of decision-making capability; there must be an actual deprivation of money or property obtained through deceptive means. This rejection forces prosecutors moving forward to plead specific allegations regarding tangible losses rather than abstract harms related to information flow or managerial discretion.

## Application Here: The Indictment Avoids *Ciminelli* Pitfalls by Alleging Money Loss

Applying these principles back to *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit found that while defendants conceded they intended to deprive followers of economic information (which would trigger *Ciminelli*), they ignored another crucial aspect of their alleged conduct: inducing followers to part with actual money. The indictment did not rest on a claim that defendants withheld information about market trends or manipulated data points in isolation; rather, it alleged that defendants made false statements about securities prices and trading positions specifically designed to induce followers to purchase those securities immediately following those posts.

The court emphasized that this factual scenario falls outside the prohibition established in *Ciminelli*. Unlike in *Ciminelli*, where no money changed hands due to deception but rather competitors were denied access to bids, here defendants actively solicited transactions where victims transferred funds in exchange for securities based on lies. The indictment successfully avoided the pitfall identified by Justice Barrett's majority opinion in *Ciminelli* by alleging that defendants utilized material misstatements not just to confuse investors regarding market conditions, but specifically "to trick [victims] into... hand[ing] over her money or property." Because the core allegation involves an exchange where money moves from victim to defendant via deception, the fact that victims received shares (rather than cash) does not negate the existence of a traditional property interest being defrauded away from them at that moment in time.

## Defining Fraudulent-Inducement: Using False Pretenses to Secure Contracts or Purchases

Having navigated past *Ciminelli*'s restriction on intangible rights, the Fifth Circuit turned its attention to what remains permissible: the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. As articulated by Justice Barrett in subsequent jurisprudence like *Kousisis*, fraudulent inducement occurs when "a defendant uses material misstatements... [and] tricks [a] victim into entering into agreements requiring [them]... hand[ing] over her money or property." This doctrine recognizes that obtaining consent through lies renders any resulting transaction voidable and constitutes theft under federal law because consent was never truly voluntary—it was manufactured through deception regarding fundamental aspects of the deal (such as price drivers or ownership stakes).

In this framework, it matters little whether the victim receives something else in return after being tricked into paying; what matters is whether they parted with their money based on false pretenses created by the defendant's scheme. In *Constantinescu*, this definition fits perfectly with allegations describing "pump and dump" mechanics: defendants post false news ("false pretenses"), causing investors who otherwise would not have bought—or would have bought differently—to purchase shares ("handing over money"). The Supreme Court has confirmed that this theory protects exactly what Congress intended: honest transactions free from manipulation designed solely for extraction of wealth through deceitful means unrelated to genuine commercial risk-sharing agreements entered into voluntarily and knowingly by all parties involved truthfully at inception.
