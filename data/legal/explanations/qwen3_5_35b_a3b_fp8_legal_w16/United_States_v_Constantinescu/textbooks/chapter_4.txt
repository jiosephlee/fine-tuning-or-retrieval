Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Applying *Kousisis* to Social Media Pump-and-Dump Schemes

## *Kousisis*: Profit Motive Does Not Negate Intent to Defraud

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit confronts a critical interpretive question arising from recent Supreme Court jurisprudence: whether a defendant's specific intent to profit, rather than specifically intend to cause financial harm to a victim, negates the "intent to defraud" required under federal fraud statutes. The district court had dismissed the indictment against the social media influencers, accepting their argument that they lacked criminal intent because their goal was self-enrichment, not the infliction of damage upon their followers. However, the appellate court reversed this dismissal by applying the logic established in *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court clarified that the core of fraud lies in the scheme to obtain another's money or property through deception, not necessarily in a subjective desire to leave that victim poorer than before.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* conceded that they made material misrepresentations but argued that these acts did not constitute fraud because they did not aim to injure their followers' "bottom lines." They contended that because they provided followers with securities that might hold market value, there was no intent to defraud absent an intent to cause net harm. The Fifth Circuit rejected this distinction as "without a difference." Drawing directly from *Kousisis*, the court emphasized that fraud statutes are satisfied when a defendant uses false pretenses to induce a victim to hand over money or property. The statute protects the integrity of transactions where consent is obtained through lies; it does not require proof that the transaction resulted in a net economic loss for the victim or that the perpetrator specifically desired such a loss. By focusing on whether the defendant had "money in mind"—that is, whether obtaining funds was an object of their conduct—the court found that the defendants' profit motive actually corroborated, rather than negated, their intent to defraud. Their scheme was designed precisely so they could obtain money through deception while providing something else in return.

## The Irrelevance of Net Pecuniary Loss in Fraud Statutes

A central pillar of the Fifth Circuit's analysis in *Constantinescu* is its application of Rule 1392 from *Kousisis*, which establishes that federal fraud laws do not mandate a "mirror image" relationship between what a defendant takes and what they give back. Under this framework, it is legally insufficient for a defendant to claim innocence simply because they delivered goods or services of perceived fair market value at the time of the transaction. In *Ciminelli v. United States*, previously decided by the Supreme Court, just eight months prior, Justice Barrett noted that while certain non-traditional interests like "information rights" are protected differently, traditional property interests remain robustly covered when obtained by deception. However, *Ciminelli* distinguished itself from cases involving tangible exchanges where property changes hands; it did not suggest that receiving equal value absolves one of liability if that exchange was procured through fraud.

The Fifth Circuit applied this nuance strictly within the context of social media trading schemes. The defendants argued that because their followers received securities worth what they paid (or perhaps more), no "injury" occurred in a traditional sense. The court countered by explaining that fraud is complete once money is transferred based on materially false pretenses, irrespective of subsequent market fluctuations or immediate value equivalence. As noted in *Kousisis*, a defendant may provide something of equal value in return and still be liable for fraud if that provision was merely part of a scheme to appropriate property through deceit. Therefore, in *Constantinescu*, the fact that followers purchased stocks at inflated prices due to misinformation did not negate the existence of an injury for legal purposes; rather, it highlighted exactly how much money was being extracted through artificial inflation caused by lies. The law focuses on the illegality of obtaining possession through trickery, not on balancing ledgers after the fact. This principle ensures that actors cannot immunize themselves from prosecution simply because they structured their scams so victims received nominal consideration at face value during peak manipulation moments.

## 'Obtaining Money or Property' as Sufficient Injury

To sustain an indictment for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and related conspiracy statutes, prosecutors must allege both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud involving an attempt to obtain money or property from another person using false pretenses (*Evans*; *Greenlaw*). In dismissing this case initially, the district court appeared confused about what constituted "property" or "injury" within these statutory definitions following early post-*Ciminelli* confusion regarding intangible rights versus tangible assets. However, upon closer inspection using guidance from both *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, it became clear that securities themselves—and crucially, cash—are indisputable forms of protected property interest under federal law when acquired via fraudulent inducement (*Ciminelli* distinguishes information-only schemes but affirms protection for monetary transfers).

The Fifth Circuit reasoned that when defendants posted false information about trading positions and price potentials with the specific purpose of inducing purchases ("pumping"), they were actively soliciting followers' capital (cash/property) based on fabricated premises ("false and misleading information"). This fits squarely within the "fraudulent-inducement theory" affirmed in *Kousisis*. Under this theory, liability attaches whenever a material misstatement tricks a victim into contracting for an asset transfer—specifically handing over money—in reliance on those falsehoods. The injury defined by statute is not limited to total ruin; it encompasses any unauthorized deprivation resulting from deception used as leverage for acquisition ($114 million alleged profit). Thus, even if followers eventually sold shares at break-even points later on (hypothetically), or if some shares retained intrinsic value independent of manipulation, those facts do not erase the initial act of securing funds through lies. The indictment successfully alleged this dynamic: defendants created artificial demand via deception specifically so others would surrender cash into accounts controlled by defendants or entities linked to them ("dumping"). Consequently, obtaining money via fraudulent inducement satisfies all elements required for criminal liability without needing additional proof of net loss per se at trial stage for pleading sufficiency purposes (*Fontenot*).
