Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Chapter II: Standard of Review and Elements of Securities Fraud

To evaluate the validity of the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit first established the legal lens through which the case would be viewed. The appellate court applies a **de novo** standard of review when assessing the sufficiency of an indictment. This standard mandates that the court must accept all factual allegations within the indictment as true and then determine whether those facts constitute a recognized offense under federal law. This approach ensures that at the pleading stage, the court does not weigh evidence or assess credibility but focuses strictly on whether the government has laid out a plausible criminal theory. The purpose of this rigorous textual analysis is to ensure that an indictment meets three specific constitutional requirements: it must enumerate every prima facie element of the charged offense; it must fairly inform the defendant of the nature of the accusations to allow for preparation; and it must provide adequate protection against double jeopardy by preventing future prosecutions for the same conduct. Ultimately, an indictment is sufficient if it alleges each material element of the crime charged.

Having defined the procedural standard, the court turned to substantive interpretation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the federal securities fraud statute. The text of § 1348 prohibits two primary actions: schemes to "defraud any person in connection with... any security" and schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses... any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale... of any security." Recognizing that there is limited precedent specifically construing § 1348 within the Fifth Circuit, judges look to analogous provisions in mail and wire fraud statutes for guidance. Consequently, courts apply similar interpretative frameworks to both sets of laws. Under this borrowed framework, establishing securities fraud requires proving two distinct components: a "scheme to defraud" enacted with an "intent to defraud." These elements function as interlocking gears; one cannot exist meaningfully without the other in a criminal prosecution.

The first core element, a "scheme to defraud," hinges on the presence of a **material misrepresentation**. A scheme is not established merely by making false statements; rather, those statements must be intended to deceive another party specifically to obtain something of value from them. This requirement distinguishes actionable fraud from mere business disputes or unfulfilled expectations. The Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis* clarified that obtaining money or property must be an explicit object—or goal—of the defendant's deceptive conduct. If deception occurs but lacks this objective financial target tied directly to a transaction involving securities, it falls outside statutory reach. Therefore, for an indictment to survive a motion to dismiss regarding this element, it must allege more than just misinformation; it must allege that defendants used lies as tools to extract money or property from victims in exchange for securities transactions they knew were based on falsehoods.

The second core element concerns **"intent to defraud,"** which carries two prongs: an intent to deceive and an intent that harm result from that deceit. In *Constantinescu*, defendants argued that their intent was merely to profit personally rather than actively harm their followers' wallets, suggesting this distinction negated criminal liability. However, recent Supreme Court jurisprudence rejects this dichotomy between profiting and harming victims in fraud cases. Under *Kousisis*, a defendant violates fraud statutes whenever they scheme "to obtain" money or property through deception, regardless of whether they simultaneously intend for the victim to suffer a net economic loss or receive goods/services of equal value in return. The statute protects against unauthorized transfers induced by lies; it does not require proof that the victim ended up financially worse off after receiving what they paid for under false pretenses. Thus, alleging that defendants sought personal enrichment through fraudulent inducement satisfies both prongs of intent: they intended to deceive (by posting false data) and intended for their victims' property (money) to move as a direct result of that deception (by purchasing overvalued stocks), completing the cycle required for liability under § 1348.
