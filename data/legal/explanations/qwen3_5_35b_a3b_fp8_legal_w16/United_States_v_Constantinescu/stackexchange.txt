Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does the 'fraudulent inducement' theory survive *Ciminelli*'s rejection of the 'right-to-control' theory in a social media pump-and-dump case?
Question:
The district court dismissed the indictment arguing that the defendants only deprived victims of 'valuable economic information,' citing *United States v. Ciminelli*. However, the Fifth Circuit reversed, distinguishing between the 'right-to-control' theory (which *Ciminelli* killed) and the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory. I am struggling to understand exactly how the court distinguishes these two theories in practice. Specifically, if both involve deception leading to a financial decision, why does inducing someone to buy a stock (where they receive something of value in return) count as defrauding them of property under *Kousisis*, whereas withholding insider information does not? Is the critical distinction simply that one involves a direct transfer of money/property versus just a lost opportunity?
Answer:
The Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* distinguishes the "fraudulent inducement" theory from the "right-to-control" theory rejected in *Ciminelli* by focusing on **what specific asset is transferred** during the transaction, rather than the nature of the information exchanged.

Under the **right-to-control theory**, which *Ciminelli* invalidated, a defendant is deemed to have committed fraud merely by deceiving a victim into making a bad economic decision, thereby depriving them of their "right to control" their assets. As *Constantinescu* explains, this theory treats "potentially valuable economic information" itself as the property interest. Because *Ciminelli* held that federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, not abstract rights to control assets via information, convictions based solely on this theory are impermissible.

In contrast, the **fraudulent inducement theory** survives because it targets a direct transfer of **money or tangible property**. As outlined in Section III(A) of *Constantinescu*, this theory applies when a defendant uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into entering a contract that requires them to hand over their money or property. In the context of the pump-and-dump scheme alleged in *Constantinescu*, the victims were not merely deprived of information; they were actively induced to **purchase securities**—meaning they parted with actual money in exchange for stock. The court emphasizes that this constitutes a deprivation of protected property (the money), distinct from the deprivation of informational rights condemned in *Ciminelli*.

Furthermore, regarding your question about why receiving something of value (the stock) matters less than losing an opportunity: Section III(B) clarifies that under *Kousisis*, fraud does not require the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss or for the defendant's intent to be specifically to harm the victim's bottom line. The Supreme Court clarified that liability attaches when a defendant schemes to **"obtain"** another's money or property through false pretenses, even if they provide something of equal value in return. Therefore, in a fraudulent inducement scenario like *Constantinescu*, the crime is completed at the moment the victim transfers funds based on lies ("induced... to purchase"), regardless of whether they received market-value shares in return or whether the defendant intended to cause them a net loss. The critical distinction is not between "direct transfer vs. lost opportunity," but rather between **depriving someone of their right to decide** (informational/right-to-control) versus **tricking someone into giving you their cash** (fraudulent inducement).

### Does *Kousisis* eliminate the requirement that a fraud defendant must intend to cause actual economic harm or loss to the victim?
Question:
The defendants argued that their intent was merely to profit for themselves, not necessarily to harm their followers financially (since the followers received market-value securities after the 'pump'). The court cites *Kousisis* to say fraud statutes don't require a victim to suffer net pecuniary loss. This seems counter-intuitive: isn't fraud inherently about causing loss? If I trick you into buying a car worth $10k for $10k using lies about its color, but then sell it immediately for more than I paid, have I committed fraud if you didn't lose money? How does *Kousisis* redefine 'harm' in this context—is it purely about the unauthorized taking of possession/control rather than the final balance sheet outcome?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. *United States v. Constantinescu*, relying heavily on *Kousisis*, confirms that the fraud statutes do **not** require a defendant to intend to cause "actual economic harm" or a "net pecuniary loss" to the victim. A fraud conviction stands if the defendant intends to deceive another into transferring money or property, even if the victim receives something of equivalent value in return and suffers no financial deficit.

### Legal Analysis

The core issue is distinguishing between the **object of the fraud** (obtaining property) and the **resulting impact on the victim's wealth**.

Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud), courts borrow concepts from mail and wire fraud statutes. As established in *Kousisis* and applied by the Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu*, these statutes protect two distinct interests depending on the theory of liability:
1.  **Right-to-Control Theory:** Protects the right to control one's own assets (rejected for pure information deprivation in *Ciminelli*).
2.  **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory:** Protects money and property by prohibiting deception used to induce a transfer of those assets (*Constantinescu* citing *Kousisis*).

In your hypothetical scenario—tricking someone into buying a car worth \$10k for \$10k via lies about its color—you have successfully transferred their money (\$10k) based on false pretenses regarding the car's attributes. Under *Kousisis*, this satisfies the elements of fraud even though your "profit" is exactly offset by your delivery of a full-value asset, leaving the buyer with no net loss.

### Application to *Constantinescu* and *Kousisis*

The Fifth Circuit explicitly addressed this exact argument raised by defendants in *Constantinescu*. The defendants argued that because they provided market-value securities after pumping the price, their followers did not suffer economic harm, and thus there was no intent to defraud. The court rejected this reasoning based on three key points from *Kousisis*:

**1. Intent is defined by obtaining property, not inflicting loss.**
*Kousisis* held that a defendant violates fraud statutes when they scheme "to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." (*Constantinescu* at Section III.B). The statutory focus is on the unauthorized acquisition of funds through deception, not on balancing the victim's ledger afterward. Therefore, a defendant can satisfy the "intent to defraud" element simply by intending to secure money/property through lies, even if they plan to deliver goods/services later that match or exceed that value.

**2. The "Mirror Image" Requirement Does Not Exist.**
The court clarified that fraud laws do not impose a requirement that "the victim's loss... supply[d] the defendant's gain." (*Constantinescu* citing *United States v. Baker*). In your car example, if you lie about color to get \$10k and give back a \$10k car, you still committed fraud because you obtained \$10k via deception. The fact that you gave back \$10k does not negate the initial fraudulent taking of possession/control.

**3. Fraud is Complete Upon Deprivation.**
As noted in footnote 5 of *Kousisis* (cited in *Constantinescu*), "[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—regardless of whether the victim eventually recovers value or suffers no net loss (*Constantinescu* at Section III.B). The crime occurs at the moment of inducement; subsequent events (like receiving fair market value securities) are irrelevant to establishing guilt for the initial scheme.

### Conclusion for Your Scenario
If you trick someone into buying a car worth \$10k for \$10k using lies about its color:
*   You have deceived them regarding a material fact (color).
*   You have obtained their money (\$10k).
*   You have caused them to part with property (cash) under false pretenses.

Even if they receive a car worth exactly what they paid, **you have committed fraud**. The lack of "net pecuniary loss" does not immunize you from prosecution under § 1343 (wire), § 1344 (bank), or § 1348 (securities) because these statutes punish the deceptive acquisition itself, not just destructive outcomes where one party ends up poorer than before.

### What constitutes a 'material misrepresentation' in a social media pump-and-dump scheme where no physical asset changes hands initially?
Question:
The opinion states that securities fraud requires a 'scheme to defraud' involving a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others into obtaining something of value. In this case, the defendants posted false info on social media about trading positions. My confusion lies in applying traditional fraud elements to digital content. Does posting a tweet constitute making a 'misrepresentation' in the same way as lying on a financial statement? Furthermore, since they alleged they profited $114 million from selling shares later ('dump'), does the misrepresentation need to directly relate to the specific transaction where money changed hands, or can it be general hype designed to move the market generally before any specific sale occurs?
Answer:
**Answer:**

Yes, posting a false tweet in a "pump-and-dump" scheme constitutes a material misrepresentation just as effectively as lying on a financial statement, provided the post induces the victim to part with money or property. The Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies that the medium of communication (social media vs. paper filings) is irrelevant to the definition of fraud; rather, the analysis turns on whether the misrepresentation was used to **fraudulently induce** a transaction involving money or property.

Regarding your first question about the nature of the representation: The court explicitly distinguishes between two types of fraud theories following recent Supreme Court guidance (*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*). You are correct that simply withholding or distorting "valuable economic information" (like general market hype) is insufficient if it does not involve a traditional property interest. However, the court rejects the idea that social media posts are immune from scrutiny simply because they lack physical assets. Instead, the indictment succeeded because it alleged a **"fraudulent-inducement theory."** Under this theory, defendants used material misstatements (false tweets regarding their trading positions and security values) specifically to "trick a victim into... hand[ing] over her money or property." By posting false information to induce followers to buy securities, defendants caused victims to transfer money in exchange for what turned out to be worthless assets due to the deception. The tweet itself served as the "false pretense" that secured the transfer of funds.

Regarding your second question about timing and specificity: The misrepresentation does **not** need to relate directly to a specific moment when money changes hands between the defendant and victim at that exact instant, nor does it require proof that the victim suffered a net economic loss compared to what they received. The court relies heavily on *Kousisis*, noting that fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" relationship where every dollar lost by one party equals every dollar gained by another. Crucially, the scheme's objective—to obtain money—can be achieved through general hype designed to move the market before any specific sale occurs. As long as the defendant intends to deceive others into purchasing securities (parting with property) based on those false statements, and subsequently profits from that artificial inflation ("dump"), the elements are met. The fact that defendants profited $114 million from selling shares later confirms their intent was "money in mind," satisfying the requirement that obtaining value was an object of their fraud, regardless of whether they intended to cause immediate net harm or injury beyond the initial inducement.

In summary, *Constantinescu* establishes that social media posts function as valid vehicles for material misrepresentation in securities fraud when they are instrumental in inducing victims to purchase securities through deception. The focus remains on the **inducement of a property transfer**, not on whether the misinformation was delivered via paper document or digital platform, nor on whether there was an immediate direct exchange at the time of posting.

### If an indictment relies on *Kousisis*, does it still need to explicitly allege that victims suffered an economic loss beyond just being induced to transact?
Question:
Under Section II of this opinion, an indictment must '(2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges.' Given that *Kousisis* allows liability even when victims receive equivalent value (e.g., stocks bought at fair market price), what specific factual allegations must now be included in an indictment for securities fraud? Do prosecutors need to allege that victims were left with worthless paper after dumping occurred, or is it sufficient to merely allege that they were tricked into handing over money via false pretenses regarding their investment strategy? If they don't allege specific losses, will future indictments be vulnerable on double jeopardy grounds or failure-to-state-a-claim grounds similar to what happened here?
Answer:
No. Under *United States v. Constantinescu*, an indictment relying on the "fraudulent-inducement" theory established in *Kousisis* **does not** need to allege that victims suffered a net economic loss or ended up with "worthless paper." It is sufficient for the prosecution to allege that the defendants used material misrepresentations to trick victims into handing over money or property (in this case, purchasing securities), regardless of whether the victims received equivalent value in return.

Here is the breakdown of the relevant legal standards derived from the opinion:

**1. The "Fraudulent-Inducement" Theory Does Not Require Net Loss**
The core issue addressed in *Constantinescu* is whether a defendant must intend to cause a victim's financial ruin to satisfy the "intent to defraud" element. The Fifth Circuit explicitly rejects this requirement, citing *Kousisis*. As noted in Section III(B) of the opinion, *Kousisis* holds that fraud statutes are violated when a defendant schemes to "obtain" a victim's "money or property," **"regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off"** (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).

Therefore, an indictment need not allege that the stock purchased by followers later crashed in value or became worthless. Even if the followers received shares worth exactly what they paid (fair market value), the fraud is complete once they are induced to hand over their money based on false pretenses.

**2. Distinction from the Rejected "Right-to-Control" Theory**
It is crucial to distinguish between two theories of fraud discussed in Section III(A):
*   **Right-to-Control:** This theory posits that a victim loses a property interest simply by being denied access to confidential information needed to make decisions (as rejected in *Ciminelli*). An indictment relying on this theory would indeed fail if it alleged only that victims lost information but not money/property.
*   **Fraudulent-Inducement:** This theory applies when a defendant uses lies to trick a victim into entering a transaction requiring them to transfer money or property (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388).

*Constantinescu* clarifies that an indictment survives dismissal if it alleges the latter. The court reversed the dismissal because the indictment alleged defendants "induced their followers... to purchase securities" through false posts about trading positions and prices (§ 1348). By alleging that victims parted with their money due to deception, the indictment satisfied the requirement of depriving a protected property interest without needing to prove subsequent loss.

**3. Implications for Future Indictments and Double Jeopardy**
Regarding your concern about vulnerability on double jeopardy or failure-to-state-a-claim grounds:
*   **Failure-to-State-a-Claim:** As held in Section IV, an indictment stating that defendants "posted false and misleading information... so that other investors were induced to purchase" satisfies all elements of securities fraud under current precedent (*Greenlaw*; *Evans*; *Kousisis*). Prosecutors do not need specific allegations of post-dumping losses; they only need allegations of deceptive inducement resulting in a transfer of funds/assets.
*   **Double Jeopardy:** The opinion notes in Section II that an indictment must provide a defense against future prosecutions ("provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense"). However, this requirement is met by enumerating the elements of the crime (deception + obtaining money/property). Since *Kousisis* establishes that obtaining money via deception is itself the harm protected by statute—even absent net loss—the specific outcome of the investment (loss vs. break-even) is not an essential element defining distinct offenses for double jeopardy purposes in this context. The offense is defined by the act of defrauding someone out of property, not by calculating their final balance sheet error.

**Conclusion**
To survive dismissal under *Constantinescu*, an indictment must allege:
1.  A scheme involving material misrepresentations;
2.  Intent to induce victims to part with money or property; and
3.  That such inducement actually occurred (victims handed over funds).

It **need not** allege that victims suffered a net pecuniary loss or that they were left with worthless assets following a pump-and-dump scheme, provided it clearly alleges they were tricked into transferring their own capital based on those lies.

### How do courts reconcile the 'deception + obtaining property' test from *Ciminelli/Kousisis* with cases involving pure informational asymmetry?
Question:
*Ciminelli* established that you can't prosecute someone for hiding information (right-to-control), while *Kousisis* confirms you can prosecute someone who actively lies (fraudulent inducement). In this case, defendants allegedly misrepresented their own trading positions and future price predictions. Where is the bright line between providing false opinions/predictions (which might be protected speech or non-actionable puffery) and actionable fraud? If I tell my Instagram followers 'This stock will go up tomorrow!' and it doesn't, is that different from telling them 'I own 50% of this company' when I actually own none? How does this opinion define where active deception ends and mere bad advice begins?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
Under *United States v. Constantinescu*, the "bright line" between actionable fraud and non-actionable misinformation is defined by **what the victim surrenders**, not just what they lose. A statement like "This stock will go up tomorrow!" is generally protected opinion or puffery unless coupled with a specific, material lie about a fact (e.g., ownership or position) that induces the victim to surrender **money or property**. Conversely, lying about one's trading position ("I own 50%") constitutes actionable fraud because it is a factual misrepresentation designed to induce the transfer of property (cash for stock), satisfying the "obtaining property" prong of *Kousisis*.

**Detailed Analysis:**

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* clarifies how courts distinguish between the prohibited "right-to-control" theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) and permissible "fraudulent inducement" theories (affirmed in *Kousisis*). The court establishes that the core inquiry is whether the scheme involves the deprivation of a **traditional property interest** via deception, rather than merely depriving a victim of valuable economic information.

**1. The Distinction Between Information and Property**
As noted in your question, *Ciminelli* held that a defendant cannot be prosecuted for fraud simply for withholding information that prevents a victim from making optimal economic decisions (the "right-to-control" theory). However, *Constantinescu* emphasizes that this does not immunize all deceptive statements regarding future events. The court distinguishes between two scenarios:
*   **Non-Actionable:** Providing false opinions or predictions without underlying factual misrepresentations regarding the transaction itself. This falls under general market speculation or puffery.
*   **Actionable Fraud:** Making material misrepresentations of fact specifically to induce the transfer of money or property.

In *Constantinescu*, the indictment succeeded because it alleged defendants made false claims about their **trading positions** and future price predictions to induce followers to purchase securities. The court reasoned that this was not merely depriving followers of information; it was using those lies to trick victims into signing a contract requiring them to hand over money (by buying stocks). As the court states, "[u]nder the fraudulent-inducement theory... a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." (*Id.* at 1388).

**2. Applying the Test: Opinions vs. Lies About Facts**
To answer your hypothetical:
*   **"This stock will go up tomorrow!"**: Standing alone, this is likely protected speech or non-actionable puffery because it represents an opinion about future market movement rather than a verifiable fact at the time of speaking. Unless accompanied by a lie about something else (like ownership), it does not satisfy the "obtaining property" requirement because there is no specific factual misrepresentation inducing the transfer of cash in exchange for something other than what was promised (i.e., no specific asset was transferred based on a lie about its nature).
*   **"I own 50% of this company":** This is an actionable lie if false. It is a representation of present fact regarding ownership status, distinct from an opinion on price direction. By asserting this falsehood, the defendant creates a specific condition for entry into a transaction (buying/selling based on insider knowledge). When followers buy stock based on this lie, they are being defrauded into parting with money/property based on materially false pretenses. This fits squarely within *Kousisis*: "a defendant violates [the] fraud statutes when he 'schem[s] to obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." (*Constantinescu* citing *Kousisis*).

**3. Intent and Harm Are Irrelevant to Sufficiency**
Finally, *Constantinescu* rejects the argument that fraud requires proof that the defendant intended to harm the victim's wallet or that the victim suffered a net economic loss. The court holds that liability attaches once there is an intent to deceive and an intent to obtain money/property through that deception (*id.* at 1392). Even if followers receive securities worth more than they paid immediately after buying (because everyone bought in), the fraud occurred at the moment they parted with their money based on false pretenses regarding ownership or position.

**Conclusion**
The bright line drawn by *Constantinescu* is **factual misrepresentation leading directly to property transfer**. Pure opinions/predictions are generally safe unless they serve as vehicles for concealing material facts (like hidden conflicts of interest or fake ownership) used to induce financial transactions. If you lie about your identity or holdings (*Kousisis*), you cross into criminal territory; if you simply guess wrong about market movements (*Ciminelli*'s realm), you do not.
