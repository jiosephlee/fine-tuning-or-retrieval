Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Beyond the Right-to-Control Theory: How *Constantinescu* Reaffirms Fraud Liability After *Ciminelli*

The intersection of social media influence and securities regulation has long been a gray area for prosecutors, but the Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, issued last week, offers a crucial roadmap for navigating the post-*Ciminelli* landscape. By reversing a district court's dismissal of a "pump and dump" indictment, the panel clarifies that while the Supreme Court's recent jurisprudence has narrowed the definition of "property" in fraud cases, it has not created a safe harbor for influencers to manipulate markets using deceptive social media posts. The ruling serves as a critical reminder that the federal fraud statutes still reach schemes designed to induce victims to hand over their money through lies, even if those schemes involve complex financial instruments rather than traditional tangible goods.

The core tension in *Constantinescu* stems directly from two recent Supreme Court decisions: *Klein v. United States* (better known as *Ciminelli*) and *Kousisis*. In *Ciminelli*, the Court decisively rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, holding that federal wire and mail fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. Consequently, a scheme to deprive a victim of valuable economic information necessary to make independent decisions does not constitute fraud if no specific property or money changes hands due to deception. The district court below applied this logic rigidly to the defendants' conduct, concluding that because the indictment alleged the defendants sought to deprive followers of "potentially valuable economic information," it failed to state an offense under modern precedents.

However, the Fifth Circuit correctly identified that this reading conflated two distinct theories of liability. As Judge Engelhardt explains in his opinion, there is a fundamental difference between withholding information (which might implicate *Ciminelli*) and actively lying to induce a transaction (which falls under established precedent). The court distinguished between what it termed the "right-to-control" theory—which involves manipulating a victim's ability to manage assets—and the "fraudulent inducement" theory. Under fraudulent inducement, a defendant uses material misstatements specifically to trick a victim into entering a contract or making an investment that requires them to part with money or property. Because *Constantinescu* involved allegations that defendants posted false claims about their trading positions and stock valuations specifically to induce followers to buy securities they otherwise wouldn't have purchased, the scheme targeted traditional property interests: cash exchanged for shares. The court emphasized that *Kousisis* confirmed this theory protects money and property regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return or suffers a net pecuniary loss.

Perhaps just as significant as the distinction between these theories is how the Fifth Circuit addressed the defendants' argument regarding intent. The defense contended that because they provided followers with actual securities—albeit ones whose value was manipulated—they lacked an intent to defraud since they didn't intend for victims to lose money overall; they simply intended to profit themselves. Here again, citing *Kousisis*, Judge Engelhardt dismantled this notion by reiterating that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to cause net economic harm or leave a victim worse off than before. The crime is complete when the defendant obtains money or property through deception, irrespective of whether they provide consideration in exchange. The fact that defendants profited $114 million by pumping up share prices before dumping their holdings demonstrates precisely what Congress intended to punish: obtaining another's property via false pretenses.

From a doctrinal standpoint, *Constantinescu* represents a vital preservation of federal fraud law's utility in the digital economy without expanding it beyond statutory bounds. If lower courts had accepted the district court's reasoning—that any scheme involving informational asymmetry on social media escapes liability unless it fits within strict property definitions—we would have seen a massive loophole open for sophisticated market manipulation disguised as content creation. By anchoring its decision firmly in existing "fraudulent inducement" doctrine rather than trying to stretch new categories of intangible rights, the Fifth Circuit avoided creating uncertainty while simultaneously signaling that social media presence does not immunize actors from securities laws.

Looking forward, this decision suggests that prosecutors can continue to rely on standard fraudulent inducement theories against online influencers engaging in pump-and-dump schemes without needing to prove novel forms of intangible deprivation. However, it also sets boundaries: indictments must clearly articulate how deception led directly to a transfer of money or property, avoiding vague assertions about interference with decision-making processes alone. For now, however, *Constantinescu* stands as a clear signal that while *Ciminelli* closed one door on abstract rights-to-control arguments, it left wide open for prosecution those who use lies specifically designed to extract capital from unsuspecting investors online.
