Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Profit Without Loss? The Enduring Validity of the Fraudulent-Inducement Theory Post-*Kousisis*

**By [Your Name/Persona]**  
*October 3, 2025*

The recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, handed down by the Fifth Circuit last week, serves as a crucial reminder that the Supreme Court's tightening grip on fraud statutes has not eviscerated the "pump-and-dump" playbook. In reversing a district court's dismissal of a sweeping securities fraud indictment involving seven social media influencers, the Fifth Circuit clarified that the core of federal fraud law remains focused on the *deceptive acquisition* of property, not necessarily the *net economic devastation* of the victim. This ruling is significant because it definitively applies two major Supreme Court precedents—*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*—to the modern landscape of digital finance, ensuring that sophisticated actors cannot escape liability simply by arguing their victims received assets equal to what they paid.

The case arose from an alleged scheme where high-profile social media traders convinced followers to buy specific stocks using false claims about their own positions and market projections. After artificially inflating prices ("pumping"), the defendants sold their holdings at a profit ("dumping"). The district court initially threw out the indictment, reasoning that because the defendants allegedly provided followers with genuine securities (even if overvalued), they hadn't deprived anyone of a "traditional property interest." The court seemed to suggest that since the followers walked away with something tangible—a stock—the crime wasn't complete unless there was a distinct loss beyond the initial payment. This logic, however, collapsed under scrutiny once applied to *Kousisis*.

The Fifth Circuit's analysis begins by distinguishing between two theories of fraud: the "right-to-control" theory rejected in *Ciminelli* and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory upheld in *Kousisis*. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court struck down convictions based on depriving victims of confidential economic information necessary for making decisions, holding that such intangible rights are not traditional property interests. The district court in *Constantinescu* mistakenly tried to shoe-horn this limitation onto a classic pump-and-dump scenario. The Fifth Circuit correctly noted that this case never rested on a right-to-control theory; instead, it alleged that defendants tricked investors into handing over cash for securities they otherwise wouldn't have bought. As Justice Barrett made clear in *Kousisis*, fraudulent inducement protects money and property directly transferred due to deception. It does not matter if the victim subsequently holds an asset; what matters is that they parted with their money based on lies. By confirming this distinction, *Constantinescu* prevents litigants from using *Ciminelli*'s narrow definition of property to immunize straightforward financial scams.

More critically, *Constantinescu* addresses the contentious issue of "intent to defraud," specifically regarding whether a defendant must intend for their victim to suffer a net economic loss. Defendants argued that because they provided followers with marketable securities (albeit inflated ones), and perhaps even allowed them to retain some value before dumping occurred, they lacked the requisite intent to harm. They posited that since they profited while leaving victims with assets, there was no "injury" sufficient for criminal liability. This argument attempts to inject a "mirror-image" requirement into fraud statutes—that every dollar gained by a swindler must correspond to an equivalent dollar lost by a victim after accounting for all returns.

The Fifth Circuit decisively rejected this view by leaning heavily on *Kousisis*. In that landmark 2025 decision, the Supreme Court held that fraud statutes are violated whenever a defendant schemes to obtain money or property through false pretenses, regardless of whether they seek to leave the victim economically worse off overall. If a contractor lies about meeting diversity requirements just to get paid for work actually performed at fair market rates, it is still fraud because they obtained public funds through deception. Similarly, in *Constantinescu*, obtaining millions from followers via fabricated trading data constitutes fraud even if those followers technically received shares worth something at face value upon delivery. The harm lies in the unauthorized transfer of wealth driven by deception, not in whether the final portfolio balance ends up negative relative to pre-scam levels.

This interpretation carries profound doctrinal implications for how prosecutors approach white-collar crimes in an era where transactions can be structured as complex exchanges rather than simple thefts. Prior to these rulings, defense counsel might have argued successfully that if a victim gets "value" for their money—even if obtained through lies—it negates criminal intent or damages elements entirely. *Constantinescu* shuts this door open again by affirming that the integrity of voluntary exchange is paramount; consent obtained via material misrepresentation is void ab initio regarding criminal liability. Consequently, "win-win" defenses where both parties end up with assets become legally irrelevant if one party's possession was secured through deceitful means designed solely to extract capital from another who acted under false pretenses.

Looking forward, this decision likely solidifies a pathway for federal enforcement agencies pursuing crypto-pumps and influencer-driven stock manipulation schemes across social media platforms like X (formerly Twitter) and TikTok. While courts continue to grapple with defining exactly what constitutes "property" versus "information," *Constantinescu* ensures that as long as money changes hands based on lies about investment vehicles or trading strategies, federal fraud statutes remain robust tools against such conduct. For now, it appears clear: you can keep your profits from pumping and dumping; you just can't claim your victims didn't lose anything because you gave them stock in return for their cash at gunpoint—or rather, algorithmic persuasion—but without giving them truth along with it.
