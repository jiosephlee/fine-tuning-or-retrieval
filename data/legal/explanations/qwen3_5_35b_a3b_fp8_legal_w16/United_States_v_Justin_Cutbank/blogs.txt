# Intrinsic vs. Propensity: The Eighth Circuit Reaffirms the 'Continuous Possession' Doctrine in Firearm Cases

**By [Your Name]** | *October 10, 2025*

In the high-stakes world of federal firearm prosecutions, few evidentiary battles are as frequent—or as consequential—as the attempt to exclude "bad act" testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). Defendants in these cases often argue that introducing evidence of prior interactions with a weapon is merely character assassination, designed to convince a jury that a person with a violent past is more likely to commit a crime today. However, the Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week, serves as a stark reminder that the line between impermissible propensity and permissible intrinsic evidence can be drawn quite narrowly around the concept of "continuous possession." By reaffirming its precedent in *United States v. Battle*, the court clarified that proving a defendant held onto a specific firearm days or weeks before an alleged violation is not just relevant; it is essential to establishing the very element of possession required for conviction.

The facts of *Cutbank* present a volatile scenario typical of domestic violence and drug-fueled confrontations. After consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank threatened his girlfriend with a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, holding her hostage and eventually fleeing to a nearby garage where he barricaded himself against police. During the ensuing standoff, he bit an officer and attempted to grab their weapon before surrendering. Crucially, when police recovered the sawed-off rifle from his escape route, they found DNA matching Cutbank. Yet, before trial even began, Cutbank moved to suppress testimony from his girlfriend and her housemate stating they had seen him possess that specific rifle on previous occasions. His argument was standard fare for defense counsel: admitting this testimony violated Rule 404(b) because it suggested he was a dangerous person prone to carrying weapons, rather than proving he actually possessed the gun on November 20, 2020.

The Eighth Circuit had little patience for this distinction in light of established doctrine. Writing for the panel, Judge Loken relied heavily on *Battle*, noting that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control." This reasoning transforms what might look like character evidence into intrinsic evidence—facts so closely tied to the charged offense that they explain how the defendant came to have control over the contraband in question. If Cutbank had never touched that specific sawed-off rifle before that night, his sudden appearance with it at his girlfriend's door might have seemed coincidental or planted. But by showing he had carried it "a handful of times" previously, the prosecution provided necessary context for his immediate access and exclusive control at the time of arrest. The court's refusal to treat this as propensity evidence underscores a pragmatic reality: in gun cases involving unique weapons like sawed-off rifles, history isn't just background noise; it's part of the chain of custody leading up to the crime itself.

While *Cutbank* resolves easily on evidentiary grounds due to overwhelming physical and testimonial proof—including DNA matches and eyewitness identification—the broader doctrinal implication lies in how courts define "intrinsic" versus "extrinsic" acts in possession cases. Prosecutors frequently rely on this logic to paint a picture of ongoing criminal enterprise without needing separate charges for every instance of prior use. As long as there is a logical nexus between the prior act and the charged offense—specifically regarding identity or continuity of possession—the door remains open for juries to hear about those prior interactions. This approach effectively lowers the barrier for prosecutors seeking to prove dominion over illegal firearms, particularly when dealing with distinctive items where general possession theories are insufficient.

Beyond the evidentiary win, *Cutbank* offers little respite for defendants facing steep sentencing enhancements under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). Cutbank challenged his sentence primarily on procedural grounds regarding USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which mandates a four-level enhancement if a firearm is possessed "in connection with another felony." He argued that applying this enhancement alongside ACCA penalties resulted in an overly harsh sentence that conflicted with Section 3553(a)'s mandate for proportionality. The Eighth Circuit flatly rejected this view, emphasizing that once within-range sentences are calculated correctly based on valid guideline applications, appellate courts grant district judges wide latitude under *Gall*. Given Cutbank's dozen-plus prior felonies and the violent nature of his conduct—domestic terrorization coupled with resistance against law enforcement—the court found no substantive unreasonableness in imposing 292 months behind bars.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* reinforces two critical pillars of federal criminal procedure: first, that evidence connecting a defendant to a specific weapon through prior use is often indispensable rather than prejudicial; and second, that sentencing courts retain robust discretion when navigating complex statutory overlaps like ACCA and sentencing guidelines enhancements. For practitioners litigating similar fact patterns involving unique firearms or escalating domestic situations, *Cutbank* suggests that fighting admissibility on pure Rule 404(b) grounds may be futile if the government can articulate a theory of continuous possession anchored by concrete examples of prior contact with the instrumentality itself.



# The Silent Record: Appellate Review Standards and the Burden of Preservation in Evidentiary Challenges

**By [Your Name]**  
*October 14, 2025*

In the high-stakes theater of federal criminal appeals, the most dramatic moments often occur not in the courtroom where the trial unfolded, but in the quiet silence of a brief that says too little. *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, serves as a stark reminder that appellate courts are not fact-finders for lost causes; they are guardians of procedure. While the underlying facts—a violent domestic confrontation, a barricade standoff, and a bloody encounter with law enforcement—are undeniably harrowing, the Eighth Circuit's decision turns on a far more mundane but equally dispositive legal reality: an appellant cannot win by simply pointing out errors without explaining why those errors matter under existing precedent.

The case involves Justin Cutbank, a repeat offender sentenced to 292 months under the Armed Career Criminal Act for possessing a sawed-off rifle following a terrifying ordeal involving his girlfriend and her family. On appeal, Cutbank mounted a multi-pronged attack on his conviction and sentence, citing everything from improper character evidence to flawed jury instructions and alleged violations of Rule 403 balancing tests. Yet, despite the gravity of these allegations, his appeal collapsed under its own weight. As Judge Loken wrote for a unanimous panel, "Cutbank... provided no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments." This is not merely a stylistic critique; it is a procedural death knell. In the federal system, particularly within circuits like the Eighth that adhere strictly to preservation rules, an argument raised without citation to controlling authority or meaningful analysis is effectively waived.

Section II(D) and (E) of *Cutbank* offer particularly instructive lessons on the mechanics of preservation and authentication. During trial, Cutbank objected to testimony regarding his girlfriend's deceased sister being shot with a gun—a detail introduced during cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements. The district court denied the motion to strike, noting that such testimony was responsive and lacked evident unfair prejudice under Rule 403. On appeal, Cutbank failed to identify specific instances where this testimony confused the jury or rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. He offered nothing more than a bare assertion that prejudice existed. The Eighth Circuit rightly observed that "the court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless," especially when the defendant fails to demonstrate how the jury might have been "substantially swayed" by it. Without specific citations to case law illustrating how similar testimony warranted reversal in comparable contexts, the appellate court could only conclude that any potential error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt given the overwhelming volume of other evidence against Cutbank.

Even more telling is Section II(E), which addresses attempts to impeach government witnesses using extrinsic evidence—specifically police reports detailing prior inconsistent statements made by witness C.F. Defense counsel sought to introduce portions of these reports into evidence but hit an authentication wall when they tried to read them directly into the record without establishing that C.F. authored them or verified their accuracy at that moment. The district court sustained objections regarding proper authentication procedures required by *Federal Rule of Evidence 613*. On appeal, rather than addressing whether this restriction violated Confrontation Clause rights or amounted to an abuse of discretion under established precedent (*United States v. Cumbie*), Cutbank's team largely ignored this issue in their briefing until forced upon them by government arguments in response briefs. Judge Loken did not mince words here either: "We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal." The result? Even assuming arguendo that there was error (which there wasn't), it would fail plain error review because it was neither obvious nor prejudicial enough to affect substantial rights when viewed against the totality of the record.

The doctrinal implications for defense counsel are immediate and severe. *Cutbank* reinforces a hard truth: appellate advocacy is distinct from trial advocacy. A lawyer who successfully objects at trial must immediately pivot to crafting an argument for appeal that bridges current facts with binding precedent. Merely stating "Rule 403 requires exclusion" is insufficient if one cannot point to circuit-specific jurisprudence defining exactly how much prejudice constitutes reversible error in similar domestic violence scenarios. Furthermore, reliance on general principles without specific application invites summary dismissal. The Eighth Circuit's explicit rebuke suggests that judges are increasingly willing to treat vague or unsupported assertions as forfeited issues rather than opportunities for correction.

Downstream effects may include stricter scrutiny of pro se filings or poorly drafted briefs from appointed counsel who lack specialized appellate experience. Lawyers must ensure every objection is preserved with precision—not just at trial but in writing—and every appellate argument must be anchored in specific citations to controlling authority within their jurisdiction or persuasive Supreme Court guidance where local law is silent. *Cutbank* also highlights the resilience of within-Guidelines sentences; once properly calculated and supported by robust statutory findings (as seen here with ACCA enhancements), even aggressive challenges based on minor evidentiary skirmishes rarely succeed unless they rise above mere speculation into demonstrable constitutional infirmity or clear abuse of discretion backed by solid law.

Ultimately, *United States v.* **Cutbank** stands as a cautionary tale about the cost of silence in appellate practice. The facts were ugly enough to make any reader sympathetic toward cutting corners in sentencing calculations or challenging evidentiary boundaries aggressively—but sympathy does not override procedure when an appellant refuses to engage with it meaningfully on paper. For practitioners navigating complex criminal appeals today, *Cutbank* delivers an unambiguous message: If you want your voice heard on appeal, you must speak up early, cite your sources clearly, and prove your case with precision—or accept affirmation as inevitable consequence of neglecting your duty to preserve and argue effectively below and above alike



# ACCA Overload: Navigating Conflicting Guidelines Enhancements Under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

**By [Your Name]** | *October 10, 2025*

When a defendant faces both the statutory hammer of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the intricate machinery of the Sentencing Guidelines, the interplay between the two often becomes a flashpoint for appellate litigation. Yesterday, in *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed exactly this friction, rejecting a defendant's contention that stacking an ACCA-mandated sentence with a specific Guidelines enhancement violated the "sufficient but not greater than necessary" mandate of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). While the case involves a gruesome factual backdrop involving domestic terror and resistance to arrest, its legal significance lies in clarifying how courts reconcile mandatory statutory floors with discretionary guideline calculations when multiple aggravating factors collide.

The story begins with Justin Cutbank, whose night of terror ended in a confrontation that saw him hold his girlfriend at gunpoint, barricade himself in a garage, and bite an officer attempting his detention. Convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and triggering ACCA due to three prior violent felonies, Cutbank faced a presumptive sentence under the statute. However, his appeal turned on the sentencing phase, specifically regarding the application of U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision mandates a four-level enhancement if a defendant possesses a firearm "in connection with another felony offense." The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) applied this enhancement alongside others—including those for altered serial numbers and reckless conduct—pushing his advisory Guidelines range up to 292 months. Cutbank argued that applying this enhancement was redundant or improper once ACCA had already dictated his punishment structure, effectively claiming it created an impermissible conflict with § 3553(a).

The Eighth Circuit's rejection of this argument rests on a straightforward reading of the Sentencing Commission's commentary and existing precedent regarding the relationship between statutes and guidelines. As Judge Loken noted for the panel, ACCA operates as a statutory floor; it establishes minimum penalties based on prior convictions but does not necessarily dictate the precise top-end calculation within an advisory framework. The Guidelines explicitly account for situations where an offender qualifies for both ACCA and other enhancements. Specifically, USSG § 4B1.4(b) directs courts to apply whichever offense level produces the greater result—whether derived from ACCA or from standard guideline calculations involving enhancements like § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Because Cutbank's calculated offense level under these combined factors (level 35) exceeded what ACCA alone might have yielded via its specific guideline counterpart (level 34), the district court was correct to adopt the higher figure. The court emphasized that there is no inherent contradiction between satisfying a statutory minimum and adhering to guideline recommendations designed to reflect the totality of criminal conduct.

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder that defendants cannot easily use § 3553(a)'s general proportionality principle to dismantle specific guideline enhancements merely because they coincide with statutory mandates. The Eighth Circuit rightly distinguished between procedural error—which requires strict adherence to calculation methods—and substantive unreasonableness—the ultimate weight given to those calculations after considering all factors. Since Cutbank received a sentence within the properly calculated advisory range, which included nearly twelve prior felony convictions and violent acts against family members and law enforcement, any claim that the sentence was "greater than necessary" is hard-won on appeal absent extraordinary circumstances. The court's presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences remains robust here; once the math checks out according to § 4B1.4(b), shifting burden falls heavily on the appellant to show why that mathematical outcome violates broader sentencing goals—a bar *Cutbank* failed to clear given his extensive history of violence.

Beyond this specific holding, *Cutbank* offers important doctrinal clarity for practitioners navigating multi-faceted sentencing disputes involving firearms offenses where other felonies are intertwined with possession itself. The decision reinforces that "possession in connection with another felony" is not merely duplicative when an offender is simultaneously subject to career offender status or ACCA; rather, it functions as an independent aggravator intended to capture conduct that occurs beyond mere passive ownership or simple trafficking boundaries defined by other statutes. For future litigants facing similar overlaps between ACCA and specific possession enhancements, *Cutbank* suggests that challenges must focus less on theoretical conflicts between statutes and guidelines and more on whether individual underlying facts truly support each distinct element required for every layer of enhancement applied by the district court.
