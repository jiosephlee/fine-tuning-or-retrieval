Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Must a district judge explicitly articulate every factor from 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) during sentencing to avoid procedural error?
Question:
The opinion relies heavily on *Masood* and *Maluoth* to state that courts don't need a 'mechanical recitation' of § 3553(a) factors, provided it's clear they considered them. In this case, Cutbank argued his sentence conflicted with § 3553(a)'s requirement that punishment be 'not greater than necessary.' How vague can a judge's explanation be before it becomes procedurally unreasonable? Specifically, if a judge simply says they reviewed the PSR and memoranda without addressing specific arguments about proportionality relative to his extensive criminal history versus his age or rehabilitation potential, is that enough to survive appellate review?
Answer:
No, a district judge does **not** need to explicitly articulate every factor from 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) during sentencing to avoid procedural error. Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in *United States v. Cutbank*, the law requires only that it be "clear from the record" that the court actually considered these factors; a "mechanical recitation" of each factor is unnecessary.

In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that his sentence conflicted with the § 3553(a) requirement that punishment be "not greater than necessary," specifically citing his age and rehabilitation potential against his extensive criminal history. The Eighth Circuit held that the district court's general statements—specifically noting its review of the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), sentencing memoranda, and the relevant § 3553(a) factors—were sufficient to satisfy procedural requirements.

The court reasoned that because the record demonstrated the judge had read and reviewed these materials containing Cutbank's specific arguments, the court "presume[d] that the district court considered and rejected them." Consequently, even though the judge did not engage in a detailed rebuttal of Cutbank's specific arguments regarding proportionality relative to his age or rehabilitation, this omission did not constitute procedural unreasonableness. As long as the sentencing proceeding reflects that the court was aware of and considered the statutory factors and party arguments, a vague or generalized explanation survives appellate review, particularly when imposing a within-Guidelines sentence where reasonableness is presumed.
