Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What constitutes 'substantial prejudice' required to reverse a conviction based on cumulative evidentiary errors?
Question:
The appellate court affirmed despite acknowledging several evidentiary rulings where defense objections were overruled (e.g., testimony about D.F.'s sister being killed by a gun). The standard cited is that reversal only occurs if errors were not harmless—meaning the jury wasn't 'substantially swayed.' Given that multiple pieces of potentially prejudicial evidence were admitted, how does a court determine if those errors cumulatively crossed the line into reversible error? Is there a quantitative threshold for how many improper admissions constitute 'substantial prejudice,' or is it purely qualitative based on the weight of independent corroborating evidence?
Answer:
Based on *United States v. Cutbank*, 10 F.4th (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), the determination of "substantial prejudice" in the context of cumulative evidentiary errors is **qualitative, not quantitative**. The Eighth Circuit explicitly rejects the idea that there is a specific numerical threshold of improper admissions that automatically triggers reversal. Instead, the court focuses entirely on the **weight of the independently admissible evidence** and whether the errors influenced the verdict.

The court establishes that reversal requires a showing that "the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." In *Cutbank*, even though the appellate court acknowledged multiple instances where objections were overruled—including testimony regarding D.F.'s sister's death and repeated observations of the gun—the court affirmed the conviction because the government's case was otherwise overwhelming. The opinion notes that "the government's evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial," leading to the conclusion that any errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict."

Consequently, a law student should understand that under *Cutbank*, you cannot argue reversibility simply by tallying up errors (e.g., "there were five prejudicial rulings"). The inquiry is strictly whether, in light of all properly admitted evidence (such as DNA matches, eyewitness identification, and physical recovery of weapons), the cumulative effect of the errors created a realistic possibility that they affected the outcome. Because *Cutbank* emphasizes that "we will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice," a strong body of independent corroborating evidence effectively neutralizes multiple minor or moderate evidentiary mistakes.
