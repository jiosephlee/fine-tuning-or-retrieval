Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does 'intrinsic' evidence differ from 'Rule 404(b)' propensity evidence in firearm possession cases?
Question:
The court upheld the admission of testimony that the defendant had previously owned the specific sawed-off rifle, calling it 'intrinsic evidence.' However, my notes suggest that introducing prior bad acts usually falls under Rule 404(b) and requires a balancing test for prejudice. Why did the court classify this prior observation as intrinsic rather than 404(b) character evidence? Does this mean any prior knowledge of a weapon automatically becomes intrinsic, or is there a specific threshold for 'continuous possession' that triggers this exception?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
The court classified the testimony as **intrinsic evidence** rather than Rule 404(b) evidence because the prior observations were of the *same* weapon central to the charged offense, offered to prove "continuous possession" and ownership, rather than to show a general criminal disposition. Under Eighth Circuit precedent (*United States v. Battle*), prior possession of a specific firearm is directly relevant to establishing that the defendant had actual control over that specific firearm at the time of the alleged crime. Consequently, this type of evidence does not trigger the strict balancing test required for Rule 404(b) character evidence; instead, it is treated as part of the complete story of the crime itself.

**Detailed Explanation**

**1. The Distinction Between Intrinsic Evidence and Rule 404(b)**
Rule 404(b) generally prohibits admitting evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in accordance with that character. Such evidence requires a rigorous balancing test under Rule 403 to ensure probative value outweighs unfair prejudice.

However, courts distinguish between "other acts" used for propensity (improper) and "intrinsic" acts (proper). Intrinsic evidence consists of uncharged conduct that is:
*   Part of the same transaction or series of transactions as the charged offense;
*   Necessary to provide context for the crime; or
*   Essential to explain how the charged offense occurred.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit held that testimony regarding previous sightings of the specific sawed-off rifle was intrinsic because it helped establish **ownership and continuous possession**. As the opinion states, "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control." Because this evidence explains *how* Cutbank came to possess the gun at the time of his arrest (by showing he had been handling it recently), it is viewed as part of the narrative of the charged crime rather than an extrinsic act introduced solely to paint him as a dangerous person.

**2. Application to Firearm Possession Cases**
You asked if there is a specific threshold for "continuous possession." While *Cutbank* does not define a rigid temporal line (e.g., "must be within X days"), it establishes that for firearm possession charges, proof often hinges on establishing dominion and control over a specific object. If witnesses testify they saw the defendant with that exact weapon shortly before or leading up to the incident, this creates a chain of continuity linking past behavior to present possession.

In *Cutbank*, witnesses testified they saw Cutbank with the rifle "a handful of times" before the incident involving his girlfriend and police confrontation. The court reasoned this was not an isolated past act but evidence demonstrating an ongoing relationship with that specific instrumentality. This contrasts sharply with Rule 404(b) scenarios where a defendant might be accused of unrelated thefts or assaults merely to suggest they are prone to breaking laws generally.

**3. Why No Balancing Test Was Required**
Because the court deemed this intrinsic evidence, it did not need to apply the Rule 404(b) framework or weigh its prejudicial effect against its probative value in the same way it would for unrelated bad acts. The relevance was direct: without knowing Cutbank had handled this gun before, proving he possessed it at midnight on November 20 would rely entirely on forensic matches and circumstantial inference regarding how he got it into his hands at that moment. The testimony filled that gap naturally within the story of what happened on November 19–20.

As noted in Section II.A and II.C of *Cutbank*, citing *Battle*, 774 F.3d at 511, such testimony is admissible because it proves an element of the crime (possession/control) rather than just character traits like violence or dishonesty generally associated with prior convictions under statutes like § 922(g)(1).

**Conclusion**
The classification turns on **relevance**, not just timing. Any prior knowledge does not automatically become intrinsic; however, when prior observations involve **the identical weapon** central to a possession charge and serve to demonstrate **uninterrupted dominion**, they fall under intrinsic evidence exceptions in Eighth Circuit jurisprudence. They are admitted as part of the "complete story" necessary to prove possession, bypassing Rule 404(b)'s prohibition on character-based propensity reasoning.
