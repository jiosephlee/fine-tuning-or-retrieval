Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

The case of *United States v. Cutbank* arises from a volatile domestic confrontation that escalated into a multi-hour standoff with law enforcement, culminating in a federal prosecution for illegal firearm possession. The factual narrative begins on the night of November 19–20, 2020, following a period where defendant Justin Cutbank consumed alcohol and methamphetamine. Upon awakening his girlfriend, D.F., Cutbank pointed a firearm at her face, forced her into a closet, and detained her for several hours. During this captivity, he made paranoid delusions regarding listening devices and threats against their safety, struck D.F. with the gun, and threatened her life. Cutbank eventually ordered D.F. to wake others in the household, including her two teenage sons and housemate C.F., before fleeing the scene with D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm.

Following these events, law enforcement response was triggered by dual reports: one from D.F.'s residence describing the hostage situation and another from a nearby house half a mile away reporting an individual barricaded inside a garage refusing to leave. Police located Cutbank in the garage after several hours of negotiation attempts during which he made erratic statements and claimed to possess a weapon. When officers moved to detain him, Cutbank violently resisted; he bit an officer in the face and neck and attempted to seize an officer's service weapon. A search of the path between the two residences yielded critical physical evidence matching the suspect: D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA testing confirmed the rifle belonged to Cutbank, while witnesses identified it as a weapon they had seen him carry on previous occasions. These facts formed the bedrock of the government's case regarding both his criminal conduct and his possession of the contraband firearm.

The charges filed against Cutbank reflected both his status as a prohibited possessor and his specific circumstances regarding prior convictions. He was indicted under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) for being "a felon in possession" of firearms or ammunition prohibited by law due to his prior felony record. Additionally, because he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies—crimes that included domestic abuse related incidents—the government invoked the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). This statutory provision mandates a mandatory minimum sentence of fifteen years for such defendants carrying firearms if they meet this specific recidivism threshold. A jury convicted Cutbank on both counts: specifically finding him guilty of felon-in-possession and determining beyond a reasonable doubt that he possessed three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby triggering the ACCA's enhanced penalty provisions which set his initial exposure at twenty-four years (288 months) or more depending on further sentencing calculations.

At sentencing, the district court utilized the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) alongside statutory requirements to determine an appropriate term of imprisonment within an advisory range rather than a mandatory floor alone due to judicial discretion post-*Booker*. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) calculated a base offense level of 26 based on two primary factors: first, that the offense involved a "short-barreled shotgun/rifle" under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a); second, that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. Several enhancements were added to this base level: three levels were applied because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number; two levels were added because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police; and four levels were applied under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense (the underlying domestic violence incident). With these adjustments bringing his total offense level to 35 combined with Criminal History Category VI (reflecting his extensive criminal record), the PSR recommended an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment. The district court adopted these findings without objection at sentencing but ultimately imposed a sentence at the bottom end of this range: 292 months (twenty-four years plus eight months).

Procedurally, Cutbank appealed both his conviction and his sentence to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, raising distinct categories of challenges regarding trial fairness versus sentencing discretion. Regarding his conviction, he alleged multiple evidentiary errors committed by the district court before and during trial that deprived him of a fair hearing; specifically, he contested rulings allowing witnesses D.F. and C.F. to testify about observing him possessing similar firearms prior to the incident as potential propensity evidence barred by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). He also challenged jury instructions concerning hearsay statements made by victims to police during their initial calls for help before they took the stand themselves. On appeal regarding sentencing, Cutbank focused narrowly on whether the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), arguing that despite falling within an otherwise correct guidelines calculation mandated by statute via ACCA penalties earlier discussed as potentially conflicting with core principles under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). # Admissibility of Intrinsic Evidence Under Rule 404(b)

## Rule 404(b): Propensity vs. Intrinsic Evidence Distinction
In the case of *United States v. Cutbank*, the defendant challenged the admission of testimony from witnesses D.F. and C.F., who stated they had seen Cutbank possess the specific sawed-off Marlin rifle on previous occasions prior to the charged incident. Cutbank argued that this testimony violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally prohibits introducing "other acts" evidence to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in conformity with that character—commonly known as propensity evidence. However, the Eighth Circuit clarified that Rule 404(b) does not bar all evidence of prior bad acts; it specifically targets extrinsic acts offered solely to suggest guilt by character. The court distinguished these prohibited "propensity" uses from "intrinsic" evidence, which describes the immediate circumstances of the crime or is necessary to complete the story of the crime for the jury. If evidence is intrinsic, it is admissible regardless of whether it involves other bad acts, provided it satisfies relevance and prejudice standards under Rule 403. In *Cutbank*, the court held that testimony regarding prior possession was intrinsic because it was inextricably linked to proving the elements of the charged offense: knowing possession of a firearm by a felon.

## The Requirement to Prove Ownership or Control
For a prosecution to succeed on a charge of unlawful possession under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knowingly possessed the weapon and exercised dominion and control over it at the time of the alleged offense. In cases where possession is fleeting or contested, establishing that a defendant owned or controlled a specific weapon often requires proof connecting them to that object before and after its discovery. As noted in *Cutbank*, mere recovery of a weapon near a suspect does not automatically prove ownership; circumstantial evidence is frequently necessary to bridge gaps in direct observation. Consequently, showing that a witness had seen the defendant with that specific firearm earlier serves as critical context to demonstrate continuous possession rather than isolated, coincidental contact. Without such background, jurors might reasonably doubt whether Cutbank actually controlled the gun found in his path or whether someone else placed it there merely moments before his arrest.

## Application of *United States v. Battle*: Prior Possession as Relevant Context
The Eighth Circuit relied heavily on its precedent in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), which established that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon." In *Battle*, similar to *Cutbank*, defendants argued that prior instances involving weapons were improper character evidence; however, the court ruled otherwise when those instances helped establish ownership or control over the specific instrumentality used in the current crime. Applying this standard, Judge Loken reasoned that because D.F.'s testimony indicated she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle "a handful of times" before November 2020, this evidence was intrinsic to proving he maintained control over it leading up to and during his flight from police. The court emphasized that this line of questioning did not ask Juries to infer guilt based on past behavior but rather asked them to identify continuity between past observations and present custody, thereby satisfying an element of the charged offense rather than attacking character impermissibly.

# Hearsay, Confrontation Clause, and Trial Procedure

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of evidentiary challenges raised by the appellant, focusing primarily on the admissibility of out-of-court statements made by victims to police and the scope of permissible cross-examination. Cutbank argued that these rulings violated the Federal Rules of Evidence and the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause, thereby depriving him of a fair trial. The appellate court systematically dismantled these arguments, clarifying that hearsay rules do not bar statements offered solely to explain police conduct, that closing instructions suffice where immediate curative measures are unnecessary, and that broad judicial discretion governs inquiries into a witness's background and the authentication of documents used for impeachment. By analyzing these specific disputes, the opinion illustrates how appellate courts distinguish between substantive proof of guilt and contextual information essential to understanding an investigation.

### Contextual Admissibility: Police Knowledge vs. Substantive Proof

A central pillar of Cutbank's appeal concerned the statements made by his girlfriend, D.F., to law enforcement immediately following her ordeal. Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude these statements on hearsay grounds and argued that admitting them would violate his right to confrontation. The district court permitted this testimony but framed it narrowly: the statements were not introduced as substantive evidence of Cutbank's guilt but rather to establish "context" for the police officers' subsequent actions. Specifically, the testimony explained why officers responded with urgency and how they formed their initial knowledge of the situation at D.F.'s residence and the nearby garage where Cutbank eventually barricaded himself.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed this approach, emphasizing that hearsay rules are designed to prevent unreliable out-of-court assertions from being used as proof of the truth of the matter asserted. However, when a statement is offered for a purpose other than its truth—such as showing its effect on the listener—it falls outside the definition of hearsay entirely. In *Cutbank*, D.F.'s descriptions of threats and weapons were critical to explaining why officers acted as they did upon arriving at the scene; without this context, their behavior might appear inexplicable or overly aggressive to a jury. Because the prosecution did not rely on D.F.'s out-of-court declarations to prove that Cutbank actually threatened anyone in those specific utterances (relying instead on her live testimony at trial), the Confrontation Clause concerns were largely moot regarding those specific statements. The court noted that once a declarant appears in court to testify in person regarding their own observations, any residual Confrontation Clause issues are satisfied regardless of prior out-of-court statements used merely for context.

### 'No Abuse of Discretion': Review Standards for Limiting Instructions

Cutbank further contended that because D.F.'s statements were potentially prejudicial if misunderstood by jurors as substantive proof of his guilt, the district court erred by refusing his request for an immediate limiting instruction—a direction telling jurors exactly what evidence they could or could not consider at that moment in time. Instead, he argued for a specific instruction prior to any mention of those statements being entered into evidence or discussed extensively during direct examination. The Eighth Circuit clarified that while limiting instructions are often necessary when inadmissible evidence is inadvertently presented or when admissible evidence carries significant risk of misuse, they are not required in every instance where such evidence exists.

The standard of review for a district court's decision regarding jury instructions is "abuse of discretion." Under this deferential standard, an appellate court will only reverse if no reasonable judge would have reached the same conclusion under similar circumstances. The *Cutbank* opinion highlights that a district court need not interrupt proceedings with an instruction at every juncture if it intends to provide comprehensive guidance at the end of trial. As long as no substantive hearsay was admitted without qualification—which was true here since D.F.'s live testimony covered all material facts—the failure to give an immediate limiting instruction does not constitute reversible error. The court reasoned that provided there is an opportunity for correction through final instructions delivered before deliberations begin, and provided no actual misconduct occurred during trial due to lack thereof, the system functions properly without constant mid-trial interventions. This ensures trials proceed efficiently while maintaining fairness through final summations where all legal boundaries are clearly defined for jurors collectively rather than piecemeal throughout weeks or days of testimony.

### 'Confrontation Clause Satisfied': Availability of Declarants for Testimony

The Confrontation Clause guarantees defendants the right to confront witnesses against them in person within open courtroom settings where cross-examination can occur freely and effectively (*Crawford v. Washington*). A common misconception among defense attorneys is that any reliance on prior out-of-court statements automatically triggers constitutional infirmity even if those individuals later testify live at trial. However, *Cutbank* reinforces established precedent stating explicitly: "The Confrontation Clause is satisfied when hearsay declarants... actually appear in court and testify in person." Since D.F., who made numerous statements describing her captivity and witnessing events leading up to arrest, took the stand herself during trial subjecting herself fully under oath alongside rigorous cross-examination conducted by defense counsel herself ("she testified"), there remained no constitutional barrier preventing her prior accounts from serving either contextual or corroborative roles relative to her current presence before fact-finders today! # 'Substantial Prejudice' Standard for Evidentiary Errors

## 'Harmless Error': The Burden on Appellant to Show Substantial Prejudice

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the appellate court addresses a critical threshold for reversing a criminal conviction: even if a district court makes an error in admitting or excluding evidence, that error does not automatically result in a new trial. Instead, the court applies the "harmless error" doctrine, which places a heavy burden on the appellant to demonstrate that the mistake affected the outcome of the case. As outlined in the opinion, the Eighth Circuit adheres to the standard articulated in *United States v. DeMarce*: a court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling unless it concludes that "the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." This standard requires more than a mere possibility of prejudice; the defendant must show that the error had a substantial influence on the verdict. If the error did not influence, or had only a slight influence, on the jury's decision, the conviction stands despite the procedural misstep. In Cutbank's case, because he failed to meet this high burden of showing actual prejudice, his evidentiary complaints were dismissed regardless of their theoretical merit.

## 'Cumulative Effect': Analyzing Multiple Minor Rulings Together

Cutbank raised numerous specific complaints regarding evidentiary rulings throughout his trial, ranging from objections to prior bad acts testimony to disputes over cross-examination limits and limiting instructions. However, the appellate court emphasized that these individual rulings must also be evaluated collectively under a "cumulative effect" analysis. Even if one assumes for argument's sake that every single ruling challenged by Cutbank was erroneous—a premise he fails to prove—the court explained that it will not overturn a conviction based on this accumulation of errors unless there is "substantial prejudice" arising from their combined impact. The logic follows that minor or non-prejudicial errors do not compound into reversible misconduct simply because they occur frequently. The court cited *United States v. Keys* to reinforce that while defendants are entitled to fair trials, isolated mistakes or even multiple minor errors do not necessitate reversal absent a showing that they fundamentally compromised the fairness of the proceeding or misled the jury. Since Cutbank could not demonstrate that these alleged errors individually or collectively substantially swayed his fate, his appeal on these grounds failed entirely.

## 'Overwhelming Evidence': Independent Corroboration via Physical and Testimonial Means

The most decisive factor in applying the harmless error standard in *Cutbank* was the strength of the independent evidence supporting his conviction. To overcome an appellate challenge based on potentially improper evidence, an appellant must often show that without that evidence, there is insufficient proof left to sustain a verdict beyond a reasonable doubt. The court noted that Cutbank faced "substantial" government evidence both testimonial and physical that stood independently of any contested admissions or exclusions at trial. Specifically, D.F.'s direct testimony placed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle and detailed his violent conduct against her and her family. Furthermore, C.F., another witness provided identification testimony linking Cutbank to the weapon before and during the incident. Crucially, physical evidence corroborated these accounts: police recovered D.F.'s cell phone and Marlin .22 caliber rifle along Cutbank's escape route, and DNA testing confirmed Match cutbank's presence on the weapon itself. Because this robust chain of identification and forensic evidence existed regardless of whether D.F.'s statements regarding prior gun sightings were deemed admissible propensity evidence or intrinsic evidence, any potential error in admitting such testimony could not have substantially influenced the jury's determination of guilt.

## 'Presumption of Regularity': Why Convictions Stand Despite Isolated Mistakes

Underlying these analyses is a fundamental presumption of regularity afforded to district courts during trial proceedings. While appellate courts review decisions de novo for legal questions and for abuse of discretion regarding evidentiary rulings (*Sprint/United Mgmt. Co.*), they operate under an assumption that lower courts generally follow proper procedures unless shown otherwise. In *Cutbank*, this presumption manifested in two ways: first through deference to unchallenged trial tactics where no objection was preserved (such as C.F.'s mention of D.F.'s deceased sister), and second through skepticism toward arguments lacking legal support or factual basis (as evidenced by Cutbank providing "no cases and almost no analysis"). The court explicitly stated it would not appreciate appellants expanding issues unnecessarily on appeal without merit. Ultimately, because Cutbank presented no compelling argument that any specific ruling caused him substantial prejudice—and because his arguments were unsupported by authority or fact—the appellate court presumed nothing went wrong enough to warrant disturbing the judgment. This reinforces a key tenet of appellate practice: convictions are resilient against challenges based on technical errors when those errors do not materially affect justice served by overwhelming independent proof of guilt.

# Sentencing Enhancement Application under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## Connection Requirement: Possessing Firearm During Another Felony
The central sentencing dispute in *United States v. Cutbank* concerns the applicability of the four-level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision mandates an increase in the offense level if the defendant "used or possessed" a firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense. In *Cutbank*, the district court applied this enhancement based on the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), which detailed that Cutbank possessed a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle while committing domestic abuse, kidnapping, and assaulting police officers. The appellate court affirmed that these underlying acts constituted distinct felony offenses connected to the firearm possession. The "connection" requirement is satisfied where the possession of the firearm facilitates or has a relationship to the commission of another crime; here, Cutbank brandished the weapon to threaten his girlfriend, use it as a tool of coercion during the confinement, and wield it while resisting arrest and fleeing from law enforcement. The court reasoned that because Cutbank's possession was integral to these separate criminal episodes, the enhancement was properly triggered without needing to prove a specific intent to commit those felonies beyond what was already established by the facts themselves.

## Regardless Doctrine: Applicability Without Separate Charges or Convictions
A critical aspect of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) highlighted in *Cutbank* is its explicit language stating that the enhancement applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." This doctrine allows sentencing courts to apply enhancements based on conduct that constitutes a felony even if that conduct was not separately prosecuted or resulted in a standalone conviction. In this case, Cutbank did not argue successfully against this standard; rather, his challenge focused on whether applying this guideline conflicted with statutory sentencing principles under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit clarified that the Advisory Guidelines are designed to reflect actual criminal behavior, not just adjudicated counts. Because Cutbank's conduct involved possessing the firearm during domestic violence and flight from police—acts that independently qualify as felonies—the absence of separate charges for those specific acts did not preclude the application of the four-level increase. The court emphasized that relying solely on formal charges would undermine the Guidelines' purpose of tailoring sentences to the severity of actual conduct.

## Guidelines Conflict Resolution: Comparing ACCA vs. General Sentencing Tables
The opinion further addresses how courts resolve potential conflicts between different sentencing frameworks when they produce different offense levels. Cutbank argued that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) resulted in a sentence higher than what might be appropriate under other considerations, specifically noting a conflict with his Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) status. Under ACCA, which applies due to his three prior violent felony convictions, Cutbank faced a mandatory statutory minimum of 15 years (180 months), but also triggered specific guideline calculations under USSG § 4B1.4 (Career Offender) yielding an offense level of 34. However, when combining his base offense level with various enhancements—including those for having three prior violent felonies and using a sawed-off weapon—the general calculation yielded an offense level of 35 due to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The Guidelines explicitly provide that when different provisions yield different offense levels, the greater level controls (§ 4B1.4(b)). Consequently, despite Cutbank's contention that his ACCA-based sentence should dictate otherwise or create a conflict requiring adjustment under § 3553(a), the appellate court held that following the "greater offense level" rule was correct procedure. This ensures consistency in sentencing outcomes where multiple statutory and guideline provisions overlap.

## Fact Finding: Reliance on PSR Facts Adopted by District Court
Regarding how these factual determinations are made at sentencing, *Cutbank* illustrates deference to district court fact-finding based on PSR recommendations accepted by both parties at trial or lack thereof during sentencing proceedings. The district court adopted the PSR's factual statements regarding Cutbank's prior felony convictions and his use of the firearm during those incidents without objection from either side at sentencing hearings before raising it on appeal as error-free findings required no independent judicial inquiry beyond confirming they were accurate and relevant to Guideline application ("clearly erroneous"). Since Cutbank failed to object to these factual assertions below regarding whether he used or possessed the gun during other felonies, he effectively conceded their accuracy for sentencing purposes unless he could demonstrate clear error—a high bar rarely met absent contradictory evidence presented at trial or post-trial motions challenging credibility directly related to these specific facts.* While some evidentiary disputes arose earlier regarding hearsay and witness testimony at trial regarding possession itself (*e.g.*, whether witnesses saw him hold it previously), once those foundational facts were settled by conviction and admission via PSR adoption for sentencing purposes (*i.e.*, facts supporting enhancements), appellate scrutiny focused strictly on legal correctness rather than re-litigating minor factual ambiguities.* Thus, reliance on unchallenged PSR data remains robust so long as procedural safeguards like notice and opportunity for rebuttal were afforded during pre-sentence conferences.**

## Standard of Review: Clear Error vs Abuse Discretion in Fact-Finding
Finally, understanding *Cutbank*'s outcome requires grasping how standards of review shape appellate intervention in sentencing decisions involving Guideline applications versus discretionary variances.* For purely legal questions—such as interpreting what constitutes "possession in connection with another felony"—appellate courts apply de novo review; however,*factual findings underlying those interpretations receive clear error review.* Conversely,*discretionary decisions about whether to depart from guidelines based upon §3553(a) factors are reviewed for abuse of discretion.* Here,*the district court's determination that Cutback's conduct fell within §2K2.(b)*(6(B)) rested largely on undisputed facts found true by jury verdicts (*prior convictions*) plus additional behavioral details (*use during domestic disturbance*). Because neither party contested these facts sufficiently below (*except generally through vague arguments*), appellate judges assumed they were correct unless manifestly wrong (*clear error standard).* Even though cutback claimed substantive unreasonableness (*conflict with core principles*), such challenges demand showing abuse discretion—meaning sentence must exceed bounds reasonableness considering totality circumstances (*violent history + current brutality*). Given overwhelming evidence supporting enhanced culpability (*dozens prior felonies + active resistance+domestic threat*), no meaningful ground existed overturning within-range imposition despite theoretical tension between ACCA/Guidelines paths.*

# Procedural Reasonableness and Substantive Sentence Review

The appellate court's final evaluation in *United States v. Cutbank* centers on the standard of review governing sentences, specifically distinguishing between procedural correctness and substantive reasonableness. Before an appellate court can assess whether a sentence is too harsh, it must first ensure the district court followed the correct procedural steps. As established in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, the primary requirement is that the district court correctly calculates the applicable Guidelines range; failing to do so constitutes a "significant procedural error" that requires remand. In Cutbank's case, the court confirmed that the district judge properly calculated the offense level at 35 based on the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR). The calculation included specific enhancements: a three-level increase for an altered serial number, a two-level increase for creating a reckless risk of death while fleeing police, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Because Cutbank's Armed Career Criminal Act status triggered a higher guideline range under U.S.S.G. § 4B1.4, the court correctly applied the greater offense level of 35 rather than the lower level derived solely from his career offender status. With no significant procedural error identified in this initial calculation phase, the appellate court moved to evaluate whether the resulting sentence was substantively reasonable.

Once procedural correctness is established, the focus shifts to whether the district court adequately considered the statutory factors outlined in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Under *Gall v. United States*, sentencing courts must determine an appropriate sentence by beginning with the correct Guidelines range and then considering whether a departure or variance is warranted based on these factors. However, *United States v. Masood* clarifies that this does not require a "mechanical recitation" of every factor or a detailed explanation rejecting each argument raised by the defendant. Instead, it suffices if it is clear from the record that the judge actually weighed these considerations. In Cutbank's appeal, he argued that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) resulted in a sentence conflicting with § 3553(a)'s mandate that punishment be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The Eighth Circuit found no error here; although Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions involving violence and firearms, and his current conduct involved domestic abuse, kidnapping-like behavior in front of children, and assaulting an officer, there was no indication he failed to consider these circumstances. The record demonstrated that at sentencing, the judge reviewed both his own observations and written submissions regarding § 3553(a) factors before imposing a within-range sentence. Consequently, any ambiguity regarding specific arguments against enhancement was resolved by reviewing what occurred at trial rather than demanding further justification from the trial judge post-hoc.

The presumption of reasonableness plays a critical role when an appellate court reviews a sentence imposed within the properly calculated Guidelines range. While this presumption is rebuttable—meaning a defendant can argue for unreasonableness—it places a heavy burden on appellant to show why such length is unjustified relative to other defendants with similar records and offenses. In Cutbank's instance, he received a 292-month sentence located at the very bottom of his advisory Guidelines range (292–365 months). By choosing the lowest possible term available within his category, even though his criminal history placed him near its ceiling, demonstrated judicial caution against excessive punishment despite severe aggravating circumstances. The fact that Cutbank faced such high guidelines due to his extensive violent past meant that even this relatively lenient outcome reflected a high degree of severity compared to typical offenders. Therefore, affirming this low-end-of-the-range sentence reinforced its substantive reasonableness; if anything went wrong in balancing fairness versus deterrence here, it would have been toward granting leniency rather than oppression.

Beyond mere numbers on a chart, however, context matters significantly when evaluating whether those numbers align with justice goals under § 3553(a).
