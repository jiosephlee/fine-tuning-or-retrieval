Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Factual Narrative: The Incident on November 19-20, 2020

The underlying facts of *United States v. Cutbank* paint a harrowing picture of domestic violence escalating into a confrontation with law enforcement. On the night of November 19–20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Justin Cutbank awakened his girlfriend, D.F., in their shared residence. In a state of paranoia fueled by drug use, Cutbank pointed a gun at D.F.'s face, directing her into a closet where he held her hostage for what she testified felt like hours. During this ordeal, he made irrational accusations that "listening devices" were present and that individuals were waiting outside to harm him. His threats escalated to physical violence; he struck D.F. across the face with the weapon and threatened her life repeatedly. At one point, Cutbank forced D.F. to wake other occupants in the home, including her two teenage sons and their housemate, C.F., further heightening the danger within the household. Ultimately, Cutbank fled the scene taking D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm used during the captivity.

## Evidence Collection: Police Response and Physical Evidence Recovery

The police response began after D.F. contacted authorities from inside the closet. Simultaneously, law enforcement received a separate report from a residence located approximately half a mile away indicating that an individual was barricaded in a garage and refusing to leave officers' commands. Upon arrival at this second location, officers discovered Cutbank barricaded inside the garage for several hours. Throughout this standoff, Cutbank made erratic statements claiming to possess a weapon and resisting detention efforts violently; notably, he bit an officer on the face and neck and attempted to seize the officer's service weapon. While officers searched the area along the path connecting D.F.'s home to the garage where Cutbank likely traveled, they recovered critical physical evidence: D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. Forensic testing confirmed that DNA on the rifle matched Cutbank's profile. Crucially, both D.F. and C.F. identified this specific weapon as one they had observed Cutbank possessing prior to the night of his arrest, establishing a link between his past behavior and the charged conduct.

## Charges and Statutory Framework: Felon in Possession and ACCA

Based on these facts, prosecutors charged Cutbank with violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), which prohibits any person who has been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year from knowingly possessing any firearm or ammunition affecting interstate commerce—a status known as being "a felon." The indictment also included allegations supporting an enhanced penalty under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), commonly referred to as the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). To trigger ACCA penalties, the statute requires proof that a defendant violated § 922(g) while having three or more prior convictions for "violent felonies" or serious drug offenses committed on occasions different from one another. The government presented evidence through witnesses identifying previous violent conduct by Cutbank to satisfy this element during trial. The jury ultimately returned findings confirming both elements: that Cutbank was indeed an unlicensed possessor of a firearm due to his prior felony status and that he met the threshold of three qualifying prior violent felonies required for mandatory enhancement under § 924(e).

## Sentencing Calculation: Base Offense Level and Guidelines Range

Following his conviction on December 7, 2020 (implied by trial dates), Cutbank faced sentencing under the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG). The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) served as the baseline for calculation but contained specific adjustments based on statutory mandates and guideline provisions. First, because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm defined under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), PSR officials assigned a base offense level of 26 pursuant to USSG § 2K2.1(a)(3). Second, additional increases were applied: three levels for having at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence; three levels because serial numbers on certain weapons had been altered or obliterated; two levels because he recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police; and four levels because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). These calculations resulted in an aggregate offense level of 35 combined with Criminal History Category VI due to his extensive record of nearly twelve prior felony convictions involving violence and firearms. This combination yielded an advisory sentencing range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment under standard guideline tables before considering statutory minimums or enhancements like those mandated by ACCA itself or potential variances based on § 3553(a) factors such as deterrence and public safety concerns inherent in domestic violence cases involving children present in the home.

# Admissibility of Prior Acts Under Rule 404(b)

In *United States v. Cutbank*, one of the primary evidentiary disputes concerned the admissibility of testimony regarding the defendant's prior interactions with the weapon central to the prosecution's case. Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from witnesses D.F. and C.F., who intended to state that they had previously seen Cutbank possess the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. Cutbank characterized this proposed testimony as "propensity evidence" prohibited by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), arguing that allowing such testimony would invite the jury to convict him based on his bad character or past conduct rather than the specific facts of the charged crime. The district court denied this motion, classifying the evidence as "intrinsic," meaning it was so closely linked to the charged offense that it formed part of a single criminal transaction or provided essential context for understanding the possession charge. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, holding that prior possession is directly relevant to proving later possession and is therefore admissible not as character evidence, but as intrinsic evidence necessary to establish ownership and control of the specific firearm.

### The Defense Motion: Excluding Testimony as Propensity Evidence

Rule 404(b) generally prohibits the admission of evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in accordance with that character. In practical terms, this means prosecutors cannot tell a jury, "He stole a car last year; therefore, he must have stolen this car." However, Rule 404(b) does allow such evidence for other purposes, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident. A critical threshold inquiry in any appeal challenging such evidence is whether it falls outside Rule 404(b) entirely because it is "intrinsic" to the charged offense. If evidence is intrinsic, it need not satisfy Rule 404(b)'s strictures because it is not offered to prove character through other acts; rather, it describes the circumstances surrounding the crime itself.

In *Cutbank*, the defense framed its argument strictly around propensity: admitting testimony that Cutbank owned or handled the gun previously would lead jurors to conclude he was a dangerous person prone to carrying weapons. By labeling it propensity evidence, Cutbank sought to trigger an automatic exclusion unless he could fit it into one of Rule 404(b)'s specific permissible categories (like intent or identity). The court recognized that this framing missed a fundamental point about how possession crimes are proven: unlike murder or fraud where an act might be distinct from prior behavior, possession often relies on establishing a nexus between the defendant and an object over time. The defense failed to distinguish between using past acts solely to paint a portrait of guilt versus using them as foundational elements required to prove the current charge existed at all.

### Legal Standard: Rule 404(b) vs. Intrinsic Evidence Doctrine

The Eighth Circuit clarified that when evidence relates directly to the charged offense—specifically when it proves continuity or identity—it is treated as intrinsic rather than extrinsic "other acts" governed by Rule 404(b). Intrinsic evidence includes acts that are part of the same criminal episode or transaction as the charged offense. For example, if a defendant kills a victim with a gun he used earlier in an assault against that same victim during an ongoing confrontation, those earlier assaults are intrinsic parts of the homicide narrative and are not barred by Rule 404(b). Similarly, in drug trafficking cases involving long-term storage operations or conspiracy charges involving continuous communication courts frequently find related communications intrinsic because they explain how and why drugs were stored together over time.

The distinction matters procedurally because intrinsic evidence does not require notice under Rule 406(a) nor does it undergo the balancing test for unfair prejudice under Rule 403 in the same way extrinsic other-acts evidence does (though general relevance remains required). More importantly for *Cutbank*, if an act is intrinsic to proving possession—meaning you cannot logically prove "possession" without showing prior contact—the rule against propensity simply does not apply. The court emphasized that while defendants have a right not be judged by their past misconduct unrelated to the current charge, they do not have a right to exclude facts necessary to define what happened during their alleged illegal activity. The key inquiry becomes whether there is sufficient overlap between the prior act and the charged conduct such that they form part of one continuous story rather than separate instances used merely for inference.

### Application to Facts: Establishing Ownership and Continuous Possession

Applying these principles to *Cutbank*'s situation revealed why excluding witness testimony would have severely hampered the government's ability to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. To secure a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), prosecutors must prove two things: first, that Cutbank knew he was possessing a firearm; second, that he knew he was legally prohibited from doing so due to his status as a felon. While knowledge of legal disability can sometimes be inferred from circumstantial evidence regarding education and background (as established in *Lipscomb*), knowledge of actual physical control requires proof linking him specifically to *that* gun at *that* time. Because firearms are fungible objects—a .22 caliber rifle looks very similar regardless of who owns it—proving exclusive control often depends on showing familiarity with specific features unique identification marks like scratches modifications or even just habitual handling patterns witnessed by acquaintances over time.

Witnesses D.F. and C.F.'s testimony served exactly this purpose: they testified they had seen Cutbank handle this specific sawed-off rifle numerous times before November 2020 ("a handful of times"). # Hearsay, Confrontation Clause, and Trial Procedure Errors

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of procedural and evidentiary challenges raised by the appellant regarding the admission of witness statements to police, the adequacy of jury instructions, and the scope of permissible cross-examination. Cutbank argued that the district court erred by admitting out-of-court statements by his girlfriend, D.F., and her housemate, C.F., which he characterized as hearsay violations that also infringed upon his Sixth Amendment right to confrontation. Furthermore, he contended that the court failed to provide specific limiting instructions mid-trial to prevent the jury from treating these statements as substantive evidence of his guilt. The appellate court systematically dismantled these arguments by clarifying that when a declarant appears in court to testify, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied regardless of whether their prior statements were admitted for substantive purposes. Additionally, the court emphasized that trial judges possess broad discretion in managing proceedings, particularly when out-of-court statements serve a non-hearsay purpose—such as explaining an officer's conduct—and when limiting instructions are provided at the close of trial rather than immediately upon evidence admission. Finally, the opinion illustrates how courts balance probative value against unfair prejudice under Federal Rule of Evidence 403 when defense counsel attempts to introduce sensitive personal history during cross-examination.

## Confrontation Clause Compliance via Witness Testimony

The cornerstone of Cutbank's constitutional challenge rested on the assertion that admitting D.F.'s prior statements to police violated his rights under the Confrontation Clause. The Confrontation Clause guarantees a criminal defendant's right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." However, this right is fundamentally satisfied when the declarant actually appears in court and testifies under oath. As established in *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860 (8th Cir. 2010), and reiterated in *Cutbank*, "the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarants... actually appear in court and testify in person." Because D.F. took the stand at trial to recount her experience with Cutbank—including her account of being held at gunpoint—the primary barrier to admissibility was removed. The appellate court noted that while D.F.'s earlier statements to police might otherwise constitute inadmissible hearsay if offered for their truth without her presence, her live testimony rendered any potential confrontation error moot. Consequently, even if those prior statements were admitted as substantive evidence or merely for context, Cutbank's opportunity to cross-examine D.F. about inconsistencies between her trial testimony and her initial reports ensured compliance with constitutional mandates.

## 'Contextual' Hearsay Admissibility for Officer Knowledge

Beyond the Confrontation Clause argument, Cutbank challenged the admission of D.F.'s statements on traditional hearsay grounds under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c). Generally, an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted is inadmissible hearsay unless it falls under a recognized exception. However, not every out-of-court statement is offered for its truth; sometimes it is introduced solely to explain why a law enforcement officer acted in a certain way or possessed specific knowledge at a particular moment. In *Cutbank*, D.F.'s calls to dispatch describing Cutbank holding her hostage and claiming he had weapons provided critical context for why officers arrived at specific locations with heightened alertness and采取了 specific tactical approaches during their search and detention efforts. The government successfully argued that these statements were not offered to prove that Cutbank actually possessed a weapon or threatened D.F., but rather to demonstrate what prompted the officers' subsequent actions and their state of mind upon arrival at scene two—a garage where Cutbank was eventually found barricaded inside. Since these statements were not offered for their truth but rather as background information essential to understanding the narrative flow of law enforcement activity, they fell outside the definition of hearsay entirely or qualified under exceptions for non-testimonial contexts where reliability is inferred through other means (such as immediate reaction). The appellate court accepted this distinction, noting that admitting such evidence does not violate evidentiary rules so long as it serves a legitimate non-substantive purpose related to officer conduct or investigation continuity.

## 'No Abuse of Discretion': Failure to Provide Limiting Instructions Mid-Trial

Cutbank further argued that because potentially problematic hearsay-like material was admitted early in proceedings (specifically regarding D.F.'s initial reports), the district court erred by refusing his request for immediate limiting instructions telling jurors they could not consider those statements as proof of guilt until D.F.'s own testimony was delivered later in trial. This contention touches upon standard procedure governing how courts manage juror comprehension during complex trials involving mixed evidence types. Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in *Cutbank* (*United States v. Bennett*), a district court need not issue limiting instructions immediately after every piece of questionable evidence is admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the conclusion of all evidence ("at close"). The appellate panel reasoned that requiring mid-trial interruptions for every potential evidentiary nuance would disrupt trial efficiency without necessarily enhancing fairness, especially when final charges can effectively guide deliberations retrospectively. Moreover, since D.F.'s live testimony ultimately covered all material aspects alleged in her prior reports—and thus cured any ambiguity regarding what constituted direct versus circumstantial proof—the absence of interim warnings did not constitute reversible error. The reviewing standard here was "abuse of discretion," meaning reversal would occur only if no reasonable judge could have concluded that delaying instructions until closing arguments was adequate # 'Harmless Error' Analysis for Cumulative Evidentiary Rulings

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of evidentiary challenges raised by the appellant following his conviction for being a felon in possession of a firearm. While the district court faced various objections regarding the admission of testimony concerning prior observations of the weapon and the scope of cross-examination, the appellate court affirmed the conviction largely based on the "harmless error" doctrine. This section analyzes how the court applied specific standards to determine that even if individual rulings were technically erroneous, they did not warrant reversing the jury's verdict due to the overwhelming strength of the government's case. Understanding this analysis is crucial for recognizing that appellate courts do not act as mere fault-finders; rather, they assess whether an error materially influenced the fairness or accuracy of the trial's outcome.

### 'Substantial Influence' Standard for Reversal (*United States v. DeMarce*)

The foundational principle governing Cutbank's evidentiary claims is articulated through the standard established in *United States v. DeMarce*. As noted in the opinion, this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling unless it finds that "the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." This standard places a heavy burden on the appellant to demonstrate that an error was not merely technical but had a tangible impact on the fact-finding process. In Cutbank's case, despite his numerous objections to testimony regarding D.F.'s past observations of the rifle and comments about her deceased sister, he failed to meet this threshold. The court reasoned that for a reversal to occur, there must be a reasonable probability that excluding or altering certain evidence would have changed the result. Because Cutbank could not show that these specific pieces of evidence were dispositive or uniquely prejudicial compared to other admissible proof, any isolated mistakes in admitting them were deemed harmless under *DeMarce*.

### 'Cumulative Effect' Doctrine (*United States v. Keys*)

Cutbank further argued that while no single error might be fatal on its own, their combined weight constituted reversible error. The appellate court explicitly addressed this "cumulative effect" argument by invoking *United States v. Keys*. Under this doctrine, an appellate court will only overturn a conviction based on multiple trial errors if there is "substantial prejudice" resulting from their aggregation. The court emphasized that minor evidentiary slips do not accumulate into constitutional violations absent a showing of significant overall prejudice against the defendant. In analyzing Cutbank's situation, the Eighth Circuit looked at whether these alleged errors collectively undermined confidence in the verdict. Given that most of Cutbank's claims lacked supporting legal authority and failed to identify specific unfair prejudice or confusion caused by each ruling individually, there was no logical basis for finding that their sum reached a level of substantial prejudice required to disturb a jury verdict under *Keys*.

### 'Overwhelming Evidence' Factor in Harmless Error Analysis

Central to applying both *DeMarce* and *Keys* in *Cutbank* was an assessment of the totality of evidence presented at trial—a factor often termed "overwhelming evidence." The opinion highlights that regardless of how one views specific contested rulings, the government's case remained robust enough to sustain a conviction independent of those rulings. The record contained substantial testimonial evidence from D.F., who provided detailed accounts of Cutbank holding her hostage and brandishing weapons, as well as C.F., who corroborated sightings of firearm usage. Crucially, this testimony was bolstered by irrefutable physical evidence: DNA matching Cutbank was found on a sawed-off rifle recovered near where he fled, alongside his cell phone and other incriminating items like a butcher knife. When viewed together, this body of proof left little room for doubt regarding guilt. Consequently, even if hypothetical errors occurred in how certain background details were introduced or how cross-examination proceeded, they could not have reasonably altered a rational juror's conclusion given such dense probative value surrounding identity and possession.

### 'Presumption of Correctness': Why Minor Errors Do Not Defeat Verdicts

Finally, *Cutbank* illustrates why appellate courts maintain a presumption that minor errors do not defeat otherwise valid verdicts. The opinion notes that Cutbank provided "no cases and almost no analysis" to support his contention that these rulings deprived him of a fair trial, effectively forfeiting his ability to prove reversible harm beyond speculation. By adhering strictly to procedural requirements and demanding concrete demonstration of prejudice rather than abstract complaints about courtroom atmosphere or tone (such as references to family tragedy during cross-examination), courts preserve judicial efficiency and respect for jury determinations. The affirmation serves as a reminder that unless an error rises above triviality or tangential relevance—and specifically impacts the core elements proven by strong evidence—it does not constitute grounds for reversal. In essence, because Cutbank could not bridge the gap between alleged procedural impropriety and actual substantive injustice amidst such overwhelming proof, his conviction stood firm against all cumulative challenges.

# Sentencing Enhancement Under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a specific sentencing challenge regarding the application of a four-level enhancement under United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision increases the base offense level when a defendant possesses a firearm "in connection with another felony offense." Cutbank argued that the district court erred in applying this enhancement, contending that doing so conflicted with the statutory mandate under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." To resolve this, the appellate court examined the definition of "connection," the procedural weight given to factual findings in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), and the interplay between this guideline enhancement and the mandatory penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA).

## "Connection" Requirement Under USSG Commentary Note 14(C)

The central legal question regarding § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is establishing the requisite link between the firearm possession and the underlying felony. The text of the guideline itself states that an enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed a firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense, regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained for that second offense. However, mere temporal coincidence is insufficient; there must be a causal or facilitating relationship where the possession advances or facilitates the commission of the other crime.

Commentary note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 clarifies this standard by listing examples of circumstances where such a connection exists, such as possessing a gun to facilitate drug trafficking or robbery. In *Cutbank*, the district court relied on factual findings in the PSR indicating that Cutbank possessed the sawed-off rifle while engaging in domestic violence, kidnapping his girlfriend at gunpoint, and subsequently resisting arrest by biting an officer and attempting to grab his weapon. The Eighth Circuit affirmed that these facts established a clear nexus: Cutbank did not merely possess the gun coincidentally during these events; he utilized it as an instrument of coercion and force to commit those offenses. Because Cutbank failed to cite any authority refuting this intrinsic link between his possession and his conduct during domestic abuse and flight from police, the court held that the factual predicate for the enhancement was met without needing a separate conviction for distinct charges related to those specific acts of violence.

## "Without Objection": Adoption of PSR Factual Findings

Procedurally, Cutbank's challenge hinged on how quickly he contested the factual basis for this enhancement. During sentencing proceedings, Cutbank raised no objection to the PSR's factual statements concluding that he possessed the firearm in connection with other felonies. Instead, he argued primarily against the legal consequence—the severity of applying such an enhancement—rather than disputing whether he actually committed those acts. The district court explicitly adopted these factual statements without objection from either party before imposing sentence.

Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in *Cutbank* (*United States v. Dunn*), when a district court adopts PSR factual findings without objection, those findings are entitled to deference on appeal unless they are clearly erroneous. Because Cutbank did not contest that he brandished or used weapons while committing domestic violence or resisting arrest at sentencing, he waived any argument challenging those specific facts on appeal. The appellate court emphasized that it need only determine whether sufficient evidence existed to support those unchallenged findings if reviewing them de novo; however, since they were adopted by agreement below, any error would have to be plain and affect substantial rights—a high bar not met here given his extensive criminal history and violent conduct described above. Consequently, because no objection was lodged regarding whether Gun A facilitated Felony B during trial or sentencing colloquy, Cutbank could not successfully argue on appeal that there was no connection between his possession and his subsequent crimes.

## Comparison: ACCA Guidelines vs. USSG § 2K2.1 Enhancements

A critical component of *Cutbank*'s sentencing argument involved potential double counting between two different statutory frameworks: ACCA and USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Cutbank faced mandatory minimum sentences under ACCA due to having three prior violent felony convictions ("career offender" status). Simultaneously, he faced guideline enhancements under § 2K2.1 for possessing a sawed-off firearm (which increased his base level) and possessing it in connection with another felony (§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)). His primary contention was that applying both regimes violated § 3553(a)'s requirement for proportionality by inflating his sentence beyond necessity through redundant calculations.

The Eighth Circuit clarified that these two mechanisms serve distinct functions and do not constitute impermissible double counting when handled according to specific guidelines rules. While ACCA imposes statutory mandatory minimums based solely on prior convictions regardless of current conduct details beyond basic elements, USSG enhancements adjust within-advisory ranges based on specific aggravating features of instant behavior (like using a gun during domestic abuse). Crucially, Comment n.(C) to USSG § 4B1 indicates how courts reconcile overlapping enhancements: when both ACCA guidelines (§ 4B1.) and specific enhancements apply (§ 2K2.), courts must adopt whichever results in the higher offense level rather than stacking them additively inappropriately or ignoring one entirely due to overlap concerns unrelated to legislative intent (*United States v.* Dunn). In *Cutbank*, although ACCA might have yielded an offense level slightly lower than what occurred with all enhancements combined (Level 34 vs Level 35), Guidelines § 4B1 mandates selecting greater levels when conflicts arise between career offender calculations and specific enhancements like § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Therefore, adopting Level 35 via § 2K2 rather than Level 34 via pure ACCA logic did not violate principles against double counting but followed explicit directive instructions within sentencing policy itself to maximize public safety concerns associated with armed recidivism involving active violence during commission of new crimes.

# Reasonableness Review Under § 3553(a)

The final stage of appellate review in *United States v. Cutbank* focuses on the substantive reasonableness of the defendant's sentence. After establishing that the district court correctly calculated the advisory Guidelines range and committed no significant procedural error, the Eighth Circuit turns to whether the specific sentence imposed—292 months—is consistent with the statutory mandate of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Cutbank contends that applying the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) resulted in a sentence that conflicted with the core principle of § 3553(a): that a punishment must be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary" to achieve the purposes of sentencing. The court rejects this argument, clarifying that when a district court imposes a sentence within the properly calculated advisory Guidelines range, appellate courts apply a presumption of reasonableness. This chapter explains how this presumption operates, why mechanical recitations of statutory factors are unnecessary, and how a defendant's extensive criminal history justifies a high-end within-range sentence even when it triggers mandatory minimum enhancements.

## Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Guidelines Sentences

Under Supreme Court precedent established in *Gall v. United States*, an appellate court must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error before evaluating substantive reasonableness. Once it is confirmed that the Guidelines range was calculated correctly—as occurred here where the court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) findings without objection—the focus shifts to whether the sentence itself is reasonable. A critical component of this analysis is the standard of review applied to within-Guidelines sentences. As stated in *United States v. Goodhouse*, when a district court imposes a sentence within the applicable Guidelines range, that sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal.

This presumption does not mean an appellate court blindly accepts every within-range sentence; rather, it establishes a deferential starting point for review. It places the burden on the appellant to demonstrate that the sentence is unreasonable in light of the specific circumstances of their case and the factors listed in § 3553(a). In *Cutbank*, because District Court Judge imposed exactly 292 months—a term falling squarely within the 292-to-365-month advisory range derived from his calculations—the Eighth Circuit began its analysis with this presumption intact. To overcome it, Cutbank would need to show that his specific situation warranted a variance away from these guidelines or that the calculation itself led to an unjust result contrary to statutory goals. His failure to demonstrate such unreasonableness meant he could not surmount this initial hurdle.

## Consideration of § 3553(a) Factors by District Court

A common misconception among appellants is that a sentencing judge must explicitly list or mechanically recite every factor enumerated in § 3553(a) during sentencing proceedings to satisfy procedural requirements and preserve substantive reasonableness on appeal. The Eighth Circuit in *Cutbank* clarifies that no such mechanical formula exists. While *Rosales-Mireles* mandates that an appellate court verify no procedural error occurred—including ensuring the judge correctly calculated Guidelines and considered § 3553(a)—the manner in which those considerations are expressed is flexible.

As reiterated through citations to *Masood* and *Maluoth*, upholding a sentence requires only that it be clear from the record that the district court actually considered these factors; it does not require a verbatim enumeration or detailed discussion of each one individually if they are implicitly addressed through judicial commentary and context. In Cutbank's case, the record demonstrated that at sentencing, the district court reviewed both his PSR and various sentencing memoranda submitted by counsel. Furthermore, by noting its review of these documents and referencing § 3553(a) generally as grounds for potential variances—which it ultimately declined to grant—the judge signaled active engagement with statutory mandates.
