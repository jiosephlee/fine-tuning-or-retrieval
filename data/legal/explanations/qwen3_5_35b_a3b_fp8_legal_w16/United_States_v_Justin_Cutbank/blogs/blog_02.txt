Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# The Silent Record: Appellate Review Standards and the Burden of Preservation in Evidentiary Challenges

**By [Your Name]**  
*October 14, 2025*

In the high-stakes theater of federal criminal appeals, the most dramatic moments often occur not in the courtroom where the trial unfolded, but in the quiet silence of a brief that says too little. *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, serves as a stark reminder that appellate courts are not fact-finders for lost causes; they are guardians of procedure. While the underlying facts—a violent domestic confrontation, a barricade standoff, and a bloody encounter with law enforcement—are undeniably harrowing, the Eighth Circuit's decision turns on a far more mundane but equally dispositive legal reality: an appellant cannot win by simply pointing out errors without explaining why those errors matter under existing precedent.

The case involves Justin Cutbank, a repeat offender sentenced to 292 months under the Armed Career Criminal Act for possessing a sawed-off rifle following a terrifying ordeal involving his girlfriend and her family. On appeal, Cutbank mounted a multi-pronged attack on his conviction and sentence, citing everything from improper character evidence to flawed jury instructions and alleged violations of Rule 403 balancing tests. Yet, despite the gravity of these allegations, his appeal collapsed under its own weight. As Judge Loken wrote for a unanimous panel, "Cutbank... provided no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments." This is not merely a stylistic critique; it is a procedural death knell. In the federal system, particularly within circuits like the Eighth that adhere strictly to preservation rules, an argument raised without citation to controlling authority or meaningful analysis is effectively waived.

Section II(D) and (E) of *Cutbank* offer particularly instructive lessons on the mechanics of preservation and authentication. During trial, Cutbank objected to testimony regarding his girlfriend's deceased sister being shot with a gun—a detail introduced during cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements. The district court denied the motion to strike, noting that such testimony was responsive and lacked evident unfair prejudice under Rule 403. On appeal, Cutbank failed to identify specific instances where this testimony confused the jury or rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. He offered nothing more than a bare assertion that prejudice existed. The Eighth Circuit rightly observed that "the court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless," especially when the defendant fails to demonstrate how the jury might have been "substantially swayed" by it. Without specific citations to case law illustrating how similar testimony warranted reversal in comparable contexts, the appellate court could only conclude that any potential error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt given the overwhelming volume of other evidence against Cutbank.

Even more telling is Section II(E), which addresses attempts to impeach government witnesses using extrinsic evidence—specifically police reports detailing prior inconsistent statements made by witness C.F. Defense counsel sought to introduce portions of these reports into evidence but hit an authentication wall when they tried to read them directly into the record without establishing that C.F. authored them or verified their accuracy at that moment. The district court sustained objections regarding proper authentication procedures required by *Federal Rule of Evidence 613*. On appeal, rather than addressing whether this restriction violated Confrontation Clause rights or amounted to an abuse of discretion under established precedent (*United States v. Cumbie*), Cutbank's team largely ignored this issue in their briefing until forced upon them by government arguments in response briefs. Judge Loken did not mince words here either: "We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal." The result? Even assuming arguendo that there was error (which there wasn't), it would fail plain error review because it was neither obvious nor prejudicial enough to affect substantial rights when viewed against the totality of the record.

The doctrinal implications for defense counsel are immediate and severe. *Cutbank* reinforces a hard truth: appellate advocacy is distinct from trial advocacy. A lawyer who successfully objects at trial must immediately pivot to crafting an argument for appeal that bridges current facts with binding precedent. Merely stating "Rule 403 requires exclusion" is insufficient if one cannot point to circuit-specific jurisprudence defining exactly how much prejudice constitutes reversible error in similar domestic violence scenarios. Furthermore, reliance on general principles without specific application invites summary dismissal. The Eighth Circuit's explicit rebuke suggests that judges are increasingly willing to treat vague or unsupported assertions as forfeited issues rather than opportunities for correction.

Downstream effects may include stricter scrutiny of pro se filings or poorly drafted briefs from appointed counsel who lack specialized appellate experience. Lawyers must ensure every objection is preserved with precision—not just at trial but in writing—and every appellate argument must be anchored in specific citations to controlling authority within their jurisdiction or persuasive Supreme Court guidance where local law is silent. *Cutbank* also highlights the resilience of within-Guidelines sentences; once properly calculated and supported by robust statutory findings (as seen here with ACCA enhancements), even aggressive challenges based on minor evidentiary skirmishes rarely succeed unless they rise above mere speculation into demonstrable constitutional infirmity or clear abuse of discretion backed by solid law.

Ultimately, *United States v.* **Cutbank** stands as a cautionary tale about the cost of silence in appellate practice. The facts were ugly enough to make any reader sympathetic toward cutting corners in sentencing calculations or challenging evidentiary boundaries aggressively—but sympathy does not override procedure when an appellant refuses to engage with it meaningfully on paper. For practitioners navigating complex criminal appeals today, *Cutbank* delivers an unambiguous message: If you want your voice heard on appeal, you must speak up early, cite your sources clearly, and prove your case with precision—or accept affirmation as inevitable consequence of neglecting your duty to preserve and argue effectively below and above alike
