Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# ACCA Overload: Navigating Conflicting Guidelines Enhancements Under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

**By [Your Name]** | *October 10, 2025*

When a defendant faces both the statutory hammer of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the intricate machinery of the Sentencing Guidelines, the interplay between the two often becomes a flashpoint for appellate litigation. Yesterday, in *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed exactly this friction, rejecting a defendant's contention that stacking an ACCA-mandated sentence with a specific Guidelines enhancement violated the "sufficient but not greater than necessary" mandate of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). While the case involves a gruesome factual backdrop involving domestic terror and resistance to arrest, its legal significance lies in clarifying how courts reconcile mandatory statutory floors with discretionary guideline calculations when multiple aggravating factors collide.

The story begins with Justin Cutbank, whose night of terror ended in a confrontation that saw him hold his girlfriend at gunpoint, barricade himself in a garage, and bite an officer attempting his detention. Convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and triggering ACCA due to three prior violent felonies, Cutbank faced a presumptive sentence under the statute. However, his appeal turned on the sentencing phase, specifically regarding the application of U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision mandates a four-level enhancement if a defendant possesses a firearm "in connection with another felony offense." The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) applied this enhancement alongside others—including those for altered serial numbers and reckless conduct—pushing his advisory Guidelines range up to 292 months. Cutbank argued that applying this enhancement was redundant or improper once ACCA had already dictated his punishment structure, effectively claiming it created an impermissible conflict with § 3553(a).

The Eighth Circuit's rejection of this argument rests on a straightforward reading of the Sentencing Commission's commentary and existing precedent regarding the relationship between statutes and guidelines. As Judge Loken noted for the panel, ACCA operates as a statutory floor; it establishes minimum penalties based on prior convictions but does not necessarily dictate the precise top-end calculation within an advisory framework. The Guidelines explicitly account for situations where an offender qualifies for both ACCA and other enhancements. Specifically, USSG § 4B1.4(b) directs courts to apply whichever offense level produces the greater result—whether derived from ACCA or from standard guideline calculations involving enhancements like § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Because Cutbank's calculated offense level under these combined factors (level 35) exceeded what ACCA alone might have yielded via its specific guideline counterpart (level 34), the district court was correct to adopt the higher figure. The court emphasized that there is no inherent contradiction between satisfying a statutory minimum and adhering to guideline recommendations designed to reflect the totality of criminal conduct.

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder that defendants cannot easily use § 3553(a)'s general proportionality principle to dismantle specific guideline enhancements merely because they coincide with statutory mandates. The Eighth Circuit rightly distinguished between procedural error—which requires strict adherence to calculation methods—and substantive unreasonableness—the ultimate weight given to those calculations after considering all factors. Since Cutbank received a sentence within the properly calculated advisory range, which included nearly twelve prior felony convictions and violent acts against family members and law enforcement, any claim that the sentence was "greater than necessary" is hard-won on appeal absent extraordinary circumstances. The court's presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences remains robust here; once the math checks out according to § 4B1.4(b), shifting burden falls heavily on the appellant to show why that mathematical outcome violates broader sentencing goals—a bar *Cutbank* failed to clear given his extensive history of violence.

Beyond this specific holding, *Cutbank* offers important doctrinal clarity for practitioners navigating multi-faceted sentencing disputes involving firearms offenses where other felonies are intertwined with possession itself. The decision reinforces that "possession in connection with another felony" is not merely duplicative when an offender is simultaneously subject to career offender status or ACCA; rather, it functions as an independent aggravator intended to capture conduct that occurs beyond mere passive ownership or simple trafficking boundaries defined by other statutes. For future litigants facing similar overlaps between ACCA and specific possession enhancements, *Cutbank* suggests that challenges must focus less on theoretical conflicts between statutes and guidelines and more on whether individual underlying facts truly support each distinct element required for every layer of enhancement applied by the district court.
