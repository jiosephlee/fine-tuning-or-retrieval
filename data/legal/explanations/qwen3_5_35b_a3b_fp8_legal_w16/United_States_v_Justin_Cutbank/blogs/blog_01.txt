Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic vs. Propensity: The Eighth Circuit Reaffirms the 'Continuous Possession' Doctrine in Firearm Cases

**By [Your Name]** | *October 10, 2025*

In the high-stakes world of federal firearm prosecutions, few evidentiary battles are as frequent—or as consequential—as the attempt to exclude "bad act" testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). Defendants in these cases often argue that introducing evidence of prior interactions with a weapon is merely character assassination, designed to convince a jury that a person with a violent past is more likely to commit a crime today. However, the Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week, serves as a stark reminder that the line between impermissible propensity and permissible intrinsic evidence can be drawn quite narrowly around the concept of "continuous possession." By reaffirming its precedent in *United States v. Battle*, the court clarified that proving a defendant held onto a specific firearm days or weeks before an alleged violation is not just relevant; it is essential to establishing the very element of possession required for conviction.

The facts of *Cutbank* present a volatile scenario typical of domestic violence and drug-fueled confrontations. After consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank threatened his girlfriend with a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, holding her hostage and eventually fleeing to a nearby garage where he barricaded himself against police. During the ensuing standoff, he bit an officer and attempted to grab their weapon before surrendering. Crucially, when police recovered the sawed-off rifle from his escape route, they found DNA matching Cutbank. Yet, before trial even began, Cutbank moved to suppress testimony from his girlfriend and her housemate stating they had seen him possess that specific rifle on previous occasions. His argument was standard fare for defense counsel: admitting this testimony violated Rule 404(b) because it suggested he was a dangerous person prone to carrying weapons, rather than proving he actually possessed the gun on November 20, 2020.

The Eighth Circuit had little patience for this distinction in light of established doctrine. Writing for the panel, Judge Loken relied heavily on *Battle*, noting that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control." This reasoning transforms what might look like character evidence into intrinsic evidence—facts so closely tied to the charged offense that they explain how the defendant came to have control over the contraband in question. If Cutbank had never touched that specific sawed-off rifle before that night, his sudden appearance with it at his girlfriend's door might have seemed coincidental or planted. But by showing he had carried it "a handful of times" previously, the prosecution provided necessary context for his immediate access and exclusive control at the time of arrest. The court's refusal to treat this as propensity evidence underscores a pragmatic reality: in gun cases involving unique weapons like sawed-off rifles, history isn't just background noise; it's part of the chain of custody leading up to the crime itself.

While *Cutbank* resolves easily on evidentiary grounds due to overwhelming physical and testimonial proof—including DNA matches and eyewitness identification—the broader doctrinal implication lies in how courts define "intrinsic" versus "extrinsic" acts in possession cases. Prosecutors frequently rely on this logic to paint a picture of ongoing criminal enterprise without needing separate charges for every instance of prior use. As long as there is a logical nexus between the prior act and the charged offense—specifically regarding identity or continuity of possession—the door remains open for juries to hear about those prior interactions. This approach effectively lowers the barrier for prosecutors seeking to prove dominion over illegal firearms, particularly when dealing with distinctive items where general possession theories are insufficient.

Beyond the evidentiary win, *Cutbank* offers little respite for defendants facing steep sentencing enhancements under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). Cutbank challenged his sentence primarily on procedural grounds regarding USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which mandates a four-level enhancement if a firearm is possessed "in connection with another felony." He argued that applying this enhancement alongside ACCA penalties resulted in an overly harsh sentence that conflicted with Section 3553(a)'s mandate for proportionality. The Eighth Circuit flatly rejected this view, emphasizing that once within-range sentences are calculated correctly based on valid guideline applications, appellate courts grant district judges wide latitude under *Gall*. Given Cutbank's dozen-plus prior felonies and the violent nature of his conduct—domestic terrorization coupled with resistance against law enforcement—the court found no substantive unreasonableness in imposing 292 months behind bars.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* reinforces two critical pillars of federal criminal procedure: first, that evidence connecting a defendant to a specific weapon through prior use is often indispensable rather than prejudicial; and second, that sentencing courts retain robust discretion when navigating complex statutory overlaps like ACCA and sentencing guidelines enhancements. For practitioners litigating similar fact patterns involving unique firearms or escalating domestic situations, *Cutbank* suggests that fighting admissibility on pure Rule 404(b) grounds may be futile if the government can articulate a theory of continuous possession anchored by concrete examples of prior contact with the instrumentality itself.
