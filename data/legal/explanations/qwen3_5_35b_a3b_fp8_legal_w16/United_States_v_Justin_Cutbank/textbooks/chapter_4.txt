Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 4: # 'Substantial Prejudice' Standard for Evidentiary Errors

## 'Harmless Error': The Burden on Appellant to Show Substantial Prejudice

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the appellate court addresses a critical threshold for reversing a criminal conviction: even if a district court makes an error in admitting or excluding evidence, that error does not automatically result in a new trial. Instead, the court applies the "harmless error" doctrine, which places a heavy burden on the appellant to demonstrate that the mistake affected the outcome of the case. As outlined in the opinion, the Eighth Circuit adheres to the standard articulated in *United States v. DeMarce*: a court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling unless it concludes that "the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." This standard requires more than a mere possibility of prejudice; the defendant must show that the error had a substantial influence on the verdict. If the error did not influence, or had only a slight influence, on the jury's decision, the conviction stands despite the procedural misstep. In Cutbank's case, because he failed to meet this high burden of showing actual prejudice, his evidentiary complaints were dismissed regardless of their theoretical merit.

## 'Cumulative Effect': Analyzing Multiple Minor Rulings Together

Cutbank raised numerous specific complaints regarding evidentiary rulings throughout his trial, ranging from objections to prior bad acts testimony to disputes over cross-examination limits and limiting instructions. However, the appellate court emphasized that these individual rulings must also be evaluated collectively under a "cumulative effect" analysis. Even if one assumes for argument's sake that every single ruling challenged by Cutbank was erroneous—a premise he fails to prove—the court explained that it will not overturn a conviction based on this accumulation of errors unless there is "substantial prejudice" arising from their combined impact. The logic follows that minor or non-prejudicial errors do not compound into reversible misconduct simply because they occur frequently. The court cited *United States v. Keys* to reinforce that while defendants are entitled to fair trials, isolated mistakes or even multiple minor errors do not necessitate reversal absent a showing that they fundamentally compromised the fairness of the proceeding or misled the jury. Since Cutbank could not demonstrate that these alleged errors individually or collectively substantially swayed his fate, his appeal on these grounds failed entirely.

## 'Overwhelming Evidence': Independent Corroboration via Physical and Testimonial Means

The most decisive factor in applying the harmless error standard in *Cutbank* was the strength of the independent evidence supporting his conviction. To overcome an appellate challenge based on potentially improper evidence, an appellant must often show that without that evidence, there is insufficient proof left to sustain a verdict beyond a reasonable doubt. The court noted that Cutbank faced "substantial" government evidence both testimonial and physical that stood independently of any contested admissions or exclusions at trial. Specifically, D.F.'s direct testimony placed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle and detailed his violent conduct against her and her family. Furthermore, C.F., another witness provided identification testimony linking Cutbank to the weapon before and during the incident. Crucially, physical evidence corroborated these accounts: police recovered D.F.'s cell phone and Marlin .22 caliber rifle along Cutbank's escape route, and DNA testing confirmed Match cutbank's presence on the weapon itself. Because this robust chain of identification and forensic evidence existed regardless of whether D.F.'s statements regarding prior gun sightings were deemed admissible propensity evidence or intrinsic evidence, any potential error in admitting such testimony could not have substantially influenced the jury's determination of guilt.

## 'Presumption of Regularity': Why Convictions Stand Despite Isolated Mistakes

Underlying these analyses is a fundamental presumption of regularity afforded to district courts during trial proceedings. While appellate courts review decisions de novo for legal questions and for abuse of discretion regarding evidentiary rulings (*Sprint/United Mgmt. Co.*), they operate under an assumption that lower courts generally follow proper procedures unless shown otherwise. In *Cutbank*, this presumption manifested in two ways: first through deference to unchallenged trial tactics where no objection was preserved (such as C.F.'s mention of D.F.'s deceased sister), and second through skepticism toward arguments lacking legal support or factual basis (as evidenced by Cutbank providing "no cases and almost no analysis"). The court explicitly stated it would not appreciate appellants expanding issues unnecessarily on appeal without merit. Ultimately, because Cutbank presented no compelling argument that any specific ruling caused him substantial prejudice—and because his arguments were unsupported by authority or fact—the appellate court presumed nothing went wrong enough to warrant disturbing the judgment. This reinforces a key tenet of appellate practice: convictions are resilient against challenges based on technical errors when those errors do not materially affect justice served by overwhelming independent proof of guilt.
