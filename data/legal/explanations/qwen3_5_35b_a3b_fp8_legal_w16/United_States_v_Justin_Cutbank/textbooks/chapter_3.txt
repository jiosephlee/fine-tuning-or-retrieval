Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Hearsay, Confrontation Clause, and Trial Procedure Errors

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of procedural and evidentiary challenges raised by the appellant regarding the admission of witness statements to police, the adequacy of jury instructions, and the scope of permissible cross-examination. Cutbank argued that the district court erred by admitting out-of-court statements by his girlfriend, D.F., and her housemate, C.F., which he characterized as hearsay violations that also infringed upon his Sixth Amendment right to confrontation. Furthermore, he contended that the court failed to provide specific limiting instructions mid-trial to prevent the jury from treating these statements as substantive evidence of his guilt. The appellate court systematically dismantled these arguments by clarifying that when a declarant appears in court to testify, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied regardless of whether their prior statements were admitted for substantive purposes. Additionally, the court emphasized that trial judges possess broad discretion in managing proceedings, particularly when out-of-court statements serve a non-hearsay purpose—such as explaining an officer's conduct—and when limiting instructions are provided at the close of trial rather than immediately upon evidence admission. Finally, the opinion illustrates how courts balance probative value against unfair prejudice under Federal Rule of Evidence 403 when defense counsel attempts to introduce sensitive personal history during cross-examination.

## Confrontation Clause Compliance via Witness Testimony

The cornerstone of Cutbank's constitutional challenge rested on the assertion that admitting D.F.'s prior statements to police violated his rights under the Confrontation Clause. The Confrontation Clause guarantees a criminal defendant's right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." However, this right is fundamentally satisfied when the declarant actually appears in court and testifies under oath. As established in *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860 (8th Cir. 2010), and reiterated in *Cutbank*, "the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarants... actually appear in court and testify in person." Because D.F. took the stand at trial to recount her experience with Cutbank—including her account of being held at gunpoint—the primary barrier to admissibility was removed. The appellate court noted that while D.F.'s earlier statements to police might otherwise constitute inadmissible hearsay if offered for their truth without her presence, her live testimony rendered any potential confrontation error moot. Consequently, even if those prior statements were admitted as substantive evidence or merely for context, Cutbank's opportunity to cross-examine D.F. about inconsistencies between her trial testimony and her initial reports ensured compliance with constitutional mandates.

## 'Contextual' Hearsay Admissibility for Officer Knowledge

Beyond the Confrontation Clause argument, Cutbank challenged the admission of D.F.'s statements on traditional hearsay grounds under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c). Generally, an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted is inadmissible hearsay unless it falls under a recognized exception. However, not every out-of-court statement is offered for its truth; sometimes it is introduced solely to explain why a law enforcement officer acted in a certain way or possessed specific knowledge at a particular moment. In *Cutbank*, D.F.'s calls to dispatch describing Cutbank holding her hostage and claiming he had weapons provided critical context for why officers arrived at specific locations with heightened alertness and采取了 specific tactical approaches during their search and detention efforts. The government successfully argued that these statements were not offered to prove that Cutbank actually possessed a weapon or threatened D.F., but rather to demonstrate what prompted the officers' subsequent actions and their state of mind upon arrival at scene two—a garage where Cutbank was eventually found barricaded inside. Since these statements were not offered for their truth but rather as background information essential to understanding the narrative flow of law enforcement activity, they fell outside the definition of hearsay entirely or qualified under exceptions for non-testimonial contexts where reliability is inferred through other means (such as immediate reaction). The appellate court accepted this distinction, noting that admitting such evidence does not violate evidentiary rules so long as it serves a legitimate non-substantive purpose related to officer conduct or investigation continuity.

## 'No Abuse of Discretion': Failure to Provide Limiting Instructions Mid-Trial

Cutbank further argued that because potentially problematic hearsay-like material was admitted early in proceedings (specifically regarding D.F.'s initial reports), the district court erred by refusing his request for immediate limiting instructions telling jurors they could not consider those statements as proof of guilt until D.F.'s own testimony was delivered later in trial. This contention touches upon standard procedure governing how courts manage juror comprehension during complex trials involving mixed evidence types. Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in *Cutbank* (*United States v. Bennett*), a district court need not issue limiting instructions immediately after every piece of questionable evidence is admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the conclusion of all evidence ("at close"). The appellate panel reasoned that requiring mid-trial interruptions for every potential evidentiary nuance would disrupt trial efficiency without necessarily enhancing fairness, especially when final charges can effectively guide deliberations retrospectively. Moreover, since D.F.'s live testimony ultimately covered all material aspects alleged in her prior reports—and thus cured any ambiguity regarding what constituted direct versus circumstantial proof—the absence of interim warnings did not constitute reversible error.
