Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 5: # Sentencing Enhancement Application under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## Connection Requirement: Possessing Firearm During Another Felony
The central sentencing dispute in *United States v. Cutbank* concerns the applicability of the four-level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision mandates an increase in the offense level if the defendant "used or possessed" a firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense. In *Cutbank*, the district court applied this enhancement based on the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), which detailed that Cutbank possessed a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle while committing domestic abuse, kidnapping, and assaulting police officers. The appellate court affirmed that these underlying acts constituted distinct felony offenses connected to the firearm possession. The "connection" requirement is satisfied where the possession of the firearm facilitates or has a relationship to the commission of another crime; here, Cutbank brandished the weapon to threaten his girlfriend, use it as a tool of coercion during the confinement, and wield it while resisting arrest and fleeing from law enforcement. The court reasoned that because Cutbank's possession was integral to these separate criminal episodes, the enhancement was properly triggered without needing to prove a specific intent to commit those felonies beyond what was already established by the facts themselves.

## Regardless Doctrine: Applicability Without Separate Charges or Convictions
A critical aspect of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) highlighted in *Cutbank* is its explicit language stating that the enhancement applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." This doctrine allows sentencing courts to apply enhancements based on conduct that constitutes a felony even if that conduct was not separately prosecuted or resulted in a standalone conviction. In this case, Cutbank did not argue successfully against this standard; rather, his challenge focused on whether applying this guideline conflicted with statutory sentencing principles under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit clarified that the Advisory Guidelines are designed to reflect actual criminal behavior, not just adjudicated counts. Because Cutbank's conduct involved possessing the firearm during domestic violence and flight from police—acts that independently qualify as felonies—the absence of separate charges for those specific acts did not preclude the application of the four-level increase. The court emphasized that relying solely on formal charges would undermine the Guidelines' purpose of tailoring sentences to the severity of actual conduct.

## Guidelines Conflict Resolution: Comparing ACCA vs. General Sentencing Tables
The opinion further addresses how courts resolve potential conflicts between different sentencing frameworks when they produce different offense levels. Cutbank argued that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) resulted in a sentence higher than what might be appropriate under other considerations, specifically noting a conflict with his Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) status. Under ACCA, which applies due to his three prior violent felony convictions, Cutbank faced a mandatory statutory minimum of 15 years (180 months), but also triggered specific guideline calculations under USSG § 4B1.4 (Career Offender) yielding an offense level of 34. However, when combining his base offense level with various enhancements—including those for having three prior violent felonies and using a sawed-off weapon—the general calculation yielded an offense level of 35 due to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The Guidelines explicitly provide that when different provisions yield different offense levels, the greater level controls (§ 4B1.4(b)). Consequently, despite Cutbank's contention that his ACCA-based sentence should dictate otherwise or create a conflict requiring adjustment under § 3553(a), the appellate court held that following the "greater offense level" rule was correct procedure. This ensures consistency in sentencing outcomes where multiple statutory and guideline provisions overlap.

## Fact Finding: Reliance on PSR Facts Adopted by District Court
Regarding how these factual determinations are made at sentencing, *Cutbank* illustrates deference to district court fact-finding based on PSR recommendations accepted by both parties at trial or lack thereof during sentencing proceedings. The district court adopted the PSR's factual statements regarding Cutbank's prior felony convictions and his use of the firearm during those incidents without objection from either side at sentencing hearings before raising it on appeal as error-free findings required no independent judicial inquiry beyond confirming they were accurate and relevant to Guideline application ("clearly erroneous"). Since Cutbank failed to object to these factual assertions below regarding whether he used or possessed the gun during other felonies, he effectively conceded their accuracy for sentencing purposes unless he could demonstrate clear error—a high bar rarely met absent contradictory evidence presented at trial or post-trial motions challenging credibility directly related to these specific facts.* While some evidentiary disputes arose earlier regarding hearsay and witness testimony at trial regarding possession itself (*e.g.*, whether witnesses saw him hold it previously), once those foundational facts were settled by conviction and admission via PSR adoption for sentencing purposes (*i.e.*, facts supporting enhancements), appellate scrutiny focused strictly on legal correctness rather than re-litigating minor factual ambiguities.* Thus, reliance on unchallenged PSR data remains robust so long as procedural safeguards like notice and opportunity for rebuttal were afforded during pre-sentence conferences.**

## Standard of Review: Clear Error vs Abuse Discretion in Fact-Finding
Finally, understanding *Cutbank*'s outcome requires grasping how standards of review shape appellate intervention in sentencing decisions involving Guideline applications versus discretionary variances.* For purely legal questions—such as interpreting what constitutes "possession in connection with another felony"—appellate courts apply de novo review; however,*factual findings underlying those interpretations receive clear error review.* Conversely,*discretionary decisions about whether to depart from guidelines based upon §3553(a) factors are reviewed for abuse of discretion.* Here,*the district court's determination that Cutback's conduct fell within §2K2.(b)*(6(B)) rested largely on undisputed facts found true by jury verdicts (*prior convictions*) plus additional behavioral details (*use during domestic disturbance*). Because neither party contested these facts sufficiently below (*except generally through vague arguments*), appellate judges assumed they were correct unless manifestly wrong (*clear error standard).* Even though cutback claimed substantive unreasonableness (*conflict with core principles*), such challenges demand showing abuse discretion—meaning sentence must exceed bounds reasonableness considering totality circumstances (*violent history + current brutality*). Given overwhelming evidence supporting enhanced culpability (*dozens prior felonies + active resistance+domestic threat*), no meaningful ground existed overturning within-range imposition despite theoretical tension between ACCA/Guidelines paths.*
