Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 5: # Sentencing Enhancement Under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a specific sentencing challenge regarding the application of a four-level enhancement under United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision increases the base offense level when a defendant possesses a firearm "in connection with another felony offense." Cutbank argued that the district court erred in applying this enhancement, contending that doing so conflicted with the statutory mandate under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." To resolve this, the appellate court examined the definition of "connection," the procedural weight given to factual findings in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), and the interplay between this guideline enhancement and the mandatory penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA).

## "Connection" Requirement Under USSG Commentary Note 14(C)

The central legal question regarding § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is establishing the requisite link between the firearm possession and the underlying felony. The text of the guideline itself states that an enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed a firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense, regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained for that second offense. However, mere temporal coincidence is insufficient; there must be a causal or facilitating relationship where the possession advances or facilitates the commission of the other crime.

Commentary note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 clarifies this standard by listing examples of circumstances where such a connection exists, such as possessing a gun to facilitate drug trafficking or robbery. In *Cutbank*, the district court relied on factual findings in the PSR indicating that Cutbank possessed the sawed-off rifle while engaging in domestic violence, kidnapping his girlfriend at gunpoint, and subsequently resisting arrest by biting an officer and attempting to grab his weapon. The Eighth Circuit affirmed that these facts established a clear nexus: Cutbank did not merely possess the gun coincidentally during these events; he utilized it as an instrument of coercion and force to commit those offenses. Because Cutbank failed to cite any authority refuting this intrinsic link between his possession and his conduct during domestic abuse and flight from police, the court held that the factual predicate for the enhancement was met without needing a separate conviction for distinct charges related to those specific acts of violence.

## "Without Objection": Adoption of PSR Factual Findings

Procedurally, Cutbank's challenge hinged on how quickly he contested the factual basis for this enhancement. During sentencing proceedings, Cutbank raised no objection to the PSR's factual statements concluding that he possessed the firearm in connection with other felonies. Instead, he argued primarily against the legal consequence—the severity of applying such an enhancement—rather than disputing whether he actually committed those acts. The district court explicitly adopted these factual statements without objection from either party before imposing sentence.

Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in *Cutbank* (*United States v. Dunn*), when a district court adopts PSR factual findings without objection, those findings are entitled to deference on appeal unless they are clearly erroneous. Because Cutbank did not contest that he brandished or used weapons while committing domestic violence or resisting arrest at sentencing, he waived any argument challenging those specific facts on appeal. The appellate court emphasized that it need only determine whether sufficient evidence existed to support those unchallenged findings if reviewing them de novo; however, since they were adopted by agreement below, any error would have to be plain and affect substantial rights—a high bar not met here given his extensive criminal history and violent conduct described above. Consequently, because no objection was lodged regarding whether Gun A facilitated Felony B during trial or sentencing colloquy, Cutbank could not successfully argue on appeal that there was no connection between his possession and his subsequent crimes.

## Comparison: ACCA Guidelines vs. USSG § 2K2.1 Enhancements

A critical component of *Cutbank*'s sentencing argument involved potential double counting between two different statutory frameworks: ACCA and USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Cutbank faced mandatory minimum sentences under ACCA due to having three prior violent felony convictions ("career offender" status). Simultaneously, he faced guideline enhancements under § 2K2.1 for possessing a sawed-off firearm (which increased his base level) and possessing it in connection with another felony (§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)). His primary contention was that applying both regimes violated § 3553(a)'s requirement for proportionality by inflating his sentence beyond necessity through redundant calculations.

The Eighth Circuit clarified that these two mechanisms serve distinct functions and do not constitute impermissible double counting when handled according to specific guidelines rules. While ACCA imposes statutory mandatory minimums based solely on prior convictions regardless of current conduct details beyond basic elements, USSG enhancements adjust within-advisory ranges based on specific aggravating features of instant behavior (like using a gun during domestic abuse). Crucially, Comment n.(C) to USSG § 4B1 indicates how courts reconcile overlapping enhancements: when both ACCA guidelines (§ 4B1.) and specific enhancements apply (§ 2K2.), courts must adopt whichever results in the higher offense level rather than stacking them additively inappropriately or ignoring one entirely due to overlap concerns unrelated to legislative intent (*United States v.* Dunn). In *Cutbank*, although ACCA might have yielded an offense level slightly lower than what occurred with all enhancements combined (Level 34 vs Level 35), Guidelines § 4B1 mandates selecting greater levels when conflicts arise between career offender calculations and specific enhancements like § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Therefore, adopting Level 35 via § 2K2 rather than Level 34 via pure ACCA logic did not violate principles against double counting but followed explicit directive instructions within sentencing policy itself to maximize public safety concerns associated with armed recidivism involving active violence during commission of new crimes.
