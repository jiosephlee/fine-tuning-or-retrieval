Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Admissibility of Intrinsic Evidence Under Rule 404(b)

## Rule 404(b): Propensity vs. Intrinsic Evidence Distinction
In the case of *United States v. Cutbank*, the defendant challenged the admission of testimony from witnesses D.F. and C.F., who stated they had seen Cutbank possess the specific sawed-off Marlin rifle on previous occasions prior to the charged incident. Cutbank argued that this testimony violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally prohibits introducing "other acts" evidence to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in conformity with that character—commonly known as propensity evidence. However, the Eighth Circuit clarified that Rule 404(b) does not bar all evidence of prior bad acts; it specifically targets extrinsic acts offered solely to suggest guilt by character. The court distinguished these prohibited "propensity" uses from "intrinsic" evidence, which describes the immediate circumstances of the crime or is necessary to complete the story of the crime for the jury. If evidence is intrinsic, it is admissible regardless of whether it involves other bad acts, provided it satisfies relevance and prejudice standards under Rule 403. In *Cutbank*, the court held that testimony regarding prior possession was intrinsic because it was inextricably linked to proving the elements of the charged offense: knowing possession of a firearm by a felon.

## The Requirement to Prove Ownership or Control
For a prosecution to succeed on a charge of unlawful possession under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knowingly possessed the weapon and exercised dominion and control over it at the time of the alleged offense. In cases where possession is fleeting or contested, establishing that a defendant owned or controlled a specific weapon often requires proof connecting them to that object before and after its discovery. As noted in *Cutbank*, mere recovery of a weapon near a suspect does not automatically prove ownership; circumstantial evidence is frequently necessary to bridge gaps in direct observation. Consequently, showing that a witness had seen the defendant with that specific firearm earlier serves as critical context to demonstrate continuous possession rather than isolated, coincidental contact. Without such background, jurors might reasonably doubt whether Cutbank actually controlled the gun found in his path or whether someone else placed it there merely moments before his arrest.

## Application of *United States v. Battle*: Prior Possession as Relevant Context
The Eighth Circuit relied heavily on its precedent in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), which established that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon." In *Battle*, similar to *Cutbank*, defendants argued that prior instances involving weapons were improper character evidence; however, the court ruled otherwise when those instances helped establish ownership or control over the specific instrumentality used in the current crime. Applying this standard, Judge Loken reasoned that because D.F.'s testimony indicated she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle "a handful of times" before November 2020, this evidence was intrinsic to proving he maintained control over it leading up to and during his flight from police. The court emphasized that this line of questioning did not ask Juries to infer guilt based on past behavior but rather asked them to identify continuity between past observations and present custody, thereby satisfying an element of the charged offense rather than attacking character impermissibly.
