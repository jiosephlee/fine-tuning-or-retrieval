Chapter 6: # Procedural Reasonableness and Substantive Sentence Review

The appellate court's final evaluation in *United States v. Cutbank* centers on the standard of review governing sentences, specifically distinguishing between procedural correctness and substantive reasonableness. Before an appellate court can assess whether a sentence is too harsh, it must first ensure the district court followed the correct procedural steps. As established in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, the primary requirement is that the district court correctly calculates the applicable Guidelines range; failing to do so constitutes a "significant procedural error" that requires remand. In Cutbank's case, the court confirmed that the district judge properly calculated the offense level at 35 based on the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR). The calculation included specific enhancements: a three-level increase for an altered serial number, a two-level increase for creating a reckless risk of death while fleeing police, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Because Cutbank's Armed Career Criminal Act status triggered a higher guideline range under U.S.S.G. § 4B1.4, the court correctly applied the greater offense level of 35 rather than the lower level derived solely from his career offender status. With no significant procedural error identified in this initial calculation phase, the appellate court moved to evaluate whether the resulting sentence was substantively reasonable.

Once procedural correctness is established, the focus shifts to whether the district court adequately considered the statutory factors outlined in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Under *Gall v. United States*, sentencing courts must determine an appropriate sentence by beginning with the correct Guidelines range and then considering whether a departure or variance is warranted based on these factors. However, *United States v. Masood* clarifies that this does not require a "mechanical recitation" of every factor or a detailed explanation rejecting each argument raised by the defendant. Instead, it suffices if it is clear from the record that the judge actually weighed these considerations. In Cutbank's appeal, he argued that applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) resulted in a sentence conflicting with § 3553(a)'s mandate that punishment be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The Eighth Circuit found no error here; although Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions involving violence and firearms, and his current conduct involved domestic abuse, kidnapping-like behavior in front of children, and assaulting an officer, there was no indication he failed to consider these circumstances. The record demonstrated that at sentencing, the judge reviewed both his own observations and written submissions regarding § 3553(a) factors before imposing a within-range sentence. Consequently, any ambiguity regarding specific arguments against enhancement was resolved by reviewing what occurred at trial rather than demanding further justification from the trial judge post-hoc.

The presumption of reasonableness plays a critical role when an appellate court reviews a sentence imposed within the properly calculated Guidelines range. While this presumption is rebuttable—meaning a defendant can argue for unreasonableness—it places a heavy burden on appellant to show why such length is unjustified relative to other defendants with similar records and offenses. In Cutbank's instance, he received a 292-month sentence located at the very bottom of his advisory Guidelines range (292–365 months). By choosing the lowest possible term available within his category, even though his criminal history placed him near its ceiling, demonstrated judicial caution against excessive punishment despite severe aggravating circumstances. The fact that Cutbank faced such high guidelines due to his extensive violent past meant that even this relatively lenient outcome reflected a high degree of severity compared to typical offenders. Therefore, affirming this low-end-of-the-range sentence reinforced its substantive reasonableness; if anything went wrong in balancing fairness versus deterrence here, it would have been toward granting leniency rather than oppression.
