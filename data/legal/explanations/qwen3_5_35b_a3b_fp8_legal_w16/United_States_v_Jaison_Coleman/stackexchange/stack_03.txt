Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### What is the scope of a 'welfare check' consent when officers subsequently discover evidence unrelated to immediate safety threats?
Question:
The court affirmed that the officers stayed within the scope of Lisa's consent to check on her children and ensure Coleman wasn't dangerous. Once inside, they found Coleman and eventually secured a warrant based on new info (kids pointing out a gun). My confusion lies in whether scanning every room to confirm no one is hiding constitutes exceeding consent if no immediate threat is found in those rooms immediately. If they had found drugs in plain view during this general sweep before getting a warrant, would that have been admissible under this specific scope of consent theory?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Under *United States v. Coleman*, a "welfare check" consent to ensure the safety of occupants includes the authority to conduct a general visual sweep of the premises to verify that no one is hiding and that no immediate threats exist. Evidence discovered in **plain view** during such a lawful, non-excessive sweep is admissible, provided the officers' actions remained objectively reasonable relative to the stated purpose of ensuring safety.

### Legal Analysis

The scope of consent is determined by **"objective reasonableness,"** asking what a typical reasonable person would have understood from the exchange between the officer and the consenting party (*Florida v. Jimeno*). In *Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit held that Lisa Coleman's consent was not limited strictly to checking one specific room; rather, it extended to a broader inspection necessary to fulfill the purpose of the visit: verifying the safety of her children and confirming that her husband (Coleman) posed no danger.

The court reasoned that **"doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions."** This language establishes that a general sweep of common areas and bedrooms to confirm occupancy status is within the bounds of a welfare-check consent. The court did not distinguish between rooms where an immediate threat was known versus rooms requiring verification; instead, it treated the "quick canvas" as a logical extension of ensuring no one was hiding and posing an imminent risk.

Consequently, if officers had encountered contraband (e.g., drugs) in **plain view** during this authorized sweep—before obtaining a warrant—that evidence would likely be admissible under this specific holding. The rationale is that the Fourth Amendment does not require officers to ignore illegal items visible while performing a permissible safety check, so long as they do not manipulate objects or open closed containers beyond what is necessary for safety verification. The *Coleman* opinion affirms that because the officers' actions were consistent with the objective scope of ensuring family safety, they did not exceed their authority merely by looking around ("scanning") until they found Coleman or received further information from the children regarding weapons.

In summary, *Coleman* clarifies that "checking on kids" implies checking for hidden individuals who might pose a threat. Therefore, a general visual scan of rooms is within scope, and plain-view discoveries made during such scans are valid without a warrant at that stage.
