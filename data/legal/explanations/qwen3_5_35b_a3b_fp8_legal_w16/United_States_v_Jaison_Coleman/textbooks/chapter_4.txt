Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Determining Whether Officers Exceeded Scope of Consent

In *United States v. Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit addressed a critical post-entry question: even assuming valid consent existed to enter a home, did the officers remain within the bounds of that consent once inside? The case illustrates that consent is not a blank check for a general search; rather, it creates a specific boundary defined by the purpose communicated during the exchange. Here, the officers entered solely to verify the safety of children and ensure no threat remained from the stepfather. The court held that conducting a "protective sweep" consistent with those goals—scanning rooms, locating individuals, and asking basic questions about their whereabouts—is objectively reasonable and does not transform a consensual entry into an unconstitutional search. By analyzing this transition from entry to investigation, students learn how courts apply the "objective reasonableness" standard to determine if an officer's actions stayed true to the grantor's understanding of why they were permitted inside.

## What Constitutes a Protective Sweep?

The concept of a protective sweep serves as a vital tool for police officers responding to volatile situations, such as domestic violence calls involving threats of death or harm. While *Maryland v. Buie* established that officers may conduct limited sweeps for safety purposes incident to arrest, *Coleman* demonstrates how similar logic applies during consensual entries driven by community caretaking functions or welfare checks. In this instance, Officer Kizer did not enter with the intent to gather evidence against Jaison Coleman immediately; rather, his primary objective was to confirm that Lisa's children were safe and that her husband posed no imminent danger. 

A protective sweep in this context is defined by its necessity and limitation. It is not an exploratory search for contraband but a cursory inspection of spaces where an accomplice might hide or where an immediate threat could lie dormant. The Seventh Circuit emphasized that because Officer Kizer had credible reports that Coleman had threatened to kill Lisa and was still inside, checking for his presence was a logical extension of ensuring child welfare. The court noted that once inside, the officers' movements were dictated by this singular purpose: verifying life safety and neutralizing potential threats. This distinction is crucial because it separates lawful protective measures from impermissible evidentiary searches performed under the guise of consent.

## Defining Boundaries Based on Stated Purpose (Child Safety)

The scope of any consent is strictly tethered to what a reasonable person would understand was authorized at the time of entry. In *Coleman*, Lisa's consent was explicitly conditioned on verifying the status of her children ("They're... okay right this second"). The court reasoned that Officer Kizer's statement—"I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—framed the entry as a welfare check regarding the minors' safety and security. Consequently, the boundaries of Lisa's consent extended only far enough to achieve those specific objectives. 

If Officer Kizer had begun rifling through drawers looking for drugs or opening locked safes while discussing child safety, he would have clearly exceeded this scope. However, because his actions were directed toward locating Coleman (who had allegedly threatened them) and confirming where the children were hiding or sleeping, his behavior aligned perfectly with Lisa's expressed intent. The court applied an objective test: what would a typical reasonable person understand by these words? A reasonable listener would conclude that allowing police into her home to check on her kids necessitated knowing where her husband was standing and ensuring he wasn't lurking nearby with weapons mentioned in prior calls. Thus, the "stated purpose" acts as a functional limit; anything beyond confirming safety and location falls outside the granted authority unless additional authorization is obtained.

## Reasonable Actions Within Scope: Locating Individuals and Asking Basic Questions

Once inside, officers engaged in activities common in emergency responses: scanning rooms, moving through hallways, asking family members where others were located, and conducting visual inspections of areas where threats might exist. The district court found—and the Seventh Circuit affirmed—that these actions fell squarely within Lisa's consent because they were necessary to fulfill her request for assurance regarding her children's well-being. For instance, when Officer Kizer asked Lisa where Coleman currently was situated ("I'm pretty sure he's in the back bedroom"), she provided direction without objection. Similarly, when officers located Coleman in a separate bedroom and conducted a protective pat-down with his permission, they adhered strictly to their mandate of assessing immediate danger rather than investigating criminal history at that moment.

The court highlighted that "asking basic questions" is inherent in determining whether occupants are safe or threatening harm. When Lieutenant Kizer asked if Coleman was "calm right now," he was gauging risk levels directly relevant to child safety inquiries made earlier by Lisa herself. Furthermore, speaking briefly with the children revealed details about past aggression (pointing a gun), but this information-gathering occurred naturally during interactions designed to establish who was present and whether they were in distress. Because these interactions did not involve rummaging through personal effects unrelated to immediate safety or searching closed containers without cause until further justification arose (which happened later via warrant), they remained within the permissible scope defined by Lisa's initial "Okay."

## The Transition from Consensual Entry to Securing a Search Warrant

Perhaps most importantly for understanding Fourth Amendment jurisprudence in *Coleman* is how consensual entry eventually morphs into formal investigative procedures requiring warrants or probable cause independent of consent alone upon discovering new facts during the sweep. Initially bound by Lisa's limited permission regarding child welfare, Officer Kizer discovered significant new information: testimony from children describing Coleman pointing a gun at their mother days prior and recent threats on April 20th itself ("threatened to kill her mother... still inside"). 

Crucially, once inside based on consent alone yielded evidence sufficient to establish probable cause—specifically regarding ongoing threats involving firearms—the legal mechanism shifted from reliance on third-party consent to reliance on newly developed probable cause supporting an arrest warrant or search warrant application here described as seeking "a warrant." Once probable cause exists based on observations made lawfully within scope (e.g., witnessing illegal weapon possession after establishing control over suspects), officers must secure judicial approval before expanding their search beyond immediate safety needs unless exceptions like plain view doctrine apply immediately upon discovery (though here they sought warrants first). This transition underscores that while initial access relies on subjective trust between citizen and officer (expressed via consent), continued detention or expanded searches require objective legal standards met independently thereafter unless exigency persists—which ended once suspects were secured and families accounted for safely per findings above).
