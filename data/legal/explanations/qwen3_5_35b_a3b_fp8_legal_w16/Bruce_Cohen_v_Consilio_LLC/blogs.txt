Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Commissioner's Exclusive Domain: Statutory Penalties Under Minnesota's Wage Laws

**By Legal Analyst | October 7, 2025**

In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided last week, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals issued a sharp reminder to wage-and-hour litigants across Minnesota: the text of a statute is not merely a suggestion; it is the boundary line between what an employee can sue for and what belongs to the state. While the case involved a putative class action against a legal document review firm over unpaid overtime, the Eighth Circuit's decision turns on a narrow but critical point of statutory construction regarding who holds the pen to assess civil penalties under Minnesota law. The court affirmed summary judgment on key claims while vacating others based on standing concerns, ultimately reinforcing a strict textualist reading that reserves specific enforcement powers for the state rather than private citizens.

The dispute originated when Consilio, LLC, altered its overtime policy in 2019, eliminating premium pay for hourly attorneys and paying them their base rate for all hours worked. Bruce Cohen sued on behalf of himself and similarly situated employees, alleging violations of three distinct Minnesota statutes: the Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Crucially, before reaching trial, Consilio paid Cohen and his co-counsel all back wages and liquidated damages demanded under the MFLSA. However, a stalemate remained regarding "penalties." Cohen sought significant statutory penalties—specifically "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA—which he argued were available to individual plaintiffs as a remedy for wage theft. The district court rejected this argument entirely, granting summary judgment for Consilio on all counts.

On appeal, the Eighth Circuit focused primarily on Cohen's MPWA claim. The central question was whether Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) authorized a private employee to recover average daily wage penalties directly from an employer without state intervention. Reading the statute de novo, Judge Kelly's majority opinion concluded that it did not. The court noted that § 181.101(a) explicitly grants the Commissioner of Labor and Industry the sole authority to serve demands for payment and subsequently charge and collect penalties if those demands go unsatisfied within ten days. Any money collected by the Commissioner is then passed to the employee; however, the statute itself reserves only the right to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages" for private individuals.

This holding relies heavily on traditional tools of statutory interpretation known as *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of another. The court observed that if the Minnesota legislature intended to grant private employees a direct right to penalties under § 181.101(a), it possessed ample precedent elsewhere in Chapter 181 where it did exactly that. For instance, Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14 expressly allow discharged or resigned employees to "charge and collect a penalty" upon making a demand for unpaid wages or commissions. By omitting similar language from § 181.101(a), which governs general failure-to-pay scenarios involving hourly workers like Cohen, the legislature signaled its intent to reserve penalty enforcement in these instances exclusively for administrative action by the Commissioner. As Justice Scalia might say here: when Congress—or in this case, Congression-like state legislators—wants you to know something is allowed privately, they usually tell you so clearly; silence speaks volumes about who holds the gavel in this courtroom drama between employers and employees via private counsel versus public officials via administrative fiat.

However, while *Cohen* settles one avenue of recovery with precision, it leaves another open on procedural grounds related to Article III standing and mootness regarding MFLSA penalties. Although Cohen stipulated that he had been paid all back wages and liquidated damages due under federal standards (which often mirror state laws), he continued to argue he was entitled to separate statutory penalties payable directly to the Commissioner rather than himself—a distinction that complicates standing analysis significantly because such fines generally benefit neither plaintiff nor defendant directly but serve as deterrence mechanisms akin to those discussed in *Friends of the Earth v.* Laidlaw*. # Voluntary Cessation and the Ghost of Mootness: When Paid-Out Claims Survive

**By [Your Name/Analyst]**  
*October 10, 2025*

In the high-stakes game of employment litigation, employers often calculate that paying off a plaintiff's back wages and liquidated damages is sufficient to neutralize a threat. It is a classic business calculus: cut losses, close the file, and move on. But in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, the court reminded litigants—and lower courts—that money talks, but it doesn't always buy finality. By vacating a summary judgment order despite the plaintiff having been fully compensated for his direct economic injuries, the Eighth Circuit resurrected the specter of mootness in wage-and-hour cases where statutory penalties are at stake. The decision underscores a critical tension in modern labor enforcement: whether an employer can insulate itself from judicial scrutiny merely by curing its financial liabilities while leaving its underlying policy intact.

The facts of *Cohen* present a seemingly closed loop of liability. Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, sued Consilio for failing to pay overtime premiums after the company shifted to a flat-rate pay structure in 2019. Before trial, Consilio paid Cohen approximately $3,200 in back wages and liquidated damages directly covering his individual claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). Crucially, both parties stipulated that Cohen had received every dollar owed to him under that specific statute and no longer disputed his personal recovery. Yet, Cohen continued to pursue statutory penalties—damages distinct from back pay that are payable not to him, but to the state Commissioner of Labor—as well as claims under other Minnesota statutes like the Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and Wage Theft Act (MWTA).

The district court saw this as a straightforward victory for Consilio. Having satisfied Cohen's monetary demands, there was arguably nothing left for the court to award him personally. Consequently, it granted summary judgment on all counts. The Eighth Circuit disagreed with this conclusion regarding the MFLSA penalty claim, setting aside the dismissal based on principles of Article III standing and mootness derived from *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*. The panel reasoned that while Cohen had been made whole for his past injuries (back wages), his claim for civil penalties served a different purpose: deterring future violations. Under *Laidlaw*, "civil penalties... may afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." Therefore, if Consilio's illegal overtime policy remained active during litigation—a fact disputed by the parties—the case was not moot because Cohen faced a continuing threat of recurrence that only judicial intervention could resolve.

This distinction between compensatory damages and deterrent penalties is where *Cohen* offers significant doctrinal nuance. Traditionally, once an employer pays what is owed to an employee in a private suit involving individual relief (like back pay), the controversy ends; there is no "case or controversy" remaining because the plaintiff cannot be made more whole by further litigation against their own pocketbook. However, when Congress or state legislatures design statutes that allow private suits to extract fines payable to third-party entities (in this case, the state), they effectively create a public enforcement mechanism disguised as private litigation. The Eighth Circuit recognized that if an employer can simply pay off every employee individually while keeping its illegal payroll system running unchecked until every single worker sues and gets paid off one by one, those statutory penalty schemes would be rendered toothless. As long as there is a possibility that Consilio's policy persists and could harm others or lead to further violations affecting Cohen's status as an employee subject to that policy, he retains a concrete stake in ensuring those policies are struck down via declaratory or injunctive relief tied to those penalties.

However, *Cohen* also highlights a procedural trapdoor opened by partial settlements and stipulations. The court noted it lacked jurisdiction unless it could determine whether Consilio's policy was actually still in place at the time of appeal—a factual dispute not fully developed below because both sides wanted summary judgment now rather than discovery later. By vacating and remanding specifically on this threshold question of mootness rather than deciding merits immediately, Judge Kelly's opinion forces the district court to pause before granting blanket dismissals in similar scenarios. It signals that stipulations regarding personal compensation do not automatically extinguish claims for statutory penalties designed for public deterrence unless it is "absolutely clear" that wrongful conduct will never recur. This places a heavy burden on employers who wish to settle quickly; they cannot just write checks; they must ensure their policies are demonstrably dead before asking courts to declare themselves without jurisdiction.

From an analytical perspective, this ruling serves as a necessary check on "pay-to-play" strategies in labor law defense firms' playbooks. If employers believe they can systematically settle individual wage claims without addressing systemic compliance failures until forced out by regulators or class actions (which take years), they risk perpetuating illegal practices across their workforce indefinitely. The Eighth Circuit's willingness to keep jurisdiction alive suggests that federal courts remain vigilant about preventing defendants from manipulating procedural timelines or settlement mechanics to evade substantive legal accountability for ongoing statutory violations. That said, it leaves lower courts with messy work ahead: determining exactly when an illegal policy becomes sufficiently dormant to render penalty claims moot requires granular factual inquiries into corporate compliance protocols rather than simple accounting ledgers.

Ultimately, *Cohen v. Consilio* reinforces that standing is not static; it travels with the nature of the relief sought while denying immediate closure when public interests intersect with private rights disputes. For practitioners navigating Minnesota wage laws—or indeed any jurisdiction with similar hybrid enforcement mechanisms—the lesson is clear: settling back wages does not end the war if you haven't surrendered your weapons (the illegal policy). Until Consilio proves its overtime scheme has been definitively abandoned beyond reasonable doubt regarding recidivism risks, ghostly claims for penalties will haunt summary judgments everywhere else too much too soon? Not quite yet—but certainly enough to warrant careful drafting of settlement stipulations moving forward.</br>



# When Silence Speaks Louder Than Statutes: Cohen v. Consilio and the Cost of Under-Briefing

**By [Your Name]**

Wage-and-hour litigation often hinges on complex statutory interplay, particularly when state laws like Minnesota's overlap with federal standards. Yet, in *Cohen v. Consilio*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, the outcome turned less on the merits of the underlying overtime policy and more on a procedural reality that frequently traps litigants: the doctrine of waiver. While the court affirmed in part and vacated in part based on jurisdictional concerns regarding mootness, two significant portions of Cohen's appeal were dismissed not because the law favored Consilio, but because Cohen simply failed to argue his case effectively enough to survive summary judgment review. For practitioners navigating multi-statute wage theft claims, *Cohen* serves as a stark reminder that silence—or worse, undeveloped arguments—is often fatal on appeal.

The core dispute arose after Consilio eliminated premium overtime pay for its Minnesota-based attorney document reviewers, prompting Cohen to sue under three distinct state statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). After Consilio paid back wages and liquidated damages for some claims, the parties stipulated that only statutory penalties remained in controversy. The Eighth Circuit's decision offers a masterclass in how appellate courts treat "boilerplate" assertions versus rigorous legal analysis. Specifically, Cohen's failure to develop his MWTA claim and his mishandling of injunctive relief illustrate how easily a meritorious grievance can evaporate into thin air due to briefing deficiencies.

Consider Cohen's MWTA claim first. He alleged that Consilio violated statutory notice requirements regarding its overtime policy changes and sought penalties as a remedy. However, unlike his MPWA claim—which involved a nuanced debate about whether individual employees or only the State Commissioner could seek penalties—the MWTA claim collapsed under its own weight of omission. The Eighth Circuit noted that while Cohen complained about a lack of discussion in the district court record regarding this specific statute, he offered nothing substantive on appeal either. Crucially, he pointed to no statutory authority establishing that civil penalties were available under § 181.032(f) or § 181.032(d)(4). In appellate practice, a bare assertion that "penalties apply" without citing the specific text granting such authority is functionally equivalent to saying nothing at all. The court correctly applied standard waiver principles: when an appellant fails to support an assertion with argument or legal authority, they waive that issue for purposes of review. Here, Cohen's silence on the specific mechanics of MWTA penalties meant he forfeited his chance to overturn summary judgment on those grounds entirely.

Similarly dispositive was Cohen's attempt to secure injunctive relief against Consilio's continued enforcement of its illegal overtime policy. On appeal, Cohen pivoted from his original complaint requests to ask for an order forcing Consilio specifically to rescind its 2019 policy—a theory he had seemingly abandoned or modified during earlier stages of litigation but never formally advanced via an amended complaint until his opposition briefs at summary judgment. The Eighth Circuit refused to entertain this new request, noting that federal rules require new claims to be raised through amended pleadings rather than sprung upon opponents in opposition briefs during summary judgment motions. By failing to properly frame this relief in his initial pleading or through formal amendment processes prior to trial proceedings ending at summary judgment, Cohen left himself without a vehicle for recovery on this front. The court did not reach out of its way to fix what was arguably a strategic error; it simply held him accountable for raising claims outside the proper procedural channels.

These losses stand in sharp contrast to the portion of *Cohen* where the court actually intervened: the MFLSA penalty claim involving mootness concerns. Because penalties under that statute are payable to the Commissioner rather than directly to Cohen as compensatory damages, there was ambiguity regarding whether paying back wages satisfied Cohen's Article III standing once he admitted receiving all monetary compensation owed personally. The court recognized that if Consilio voluntarily ceased its violation permanently—and thus removed any threat of recurrence—Cohen might lack standing to pursue civil penalties intended solely for deterrence and public benefit rather than private redress. Unlike his waived claims here, this was a substantive jurisdictional question requiring deeper judicial scrutiny before dismissal could be finalized. Consequently, the court vacated and remanded specifically on this point so the district court could determine if Consilio's policy remains active or if it has truly extinguished itself as mootly as Consilio suggested.

The doctrinal implications for wage-and-hour litigation are immediate and practical first among them is that "multi-statute" strategies cannot rely on generalities when different statutes offer vastly different remedies and enforcement mechanisms.* C*ohen v.*Consili**o demonstrates that every statute invoked must be treated as a distinct cause of action with its own unique elements regarding standing, liability caps, and available relief.** A practitioner arguing under three separate acts must ensure their brief addresses each act's specific penalty scheme; assuming one can piggyback off another is a dangerous gamble.** Second,** **the case reinforces** **the rigid boundary between pleading requirements and appellate advocacy.** **It is not sufficient** **to hope** **that an appellate judge will read between lines**; **if you want injunctive relief**, **you must plead it**. **If you want penalties**, **you must identify exactly where Congress or your state legislature authorized them**. Failure to do so invites automatic waiver.**

Ultimately,*Cohen v.*Consili**o is less about whether employers can legally eliminate overtime premiums—it remains unclear whether Consilio's policy survives substantive scrutiny—and more about what happens when litigants fail to articulate their rights clearly enough for review.** The Eighth Circuit did not punish Cohen for losing; it punished him for sleeping through parts of his own case.** For attorneys managing complex employment disputes,** **this decision should serve as a cautionary tale**: **in federal appellate practice**, **silence is rarely golden**; **it is usually just another word for defeat**.
