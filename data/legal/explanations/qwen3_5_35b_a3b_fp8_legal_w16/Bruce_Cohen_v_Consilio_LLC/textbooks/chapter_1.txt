Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Plaintiff's Role and Employment History
To understand the legal controversy in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, one must first establish the factual foundation of the employment relationship. The plaintiff, Bruce Cohen, is a licensed attorney employed by Consilio, LLC, a nationwide legal document review firm with operations in Minnesota. Cohen commenced his tenure with Consilio in 2018, serving in the capacity of an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. His classification as an "hourly" employee is legally significant because it places him potentially within the protections of state wage laws designed to compensate workers for hours exceeding standard workweeks. Unlike salaried exempt employees who often fall outside overtime mandates based on job duties and salary thresholds, Cohen's status as an hourly worker meant that his entitlement to additional compensation for extra hours was central to his subsequent allegations against the employer.

## Consilio's Policy Change: Elimination of Overtime Premium Pay
The catalyst for this litigation occurred in mid-2019 when Consilio implemented a significant shift in its compensation structure. In July 2019, the company issued an email notification to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers announcing a new premium overtime policy set to take effect in August 2019. The core component of this new policy was the elimination of overtime premium pay entirely. Prior to this change, these employees presumably received additional compensation for hours worked beyond the standard threshold; however, under the revised policy, Consilio mandated that all hours worked would be compensated strictly "at [their] base pay rate." This unilateral modification effectively capped weekly earnings regardless of the number of hours worked, creating a potential discrepancy between the time actually spent working and the compensation received, which formed the basis of Cohen's claim that he was denied lawful overtime wages.

## Statutory Claims Asserted: MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA
In response to this policy change, Cohen filed suit in August 2020 on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. The complaint asserted violations across three distinct Minnesota statutory frameworks designed to protect wage earners. First, Cohen alleged breaches of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), specifically citing Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101 and 181.171, subd. 1. Second, he claimed violations of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), referencing Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8, which generally govern overtime requirements similar to their federal counterparts but operate under state jurisdiction here. Third, Cohen invoked the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), pointing to Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), and related sections concerning notice requirements for policy changes regarding wages and benefits collectively known as "wage theft." By invoking these overlapping statutes, Cohen sought a comprehensive array of remedies intended to make employees whole for lost wages while also punishing employers through statutory penalties and providing equitable relief via injunctions to stop future violations.
