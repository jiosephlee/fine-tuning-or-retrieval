Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Waiver and Preservation Issues Regarding MWTA and Injunctive Relief

While *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* primarily turned on substantive questions of statutory interpretation regarding overtime penalties, the Eighth Circuit’s decision also illustrates critical procedural doctrines that can foreclose appellate review regardless of a claim's underlying merit. In this chapter, we examine how Bruce Cohen’s failure to properly preserve two of his claims—the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) penalties and specific injunctive relief—led the court to affirm their dismissal without reaching the merits. These portions of the opinion serve as a practical lesson in the "waiver" doctrine and the strict requirements for preserving arguments for appeal. When an appellant fails to provide legal authority or raises new theories too late in the litigation process, appellate courts will deem those issues forfeited, leaving lower court rulings undisturbed.

## 'Waiver' Doctrine: Failure to Provide Statutory Authority for MWTA Penalties

The first procedural hurdle Cohen encountered involved his claim for civil penalties under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Under Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4), Cohen alleged that Consilio violated statutory notice requirements regarding its overtime policy changes and sought associated penalties. Although the district court dismissed this claim via summary judgment, Cohen’s attempt to overturn that dismissal on appeal collapsed not because of a definitive reading of the MWTA, but because he failed to meet basic appellate advocacy obligations. Specifically, Cohen complained that the district court provided insufficient analysis of his MWTA claim but offered no counter-statutory citation or legal authority demonstrating that the MWTA actually authorized private civil penalties for employees in his position.

In federal appellate practice, an issue is considered "waived" if an appellant fails to present a reasoned argument supported by legal authority in their opening brief. The Eighth Circuit relies heavily on precedents like *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, which hold that where a party's brief lacks supporting argumentation or legal citations, the court assumes waiver and declines to address the issue *sua sponte*. Here, Cohen pointed out what he perceived as a deficiency in the district court's reasoning but did not pivot to explain why the law mandated a different outcome for him personally. By failing to identify specific statutory text or binding precedent empowering employees to recover MWTA penalties directly against employers (as opposed to administrative fines), Cohen effectively abandoned his claim before the appellate judges ever reached it. Consequently, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal simply because there was "no meaningful argument in support of reversal," highlighting that an appealing party must actively persuade the court rather than merely pointing out potential errors below without providing a roadmap for correction.

## 'Preservation Error': Changing Requests for Injunctive Relief After Complaint Filing

The second significant procedural failure concerned Cohen's request for injunctive relief—a remedy intended to stop ongoing unlawful conduct rather than compensate for past harms. Procedural fairness requires that parties define their requests for relief early in litigation so that opposing parties have fair notice and an opportunity to respond at trial or during summary judgment motions. This principle ensures that appellate courts review only those issues fully litigated in the lower tribunal. In *Cohen*, however, Cohen engaged in a strategic shift mid-stream: while his initial complaint sought specific forms of injunctive relief tied to Consilio's 2019 policy, he subsequently altered his requested relief during his opposition brief at summary judgment without filing an amended complaint or formally moving to amend pleadings under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a).

This "shifting goalposts" created a preservation error because Consilio never had a chance to defend against this new theory at the trial level; consequently, the district judge naturally did not address it when granting summary judgment on other grounds. The Eighth Circuit noted that it was unreasonable to expect a trial court to consider claims or theories introduced for the first time in appellate briefing materials after being withdrawn from earlier filings or fundamentally changed thereafter. Because Congress designed Rule 15(a) and related pleading standards to ensure clarity and finality in defining disputes prior to adjudication, raising a new version of injunctive relief solely during oral argument or opposition briefs violates these structural safeguards. The appellate court therefore refused to entertain this new request, emphasizing that parties cannot manufacture record defects by failing to articulate their demands until after losing below.

## 'Appellate Review Limits': New Claims Cannot Be Raised in Opposition Briefs

Underlying both Cohen's waiver and preservation failures is a fundamental limit on judicial power: federal appellate courts function as reviewing bodies, not forums for developing new factual records or legal theories post-hoc. The Eighth Circuit explicitly cited *InfoDeli, LLC* to reinforce this boundary when addressing Cohen's revised injunctive relief claims. The rule is straightforward: new claims must be properly raised through formal mechanisms such as amended complaints or pre-trial motions; they cannot be resurrected or invented during opposition briefs filed against summary judgment motions once those motions have been decided based on existing pleadings. This limitation protects judicial efficiency and prevents prejudice against respondents who relied on the scope defined at earlier stages of litigation.

By attempting to introduce different injunctive relief terms after having already withdrawn others—and doing so only after facing summary judgment—the appellant bypassed essential adversarial testing phases allowed by procedure rules like Rule 15(a). The court observed that permitting such tactics would undermine the integrity of summary judgment practice itself; if parties could alter their requests arbitrarily just before trial concludes or shortly thereafter without amendment procedures, districts courts would struggle with inconsistent records and unfair surprise attacks from appellants who know exactly what arguments will fail initially but hope for success later through tactical shifts in briefing style alone (*see InfoDeli*). Therefore, when faced with such procedural irregularities where no meaningful record exists below regarding modified claims or newly asserted rights absent prior notice/applicability checks etc., higher tribunals decline jurisdiction over said matters entirely thereby maintaining strict adherence toward established norms governing fair play among litigants across multiple jurisdictions nationwide today!
