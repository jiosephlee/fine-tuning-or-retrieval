Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: The Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) Claim

In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit addressed the scope of remedies available to private employees under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Specifically, the court analyzed whether Bruce Cohen, a former hourly attorney document reviewer, could pursue "average daily wage penalties" against his employer for unpaid overtime wages. The district court had granted summary judgment to Consilio on this claim, and Cohen appealed. On appeal, the central issue was one of statutory interpretation: Did the text of Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101 authorize a private civil action for these specific penalties, or were those penalties exclusively reserved for enforcement by the state Commissioner? The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's decision, holding that while employees possess a substantive right to sue for unpaid wages, the authority to assess and collect daily wage penalties is strictly limited to the Commissioner following a specific administrative procedure.

To resolve this dispute, the court first examined the statutory text of § 181.101(a). This subsection serves two distinct functions within the MPWA framework. First, it establishes a substantive right for employees to receive their earned wages and commissions at specified intervals and explicitly preserves an employee's right to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages." Second, however, it outlines a mechanism involving state intervention where penalties may arise. Under § 181.101(a), if an employer fails to pay wages after being served with a formal demand by the Commissioner of Labor and Industry, the Commissioner may charge and collect both the earned wages and a penalty. Crucially, any money collected through this process is directed to "the employee concerned." Cohen argued that because § 181.171 allows an aggrieved employee to bring suit for violations of § 181.101 generally and recover "other appropriate relief," he could bypass the Commissioner's involvement and directly seek these penalties in court. The court rejected this reading, noting that § 181.171 permits recovery only for civil penalties or damages "provided for in the section violated." Since § 181.101 itself reserves the power to impose these specific daily wage penalties solely within its interaction with the Commissioner's demand-and-collection process, no such remedy exists for a private plaintiff initiating a direct lawsuit without that administrative prerequisite.

The court's analysis hinged heavily on distinguishing between an employee's independent right to sue for back wages and their ability to trigger statutory penalties independently of state administration. The judicial opinion emphasized that § 181.10(a) creates a dual track system: an employee can privately prosecute a claim for wages immediately upon non-payment; conversely, if they wish to access penalty provisions tied specifically to delayed payment after a demand period, they must rely on the Commissioner serving that initial demand. Because Cohen never demonstrated that he had secured such service from the Commissioner—or that such service was impossible—the statutory conditions precedent for assessing average daily wage penalties remained unmet in his private action. Consequently, Cohen lacked standing under § 181.10(a) to assert claims for these specific damages absent proof that he had navigated or attempted to navigate this unique administrative channel designed by Congress (or rather, here, by the Minnesota Legislature).

Furthermore, Justice Kelly employed traditional canons of statutory construction to reinforce this textualist conclusion. A primary canon applied was *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others. The court observed that if the Minnesota legislature intended to grant private employees an automatic right to recover average daily wage penalties upon filing suit without prior administrative action by the Commissioner, it knew exactly how to draft such language elsewhere in chapter 695 (which contains various MPWA sections).
