Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Analysis Under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)

In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a pivotal interpretive question regarding the remedial structure of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA): can an individual employee initiate a direct civil suit to recover "average daily wage penalties," or is this enforcement power reserved solely for state authorities? This inquiry lies at the heart of the court's affirmation of summary judgment on Cohen's MPWA claim. To resolve this, the appellate court engaged in rigorous textualist analysis, examining the specific language of Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101 while contrasting it with adjacent provisions of the same act that explicitly authorize private penalties. The resulting holding clarifies a fundamental distinction in Minnesota labor law between an employee's private right to pursue unpaid compensation and the State's exclusive administrative prerogative to impose monetary sanctions against employers who fail to comply with timely payment mandates.

The core of the court's reasoning rests on a close reading of § 181.101(a). While Cohen contended that the "plain language" of this section authorized employees to seek penalties, the Eighth Circuit determined that a careful examination revealed Congress—or rather, the Minnesota Legislature intended—a more narrow scope for private actors. Specifically, § 181.101 establishes two distinct pathways for resolving wage disputes: one where an employee prosecutes their own claim for wages, and another where the Department Revenue Commissioner acts on behalf of workers to ensure compliance. The text expressly states that when an employer fails to pay, "[w]hen money is due... and is not paid," the Commissioner serves a demand for payment. Only if payment is not rendered within ten days following this demand does the Commissioner possess the authority to "charge and collect" both the earned wages and a corresponding penalty. Crucially, subsection (a) specifies that any monies recovered by this process "must be paid to the employee." Consequently, while an individual retains a substantive right to secure back wages through private prosecution ("to prosecute[] a claim for wages"), the statute creates no parallel cause of action for that individual to independently assess or litigate penal sums without triggering Commissioner intervention first.

To reinforce its interpretation that penalties were not intended as a tool for private suits under § 181.101, the court applied established canon(s) of statutory construction known as expressio unius est exclusio alterius—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others—and specifically looked at how similar provisions elsewhere in Chapter 24 handle identical situations differently than § 181.101 does here.
