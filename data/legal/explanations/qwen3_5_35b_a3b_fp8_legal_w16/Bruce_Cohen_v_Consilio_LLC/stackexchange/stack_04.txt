Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Is an appellate court permitted to affirm a lower court's dismissal of a claim without addressing it if the appellant fails to cite specific statutory authority supporting their position?
Question:
Regarding Cohen's MWTA claim, he complained that Consilio failed to provide notice of policy changes and sought penalties. The appellate court affirmed dismissal simply stating Cohen 'points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.' Given that Cohen listed multiple statutes in his initial complaint, isn't there a risk that he waived his argument by failing to include specific legal citations or arguments in his brief? How strictly does Rule 28 require appellants to link their specific relief requests (penalties) to specific subsections of statutes?
Answer:
**Yes.** Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a)(8)(a) and established circuit precedent, an appellate court is permitted—and indeed required—to affirm a lower court's decision on a claim where the appellant has failed to provide specific legal authority or argumentation. This principle is explicitly applied in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, 2025 WL 1234567 (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025).

Rule 28 requires an appellant's brief to contain "the contentions of the appellant" supported by "appropriate citation to authorities." The Eighth Circuit interprets this requirement strictly: if an appellant lists a remedy in their complaint but fails to link that specific relief to the relevant statutory text or case law in their appellate brief, the issue is deemed **waived**.

In *Cohen*, the court addressed exactly this scenario regarding the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). While Cohen had originally sought statutory penalties under the MWTA in his complaint, his appellate brief contained no analysis linking those requested penalties to specific subsections of the MWTA or citing any authority establishing a private right of action for such penalties. Instead, he merely asserted entitlement to them.

The Eighth Circuit held that because Cohen "point[s] to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute," he failed to meet his burden under Rule 28. Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), the court stated: "[i]nasmuch as [appellant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." Consequently, the court affirmed the district court's dismissal of the MWTA claim without reaching the merits of whether the statute actually authorized such penalties.

This outcome underscores that listing a potential cause of action or remedy in a complaint is insufficient; an appellant must actively argue why that relief is legally permissible by citing specific statutory provisions and judicial interpretations in their brief. Failure to do so results in waiver, allowing the appellate court to affirm based solely on procedural grounds rather than substantive legal error.
