Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does the doctrine of voluntary cessation allow a federal court to proceed with summary judgment on an MFLSA claim when the employer has stopped paying but hasn't formally withdrawn its policy?
Question:
The court vacates and remands because there is a factual dispute over whether Consilio's new overtime policy is still in place. If Consilio simply stops enforcing the policy after being sued (voluntary cessation), doesn't *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle* generally prevent mootness unless it is 'absolutely clear' that the conduct cannot recur? Why didn't the Eighth Circuit just rule on whether the policy was still active based on existing evidence, rather than sending it back solely to determine jurisdiction/mootness?
Answer:
**Answer:**

You are correct that the doctrine of voluntary cessation generally prevents a defendant from rendering a case moot simply by stopping the challenged conduct while the litigation is pending. Under *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle*, Inc.*, a federal court retains jurisdiction unless "it is absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env't Servs.* (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000).

However, the Eighth Circuit in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* did not apply this exception to bypass a jurisdictional inquiry; rather, it applied the doctrine to **require** one. The court vacated and remanded specifically because there was a **genuine factual dispute** regarding whether Consilio's new overtime policy remained in place.

Here is why the court did not resolve this on summary judgment:

1.  **Jurisdiction Depends on Recurrence:** The court held that Cohen's claim for statutory penalties under the MFLSA depends entirely on whether he has a continuing stake in the outcome. While penalties are often payable to the state, they can confer standing if they encourage defendants to discontinue violations and deter future ones (*Laidlaw*). Therefore, if Consilio has voluntarily ceased its wrongful conduct permanently, Cohen lacks standing for penalties, and the case becomes moot. Conversely, if the policy remains active despite non-enforcement during litigation, Cohen retains standing.
2.  **Factual Dispute Precludes Summary Judgment:** The record showed that while Consilio had paid Cohen past-due wages and liquidated damages, it had not explicitly stated that its underlying policy was repealed or that it would never reinstate it. The opinion notes: *"whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute."*
    *   If the policy is still technically active (even if not currently enforced against Cohen), Voluntary Cessation does not render the case moot because the risk of recurrence exists until it is "absolutely clear" otherwise.
    *   If the policy has been effectively abandoned such that recurrence is impossible, then Voluntary Cessation applies, and Cohen lacks standing for penalties.
3.  **Proper Procedure:** Because summary judgment requires resolving all material facts in favor of the non-moving party (here, Cohen), and because the status of the policy determines whether federal subject-matter jurisdiction exists at all, the appellate court could not assume facts favorable to Cohen without potentially retaining jurisdiction where none existed (or vice versa). As noted in *Laidlaw*, courts are not "licensed to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest."

Consequently, rather than deciding whether Consilio had actually stopped enforcing the policy—a question requiring fact-finding—the Eighth Circuit correctly determined that this factual ambiguity prevented a final adjudication on merits or dismissal for mootness at this stage. The district court must first determine as a threshold matter whether Voluntary Cessation has occurred by clarifying if the policy is truly defunct or merely suspended pending litigation results. Only once that factual predicate is established can either side move for summary judgment on liability or dismissability based on mootness.
