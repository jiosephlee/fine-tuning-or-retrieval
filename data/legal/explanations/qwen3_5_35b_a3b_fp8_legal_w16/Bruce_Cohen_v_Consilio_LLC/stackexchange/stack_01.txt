Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### How can an individual plaintiff maintain Article III standing to seek statutory penalties that are payable directly to the state Commissioner rather than the plaintiff?
Question:
The court discusses *Friends of the Earth* and notes that civil penalties paid to the government 'may afford redress' by deterring future violations. However, the opinion explicitly states that under the MFLSA, these penalties go to the Commissioner, not Cohen. Since Cohen has already been fully compensated for his own wages and liquidated damages, what specific 'personal stake' or 'concrete injury' allows him to satisfy the standing requirement for a claim where he receives zero dollars? Does the threat of a penalty against the employer count as a cognizable injury to Cohen personally?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's opinion in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, an individual plaintiff can maintain Article III standing to seek statutory penalties payable to the state even when they receive no direct monetary benefit from those penalties, provided the penalties serve a "redressive" function by deterring future violations.

**The Legal Standard: Redressability Through Deterrence**
Under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env't Servs.*, the Supreme Court established that civil penalties paid to the government can satisfy the redressibility prong of standing. The Court reasoned that such penalties "may afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

**Application to Cohen's Claim**
In *Cohen*, the court acknowledged that while Cohen had already been fully compensated for his lost wages and liquidated damages (meaning he had no financial stake in recovering those specific amounts again), his claim for MFLSA penalties remained potentially viable. The court noted that because these penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than Cohen, his standing depends entirely on whether he can show a "continuing interest" in stopping the employer's wrongful conduct.

Specifically, the Eighth Circuit held that Cohen retains a concrete injury if Consilio's violative overtime policy remains in place. In this scenario, the imposition of penalties would deter Consilio from maintaining or repeating the violation, thereby providing Cohen with redress for his past injury by preventing future harm. Conversely, if Consilio has abandoned the policy and it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful behavior will not recur, Cohen loses his personal stake, rendering the claim moot regardless of whether he receives money directly.

**Conclusion**
Therefore, Cohen satisfies Article III standing not through a direct financial recovery of penalty funds, but through a **personal stake in ensuring compliance**. His injury is cognizable because he alleges an ongoing threat of continued violation; obtaining a judgment imposing penalties serves as a deterrent that addresses this continuing threat. However, this standing is conditional: if the employer voluntarily ceases the violation such that recurrence is impossible, standing evaporates until new facts arise proving a reasonable expectation of recurrence.
