Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The Indefinite Investigation: Temporal Limits on Exemption 7(A)'s "Pending" Requirement

The Freedom of Information Act exists to pierce government secrecy, but even its most ardent supporters acknowledge that some secrets are legitimate. Few exemptions are more potent—or more contested—than Exemption 7(A), which shields law enforcement records whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *Farahi v. FBI* illustrates how comfortably this exemption can stretch across decades, raising uncomfortable questions about whether "pending" proceedings can effectively become permanent ones.

The case involved Foad Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. During removal proceedings, the government presented evidence that Farahi had connections to "high-level terrorists," including alleged ties to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla. Farahi sought his FBI file through FOIA, but the agency withheld nearly all of the approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs located as responsive. The FBI invoked Exemption 7(A), claiming disclosure would interfere with ongoing counterterrorism investigations.

## The Temporal Problem

What makes *Farahi* noteworthy is not the substantive application of Exemption 7(A), but the procedural posture in which the court evaluated it. The FBI's initial declaration supporting the exemption came from David Hardy in 2019. By the time the case reached the D.C. Circuit in 2025, six years had passed. Farahi argued, not unreasonably, that this passage of time undermined the FBI's claim that proceedings remained genuinely "pending or reasonably anticipated."

The court's handling of this temporal challenge reveals something important about how Exemption 7(A) operates in practice—and raises questions about whether meaningful judicial scrutiny of stale declarations is even possible.

## The "Update" Doctrine

The district court, recognizing that six years is a long time, required the FBI to submit updated declarations confirming that investigations remained ongoing. The FBI complied through a declaration from Joseph Bender, the new head of the relevant records section. Standing alone, the Bender declaration was, as Judge Katsas acknowledged, "surprisingly terse"—merely stating that investigations "remain ongoing."

Farahi cried foul, invoking the D.C. Circuit's prior holding in *CREW v. DOJ* that "vague" assertions about ongoing investigations may not satisfy the government's burden. But the court rejected this challenge by treating the Bender declaration not as a standalone justification, but as an "update" to the Hardy declarations submitted years earlier. Those earlier declarations, the court emphasized, had provided "considerable detail" about the nature and scope of the investigation—albeit largely in ex parte filings unavailable to Farahi.

This "update" framework is analytically sensible but practically troubling. The government's burden under Exemption 7(A) requires a showing that proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated" at the time of judicial resolution. By treating a skeletal update as sufficient so long as it confirms that nothing has changed, the court effectively allows stale declarations to be perpetually renewed without fresh justification.

## The National Security Context

The court emphasized, correctly, that national security investigations present unique challenges. Counterterrorism investigations may legitimately span years or decades. Targets may travel abroad, evidence may be difficult to collect, and investigative priorities may shift. The court also noted the serious allegations against Farahi—publicly documented ties to designated terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists—which make the FBI's ongoing interest in him plausible.

And to be fair, the court did something right here: it required the government to update its declarations rather than simply accepting the 2019 Hardy declaration as sufficient. Some courts might have granted summary judgment years earlier without demanding updated information. The district court's insistence on current information, and the D.C. Circuit's careful review of the ex parte materials, reflects genuine judicial engagement.

But deference cuts both ways. The same factors that justify deference in national security cases—agency expertise, sensitive sources and methods, the impossibility of public explanation—also make meaningful judicial scrutiny exceptionally difficult. When courts cannot explain their reasoning on the public record, and when requesters cannot meaningfully challenge ex parte submissions, the system relies almost entirely on agency good faith.

## The Indefinite Investigation Problem

The deeper concern is structural. What happens when an investigation never formally closes but also never results in charges? Farahi's alleged ties to terrorism have been known to the government for over two decades. He has been in immigration proceedings since 2002. The FBI's investigation has apparently been "ongoing" throughout this entire period. At what point does a "pending" proceeding become an indefinite shield against disclosure?

The court offered no clear answer. It acknowledged that the Bender declaration alone would have been insufficient, but it refused to require more detailed updated information because the original Hardy declarations had provided adequate detail. This creates a problematic precedent: agencies can establish the existence of pending proceedings through detailed initial declarations, then maintain that status indefinitely through conclusory updates that merely confirm nothing has changed.

This is not to say the FBI is hiding anything inappropriate. The ex parte declarations may well contain compelling evidence that disclosure would genuinely harm ongoing investigations. The problem is that there is no mechanism for requesters—or the public—to know whether the passage of time has eroded the government's justification.

## Downstream Implications

*Farahi* does not break new doctrinal ground, but it illustrates a persistent tension in FOIA litigation involving national security. Courts have repeatedly held that Exemption 7(A) requires proceedings to be genuinely pending or reasonably anticipated—not merely theoretical or indefinitely suspended. But courts have also recognized that counterterrorism investigations may extend for years without visible public action.

The practical implication for FOIA requesters is sobering: if an agency claims an investigation is ongoing, and provides any minimally adequate initial justification, subsequent time delays may not meaningfully weaken that claim. Requesters facing stale declarations would be well-advised to demand not just updates confirming ongoing status, but updated detailed justifications explaining why the investigation remains live and why disclosure would still cause interference.

Congress could address this by amending FOIA to require periodic judicial review of Exemption 7(A) claims in long-running cases, or by requiring agencies to provide more detailed public justifications for extended withholdings. Until then, requesters will continue to face the difficult burden of challenging investigations whose details remain hidden behind the very exemption they seek to overcome.



# Blindfolded Adversaries: The Challenge of Contesting Secret Evidence in FOIA

The D.C. Circuit's decision last week in *Farahi v. FBI* illustrates a recurring dilemma in national security FOIA litigation: how can a requester meaningfully challenge the government's justification for withholding records when that justification itself is secret? The case reaffirms that ex parte, sealed declarations are a fact of life in FOIA cases involving sensitive law enforcement information. But it also exposes the inherent tension between the government's legitimate secrecy interests and the adversarial process that FOIA litigation is supposed to provide.

## The FOIA Framework and Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act embodies a presumption of disclosure. Federal agencies must release records upon request unless an enumerated exemption applies. Exemption 7(A) shields records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Critically, the D.C. Circuit has interpreted this exemption to apply only when proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated"—not when an investigation has gone cold or the government is merely speculating about hypothetical future action.

This limitation matters because the government cannot invoke Exemption 7(A) indefinitely. At some point, the passage of time undermines the claim that enforcement proceedings remain genuinely pending or reasonably anticipated. The question in *Farahi* was whether that point had been reached.

## Foad Farahi's Unusual Position

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. According to the FBI's own public allegations in his immigration proceedings, Farahi has connections to high-level terrorists. The government has claimed he was "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," and was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists," including Jose Padilla.

Farahi sought his FBI file through FOIA. The agency located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive material—and withheld most of it. To justify the withholdings, the FBI submitted declarations from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section. Some declarations were public; others were filed under seal and ex parte, meaning Farahi and his counsel could not see them.

The district court found that the Hardy declarations "seemingly" established that Exemption 7(A) applied, but given the passage of time—it had been several years since the initial filings—the court ordered the FBI to update its assessment. The FBI then submitted additional declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief, confirming that investigations remained "ongoing." The district court granted summary judgment for the government after reviewing the ex parte materials.

## The Problem of Conclusory Assertions

On appeal, Farahi raised a sensible objection: the Bender declaration was surprisingly terse. It simply asserted that investigations "remain ongoing" without elaboration. The D.C. Circuit has previously warned that "vague" assertions about ongoing investigations may not suffice to support Exemption 7(A) invocations.

Judge Katsas, writing for the panel, acknowledged that "the Bender declaration, considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." But the court declined to consider it in isolation. Instead, it read the Bender declaration as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations—which had been filed years earlier. The government had established the pendency of proceedings through Hardy's public and sealed declarations; Bender was simply confirming that nothing had changed.

This interpretive move is defensible, but it highlights a structural problem: Farahi could not meaningfully challenge the government's characterization of what the Hardy declarations showed because he could not see the sealed ones. He had to take the government's word that the ex parte materials provided the necessary "considerable detail."

## The Segregability Problem

The segregability analysis illustrates the handicap even more starkly. FOIA requires agencies to release any "reasonably segregable portion" of a record after redacting exempt information. The district court concluded, based on Bender's supplemental ex parte submission, that the FBI had adequately explained why no meaningful information was segregable.

Farahi objected that the district court relied too heavily on an ex parte declaration he could not see or contest. The court's response was essentially procedural: Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he forfeited the argument. Moreover, the court noted, this handicap is "inherent in those FOIA cases where ... an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

That is true as far as it goes. But it raises a fundamental question: if the adversary cannot see the evidence, what exactly does "adversarial testing" mean in this context?

## The Inherent Tension

The D.C. Circuit's approach reflects an uncomfortable reality. In national security FOIA cases, detailed public explanations of why documents must be withheld could themselves reveal the sensitive information the exemption is designed to protect. The government cannot publicly explain that disclosing certain documents would tip off a target about the scope of an investigation without potentially tipping off that target. Ex parte and in camera review by the judge becomes the only viable mechanism.

Yet this mechanism has obvious limitations. Judges are not subject-matter experts on counterterrorism investigations. They must rely on the representations of executive branch officials—the same officials whose disclosure decisions are being challenged. The court in *Farahi* explicitly noted that the FBI's determinations are "entitled to deference" and that such "deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security."

The court's analysis of whether proceedings remained "pending or reasonably anticipated" illustrates the dynamic. Farahi argued that the passage of time—years of investigation without charges—suggested that proceedings were no longer genuinely anticipated. The court rejected this argument, citing the "breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation" and the seriousness of the alleged connections to terrorist organizations. But the court's confidence in the investigation's ongoing nature ultimately rested on representations in sealed declarations that Farahi could not contest.

## Downstream Implications

*Farahi* does not break new doctrinal ground. It reaffirms well-established principles: Exemption 7(A) applies to ongoing investigations; agencies may justify withholdings through ex parte declarations; courts owe deference to executive branch assessments of national security risks. But the decision illustrates how these principles interact in ways that can leave requesters with limited tools to challenge even stale or conclusory government assertions.

The decision also highlights an important practical point for FOIA litigants. Farahi forfeited certain arguments by failing to object to the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations. Future litigants would be wise to make timely objections, forcing courts to explicitly address the due process concerns that ex parte submissions raise—even if the outcome remains the same.

More broadly, *Farahi* raises a question that courts have never fully resolved: what procedural safeguards are necessary when the government relies on secret evidence? Judicial in camera review provides some check on executive overreaching. But it is a thin reed. The adversarial process assumes that both sides can test the evidence. When one side is blindfolded, the judge becomes the only real adversarial check—and judges are ill-equipped to second-guess agency officials on matters of national security.

The court's decision in *Farahi* was probably correct given existing precedent. But the case serves as a reminder that FOIA's promise of transparency remains bounded by secrecy claims that requesters can rarely penetrate. Whether this balance appropriately serves the public interest is a question that extends far beyond any single case.



# Beyond Words: What Counts as "Meaningful Information" Under FOIA's Segregability Requirement

When the Freedom of Information Act requires agencies to release "any reasonably segregable portion of a record," what exactly must be produced? The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *Farahi v. FBI* offers a revealing answer: not much, at least when the records in question involve ongoing counterterrorism investigations.

The case involves Foad Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993 and has been entangled in immigration proceedings for over two decades. When Farahi requested his FBI file, the agency located some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive material—and then withheld nearly all of it. The question before the court was whether the FBI properly invoked Exemption 7(A), which protects law enforcement records whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings."

But the more instructive portion of Judge Katsas's opinion addresses a different question: even if the bulk of the records were properly withheld, didn't the FBI have an obligation to release whatever scraps of non-exempt information remained?

## The Segregability Requirement in Theory and Practice

FOIA is unambiguous on this point. Agencies must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." The statute expressly provides that "any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided" after deletion of exempt portions. This is not a perfunctory obligation—courts take it seriously, and agencies must justify not only what they withhold but also why they cannot extract and release anything else.

In practice, however, the segregability requirement often yields little for requesters, particularly when the records at issue are law enforcement files. The government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." And here lies the crucial distinction that *Farahi* reinforces: FOIA's focus is on "information," not merely "words."

## Information Versus Words

The D.C. Circuit has long recognized that isolated words or phrases scattered throughout a document may not constitute "reasonably segregable" information. As the court explained in *Mead Data Central v. Department of the Air Force*, a foundational 1977 decision, if a small amount of non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." The logic is straightforward: FOIA exists to provide meaningful information to the public, not to release fragmentary words that convey nothing comprehensible.

Judge Katsas's opinion in *Farahi* reaffirms this principle with notable clarity. Farahi objected that in a trove of over 10,000 pages, there simply had to be some segregable and releasable information. The court was unmoved. Even if "individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI's interests," the court explained, "that is neither here nor there." The district court had properly concluded that no "meaningful information" was reasonably segregable.

This framing—"meaningful information"—deserves attention. It suggests that courts will not require agencies to engage in futile line-by-line scrubbing when the result would be a redacted document that reads like a censored letter from a prisoner of war: nouns and verbs without context, facts without narrative, words without meaning.

## The Ex Parte Problem

The court's segregability analysis, however, raises a persistent tension in FOIA litigation. The district court relied heavily on an ex parte declaration from the FBI to conclude that no meaningful information could be segregated. Farahi complained that this reliance was excessive—that he was unable to contest evidence he could not see.

The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that ex parte declarations "no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs." But the court correctly noted that this disadvantage is "inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." The alternative—forcing the FBI to explain publicly why specific pieces of information cannot be separated from exempt material—would itself risk revealing the investigative details the exemption is designed to protect.

Yet the consequence is that requesters must essentially trust the court to serve as an adequate check on the government's representations. In counterterrorism cases, where the risk of tipping off subjects is paramount, this judicial trust is routinely extended.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

*Farahi* does not break new doctrinal ground so much as it illustrates the practical limits of FOIA's segregability requirement in the national security context. Several implications follow.

First, the case confirms that the volume of records alone does not create a segregability obligation. The instinctive intuition—that more documents must yield more disclosable material—finds no support in the statute or case law. If every page is sufficiently saturated with exempt information, the entire file may be withheld regardless of its size.

Second, the decision reinforces that ongoing investigations enjoy particularly robust protection. The court's Exemption 7(A) analysis emphasizes deference to the executive on national security matters, and this deference carries over to the segregability inquiry. If the FBI represents that releasing even isolated details would compromise sources, methods, or investigative strategies, courts are unlikely to second-guess that judgment.

Third, and more broadly, *Farahi* underscores the challenge facing requesters who seek records from active law enforcement files. The combination of Exemption 7(A)'s broad protection for pending investigations and the segregability requirement's focus on "meaningful information" creates a formidable barrier. Requesters may wait years for an investigation to conclude, only to receive heavily redacted documents or, in cases like Farahi's, nothing at all.

## The Road Not Taken

One might ask whether FOIA's segregability requirement should be more demanding. Congress could require agencies to release any "words" that can be extracted without harm, not merely "meaningful information." But such a requirement would impose significant administrative burdens on agencies and could produce release documents of dubious value to requesters.

The current approach—focusing on information rather than isolated words—strikes a reasonable balance. It prevents agencies from hiding behind trivial logistical challenges while recognizing that the point of FOIA is to inform the public, not to generate incomprehensible redaction puzzles. The limitation, rather, lies in the scope of Exemption 7(A) itself and in the deference courts afford to agency assessments of investigative risk.

For requesters like Farahi, the decision is the end of a long road. The court's opinion reveals that the FBI has investigated him for connections to high-level terrorists, including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla, and that the agency views him as connected to designated foreign terrorist organizations. But about the substance of that investigation—and whatever exculpatory or inculpatory evidence his file might contain—he will learn nothing from FOIA. The information, meaningful or otherwise, remains locked away.



# The Deference Dilemma: Judicial Review of National Security FOIA Claims

## Why This Case Matters

The Freedom of Information Act embodies a simple but ambitious proposition: the government works for the people, and the people have a right to know what their government is doing. But in cases touching national security, that proposition runs headlong into the executive branch's constitutional prerogatives and institutional expertise. Last week's decision from the D.C. Circuit in *Farahi v. FBI* offers a revealing window into how courts navigate that tension—and suggests that the balance may have tilted decisively toward deference.

The case arose from a FOIA request by Foad Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. The FBI has investigated Farahi for alleged connections to high-level terrorists, including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla. When Farahi sought his FBI file under FOIA, the agency withheld nearly all responsive records—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs—invoking Exemption 7(A), which protects law enforcement records whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings."

The D.C. Circuit's opinion, written by Judge Gregory Katsas, ultimately sided with the FBI. But the reasoning along the way illuminates a broader doctrinal development: the degree to which national security considerations have transformed the government's statutory burden into something closer to a pro forma requirement.

## The Court's Reasoning and the Role of Deference

The court's analysis proceeded along familiar FOIA tracks. Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and that disclosure could interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings. On both questions, the court readily accepted the FBI's submissions.

What's striking is how the court framed its standard of review. The FBI's invocation of Exemption 7, the court explained, is "entitled to deference" because the agency "specializes in law enforcement." That deference, the court emphasized, is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security." Citing its 2003 decision in *Center for National Security Studies v. Department of Justice*, the court noted that it has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated."

This deferential posture shaped the court's treatment of the FBI's evidentiary submissions. The initial declaration from FBI records chief David Hardy was submitted in 2019. By the time the case reached the district court for substantive review in 2022, the judge observed that three years had passed and ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether proceedings remained pending. The updated submission from Hardy's successor, Joseph Bender, was, by the court's own description, "surprisingly terse." The opinion notes that Bender's declaration, "considered in isolation," provided little more than a conclusory assertion that investigations "remain ongoing."

But the court declined to consider that declaration in isolation. Instead, it treated Bender's submission as a mere "update" to the earlier Hardy declarations—despite the passage of nearly four years between Hardy's initial submission and Bender's update. And the court found that earlier submission "plausible" in part because of the "breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation," which the opinion details at some length based on public allegations from Farahi's immigration proceedings.

## The Doctrinal Framework: From Burden to Presumption

The FOIA statute explicitly places the burden on the government to demonstrate that an exemption applies. In theory, that burden remains unchanged in national security cases. In practice, decisions like *Farahi* suggest that the burden has been substantially lightened—or even effectively reversed.

The theoretical justifications for deference are well-established and not without merit. Courts lack the expertise and institutional capacity to second-guess intelligence judgments about what disclosures might compromise ongoing investigations. The executive branch has constitutional responsibilities for national defense and foreign affairs that counsel judicial restraint. And ex parte, in camera review of sensitive materials necessarily limits the adversarial process that normally ensures rigorous scrutiny of government claims.

But the *Farahi* decision demonstrates how those justifications can combine to produce something approaching a presumption of validity. Consider the court's approach to the segregability requirement. FOIA commands agencies to release "any reasonably segregable portion" of a partially exempt record. Farahi argued that in a file spanning more than 10,000 pages, there must be *some* non-exempt material that could be released without harm. The court acknowledged that "individual words or phrases" might theoretically be disclosable, but held that extracting such fragments would not yield "meaningful information." The court relied heavily on Bender's ex parte declaration to conclude that no segregable information existed—but that analysis remains entirely invisible to the plaintiff and the public.

The court candidly acknowledged that ex parte proceedings "handicap FOIA plaintiffs." But it characterized that disadvantage as "inherent" to cases involving sensitive material, rather than something demanding heightened judicial scrutiny.

## The Practical Effects

The practical implications for FOIA litigants are stark. When the government invokes national security, plaintiffs face an uphill battle that approaches the vertical. The government's evidentiary submissions can be "surprisingly terse" and still survive judicial review. Ex parte declarations shield the government's rationale from meaningful adversarial testing. And the court's deference extends not only to factual judgments about ongoing investigations, but also to methodological questions about how agencies define document categories and assess segregability.

None of this is to say that the FBI's representations in *Farahi* were inaccurate or improper. The court's description of Farahi's alleged connections to designated terrorist organizations and individuals convicted of terrorism-related offenses suggests a serious ongoing national security concern. The deference doctrine may well produce correct outcomes in individual cases.

But the doctrinal trajectory warrants attention. The D.C. Circuit's approach has evolved from asking whether agencies have met their burden to asking whether agencies have offered a minimally plausible justification. In national security cases, that latter inquiry may reduce to: Did the agency say the magic words? If so, the court's role becomes largely ceremonial.

## Downstream Implications

*Farahi* reinforces an already-formidable barrier to transparency in national security cases. For future litigants, the decision sends a clear message: courts will not probe the government's assertions about pending investigations, even when those assertions are updated years later with scant detail.

The decision also illustrates the limits of procedural safeguards that were designed to cabin judicial deference. The requirement that proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated," rather than merely hypothetical, was supposed to prevent agencies from invoking Exemption 7(A) to create permanent zones of secrecy. In practice, the government can meet that requirement with a declaration that an investigation "remains ongoing"—and can preserve that status indefinitely through periodic updates that the court will accept without probing.

The segregability analysis similarly demonstrates how a meaningful statutory mandate can become largely unenforceable. Congress directed agencies to release all non-exempt information, but when that determination occurs entirely ex parte, plaintiffs have no meaningful opportunity to challenge the agency's approach.

## Conclusion

The tension between transparency and security in FOIA cases is genuine and difficult. There are no easy answers when disclosure could compromise investigations into terrorism. But *Farahi* raises a more fundamental question about the judicial role: When deference to the executive becomes this comprehensive, is the court still performing meaningful review, or merely ratifying government decisions after the fact?

The D.C. Circuit's approach may reflect a pragmatic accommodation to the realities of national security litigation. But it also represents a significant departure from FOIA's foundational premise that the government must justify its secrecy to an independent judiciary. The burden, theoretically on the government, has in practice shifted to plaintiffs—who must now overcome presumptions that are effectively irrebuttable.
