Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The FOIA Framework and Exemption 7(A)

## FOIA's Presumption of Disclosure and the Statutory Exemption Structure

The Freedom of Information Act codifies a fundamental principle of open government: federal agencies must release records to members of the public upon request. As the court in *Farahi* explains, FOIA establishes a "presumption of disclosure" that places the burden on agencies to justify any refusal to produce requested materials. This presumption reflects the statute's central purpose of promoting transparency and accountability in government operations.

However, Congress recognized that unlimited disclosure could harm legitimate governmental interests. FOIA therefore creates a carefully bounded exception to its disclosure mandate. Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b), agencies may withhold records only if one of nine enumerated statutory exemptions applies. These exemptions are exclusive—an agency cannot refuse disclosure based on general concerns or policy arguments not captured by the statutory text. The exemptions function as a comprehensive list of permissible justifications for nondisclosure, and courts interpret them narrowly to preserve FOIA's disclosure-oriented purpose.

This structure creates the basic analytical framework for any FOIA case. The requester submits a request, the agency searches for responsive records, and then either discloses them or invokes one or more exemptions to justify withholding. If the requester challenges the agency's decision in court, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that the exemption applies. The court's task is to determine whether the agency has met that burden as to each category of withheld records.

## The Text and Elements of Exemption 7(A)

The exemption at issue in *Farahi* is Exemption 7(A), one of several exemptions addressing law enforcement records. The exemption applies to records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The court reads this statutory language as establishing two distinct elements that the government must prove.

First, the records must have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes." This requirement ensures that the exemption protects only those documents actually created or gathered in connection with legitimate law enforcement activities, not merely any documents that happen to be in an agency's possession. The court notes that because the FBI "specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference" on this threshold question. Even so, the agency's declarations must "establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties'" and a "connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'" The court applies this test by examining both the public and ex parte declarations submitted by the FBI, as well as context from Farahi's own submissions in related proceedings.

Second, disclosure of the records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." This interference prong requires the agency to identify a causal connection between disclosure and some harm to an enforcement proceeding. The court explains that disclosure can interfere with investigations by "tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation"—a justification the court describes as "widely accepted" in Exemption 7(A) cases. The court also notes that deference to the executive branch is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," including counterterrorism investigations like the one at issue here.

## The "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated" Requirement

A critical interpretive question in applying Exemption 7(A) is what qualifies as an "enforcement proceeding" that could be interfered with through disclosure. The D.C. Circuit has held that such proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." This requirement, established in prior precedent and reaffirmed in *Farahi*, prevents agencies from invoking the exemption based on purely speculative enforcement actions.

The court explains that "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings'" for purposes of the exemption. This interpretive ruling expands the ordinary meaning of "proceeding"—which might suggest a formal adjudicatory process—to encompass the investigative phase that precedes any formal charges. The rationale is that the investigative stage is precisely when premature disclosure poses the greatest risk: revealing the existence, scope, or direction of an investigation can allow targets to destroy evidence, coordinate alibis, or otherwise undermine the government's ability to build its case.

The government may satisfy its burden to show pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings "simply by attesting to their 'existence.'" However, this attestation must be sufficiently specific. The court in *Farahi* addresses the defendant's argument that the passage of time cast doubt on whether proceedings remained pending. While acknowledging that one declaration, "considered in isolation, appear[ed] surprisingly terse," the court looked to the broader record—including prior detailed declarations—to conclude that the government had met its burden. The court emphasized that the supplemental declaration was "an update to [earlier] declarations, not as a standalone document."

Importantly, the court also considered the nature of the underlying investigation in evaluating whether ongoing proceedings remained plausible. The FBI had publicly alleged in immigration proceedings that Farahi had connections to designated terrorist organizations and individuals convicted of terrorism-related charges. Given "the breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation," the court found it "plausible" that the investigation remained ongoing, supporting the conclusion that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist.

## The Segregability Requirement

Even when a FOIA exemption applies to some portion of a responsive record, the agency's obligations do not end there. FOIA imposes a segregability requirement: agencies must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." This requirement implements the statute's fundamental commitment to maximum disclosure consistent with the exemptions. The government cannot use an exemption as a pretext to withhold entire documents when only portions contain protected information.

The segregability inquiry focuses on whether nonexempt information can be meaningfully separated from exempt material. The court explains that if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not "reasonably segregable." This is because "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." The court thus rejected Farahi's argument that some segregable information must exist somewhere in the "vast volume of responsive records"—approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs.

A practical challenge arises when determining segregability in cases involving sensitive law enforcement or national security information. The court in *Farahi* relied on ex parte declarations—submissions reviewed by the court but not shared with the opposing party—to evaluate the government's segregability arguments. The court noted that while ex parte proceedings "no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs," this problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where … an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

## Standard of Review and Burden Allocation

FOIA cases follow the familiar procedural posture of summary judgment litigation, but with specialized rules regarding burdens of proof. The court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, giving no deference to the district court's legal conclusions. This standard reflects the fact that FOIA determinations ultimately present questions of statutory interpretation and application.

Critically, "the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies." This burden allocation implements FOIA's presumption of disclosure. The agency seeking to withhold records must justify that decision; the requester bears no obligation to demonstrate that disclosure would be harmless. The court applies this framework by examining whether the government's evidentiary submissions—typically agency declarations describing the withheld records and the reasons for nondisclosure—meet the applicable legal standards.

When evaluating agency declarations, courts apply a standard of "reasonable specificity." Agency affidavits must "show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." In *Farahi*, the court found the government's declarations sufficient, crediting the FBI's explanation that disclosure would tip off targets and that the investigation remained ongoing. The court also noted the appropriateness of deference in national security contexts, citing precedent holding that courts should be "particularly deferential" when reviewing agency decisions in this area.

Finally, the court addressed a procedural forfeiture issue. Farahi objected that the government's document categories were overbroad, but he "provide[d] no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." The court held that Farahi had "forfeited his arguments on this score." This ruling illustrates an important procedural principle: courts will not develop arguments on a party's behalf, and litigants must articulate specific objections to agency categorization decisions to preserve those issues for appeal.

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Farahi's Background: Iranian National, Removal Proceedings, and Alleged Terrorist Connections

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. His encounters with federal law enforcement began in 2002, when the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on a visa overstay. Farahi's immigration case has been protracted and multifaceted: at various stages, he has sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.

The government's opposition to Farahi's requests for relief has drawn heavily on intelligence gathered by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. During immigration proceedings, DHS presented both documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who, in the words of the Eleventh Circuit, "documented Farahi's connections to high-level terrorists." The public record in this FOIA litigation reveals more specific allegations: Farahi was identified as "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists" including Jose Padilla (who was detained as an enemy combatant and later convicted on terrorism-related charges in federal court), and supported "charities that financed terrorism," including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation.

These allegations are central to understanding the FBI's resistance to disclosing Farahi's file. The government's representation that Farahi has connections to designated foreign terrorist organizations and individuals implicated in serious terrorism cases underscores the national security dimensions of this case and helps explain why the FBI has sought to keep its investigative file confidential.

## The FOIA Request: Farahi Sought His FBI File

Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The Bureau's response revealed the substantial scope of its documentation: the agency located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive information. This volume suggests that the FBI had devoted significant investigative resources to Farahi over an extended period.

Farahi filed suit after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to his request. Over the next several years, the agency conducted a page-by-page review of the potentially responsive records. At the conclusion of this process, the FBI had disclosed some records to Farahi but withheld the vast majority. The question before the courts became whether the FBI had properly justified its decision to withhold.

## The FBI's Initial Response and the Hardy Declaration Supporting Withholding Under Exemption 7(A)

In October 2019, after completing its review of the responsive records, the FBI moved for summary judgment. In support of its motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration is the government's primary evidentiary vehicle for establishing that the withheld records fall within a FOIA exemption.

In this case, Hardy's public declaration asserted that the withheld records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes"—a threshold requirement for Exemption 7—and that their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that were pending or reasonably anticipated. Hardy explained that disclosure of information regarding an ongoing investigation could tip off targets about the scope of that investigation, thereby compromising it. The declaration further attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing."

The FBI also moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte—meaning for the court's eyes only, without disclosure to Farahi. Farahi did not oppose this motion, and the district court granted it in October 2020. This procedural choice is significant in FOIA litigation involving national security: ex parte submissions allow agencies to provide sensitive details that support their exemption claims without revealing the very information they seek to protect.

## The District Court's Concern About the Passage of Time and Its Order Requiring Updated Declarations

The district court did not rule on the FBI's summary judgment motion until November 2022—more than three years after the motion was filed and more than two years after the supplemental Hardy declaration. When the court finally addressed the motion, it expressed concern about the staleness of the FBI's evidentiary submissions.

The court concluded that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show that release of the records could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. However, the "significant passage of time" since the Hardy declarations gave the court pause. Exemption 7(A) requires that enforcement proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated"—not merely that they existed at some point in the past. The district court therefore ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated.

The court also identified a deficiency regarding segregability. Under FOIA, agencies must release "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record" after deleting exempt portions. The district court concluded that it needed more information to assess whether the FBI had met this obligation, though it could not state its specific concerns on the public record. For both reasons, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice, leaving the door open for the FBI to renew its motion with updated evidence.

## The Renewed Motion with Bender Declarations and the District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment

In response to the district court's order, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency submitted declarations from Joseph Bender, who had become the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The FBI provided both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration from Bender.

Bender's public declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration supplied additional details about the nature of the investigation and addressed the court's segregability concerns with more specificity.

The district court granted the renewed motion. It credited the FBI's confirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." Based on its in camera review of the ex parte submission, the court also concluded that the FBI had adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable from non-exempt material. Having satisfied both requirements—Exemption 7(A) and segregability—the FBI was entitled to summary judgment. Farahi appealed, bringing the case before the D.C. Circuit.

# Applying Exemption 7(A): The Two-Part Test

## The "Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes" Inquiry

Exemption 7(A) applies only to records "compiled for law enforcement purposes." This threshold requirement ensures that the exemption does not sweep more broadly than Congress intended. The D.C. Circuit has developed a framework for evaluating whether an agency has met this standard, particularly when the agency claiming the exemption is the FBI.

Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its determination that records were compiled for law enforcement purposes is entitled to "a fair measure of deference." The court in *Farahi* cited prior precedent establishing this principle, including *Campbell v. Department of Justice*, which recognized that the FBI's "decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." However, this deference is not unlimited. The agency's declarations must still "establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,'" as well as a "connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'" This two-part test originates from *Pratt v. Webster*, a foundational case in this area.

In *Farahi*, the FBI easily satisfied this threshold. The Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject's association with individuals directly involved in such violations." The court found this sufficient, particularly given what Farahi himself had conceded in district court proceedings: that the FBI had investigated him for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and convicted on terrorism-related charges. The court had "no doubt" that the FBI carried its burden on this element.

## The "Interference with Enforcement Proceedings" Requirement

The second element of Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure of the records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The D.C. Circuit has interpreted this provision to require that such proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated." Importantly, the term "proceedings" encompasses more than formal court cases. Ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings" for purposes of the exemption. The government may establish the existence of such proceedings simply by attesting to them. As the court explained, quoting *Juarez v. Department of Justice*, "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

The FBI's declaration in *Farahi* provided two related justifications for why disclosure could interfere with pending proceedings. First, disclosure could "tip off the targets about the scope of the investigation"—a rationale the court noted is "widely accepted" in Exemption 7(A) cases. Second, the declaration attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing." The ex parte Hardy declaration provided additional detail about the nature and scope of that investigation, which the court reviewed but could not publicly describe.

## The Role of Agency Declarations in Establishing Pending Proceedings

Agency declarations serve as the primary mechanism by which the government meets its burden in FOIA exemption cases. The government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies, and it typically does so through affidavits or declarations from agency officials. As the court noted, a court "may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated."

In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted declarations from two officials: David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), and later, Joseph Bender, who was then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The Hardy declaration was comprehensive, explaining both the law enforcement purpose and the ongoing nature of the investigation. It included both public and ex parte components—the latter providing sensitive details that could not be disclosed on the public record. The Bender declaration, submitted later, confirmed that the investigation remained ongoing as of March 2023.

## Heightened Deference in National Security Cases

The D.C. Circuit applies particularly robust deference when FOIA requests implicate national security concerns. The court in *Farahi* emphasized that it has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated." This principle applies "specifically in Exemption 7(A) cases seeking records related to counterterrorism investigations."

This heightened deference reflects both institutional competence and constitutional considerations. The executive branch possesses specialized expertise in matters of national security and counterterrorism. Courts recognize that they are ill-positioned to second-guess agency assessments of whether disclosure might compromise ongoing investigations in this sensitive domain. The court cited *Center for National Security Studies v. Department of Justice* as establishing this principle of enhanced deference in the national security context.

The national security dimension of *Farahi* is unmistakable. The FBI's public allegations about Farahi—that he was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other Al Qaeda members, linked to Jose Padilla, and involved with charities financing terrorism—illustrate why the court was particularly reluctant to compel disclosure. The investigation implicated high-level terrorism matters, and the court accorded the FBI's judgment appropriate deference.

## Addressing Farahi's Staleness Argument

Farahi challenged the application of Exemption 7(A) on the ground that the government's showing had grown stale. He invoked the D.C. Circuit's statement in *CREW v. Department of Justice* that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support the exemption. Farahi contended that the Bender declaration, which stated only that the investigations "remain ongoing," was similarly conclusory and that the passage of time undermined any claim that proceedings remained pending.

The court acknowledged that the Bender declaration, "considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." However, the court rejected the invitation to consider it in isolation. The government had already established the existence of enforcement proceedings through the Hardy declarations—both the public declaration, which attested to the investigation's existence, and the ex parte declaration, which provided "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." The Bender declaration was submitted "not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending."

The court found further support in the breadth of the underlying investigation. The FBI's public allegations about Farahi's connections to designated terrorist organizations, Al Qaeda members, and convicted terrorists made it plausible that an investigation of such magnitude would remain ongoing over an extended period. Based on both the public and sealed declarations, the court concluded that the FBI had shown that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist.

## The Bender Declaration as an Update, Not a Standalone Submission

Understanding how the court treated the Bender declaration is essential to following its reasoning. The procedural posture is critical: the district court had initially denied the FBI's summary judgment motion without prejudice because, while the FBI "seemingly" met its burden, the significant passage of time required an updated assessment. The district court concluded that it needed more information about whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated.

When the FBI renewed its motion, it submitted the Bender declaration to address precisely this concern. The declaration confirmed that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file and that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte component of Bender's declaration provided additional detail. Reading this submission in context, the court understood it as an update confirming that the status quo had not changed—not as a freestanding attempt to establish pending proceedings without adequate support.

This contextual reading allowed the court to reject Farahi's argument that the Bender declaration was too conclusory to carry the government's burden. The government had already provided adequate detail in the Hardy declarations; Bender's submission simply confirmed that nothing had changed during the intervening period. This approach reflects a practical understanding of how litigation unfolds and how agencies can satisfy their burden through a combination of initial and supplemental submissions.

# Segregability, Ex Parte Declarations, and Procedural Safeguards

## The Segregability Analysis: When Nonexempt Material Is 'Inextricably Intertwined' with Exempt Portions

Even when a FOIA exemption applies to portions of a requested record, the agency may not simply withhold the entire document. FOIA requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The statute explicitly provides that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt." Id. § 552(b). This segregability requirement reflects FOIA's fundamental policy of maximum disclosure: agencies must disclose whatever they can without harming the interests protected by exemptions.

However, segregability has its limits. The D.C. Circuit has long recognized that agencies may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008). This standard acknowledges that some records cannot be meaningfully redacted without destroying the utility of whatever information might remain. The question is not whether some isolated words or phrases could theoretically be extracted, but whether any meaningful nonexempt information can be reasonably separated from the exempt material.

In this case, the FBI withheld approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs worth of records. Farahi argued that such a vast volume of material must contain some segregable, nonexempt information. The court rejected this intuitive argument, explaining that the mere quantity of withheld records does not determine whether meaningful segregation is possible.

## The Distinction Between Isolated Words and 'Reasonably Segregable Information' Under Mead Data

The court's analysis drew heavily on *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977), a foundational segregability decision. In *Mead Data*, the court explained that if a small amount of nonexempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." 566 F.2d at 261. The court offered a crucial insight: "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Id. at 261 n.55.

This distinction between "information" and mere "words" matters because it prevents a hypertechnical approach to segregability. A plaintiff cannot argue that individual words—names, dates, common nouns—could be released without harm. The question is whether those words, taken together, communicate any meaningful information that can be separated from the exempt material. When exempt and nonexempt material are so intermingled that extracting nonexempt words yields nothing coherent, the agency has met its segregability burden.

The court applied this principle to Farahi's case. The FBI's records concerned an ongoing counterterrorism investigation. While Farahi was correct that individual words or phrases might technically be disclosed without harming investigative interests, the court found this argument "neither here nor there." The proper inquiry was whether any meaningful information—the "focus of FOIA"—was reasonably segregable. Based on the FBI's sealed submissions, the court concluded that no such meaningful information existed.

## The Government's Burden to Justify Non-Segregability with Reasonable Specificity

The government bears the burden of demonstrating that withheld records fall within a FOIA exemption, and this burden extends to segregability. Courts may not simply accept an agency's conclusory assertion that no segregable information exists. Rather, the government must "show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61.

This "reasonable specificity" requirement does not demand that agencies parse every document line by line in their public filings. Instead, agencies must provide sufficient explanation for a court to evaluate whether their segregability determinations were proper. Often, this explanation can be provided through agency affidavits or declarations that describe the nature of the records and why nonexempt material cannot be meaningfully separated.

In this case, the FBI provided its segregability justification through a supplemental ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of the Records/Information Dissemination Section. The district court reviewed this submission and found that it provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. The court concluded that this submission satisfied the agency's burden. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, deferring to the district court's assessment of the sealed material.

## Ex Parte Declarations in FOIA Litigation: Why They Are Sometimes Necessary and Their Inherent Limitations for Plaintiffs

FOIA litigation presents a unique procedural challenge when the very information sought to be protected cannot be disclosed without defeating the purpose of the exemption. Ex parte declarations—submissions filed with the court without disclosure to the opposing party—provide a mechanism for agencies to justify withholdings in such cases. These declarations allow courts to evaluate the government's justifications without risking disclosure of sensitive information.

The court acknowledged that ex parte declarations "no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs." Plaintiffs cannot see, challenge, or respond to the government's specific justifications. This disadvantage is particularly acute when, as here, the court's analysis of segregability depends almost entirely on information the plaintiff cannot access. A plaintiff has no opportunity to point out alleged inconsistencies, suggest alternative redactions, or argue that the agency's descriptions do not support its claims.

Nevertheless, the court found this limitation inherent in certain FOIA cases. As the court explained, quoting *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, "that problem 'is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.'" 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The alternative—requiring full disclosure of the government's justifications—would undermine the exemptions themselves in cases involving sensitive law enforcement or national security information.

## Waiver Principles: Farahi's Failure to Oppose Ex Parte Submissions Foreclosed His Challenge on Appeal

A critical procedural aspect of this case involved Farahi's failure to oppose the FBI's ex parte submissions. When the FBI moved to file supplemental declarations under seal and ex parte, Farahi did not oppose the motion. The district court granted it. Only on appeal did Farahi challenge the district court's reliance on those ex parte declarations.

The court held that Farahi had forfeited this argument. Citing *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019), the court explained that arguments not raised below are typically forfeited on appeal. Because Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, he could not challenge that reliance on appeal.

This waiver principle serves important purposes in FOIA litigation. It prevents parties from staying silent during district court proceedings—perhaps hoping for a favorable ruling—and then raising objections only after an adverse decision. It also preserves judicial efficiency: if Farahi had opposed ex parte submissions, the district court could have addressed those objections in the first instance, potentially through modified procedures or additional briefing.

The court noted another constraint on Farahi's challenge: "nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration." When courts rely on ex parte submissions, they cannot publicly explain their reasoning in detail without potentially revealing the very information protected by the exemption. Farahi's procedural choices—declining to challenge ex parte procedures when they were proposed—left him without a viable avenue to contest the district court's segregability analysis on appeal.
