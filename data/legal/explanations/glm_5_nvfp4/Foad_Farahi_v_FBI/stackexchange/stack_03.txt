Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What makes nonexempt information "inextricably intertwined" with exempt information under FOIA's segregability requirement?
Question:
The court rejects Farahi's segregability argument by saying that even if individual words could be disclosed, if exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it's not "reasonably segregable." The court cites Mead Data for the proposition that FOIA is about "information, not simply words." I'm struggling with this distinction. If a document has some exempt facts and some nonexempt facts, how does a court decide whether the nonexempt facts constitute "information" that must be released versus mere "words" that can be withheld as intertwined? What practical standard do courts use?
Answer:
### The Distinction Between "Information" and "Words"

The court's analysis turns on whether the nonexempt material, once separated from the exempt material, would constitute meaningful, coherent "information" rather than merely isolated "words" that lack independent value.

**The Legal Standard**

FOIA requires agencies to release "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record" after deleting exempt portions. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). However, agencies may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

**The "Information, Not Words" Principle**

The court quotes *Mead Data* for the foundational principle that "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 261 n.55 (D.C. Cir. 1977).

This means the inquiry is not whether you can technically carve out individual nonexempt words or phrases—the question is whether doing so would yield any coherent, useful information. The court applies a practical test: if exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," then extracting the nonexempt portions would leave behind only fragmented words or phrases, not meaningful information.

**The Practical Standard**

The court articulates a straightforward standard: when nonexempt material is "randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,' that material is not likely to be 'reasonably segregable.'" The underlying logic is that releasing isolated words or phrases—even if technically nonexempt—does not advance FOIA's purpose of informing the public.

In *Farahi*, the court rejected the argument that there "must be some segregable and releasable information in the vast volume of responsive records." Even if "individual words or phrases" could theoretically be disclosed without harming law enforcement interests, that does not mean those words constitute "meaningful information" that FOIA requires to be segregated and released.

**Bottom Line**

Courts examine whether the nonexempt material can stand alone as coherent, informative content. If extraction would produce only disconnected words or sentence fragments—because the exempt and nonexempt material are woven together line-by-line—then the nonexempt material is not "reasonably segregable" and the agency may withhold the document in full.
