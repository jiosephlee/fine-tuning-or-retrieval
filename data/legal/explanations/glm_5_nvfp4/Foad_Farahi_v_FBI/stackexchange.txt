Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What constitutes a "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceeding under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The court states that ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" for purposes of Exemption 7(A), citing CREW. But I'm confused about the boundaries here. If an investigation has been ongoing for years (Farahi's case involved records from 2002 through at least 2023), at what point does it stop being "reasonably anticipated" that enforcement proceedings will actually occur? The court mentions that the investigation must be gathering evidence "for a possible future criminal case" - but is there any temporal limit? Can an agency just claim an investigation is indefinitely "ongoing" to permanently withhold records?
Answer:
The short answer is that there is no bright-line temporal limit, but the agency's burden to show a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" persists throughout the litigation—and courts can require updates when declarations grow stale.

Here's the framework the opinion establishes:

**The Legal Standard**

Exemption 7(A) requires proceedings to be "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court reaffirms that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings" for this purpose, citing *CREW*. The key inquiry is whether the investigation "continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence."

**The Temporal Concern Has Judicial Check**

Farahi argued that the passage of time undermined the FBI's claim. The court acknowledged this concern had merit in principle—quoting *CREW* that "vague" assertions may not suffice. But importantly, the district court had already exercised this check: it denied the FBI's first motion for summary judgment and ordered updated declarations precisely because of the "significant passage of time."

This shows that indefinite claims are not automatically accepted—courts can and do require agencies to refresh the record.

**Context Matters for Plausibility**

The court looked beyond the bare assertion that investigations "remain ongoing." It found the claim plausible given:

1. The **breadth of the underlying investigation**—the FBI's public allegations about Farahi's connections to designated terrorist organizations, Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, Jose Padilla, and terrorism-financing charities
2. The **detail in prior submissions**—the Hardy declarations (particularly ex parte) had already established the investigation's nature and scope
3. The **limited purpose of the updated declaration**—Bender's submission was meant to confirm nothing had changed, not establish existence from scratch

**Deference in National Security Contexts**

The court emphasizes "particular" deference when national security is implicated. But note that deference doesn't mean rubber-stamping—the FBI still bore the burden of showing a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" exists.

**Practical Answer to Your Question**

An agency cannot simply claim an investigation is indefinitely "ongoing" to permanently withhold records. The safeguards are:

- Courts can order updated showings when time passes (as happened here)
- Declarations must have "reasonable specificity"—conclusory assertions may fail
- The proceeding must be "concrete," not hypothetical
- The underlying investigation must genuinely be gathering evidence for a possible future case

The opinion suggests that complex counterterrorism investigations may legitimately span decades, but agencies remain subject to judicial verification that the investigation is active, not dormant or abandoned.

### How should courts evaluate supplemental declarations in FOIA cases - independently or in context of prior submissions?
Question:
The court here says the Bender declaration shouldn't be considered "in isolation" because it was submitted only to "confirm that nothing had changed" since the earlier Hardy declaration. But Farahi argued that the Bender declaration was too conclusory on its face. When an agency submits an updated declaration years later, does it need to independently satisfy the government's burden, or can it rely on prior declarations to establish the underlying facts? How does a court determine whether an updated declaration is meant to 'establish from scratch' versus merely 'confirm' earlier showings?
Answer:
### Answer

The D.C. Circuit here articulates a functional approach: courts should evaluate supplemental declarations in context, not in isolation, when the purpose of the supplemental submission is to *update* or *confirm* prior showings rather than to establish the government's burden from scratch.

**The Legal Standard**

The court explicitly rejects evaluating the Bender declaration "in isolation," stating that "context counsels against" doing so. The key distinction is whether the supplemental declaration is meant to "establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch" or "only to confirm that nothing had changed" while earlier motions were pending.

Here, the FBI had already submitted detailed Hardy declarations—both public and ex parte—that established the existence and nature of ongoing enforcement proceedings. The district court had even concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden on this point before ordering an update due to the passage of time. In that procedural posture, the Bender declaration served a limited function: confirming the continued vitality of the prior showing.

**How Courts Distinguish "Confirm" from "Establish from Scratch"**

The opinion identifies several factors that indicate a supplemental declaration is meant to *confirm* rather than *establish*:

1. **Prior adequate showings**: The government had already established the underlying facts through detailed declarations (the Hardy declarations provided "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation").

2. **Prior court findings**: The district court had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden before ordering the update.

3. **Stated purpose**: The supplemental declaration was submitted in direct response to the court's order to update potentially stale information, not as an initial showing.

4. **Breadth of underlying investigation**: The broader context—including public allegations about Farahi's connections to designated terrorist organizations—supported the plausibility that investigations remained ongoing.

**Practical Implications**

This holding does not give agencies a free pass to submit conclusory supplemental declarations. The court notes it "agree[s] with Farahi that the Bender declaration, considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." The declaration survived only because it functioned as an update to adequate prior submissions, not as a standalone justification.

Had the Hardy declarations been deficient, or had the district court not already found the government had "seemingly" met its burden, a terse Bender declaration would likely have been insufficient. The government cannot cure a deficient initial showing with a conclusory supplemental one.

**Bottom Line**

Supplemental declarations are evaluated based on their *function*. When an agency submits an update after the court has already found the initial showing adequate, the supplemental declaration need only confirm the continued vitality of that showing. But if the supplemental declaration is the government's first meaningful attempt to carry its burden, or if prior submissions were inadequate, the declaration must independently satisfy the government's burden with reasonable specificity.

### What makes nonexempt information "inextricably intertwined" with exempt information under FOIA's segregability requirement?
Question:
The court rejects Farahi's segregability argument by saying that even if individual words could be disclosed, if exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it's not "reasonably segregable." The court cites Mead Data for the proposition that FOIA is about "information, not simply words." I'm struggling with this distinction. If a document has some exempt facts and some nonexempt facts, how does a court decide whether the nonexempt facts constitute "information" that must be released versus mere "words" that can be withheld as intertwined? What practical standard do courts use?
Answer:
### The Distinction Between "Information" and "Words"

The court's analysis turns on whether the nonexempt material, once separated from the exempt material, would constitute meaningful, coherent "information" rather than merely isolated "words" that lack independent value.

**The Legal Standard**

FOIA requires agencies to release "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record" after deleting exempt portions. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). However, agencies may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

**The "Information, Not Words" Principle**

The court quotes *Mead Data* for the foundational principle that "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 261 n.55 (D.C. Cir. 1977).

This means the inquiry is not whether you can technically carve out individual nonexempt words or phrases—the question is whether doing so would yield any coherent, useful information. The court applies a practical test: if exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," then extracting the nonexempt portions would leave behind only fragmented words or phrases, not meaningful information.

**The Practical Standard**

The court articulates a straightforward standard: when nonexempt material is "randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,' that material is not likely to be 'reasonably segregable.'" The underlying logic is that releasing isolated words or phrases—even if technically nonexempt—does not advance FOIA's purpose of informing the public.

In *Farahi*, the court rejected the argument that there "must be some segregable and releasable information in the vast volume of responsive records." Even if "individual words or phrases" could theoretically be disclosed without harming law enforcement interests, that does not mean those words constitute "meaningful information" that FOIA requires to be segregated and released.

**Bottom Line**

Courts examine whether the nonexempt material can stand alone as coherent, informative content. If extraction would produce only disconnected words or sentence fragments—because the exempt and nonexempt material are woven together line-by-line—then the nonexempt material is not "reasonably segregable" and the agency may withhold the document in full.

### What role do ex parte declarations play in FOIA litigation, and can plaintiffs challenge the court's reliance on them?
Question:
The court relied heavily on ex parte declarations here and acknowledges this "handicaps" FOIA plaintiffs, calling the problem "inherent" in cases where disclosure would defeat the exemption. But Farahi didn't oppose the acceptance of ex parte declarations, and the court says he therefore "cannot challenge" the court's reliance on them. Does failing to oppose the initial acceptance of ex parte submissions waive all later objections to how the court uses them? And practically speaking, how can a plaintiff ever successfully challenge ex parte evidence if they can't see what it says?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is that **failing to oppose the acceptance of ex parte declarations does waive objections to the court's reliance on them**, at least in the D.C. Circuit's view. As for how plaintiffs can challenge evidence they cannot see, the court acknowledges this is an "inherent" dilemma—but one that does not provide grounds to reject ex parte submissions outright.

---

### 1. Waiver of Objections to Ex Parte Reliance

The court addresses your question directly in the final paragraph of the segregability discussion:

> "For one thing, Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."

The court treats acceptance of the *procedural mechanism* (allowing ex parte submissions) as forfeiting objections to the *substantive reliance* on those submissions. This is a forfeiture rule: by failing to object when the government moved to file the declarations ex parte, Farahi lost the opportunity to later argue that the court placed undue weight on them or that the court's analysis should have been more transparent.

The opinion does not explore whether an earlier objection would have succeeded—it simply holds that the failure to object foreclosed the argument on appeal.

---

### 2. The "Inherent" Handicap on Plaintiffs

You asked how a plaintiff can ever successfully challenge evidence they cannot see. The court is candid that this is a structural disadvantage:

> "For another, while ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs, that problem 'is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.'" (quoting *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023))

The logic is straightforward: in cases where the very information at issue must remain secret, there is no way to give the plaintiff meaningful access to the government's justifications without risking the disclosure the exemption is designed to prevent. The court accepts this handicap as unavoidable.

Practically, plaintiffs are not entirely helpless. They can:

- **Challenge the public declarations** for vagueness or inconsistency. The court notes that it did *not* consider the Bender declaration "in isolation"—it read it as a supplement to the earlier, more detailed Hardy declarations, both public and ex parte. Farahi successfully obtained a more detailed public record in his immigration proceedings, which the court used to test the plausibility of the FBI's claims.
- **Argue that the investigation is stale or no longer ongoing.** Farahi did this, relying on the passage of time. The court rejected it on the facts, but the argument was available.
- **Oppose the acceptance of ex parte declarations in the first place.** The opinion suggests that an objection at that stage would at least preserve the issue for appeal, even if the objection might not prevail on the merits.

---

### Summary

- **Waiver:** By not opposing the motion to file ex parte declarations, Farahi forfeited the ability to challenge the court's reliance on them.
- **The inherent handicap:** Plaintiffs face a structural disadvantage, but the D.C. Circuit considers it a necessary feature of FOIA litigation involving sensitive law enforcement information, not a due process violation.
- **Practical takeaway:** If you represent a FOIA plaintiff, object to ex parte submissions at the earliest opportunity to preserve the issue, even if you expect the objection to be overruled. Otherwise, you may lose the argument before it begins.

### How does deference to the FBI in national security cases interact with the government's burden of proof under FOIA?
Question:
The court emphasizes "deference to the executive" in FOIA cases involving national security, citing CNSS. But it also says the FBI must "carry its burden" and show a "rational nexus" between the investigation and law enforcement duties. I'm confused about how deference and burden of proof work together. If the court defers to the FBI's characterization of records and its judgment about interference, isn't the government effectively relieved of its burden? What showing is the FBI actually required to make, and at what point does deference become de facto exemption from scrutiny?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important tension, but deference and burden are complementary, not contradictory. Deference shapes **how** the court evaluates the government's evidence—it doesn't eliminate the requirement that the government **produce** that evidence in the first place.

**What deference actually means here**

The court cites *Campbell* and *CNSS* for the principle that because the FBI "specializes in law enforcement," its invocation of Exemption 7 is "entitled to deference." But read carefully: deference here means the court gives weight to the FBI's **expert judgment** about whether disclosure could interfere with an investigation. It does not mean the FBI gets a free pass on showing the exemption applies.

**The burden remains real**

The opinion makes clear the FBI must still "carry its burden" by producing declarations that "show with reasonable specificity" why the exemption applies. For the "law enforcement purposes" prong, declarations must "establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties." That's a substantive requirement the FBI must meet—deference doesn't waive it.

Similarly, for the "interference with enforcement proceedings" prong, the government must show proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The FBI can do this "simply by attesting to their 'existence,'" but it still must **attest**—and that attestation must be credible in context.

**How they work together in practice**

This case illustrates the dynamic well. The FBI submitted detailed public and ex parte declarations explaining the investigation. The court then **deferred** to the FBI's judgment that disclosure would tip off targets—a "widely accepted justification" the court didn't second-guess.

But when the Bender declaration came in "surprisingly terse," the court didn't just accept it. It read the declaration "in context" with the earlier Hardy declarations, which had supplied "considerable detail." The court also looked to "broader context"—what the FBI had publicly alleged in Farahi's immigration proceedings—to assess plausibility.

Critically, the court had already required the FBI to **update** its stale declarations before granting summary judgment. That's not an agency being "relieved of its burden"—that's a court enforcing the burden.

**When assertions become too vague**

Farahi invoked *CREW* for the proposition that "vague" assertions may not suffice. The court acknowledged this principle but distinguished the case—the Bender declaration wasn't standalone; it was an update to already-detailed submissions. This shows the limit: deference has bounds, but those bounds are assessed contextually.

**The bottom line**

Deference means courts credit the FBI's expertise on national security and law enforcement judgments. The burden means the FBI must still produce evidence supporting those judgments. The FBI can't just assert an exemption—it must explain, with "reasonable specificity," why the exemption applies. The court then evaluates that explanation deferentially, but it still evaluates it.

### What level of specificity must agency declarations show under FOIA, and does it vary by issue?
Question:
The court says the government may show the existence of enforcement proceedings simply by "attesting to their existence" (citing CREW), but also that declarations must show "reasonable specificity" why documents can't be segregated (citing Juarez). These seem like different standards. Does the required level of specificity differ depending on whether the issue is the existence of proceedings versus segregability? And for the "law enforcement purposes" prong, the court accepts a "rational nexus" test - is that a lower threshold than "reasonable specificity"?
Answer:
### Different Standards for Different FOIA Issues

Yes, the required level of specificity differs depending on what the agency is establishing. The opinion applies distinct standards to three separate inquiries under Exemption 7(A), and the differences reflect what each inquiry is designed to prove.

### 1. Law Enforcement Purposes: The "Rational Nexus" Test

For the threshold requirement that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes," the court applies the "rational nexus" test from *Campbell v. DOJ*. The agency must establish:

> "a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,'" as well as a "connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'"

This standard is **structural**—it asks whether the investigation falls within the agency's law enforcement authority. The FBI's status as a law enforcement agency entitles its determination to "a fair measure of deference." Here, the Hardy declaration's explanation that records were compiled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism" sufficed, particularly given Farahi's own concessions about the nature of the FBI's interest in him.

### 2. Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings: Attesting to Existence

For showing that enforcement proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated," the standard is notably minimal:

> "the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their 'existence,' citing *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099.

However, the opinion clarifies that this attestation cannot be "vague" in isolation. The court found the terse Bender declaration acceptable only because it was an **update** to prior submissions—the Hardy declarations had already "attested to [the proceedings'] existence" with "considerable detail" in the ex parte submission. The court emphasizes that "context counsels against considering the Bender declaration in isolation."

This standard is **existence-based**—the question is simply whether proceedings exist, not their precise scope or details.

### 3. Segregability: "Reasonable Specificity"

For segregability, the court applies a more demanding standard:

> "A court may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." (citing *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61)

This standard is **granular**—it requires the agency to explain the practical mechanics of why exempt and non-exempt information cannot be separated. The district court found the FBI provided a "detailed justification" in Bender's supplemental ex parte submission, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed, noting that when non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is not "reasonably segregable."

### Why the Standards Differ

The varying levels of specificity reflect the distinct questions each standard addresses:

- **Rational nexus**: Is this the kind of investigation the agency is authorized to conduct?
- **Attesting to existence**: Does an investigation actually exist?
- **Reasonable specificity**: Why can't we carve out the non-exempt material?

The opinion also highlights that deference varies with context: "Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," and courts have "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated."

### Conclusion

The "rational nexus" test and "reasonable specificity" are not really comparable as higher or lower thresholds—they address fundamentally different questions. The former is about institutional authority and the nature of an investigation; the latter is about the practical feasibility of redacting specific documents. The "attesting to existence" standard for pending proceedings is arguably the least demanding, but as the court cautions, context matters—an overly vague attestation standing alone may not suffice.

### What showing must plaintiffs make to challenge an agency's categorical approach to FOIA withholding?
Question:
The court says Farahi "forfeited" his argument about document categories because he provided "no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." But I thought the government bears the burden in FOIA cases. Does a plaintiff have the burden to specifically identify why each category is improper, or should the court independently evaluate whether categories are "sufficiently distinct"? What level of particularity must a plaintiff show to preserve a categorical challenge, and at what point in the litigation must this argument be made?
Answer:
The government bears the ultimate burden of proving that a FOIA exemption applies, but a plaintiff must still **specifically articulate** any challenge to the agency's categorical withholding methodology to preserve the issue for review.

**The Legal Framework**

Under FOIA, "the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). When an agency withholds documents on a categorical basis, it must use categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each category. *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1088 (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB*, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)).

**The Plaintiff's Obligation**

However, as the court explained, a plaintiff who wishes to challenge an agency's categorical approach must provide "specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." Farahi failed to do so and thus "forfeited his arguments on this score." The court cited *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019), for the forfeiture principle.

This reflects a **litigation burden** on the plaintiff to specifically articulate what is wrong with the agency's categorical approach. The government still bears the burden of proof on the underlying exemption claim, but the plaintiff cannot simply point to the existence of categories and demand that the court independently evaluate their sufficiency. The plaintiff must identify *why* the categories are problematic—for example, by explaining how a particular category lumps together documents that should be treated differently, or how a category's description is so vague that the court cannot evaluate whether the exemption properly applies.

**Timing**

The argument must be raised in the plaintiff's opposition to summary judgment. By the time Farahi appealed, he had already forfeited the issue by failing to "provid[e] specific explanation" in the district court proceedings.

**Summary**

The government bears the ultimate burden of proof on FOIA exemptions, but a categorical challenge is waived if the plaintiff offers no specific explanation for why the agency's categories are overbroad or inadequately distinct. The burden is not on the court to independently audit the agency's categorization scheme.
