Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # The FOIA Framework and Exemption 7(A)

## FOIA's Presumption of Disclosure and the Statutory Exemption Structure

The Freedom of Information Act codifies a fundamental principle of open government: federal agencies must release records to members of the public upon request. As the court in *Farahi* explains, FOIA establishes a "presumption of disclosure" that places the burden on agencies to justify any refusal to produce requested materials. This presumption reflects the statute's central purpose of promoting transparency and accountability in government operations.

However, Congress recognized that unlimited disclosure could harm legitimate governmental interests. FOIA therefore creates a carefully bounded exception to its disclosure mandate. Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b), agencies may withhold records only if one of nine enumerated statutory exemptions applies. These exemptions are exclusive—an agency cannot refuse disclosure based on general concerns or policy arguments not captured by the statutory text. The exemptions function as a comprehensive list of permissible justifications for nondisclosure, and courts interpret them narrowly to preserve FOIA's disclosure-oriented purpose.

This structure creates the basic analytical framework for any FOIA case. The requester submits a request, the agency searches for responsive records, and then either discloses them or invokes one or more exemptions to justify withholding. If the requester challenges the agency's decision in court, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that the exemption applies. The court's task is to determine whether the agency has met that burden as to each category of withheld records.

## The Text and Elements of Exemption 7(A)

The exemption at issue in *Farahi* is Exemption 7(A), one of several exemptions addressing law enforcement records. The exemption applies to records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The court reads this statutory language as establishing two distinct elements that the government must prove.

First, the records must have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes." This requirement ensures that the exemption protects only those documents actually created or gathered in connection with legitimate law enforcement activities, not merely any documents that happen to be in an agency's possession. The court notes that because the FBI "specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference" on this threshold question. Even so, the agency's declarations must "establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties'" and a "connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'" The court applies this test by examining both the public and ex parte declarations submitted by the FBI, as well as context from Farahi's own submissions in related proceedings.

Second, disclosure of the records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." This interference prong requires the agency to identify a causal connection between disclosure and some harm to an enforcement proceeding. The court explains that disclosure can interfere with investigations by "tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation"—a justification the court describes as "widely accepted" in Exemption 7(A) cases. The court also notes that deference to the executive branch is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," including counterterrorism investigations like the one at issue here.

## The "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated" Requirement

A critical interpretive question in applying Exemption 7(A) is what qualifies as an "enforcement proceeding" that could be interfered with through disclosure. The D.C. Circuit has held that such proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." This requirement, established in prior precedent and reaffirmed in *Farahi*, prevents agencies from invoking the exemption based on purely speculative enforcement actions.

The court explains that "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings'" for purposes of the exemption. This interpretive ruling expands the ordinary meaning of "proceeding"—which might suggest a formal adjudicatory process—to encompass the investigative phase that precedes any formal charges. The rationale is that the investigative stage is precisely when premature disclosure poses the greatest risk: revealing the existence, scope, or direction of an investigation can allow targets to destroy evidence, coordinate alibis, or otherwise undermine the government's ability to build its case.

The government may satisfy its burden to show pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings "simply by attesting to their 'existence.'" However, this attestation must be sufficiently specific. The court in *Farahi* addresses the defendant's argument that the passage of time cast doubt on whether proceedings remained pending. While acknowledging that one declaration, "considered in isolation, appear[ed] surprisingly terse," the court looked to the broader record—including prior detailed declarations—to conclude that the government had met its burden. The court emphasized that the supplemental declaration was "an update to [earlier] declarations, not as a standalone document."

Importantly, the court also considered the nature of the underlying investigation in evaluating whether ongoing proceedings remained plausible. The FBI had publicly alleged in immigration proceedings that Farahi had connections to designated terrorist organizations and individuals convicted of terrorism-related charges. Given "the breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation," the court found it "plausible" that the investigation remained ongoing, supporting the conclusion that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist.

## The Segregability Requirement

Even when a FOIA exemption applies to some portion of a responsive record, the agency's obligations do not end there. FOIA imposes a segregability requirement: agencies must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." This requirement implements the statute's fundamental commitment to maximum disclosure consistent with the exemptions. The government cannot use an exemption as a pretext to withhold entire documents when only portions contain protected information.

The segregability inquiry focuses on whether nonexempt information can be meaningfully separated from exempt material. The court explains that if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not "reasonably segregable." This is because "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." The court thus rejected Farahi's argument that some segregable information must exist somewhere in the "vast volume of responsive records"—approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs.

A practical challenge arises when determining segregability in cases involving sensitive law enforcement or national security information. The court in *Farahi* relied on ex parte declarations—submissions reviewed by the court but not shared with the opposing party—to evaluate the government's segregability arguments. The court noted that while ex parte proceedings "no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs," this problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where … an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

## Standard of Review and Burden Allocation

FOIA cases follow the familiar procedural posture of summary judgment litigation, but with specialized rules regarding burdens of proof. The court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, giving no deference to the district court's legal conclusions. This standard reflects the fact that FOIA determinations ultimately present questions of statutory interpretation and application.

Critically, "the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies." This burden allocation implements FOIA's presumption of disclosure. The agency seeking to withhold records must justify that decision; the requester bears no obligation to demonstrate that disclosure would be harmless. The court applies this framework by examining whether the government's evidentiary submissions—typically agency declarations describing the withheld records and the reasons for nondisclosure—meet the applicable legal standards.

When evaluating agency declarations, courts apply a standard of "reasonable specificity." Agency affidavits must "show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." In *Farahi*, the court found the government's declarations sufficient, crediting the FBI's explanation that disclosure would tip off targets and that the investigation remained ongoing. The court also noted the appropriateness of deference in national security contexts, citing precedent holding that courts should be "particularly deferential" when reviewing agency decisions in this area.

Finally, the court addressed a procedural forfeiture issue. Farahi objected that the government's document categories were overbroad, but he "provide[d] no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." The court held that Farahi had "forfeited his arguments on this score." This ruling illustrates an important procedural principle: courts will not develop arguments on a party's behalf, and litigants must articulate specific objections to agency categorization decisions to preserve those issues for appeal.
