Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Farahi's Background: Iranian National, Removal Proceedings, and Alleged Terrorist Connections

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. His encounters with federal law enforcement began in 2002, when the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on a visa overstay. Farahi's immigration case has been protracted and multifaceted: at various stages, he has sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.

The government's opposition to Farahi's requests for relief has drawn heavily on intelligence gathered by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. During immigration proceedings, DHS presented both documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who, in the words of the Eleventh Circuit, "documented Farahi's connections to high-level terrorists." The public record in this FOIA litigation reveals more specific allegations: Farahi was identified as "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists" including Jose Padilla (who was detained as an enemy combatant and later convicted on terrorism-related charges in federal court), and supported "charities that financed terrorism," including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation.

These allegations are central to understanding the FBI's resistance to disclosing Farahi's file. The government's representation that Farahi has connections to designated foreign terrorist organizations and individuals implicated in serious terrorism cases underscores the national security dimensions of this case and helps explain why the FBI has sought to keep its investigative file confidential.

## The FOIA Request: Farahi Sought His FBI File

Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The Bureau's response revealed the substantial scope of its documentation: the agency located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive information. This volume suggests that the FBI had devoted significant investigative resources to Farahi over an extended period.

Farahi filed suit after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to his request. Over the next several years, the agency conducted a page-by-page review of the potentially responsive records. At the conclusion of this process, the FBI had disclosed some records to Farahi but withheld the vast majority. The question before the courts became whether the FBI had properly justified its decision to withhold.

## The FBI's Initial Response and the Hardy Declaration Supporting Withholding Under Exemption 7(A)

In October 2019, after completing its review of the responsive records, the FBI moved for summary judgment. In support of its motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration is the government's primary evidentiary vehicle for establishing that the withheld records fall within a FOIA exemption.

In this case, Hardy's public declaration asserted that the withheld records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes"—a threshold requirement for Exemption 7—and that their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that were pending or reasonably anticipated. Hardy explained that disclosure of information regarding an ongoing investigation could tip off targets about the scope of that investigation, thereby compromising it. The declaration further attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing."

The FBI also moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte—meaning for the court's eyes only, without disclosure to Farahi. Farahi did not oppose this motion, and the district court granted it in October 2020. This procedural choice is significant in FOIA litigation involving national security: ex parte submissions allow agencies to provide sensitive details that support their exemption claims without revealing the very information they seek to protect.

## The District Court's Concern About the Passage of Time and Its Order Requiring Updated Declarations

The district court did not rule on the FBI's summary judgment motion until November 2022—more than three years after the motion was filed and more than two years after the supplemental Hardy declaration. When the court finally addressed the motion, it expressed concern about the staleness of the FBI's evidentiary submissions.

The court concluded that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show that release of the records could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. However, the "significant passage of time" since the Hardy declarations gave the court pause. Exemption 7(A) requires that enforcement proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated"—not merely that they existed at some point in the past. The district court therefore ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated.

The court also identified a deficiency regarding segregability. Under FOIA, agencies must release "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record" after deleting exempt portions. The district court concluded that it needed more information to assess whether the FBI had met this obligation, though it could not state its specific concerns on the public record. For both reasons, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice, leaving the door open for the FBI to renew its motion with updated evidence.

## The Renewed Motion with Bender Declarations and the District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment

In response to the district court's order, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency submitted declarations from Joseph Bender, who had become the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The FBI provided both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration from Bender.

Bender's public declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration supplied additional details about the nature of the investigation and addressed the court's segregability concerns with more specificity.

The district court granted the renewed motion. It credited the FBI's confirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." Based on its in camera review of the ex parte submission, the court also concluded that the FBI had adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable from non-exempt material. Having satisfied both requirements—Exemption 7(A) and segregability—the FBI was entitled to summary judgment. Farahi appealed, bringing the case before the D.C. Circuit.
