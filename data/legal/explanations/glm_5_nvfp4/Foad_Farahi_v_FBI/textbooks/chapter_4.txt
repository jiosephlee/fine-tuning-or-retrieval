Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # Segregability, Ex Parte Declarations, and Procedural Safeguards

## The Segregability Analysis: When Nonexempt Material Is 'Inextricably Intertwined' with Exempt Portions

Even when a FOIA exemption applies to portions of a requested record, the agency may not simply withhold the entire document. FOIA requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The statute explicitly provides that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt." Id. § 552(b). This segregability requirement reflects FOIA's fundamental policy of maximum disclosure: agencies must disclose whatever they can without harming the interests protected by exemptions.

However, segregability has its limits. The D.C. Circuit has long recognized that agencies may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008). This standard acknowledges that some records cannot be meaningfully redacted without destroying the utility of whatever information might remain. The question is not whether some isolated words or phrases could theoretically be extracted, but whether any meaningful nonexempt information can be reasonably separated from the exempt material.

In this case, the FBI withheld approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs worth of records. Farahi argued that such a vast volume of material must contain some segregable, nonexempt information. The court rejected this intuitive argument, explaining that the mere quantity of withheld records does not determine whether meaningful segregation is possible.

## The Distinction Between Isolated Words and 'Reasonably Segregable Information' Under Mead Data

The court's analysis drew heavily on *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977), a foundational segregability decision. In *Mead Data*, the court explained that if a small amount of nonexempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." 566 F.2d at 261. The court offered a crucial insight: "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Id. at 261 n.55.

This distinction between "information" and mere "words" matters because it prevents a hypertechnical approach to segregability. A plaintiff cannot argue that individual words—names, dates, common nouns—could be released without harm. The question is whether those words, taken together, communicate any meaningful information that can be separated from the exempt material. When exempt and nonexempt material are so intermingled that extracting nonexempt words yields nothing coherent, the agency has met its segregability burden.

The court applied this principle to Farahi's case. The FBI's records concerned an ongoing counterterrorism investigation. While Farahi was correct that individual words or phrases might technically be disclosed without harming investigative interests, the court found this argument "neither here nor there." The proper inquiry was whether any meaningful information—the "focus of FOIA"—was reasonably segregable. Based on the FBI's sealed submissions, the court concluded that no such meaningful information existed.

## The Government's Burden to Justify Non-Segregability with Reasonable Specificity

The government bears the burden of demonstrating that withheld records fall within a FOIA exemption, and this burden extends to segregability. Courts may not simply accept an agency's conclusory assertion that no segregable information exists. Rather, the government must "show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61.

This "reasonable specificity" requirement does not demand that agencies parse every document line by line in their public filings. Instead, agencies must provide sufficient explanation for a court to evaluate whether their segregability determinations were proper. Often, this explanation can be provided through agency affidavits or declarations that describe the nature of the records and why nonexempt material cannot be meaningfully separated.

In this case, the FBI provided its segregability justification through a supplemental ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of the Records/Information Dissemination Section. The district court reviewed this submission and found that it provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. The court concluded that this submission satisfied the agency's burden. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, deferring to the district court's assessment of the sealed material.

## Ex Parte Declarations in FOIA Litigation: Why They Are Sometimes Necessary and Their Inherent Limitations for Plaintiffs

FOIA litigation presents a unique procedural challenge when the very information sought to be protected cannot be disclosed without defeating the purpose of the exemption. Ex parte declarations—submissions filed with the court without disclosure to the opposing party—provide a mechanism for agencies to justify withholdings in such cases. These declarations allow courts to evaluate the government's justifications without risking disclosure of sensitive information.

The court acknowledged that ex parte declarations "no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs." Plaintiffs cannot see, challenge, or respond to the government's specific justifications. This disadvantage is particularly acute when, as here, the court's analysis of segregability depends almost entirely on information the plaintiff cannot access. A plaintiff has no opportunity to point out alleged inconsistencies, suggest alternative redactions, or argue that the agency's descriptions do not support its claims.

Nevertheless, the court found this limitation inherent in certain FOIA cases. As the court explained, quoting *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, "that problem 'is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.'" 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The alternative—requiring full disclosure of the government's justifications—would undermine the exemptions themselves in cases involving sensitive law enforcement or national security information.

## Waiver Principles: Farahi's Failure to Oppose Ex Parte Submissions Foreclosed His Challenge on Appeal

A critical procedural aspect of this case involved Farahi's failure to oppose the FBI's ex parte submissions. When the FBI moved to file supplemental declarations under seal and ex parte, Farahi did not oppose the motion. The district court granted it. Only on appeal did Farahi challenge the district court's reliance on those ex parte declarations.

The court held that Farahi had forfeited this argument. Citing *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019), the court explained that arguments not raised below are typically forfeited on appeal. Because Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, he could not challenge that reliance on appeal.

This waiver principle serves important purposes in FOIA litigation. It prevents parties from staying silent during district court proceedings—perhaps hoping for a favorable ruling—and then raising objections only after an adverse decision. It also preserves judicial efficiency: if Farahi had opposed ex parte submissions, the district court could have addressed those objections in the first instance, potentially through modified procedures or additional briefing.

The court noted another constraint on Farahi's challenge: "nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration." When courts rely on ex parte submissions, they cannot publicly explain their reasoning in detail without potentially revealing the very information protected by the exemption. Farahi's procedural choices—declining to challenge ex parte procedures when they were proposed—left him without a viable avenue to contest the district court's segregability analysis on appeal.
