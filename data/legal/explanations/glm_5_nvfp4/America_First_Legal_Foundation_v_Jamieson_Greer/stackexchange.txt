Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the court vacate the district court's judgment instead of simply affirming the dismissal?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural outcome here. The district court dismissed AFL's case for failure to state a claim, which sounds like a merits dismissal. But the D.C. Circuit vacated that judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Why does this distinction matter? If the result is that AFL loses either way, why does the appeals court bother vacating rather than just affirming? Is there some practical consequence I'm missing?
Answer:
### Answer

The distinction matters because subject-matter jurisdiction is a threshold requirement that federal courts must confirm before exercising any judicial power—including the power to dismiss a case on the merits. The D.C. Circuit could not simply "affirm" a merits-based dismissal when the court below lacked jurisdiction to reach those merits in the first place.

### The Governing Principle: Jurisdiction Comes First

As the court explains, citing *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998), courts must address standing "before reaching the merits" because standing "bear[s] on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction."

This is not mere formalism. Article III of the Constitution limits federal judicial power to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." If a plaintiff lacks standing, there is no case or controversy, and the court has no power to adjudicate anything—including whether the complaint fails to state a claim.

### Why Vacatur, Not Affirmance?

The district court committed legal error by dismissing on the merits (Rule 12(b)(6)) rather than for lack of jurisdiction (Rule 12(b)(1)). The district court actually reached the substantive question of whether § 1216 committed OSC's investigation decision to agency discretion. That was beyond its power if AFL lacked standing.

An affirmance would leave intact a judgment that improperly reached the merits without jurisdiction. The proper corrective is to vacate that judgment entirely, wiping the slate clean, and remand with instructions to enter the correct disposition—a jurisdictional dismissal.

### Practical Consequences of the Distinction

There are real differences between dismissal on the merits versus dismissal for lack of jurisdiction:

1. **Preclusive effect**: A merits dismissal can have claim-preclusive (res judicata) effect, barring future litigation on the same claim. A jurisdictional dismissal does not prejudge the merits and generally does not bar a plaintiff from refiling if they can later establish standing (though that seems unlikely here given the court's analysis).

2. **Appellate precedent**: The district court's substantive conclusions about § 1216—such as its interpretation of OSC's investigative authority—should not stand as valid legal rulings if the court lacked power to issue them.

3. **Proper record**: The vacatur ensures the legal record reflects that the dismissal was for lack of jurisdiction, not because AFL's claim was substantively invalid.

In short: the appellate court couldn't affirm a merits dismissal because the district court never had the power to dismiss on the merits. The only proper disposition was to vacate and remand for a jurisdictional dismissal.

### When does a statute create an 'entitlement' to information sufficient for informational standing?
Question:
The court says AFL can't establish informational standing because section 1216 creates no 'entitlement to receive information.' It cites cases like FEC v. Akins and Public Citizen v. DOJ where statutes did create such entitlements. How do I tell the difference? Section 1216(a)(3) uses the mandatory 'shall... conduct an investigation' - doesn't that suggest AFL has some right to have the investigation happen? What makes a statute create an entitlement versus just creating a procedural requirement?
Answer:
### Answer

The distinction turns on whether the statute grants the plaintiff a right to **receive information**—not merely a right to have the government **take some action** that might incidentally involve information.

**The Standard for Informational Standing**

As the court explains, a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury when denied information that a "statute entitled him to receive." The key cases—*FEC v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*—both involved statutes that specifically provided for public access to information. In *Akins*, the Federal Election Campaign Act required the FEC to compile and make certain records available to the public. In *Public Citizen*, the Federal Advisory Committee Act required advisory committees to open their meetings and records to the public. In both cases, the statutes created an affirmative right for the plaintiff to obtain information.

**Why Section 1216 Is Different**

Section 1216(a)(3) states that OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. But mandatory language alone does not create an entitlement to receive information. The statute imposes a **duty on the agency to act**—it obligates OSC to investigate—but it does not grant AFL (or anyone else) a right to **receive** anything.

The court draws a clear line: a procedural requirement that the government act is not the same as an entitlement that the plaintiff receive information. Section 1216 dictates what OSC must do; it does not say what AFL gets.

**AFL's Theory and Why It Failed**

AFL tried to bootstrap an informational entitlement by arguing that an OSC investigation under section 1216 might trigger the notice provisions of section 1214. But as the court notes, AFL "does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations." Indeed, AFL acknowledged that OSC has **discretion** whether to apply section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation. Because AFL has no entitlement to any notices or information, denying the investigation causes no cognizable informational injury.

**The Practical Test**

To determine whether a statute creates an entitlement to information, ask:

1. Does the statute grant the plaintiff (or a class including the plaintiff) a right to **obtain or access** specific information?
2. Or does it merely require an agency to **take some action** that might involve information-gathering?

Only the first creates informational standing. Section 1216 falls into the second category: it tells OSC what to do, not what AFL gets to have.

### Why is the causal chain too attenuated when it involves independent actors not before the court?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's first standing theory because the causal chain requires speculation about actions by MSPB and DOJ, who aren't parties to the case. The court cites FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine saying causation 'cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.' But doesn't almost any standing analysis involve some speculation about how third parties will react? Where exactly is the line between acceptable causation analysis and impermissible speculation about independent actors?
Answer:
The short answer is that the line turns on whether the third party's actions are **unfettered**—meaning genuinely discretionary and unconstrained—or whether some legal compulsion or predictable mechanism connects the defendant's conduct to the plaintiff's injury.

The court explains the problem with AFL's causal chain this way: Even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, redressing AFL's injury (getting the FOIA records) would require a series of speculative decisions by independent actors:

1. **OSC** would have to decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary action
2. **MSPB** (not a party) would have to decide to sanction DOJ employees
3. **DOJ** (also not a party) would have to decide to produce the records

Each of these actors has **discretion** in how to proceed. OSC has enforcement discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*. MSPB has adjudicatory discretion. DOJ has litigation and compliance discretion. None of them are legally compelled to take any particular action in response to an OSC investigation.

The key distinction, then, is between:
- **Impermissible speculation**: When the third party has genuine, unconstrained discretion ("unfettered choices") and you're simply guessing about how they *might* exercise it
- **Permissible causation**: When the third party is legally compelled to act, or when practical realities make their response sufficiently predictable

The court cites *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the principle that redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" Here, ordering OSC to investigate wouldn't bind MSPB or DOJ to do anything—they would remain free to make their own independent decisions.

This differs from cases where standing is found despite third-party involvement. In those scenarios, the third party's actions are typically constrained by law, contract, or market forces that create a sufficiently reliable causal connection. The court in *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* distinguished between acceptable causal inference based on legal compulsion versus impermissible speculation about "unfettered choices."

The attenuation here is particularly acute because **two** independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ), neither of whom are parties, would each need to exercise their discretion in AFL's favor. That's precisely the kind of speculative chain Article III forbids.

### How does Heckler v. Chaney apply when a statute uses mandatory language like 'shall'?
Question:
The court cites Heckler v. Chaney for the principle that courts generally cannot compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion. But section 1216(a)(3) says OSC 'shall... conduct an investigation' - that sounds mandatory, not discretionary. Does Heckler still apply even when Congress uses mandatory statutory language? Is there a difference between an agency's decision not to investigate at all versus its discretionary choices during an investigation?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is that this opinion does not resolve your question because the court dismisses the case on standing grounds without reaching the merits. However, the opinion's discussion of *Heckler v. Chaney* in the standing analysis is instructive on why mandatory language alone may not eliminate agency discretion.

**The Court Did Not Decide the Merits**

The D.C. Circuit vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of Article III standing. Because the court concluded that AFL lacked standing, it had no occasion to decide whether *Heckler*'s presumption against judicial review of enforcement decisions applies despite Section 1216(a)(3)'s mandatory "shall" language. The court explicitly limited its use of *Heckler* to the standing analysis.

**How the Opinion Uses Heckler**

The court invokes *Heckler* in analyzing whether AFL's claimed injury is redressable—that is, whether a favorable court decision would likely remedy the harm. The court explains that ordering OSC to investigate would still require speculation about "how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." This reasoning suggests that even if Section 1216(a)(3) creates an obligation to investigate, the *manner* of exercising that investigative authority involves discretionary choices that remain unreviewable under *Heckler*.

**The District Court's Merits Reasoning Below**

The district court did reach the question you ask. It held that even though Section 1216(a)(3) uses "shall," Section 1216(c) commits the investigation decision to OSC's discretion because it provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The district court read this "may" language as preserving enforcement discretion, analogous to the discretion inherent in the prohibited personnel practice context. The appellate court's opinion does not endorse or reject this interpretation.

**The Broader Doctrine**

Under *Heckler v. Chaney*, there is a strong presumption that agency decisions not to institute enforcement proceedings are unreviewable. This presumption can be rebutted if Congress has "indicated an intent to circumscribe agency enforcement discretion." However, the presumption operates even when statutory language suggests a duty to act, because courts must still determine what specific enforcement actions are compelled versus what remains discretionary.

**Key Takeaway**

This opinion illustrates a practical consequence of *Heckler*: even when a statute uses mandatory language, the causal chain from a court order to concrete redress may be too speculative because agencies retain discretion over how to conduct investigations and what enforcement actions to pursue. Whether the mandatory "shall" in Section 1216(a)(3) meaningfully limits that discretion remains an open question in this circuit.

### When do relaxed standing requirements apply to procedural injuries?
Question:
The opinion mentions that when plaintiffs allege injury from violation of a procedural right designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, courts relax the imminence and redressability requirements. But the court says AFL doesn't satisfy 'even a relaxed standard' - and also says it need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive. I thought the relaxed standard was specifically FOR procedural injuries. How can the court apply a relaxed standard without first deciding whether the injury is procedural? And what exactly does the plaintiff need to show under this relaxed standard?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important question about the logical structure of the court's standing analysis. The short answer is that the court applied the relaxed standard **assumptionally**—that is, it assumed without deciding that the relaxed standard might apply, and concluded that AFL would still lose even under that more favorable standard.

## The Court's Assumptional Reasoning

The court explicitly stated: *"Because AFL cannot satisfy even the more relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive."*

This is a standard judicial technique. When a plaintiff cannot satisfy even the more lenient standard that *might* apply to their case, the court can resolve the dispute without deciding which standard properly governs. The plaintiff loses either way, so the choice between standards makes no practical difference to the outcome.

Think of it as the court saying: "We'll give you the benefit of the doubt and assume the relaxed standard applies—but you still can't meet it."

## What the Relaxed Standard Requires

The opinion draws from *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education* to explain the relaxed standard:

> *"Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation."*

Under this framework, the plaintiff must show:

1. **A procedural right** afforded to them by statute;
2. **That the procedural right is designed to protect some threatened concrete interest** of the plaintiff; and
3. **That the violation of that procedural right resulted in injury** to that concrete, particularized interest.

The relaxation affects **imminence and redressability** only—elements that are relaxed but not eliminated. The requirements of **injury in fact and causation remain fully applicable**.

## Why AFL Failed Even Under the Relaxed Standard

The court rejected both of AFL's standing theories:

**First theory (denied FOIA records):** The court found fatal causation and redressability problems. The alleged causal chain required speculation about actions by MSPB and DOJ—neither of which are parties to this lawsuit. As the court explained, *"causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."* Moreover, the chain required speculation about how OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion, which courts generally cannot compel under *Heckler v. Chaney*.

**Second theory (information from the OSC investigation):** The court held that section 1216 creates no statutory entitlement to receive information. AFL pointed to notice requirements in section 1214, but as AFL itself acknowledged, OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Absent an entitlement to information, there is no concrete informational injury.

In sum, the relaxed standard does not eliminate the requirement that a plaintiff demonstrate some concrete interest affected by the procedural violation. AFL could not show that an OSC investigation would address either of its asserted concrete interests, so it failed even the more lenient standard that applies to procedural injuries.

### Why can't AFL's ongoing FOIA litigation against DOJ establish redressability?
Question:
AFL's underlying FOIA lawsuit against DOJ is apparently still ongoing, with DOJ producing records. The court says any informational injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. But if OSC investigated and found wrongdoing, couldn't that influence the FOIA litigation or pressure DOJ to produce more? The court mentions that redressability turns on 'how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors not before the court.' But couldn't an OSC finding of arbitrary withholding be legally relevant in the FOIA case?
Answer:
### Answer

The court's analysis turns on a fundamental principle of Article III standing: a federal court's judgment can only bind parties before it, not separate actors in other proceedings.

**The Redressability Problem**

AFL sued OSC, seeking to compel an investigation. But the injury AFL alleges—the withheld FOIA records—is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. The court explains that any judgment here would bind only OSC; it "would not require DOJ to do anything." The causal chain required to connect an OSC investigation to actual document production involves multiple speculative steps:

1. OSC investigates and *chooses* to pursue corrective or disciplinary action
2. The MSPB *decides* to sanction DOJ employees
3. DOJ *responds* by producing the requested records

As the court notes, this requires "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit" and "speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion." Under *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, causation cannot rely on speculation about "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

**Why the Ongoing FOIA Litigation Doesn't Help**

You suggest an OSC finding of arbitrary withholding might be "legally relevant" in the FOIA case. But the court directly addresses this: "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court'" (quoting *Haaland v. Brackeen*).

Even if OSC investigated and found wrongdoing, that finding would not bind the district court presiding over the FOIA litigation. The FOIA court makes its own determination about whether DOJ's withholding was lawful. An OSC finding might be persuasive, but it cannot compel DOJ to produce records—and Article III redressability requires that a favorable judgment would "very likely" redress the injury, not merely that it might have persuasive value in a separate case.

**The Procedural Rights Exception Doesn't Apply**

AFL invoked the relaxed standing standard for procedural rights, but the court rejected this because section 1216 "creates no entitlement to receive information." Unlike statutes that grant plaintiffs a right to obtain information, section 1216 merely requires OSC to investigate—it doesn't entitle AFL to any particular outcome or disclosure.

In short: AFL's ongoing FOIA case against DOJ cannot establish redressability against OSC because DOJ remains an independent actor, and nothing in the court's judgment here could compel DOJ to act.

### What is the relationship between section 1216 investigations and section 1214 notice requirements?
Question:
AFL argued that if OSC investigated under section 1216, it might provide the notices required under section 1214. The court notes that AFL 'acknowledges cases holding that OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary.' Does this mean section 1216 investigations are procedurally different from prohibited personnel practice investigations under section 1214? If OSC investigates a FOIA violation under 1216, what procedural rights (if any) does a complainant have?
Answer:
### Answer

Based on the court's opinion, section 1216 investigations and section 1214 investigations are procedurally distinct in a key respect: **the notice and procedural requirements that apply to section 1214 investigations are discretionary when OSC investigates under section 1216.**

The opinion explains the statutory framework clearly:

- **Section 1214** governs investigations into "prohibited personnel practices" and includes specific procedural requirements, such as "provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation."

- **Section 1216** governs investigations into other forms of misconduct, including FOIA violations. Under § 1216(c), OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved."

The critical word is "may." The court notes that AFL "acknowledges cases holding that OSC's 'decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements' in a section 1216 investigation is 'discretionary,'" citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). The court emphasizes that AFL "does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations."

Thus, if OSC investigates a FOIA violation under § 1216, **a complainant has no entitlement to the notice and information requirements that would apply in a § 1214 prohibited personnel practice investigation.** Any such procedural protections are within OSC's discretion to provide.

This distinction matters for standing purposes. AFL could not establish an informational injury based on a right to receive § 1214 notices because, as the court held, "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory."

### Why didn't the court reach the merits of whether section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits OSC's authority?
Question:
The district court held that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) doesn't limit OSC's power to investigate under section 1216, and that OSC's refusal to investigate was based on legal error. But the appeals court never addresses this - it just finds standing lacking. I thought courts were supposed to avoid constitutional questions when possible, but here the court reaches the constitutional standing issue instead of the statutory interpretation question. Isn't the statutory question about whether OSC misinterpreted its authority potentially dispositive?
Answer:
The court didn't reach the statutory merits because it couldn't—standing is a **jurisdictional prerequisite**, not an optional threshold.

## Jurisdiction Comes First

Article III standing goes to whether a federal court has subject-matter jurisdiction at all. As the opinion explains (quoting *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*), standing "bear[s] on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction." 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998).

The court had no power to decide the statutory question unless AFL first established standing. The sequence is rigid:

1. **Does the court have jurisdiction?** (standing, subject-matter jurisdiction)
2. **If yes, reach the merits.**
3. **On the merits, avoid constitutional questions when possible.**

You're thinking of step 3—the constitutional avoidance doctrine. But that doctrine only applies *after* the court confirms it has jurisdiction. Step 1 is mandatory and non-negotiable.

## The Court Explicitly States This

The opinion says plainly: "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing." The word "must" is doing important work here. This isn't discretion or judicial preference—it's a structural requirement of Article III.

## Why This Matters Practically

The district court got this wrong. It reached the merits (holding that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) doesn't limit OSC's § 1216 authority) without first confirming standing. The D.C. Circuit corrected that error, vacating the judgment and remanding "with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction."

A court cannot simply skip to a potentially dispositive statutory question because it seems efficient. If the plaintiff lacks standing, the court has no authority to say anything about the merits—right or wrong.
