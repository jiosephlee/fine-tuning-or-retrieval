Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## The Office of Special Counsel: Purpose and Function Within the Federal Civil Service System

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is an independent investigative and prosecutorial agency within the federal government. Congress established OSC to advance the goal of a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The agency's primary authority comes from the Civil Service Reform Act, which empowers OSC to investigate "prohibited personnel practices"—improper employment actions taken against federal employees—and to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). When OSC identifies a prohibited personnel practice, it may seek corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 (such as reinstatement of a wrongfully terminated employee) or disciplinary action under 5 U.S.C. § 1215 (such as suspension or removal of the responsible official). Section 1214 also includes procedural protections for complainants, including requirements that OSC provide notice about the progress of an investigation.

Beyond personnel matters, Congress has given OSC authority to investigate certain other forms of misconduct by government employees. Most relevant to this case, Section 1216 of the Act addresses violations of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). Specifically, Section 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC "shall . . . conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings that violate FOIA. If OSC finds such a violation, it may pursue corrective or disciplinary action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." This statutory language makes Section 1216 the mechanism by which OSC enforces compliance with FOIA's disclosure requirements through the civil service system.

FOIA itself contains a separate enforcement provision that implicates OSC. Under Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), when a court orders production of improperly withheld records, awards attorneys' fees against the agency, and "specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." This provision creates a mandatory duty for OSC—but only when three specific predicates are satisfied: a court order for production, an award of attorneys' fees, and an express judicial finding questioning arbitrary or capricious conduct. The relationship between this provision and Section 1216's more general investigatory mandate lies at the heart of this dispute.

## AFL's FOIA Request to DOJ and Its Allegations of Arbitrary Withholding

America First Legal Foundation (AFL), a nonprofit organization, submitted a FOIA request to the Department of Justice as part of what the opinion describes as "its effort to investigate judicial recusals." Specifically, AFL sought emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division. According to AFL, the Civil Division responded with what AFL considered a "dubious ground" for denial: the Division claimed it does not search employee emails for responsive records without the employee's consent. AFL viewed this response as evidence of an arbitrary policy that violated FOIA's requirements.

AFL then filed suit against DOJ under FOIA to compel production of the requested records. Importantly, during that separate lawsuit, DOJ began producing responsive records and continued to do so. The opinion notes that "the sufficiency of this production is not at issue" in the present case. Thus, AFL's underlying FOIA request was being satisfied through traditional FOIA litigation against the agency that possessed the records.

## AFL's Complaint to OSC Invoking Section 1216

Separate from its FOIA lawsuit against DOJ, AFL also filed a complaint with OSC. AFL asked OSC to investigate what it alleged was DOJ's "FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent." In support of this request, AFL invoked Section 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall . . . conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. AFL's theory was that Section 1216 creates a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding—regardless of whether the other statutory triggers in FOIA's own enforcement provision had been satisfied.

This argument represented an aggressive reading of Section 1216. If accepted, it would mean that any person who believed an agency had arbitrarily withheld FOIA records could compel OSC to open an investigation, even without first obtaining a court order finding wrongdoing. AFL was essentially asking the court to interpret Section 1216 as creating an independent, self-executing enforcement mechanism parallel to—but not dependent on—the procedures set forth in FOIA itself.

## OSC's Refusal to Investigate Based on Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) Predicates

OSC declined to investigate AFL's allegations. The agency took the position that it lacked authority to investigate because the three predicates required by Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been satisfied. Recall that provision requires a court to have: (1) ordered production of improperly withheld records, (2) awarded attorneys' fees, and (3) specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. None of these conditions had occurred in AFL's FOIA litigation against DOJ.

OSC's position, therefore, was that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) defines the exclusive circumstances under which it must investigate FOIA violations. AFL disagreed, arguing that Section 1216 mandated an investigation into its allegations even if the FOIA enforcement provision's requirements were not met. This dispute presented a pure question of statutory interpretation: Does Section 1216 create an independent obligation to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, or does Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) cabin that authority by specifying the only circumstances triggering an investigation?

## District Court's Ruling: Legal Error by OSC but Discretionary Authority Under Section 1216(c)

AFL filed suit against OSC (then headed by Acting Special Counsel Kerner) in federal district court to compel an investigation. The district court ruled in part for AFL, agreeing with AFL's interpretation of the statutory relationship. The court concluded that "Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216." In other words, OSC had committed legal error by treating the FOIA provision's predicates as limiting its Section 1216 authority.

However, this partial victory for AFL did not translate into the ultimate relief it sought. The district court declined to order OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy. The court reasoned that Section 1216(c)—which provides that OSC may investigate FOIA violations "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved"—committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion. Even though OSC had misinterpreted the scope of its authority, the court concluded that the ultimate decision to investigate remained discretionary rather than mandatory.

Having found that OSC had discretion over whether to investigate, the district court remanded the case to OSC for further consideration. This procedural posture is significant: the court found legal error in OSC's reasoning but nonetheless determined that the statute did not compel the particular remedy AFL sought.

## OSC's Second Refusal After Remand and Final Judgment

On remand, OSC again declined to investigate AFL's allegations. The opinion does not specify the grounds for this second refusal, but the result was clear: OSC, having reconsidered in light of the district court's ruling, still would not open an investigation into DOJ's FOIA practices. Following this second refusal, AFL moved for entry of final judgment in the district court, presumably seeking to preserve its right to appeal the adverse ruling.

The district court dismissed AFL's remaining claims, entering final judgment against AFL. This dismissal ended the litigation in the district court and set the stage for AFL's appeal to the D.C. Circuit. The procedural posture on appeal was a final judgment dismissing AFL's claims for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.

## Appeal and the Court's Decision to Order Supplemental Briefing on Standing

AFL appealed the district court's dismissal to the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. The standard of review for a district court's dismissal is de novo, meaning the appellate court reviews the legal conclusions afresh without deference to the district court's reasoning.

Significantly, neither party initially addressed the question of standing in their appellate briefs. Standing—the requirement that a plaintiff demonstrate a concrete injury causally connected to the defendant's conduct that a favorable court decision can redress—is a jurisdictional prerequisite. Federal courts lack subject-matter jurisdiction over cases where the plaintiff lacks standing. The Supreme Court has held that courts must address standing before reaching the merits of a dispute.

Because standing is jurisdictional and the parties had not briefed the issue, the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing. This order signaled that the court had identified a potential jurisdictional problem that could resolve the case without reaching the merits of the statutory interpretation dispute. Indeed, as the opinion ultimately demonstrates, standing became the dispositive issue—the court never reached the merits of whether Section 1216 mandates an investigation or whether the district court correctly interpreted the statutory scheme. Instead, the court concluded that AFL lacked standing to pursue its claims, requiring dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# The Statutory Framework Governing OSC's Authority

## OSC's General Authority to Investigate Prohibited Personnel Practices under the Civil Service Reform Act

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial agency within the federal government. Congress established OSC pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act with a mandate to pursue "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The agency's core mission involves investigating what the Act terms "prohibited personnel practices"—improper actions by federal agencies affecting government employees. When OSC uncovers evidence of such practices, it may initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), an administrative tribunal that adjudicates federal employment disputes. The Act empowers OSC to seek two distinct forms of relief from the MSPB: corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, which aims to remedy harm to affected individuals, and disciplinary action under 5 U.S.C. § 1215, which punishes wrongdoing by agency officials.

This dual-track enforcement structure—corrective and disciplinary—forms the backbone of OSC's operational authority. The corrective track focuses on making whole any employee who suffered from a prohibited personnel practice, while the disciplinary track addresses culpable officials through sanctions. Understanding this framework is essential because, as we shall see, Congress extended this same enforcement architecture to certain Freedom of Information Act violations through Section 1216.

## Section 1216(a)(3): Mandatory Investigation of Arbitrary FOIA Withdrawings

Section 1216 expands OSC's jurisdiction beyond traditional personnel matters to encompass certain forms of misconduct by government employees. Most relevant to this case, Section 1216(a)(3) addresses FOIA compliance. The statute provides that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings that violate FOIA's disclosure requirements.

The statutory language deserves careful attention. The word "shall" in Section 1216(a)(3) suggests a mandatory duty: upon receiving an allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, OSC must investigate. This mandatory language became the centerpiece of AFL's legal theory. AFL argued that because the statute uses "shall" rather than "may," OSC lacks discretion to decline investigation when presented with a colorable allegation of arbitrary withholding. The word "arbitrary or capricious" in the statute mirrors the standard from Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), suggesting Congress intended OSC to investigate precisely the type of conduct at issue in this case.

## Section 1216(c): OSC's Authority to Seek Corrective and Disciplinary Action for FOIA Violations

Section 1216(c) specifies what happens after OSC investigates an alleged FOIA violation. It provides that for such violations, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." This provision creates a bridge between FOIA enforcement and OSC's preexisting enforcement machinery.

Several aspects of Section 1216(c) warrant attention. First, the statute uses "may" rather than "shall," suggesting that the decision to pursue enforcement after investigation lies within OSC's discretion. Second, the statute makes Section 1214 and Section 1215—the corrective and disciplinary tracks—available for FOIA violations just as they are for prohibited personnel practices. This means that if OSC elects to pursue enforcement, it can seek both remedies for the aggrieved requester (corrective action) and sanctions against responsible officials (disciplinary action). Third, by referencing the MSPB process, Congress integrated FOIA enforcement into the existing administrative framework rather than creating a separate remedial scheme.

## Section 1214: Procedures for Corrective Actions and Notification Requirements

Section 1214 establishes procedural requirements governing corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices. The statute includes specific provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation. For example, Section 1214(a)(1)(B) through (D) detail various notification requirements that keep complainants informed as OSC processes their allegations.

These notification provisions became relevant to AFL's standing argument. AFL contended that OSC's refusal to investigate deprived AFL of information it would have received had OSC proceeded under Section 1214's procedural requirements. However, as the court noted, Section 1216 investigations are not automatically subject to Section 1214's notification requirements. The relationship between these provisions—whether Section 1214's procedures apply in Section 1216 investigations—remains a contested legal question that factored into the standing analysis.

## Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i): FOIA's Separate Enforcement Provision Requiring Court Order Before OSC Disciplinary Proceedings

FOIA contains its own enforcement mechanism that operates independently of Section 1216. Under Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), OSC must initiate a disciplinary proceeding when three specific conditions are met. First, a court must have ordered production of agency records that were improperly withheld under FOIA. Second, the court must have awarded attorneys' fees against the agency. Third, the court must have "specifically question[ed] whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding."

Only when all three predicates are satisfied does Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) require OSC to "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." This provision represents Congress's decision to tether FOIA enforcement through OSC to prior judicial involvement. The statute creates a gatekeeping role for federal courts: only after a court has found a violation, awarded fees, and specifically questioned the agency's conduct does the mandatory OSC proceeding begin.

The three-predicate structure reveals Congress's careful calibration of this enforcement mechanism. Mere success in FOIA litigation is insufficient; the court must take the additional step of questioning whether the agency's conduct was arbitrary or capricious. This higher threshold suggests Congress intended Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to address only the most egregious FOIA violations—those warranting judicial reproach beyond mere findings of improper withholding.

## The Relationship Between Section 1216 and Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i): Competing Interpretations of OSC's Authority

The central interpretive dispute in this case concerns how Section 1216 relates to Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The parties advanced starkly different readings of these overlapping provisions.

OSC maintained that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) establishes the exclusive trigger for its FOIA enforcement authority. Under this view, OSC may investigate FOIA violations only after a court has satisfied all three predicates—the production order, the fee award, and the specific questioning of arbitrary conduct. Because none of these predicates had occurred in AFL's case—DOJ was producing records through ongoing litigation, and no court had yet questioned arbitrary conduct—OSC concluded it lacked authority to investigate AFL's allegation.

AFL countered that Section 1216(a)(3) operates independently of Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL emphasized the mandatory "shall" language in Section 1216(a)(3), arguing that this provision creates a freestanding obligation on OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, regardless of whether the Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates have been satisfied. Under AFL's reading, the two statutes address different circumstances: Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) mandates OSC action after certain judicial findings, while Section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate allegations even without prior court involvement.

The district court adopted something of a middle position. It concluded that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under Section 1216—a ruling in AFL's favor on the legal question. The court therefore held that OSC's refusal to investigate rested on a legal error. However, the district court ultimately declined to order OSC to investigate, reasoning that Section 1216(c) committed such decisions to agency discretion. The court remanded to OSC for further consideration, and when OSC again declined to investigate, the district court dismissed AFL's remaining claims, prompting this appeal.

As the D.C. Circuit noted, the court did not need to resolve this statutory interpretation question because AFL lacked Article III standing regardless. The jurisdictional defect precluded reaching the merits of whether Section 1216 mandated an investigation. Nevertheless, understanding the parties' competing interpretations remains essential to grasping why this lawsuit proceeded as it did and how the standing question ultimately proved dispositive.

# Article III Standing Doctrine and Procedural Injury

## Article III's Case-or-Controversy Requirement and Its Constitutional Basis

Article III of the United States Constitution limits the federal "judicial Power" to resolving certain "Cases" and "Controversies." This constitutional limitation, found in Article III, Section 2, Clause 1, serves as the foundation for standing doctrine. As the Supreme Court explained in *Allen v. Wright*, standing doctrine flows directly from this case-or-controversy requirement. The purpose of this limitation is to ensure that federal courts address only actual, ongoing disputes between adverse parties, rather than issuing advisory opinions on abstract questions of law.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit confronted a threshold jurisdictional question that the parties had not initially addressed. Before reaching the merits of AFL's claims, the court was obligated to determine whether AFL had satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, which bears directly on whether the district court possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The court invoked the principle from *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment* that courts must address standing before proceeding to the merits. Neither party had initially briefed the standing issue, so the court ordered supplemental briefing—a demonstration of how standing serves as an essential gateway question that courts must resolve regardless of whether the parties raise it.

## The Three Elements of Standing: Injury in Fact, Causation, and Redressability

To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate three core elements: the plaintiff must have suffered an injury that is caused by the defendant and is likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. The Supreme Court articulated this tripartite framework in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, and the D.C. Circuit applied it methodically to AFL's claims. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff need not prove these elements but must allege facts that plausibly support each one.

The court's analysis of AFL's standing illustrates how these three elements operate in practice. AFL needed to show that it suffered a concrete injury, that this injury was fairly traceable to OSC's conduct, and that a favorable court decision would likely remedy the injury. The court examined each of AFL's theories and found them wanting, particularly on causation and redressability grounds. The analysis demonstrates that standing is not a mere formality but a substantive constitutional requirement that filters which disputes federal courts may entertain.

## Requirements for Injury in Fact: Concrete, Particularized, Actual or Imminent

The injury-in-fact requirement demands that a plaintiff demonstrate "an invasion of a legally protected interest" that is both "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." The concreteness requirement ensures that the plaintiff has suffered a real harm rather than an abstract grievance. Particularization requires that the injury affect the plaintiff in a personal and individual way. The actual-or-imminent requirement prevents courts from adjudicating disputes based on speculative or hypothetical future harms.

In this case, AFL attempted to articulate two informational injuries that it claimed were concrete and particularized. First, AFL pointed to DOJ's failure to provide the records requested through FOIA. Second, AFL invoked the notices and information that OSC would have provided had it conducted an investigation under section 1216. The court's analysis of these alleged injuries reveals how the concreteness requirement operates when plaintiffs claim injury from the denial of a statutorily created procedure.

## Procedural Injury Doctrine: When Violation of a Procedural Right Can Confer Standing

AFL grounded its standing argument in its alleged procedural right to an OSC investigation under section 1216. However, the court rejected this approach, citing the Supreme Court's teaching in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer Article III standing. This principle prevents plaintiffs from manufacturing standing simply by citing a statutory requirement that some government action be taken, without showing how the plaintiff is personally harmed by the failure to act.

The procedural injury doctrine addresses a fundamental tension: if a statute requires an agency to take some action, and the agency fails to act, does any citizen have standing to sue? The answer is no. Something more is required—a concrete interest of the plaintiff that is threatened by the procedural violation. The court explained that the procedural right must be "designed to protect their threatened concrete interest." This requirement ensures that plaintiffs invoking procedural injuries are not merely seeking to enforce the law in the abstract but are vindicating their own legitimate stake in the outcome.

## Relaxed Standards for Imminence and Redressability in Procedural Injury Cases

The court invoked an important doctrinal refinement for cases involving procedural injury. When a plaintiff alleges injury from the violation of a procedural statutory right that is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest, courts apply a more relaxed standard for imminence and redressability—but not for injury in fact or causation. The D.C. Circuit drew this principle from its decision in *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education* and the en banc decision in *Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen*.

This relaxation makes practical sense. When a statute requires an agency to follow certain procedures before taking action that might harm the plaintiff, it would be unfair to require the plaintiff to show with precision how the procedural violation caused or would cause specific harm. However, the court emphasized that this relaxation has limits. Plaintiffs still must demonstrate that the procedural violation resulted in injury to a "concrete, particularized interest." AFL's failure to satisfy even these relaxed standards proved fatal to its case.

## Informational Injury: Standing Based on Denial of Information to Which Plaintiff Is Statutorily Entitled

AFL's second standing theory invoked informational injury—the harm suffered when a plaintiff is denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive. The court explained that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the requested information. Citing cases including *FEC v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, the court noted that plaintiffs have standing when they are denied information that a statute requires the government to provide.

This doctrine is distinct from the harm alleged in a typical FOIA case, where the plaintiff seeks specific agency records. Here, AFL was not seeking records from OSC but rather seeking to compel OSC to investigate DOJ's FOIA practices. AFL argued that if OSC conducted an investigation under section 1216, it might provide AFL with notices and information similar to what section 1214 requires for investigations of prohibited personnel practices. However, the court rejected this theory because section 1216 itself creates no entitlement to receive information. Moreover, AFL acknowledged that OSC's decision whether to apply section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, AFL could not establish the concrete informational injury necessary for standing.

## The Requirement That Procedural Rights Must Be Tied to a Concrete Interest

The court's resolution of this case turns on the fundamental principle that procedural rights alone cannot generate standing. AFL invoked section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. But AFL could not identify a concrete interest that the procedural right was designed to protect. The court found both of AFL's proffered interests inadequate. The first—the interest in obtaining records from DOJ—suffered from fatal causation and redressability defects because the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ production of records was too speculative and attenuated. The second—the interest in information from the OSC investigation—failed because no statute entitled AFL to receive such information.

The court's causation analysis is particularly instructive. AFL argued that if OSC had investigated, it might have pursued corrective or disciplinary action, the Merit Systems Protection Board might have sanctioned DOJ employees, and DOJ then might have produced the records. The court rejected this speculative chain, citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the principle that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The court also invoked *Heckler v. Chaney* for the proposition that courts generally may not compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion. Because AFL's standing depended on speculation about decisions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—all involving discretion at each step—AFL could not establish that its injury was caused by OSC or would be redressed by a favorable judgment against OSC. The court therefore concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing, vacated the district court's judgment, and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Application: AFL's Standing Theories and the Court's Analysis

Having established the framework for Article III standing, the court turns to apply these principles to AFL's specific claims. This section of the opinion demonstrates how standing doctrine operates in practice and why even plausible grievances may fail to meet constitutional thresholds for federal court jurisdiction.

## AFL's First Theory: Injury from Not Receiving FOIA Records from DOJ

AFL's first theory of standing connects its alleged injury to the underlying FOIA request that prompted this litigation. AFL claimed that because OSC failed to investigate DOJ's allegedly arbitrary FOIA withholding, AFL was denied access to the records it had requested from DOJ. Framing this as an informational injury, AFL argued that OSC's inaction caused it harm that a court order compelling an investigation could redress.

This theory illustrates an important principle in standing analysis: the party who causes an injury is not always the party best positioned to redress it through litigation. AFL sought to sue OSC for an injury—the withholding of records—that DOJ, a separate agency, had directly caused. The court recognized this mismatch as a fundamental problem for AFL's standing theory.

## Causation Problems: Tracing the Injury to DOJ Rather than OSC

The court identified a straightforward causation defect in AFL's first theory: "any informational injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." This statement reflects the requirement that a plaintiff's injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, not merely connected to it in some attenuated sense.

The traceability requirement, sometimes called causation, demands that the plaintiff establish a causal connection between the injury and the conduct complained of. Here, AFL sued OSC, but DOJ was the agency that denied the FOIA request. AFL's theory thus required the court to trace the injury back from DOJ's refusal, through OSC's failure to investigate, to OSC's alleged statutory violation. The court found this connection "too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."

## Redressability Problems: The Speculative Causal Chain Involving OSC, MSPB, and DOJ

Even if OSC had caused AFL's injury in some sense, the court found that AFL could not establish redressability—that a favorable court decision would likely remedy the harm. The court laid out the causal chain that AFL's theory would require:

> If OSC had conducted the assertedly mandatory section 1216 investigation, it might have decided to pursue a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action under section 1215, against offending DOJ employees. Then, the MSPB might have decided to sanction those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Next, DOJ might have decided to produce the requested records.

Each step in this chain involves discretionary decisions by actors who are not parties to the lawsuit. OSC must decide whether to pursue enforcement action; the MSPB must decide whether to impose sanctions; and DOJ must decide whether to produce records in response. The court emphasized that this speculative sequence involves "choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit."

## The Principle That Courts Cannot Rely on Speculation About Independent Actors Not Before the Court

The court grounded its redressability analysis in a fundamental principle of standing doctrine. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court stated that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

This principle serves an important jurisdictional function. Federal courts exist to resolve concrete disputes between adverse parties, not to issue advisory opinions about how third parties might respond to a court's reasoning. When redressability depends on actions by non-parties, a plaintiff cannot demonstrate that a court judgment will actually remedy the alleged harm.

The court further noted that "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" This distinction between binding judgments and persuasive reasoning marks the boundary between justiciable controversies and advisory opinions.

## Heckler v. Chaney and Enforcement Discretion: Courts Generally May Not Compel Agency Action

The court's redressability analysis drew on an additional principle from administrative law. Citing *Heckler v. Chaney*, the court observed that its analysis "requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."

*Heckler v. Chaney* established that an agency's decision not to take enforcement action is presumptively unreviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act. This presumption reflects both the agency's comparative expertise in allocating enforcement resources and the practical reality that enforcement decisions often involve complex policy judgments unsuitable for judicial resolution. The court invoked this principle to highlight another layer of speculation in AFL's theory: even if ordered to investigate, OSC would retain discretion over whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action.

## AFL's Second Theory: Informational Injury from Not Receiving Investigation Notices

AFL's second standing theory shifted focus from the underlying FOIA records to the investigation itself. AFL argued that if OSC conducted a section 1216 investigation, AFL might receive notices and information that OSC provides when investigating "prohibited personnel practices" under section 1214. This theory frames the injury as a denial of information to which AFL claims entitlement.

The court approached this theory through the framework of informational standing. A plaintiff claiming informational injury must show that a statute entitled them to receive the information they were denied. Without such an entitlement, the claimed injury remains abstract and insufficiently concrete.

## Why Section 1216 Does Not Create a Statutory Entitlement to Information

The court found AFL's second theory defective because section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, does not create an entitlement to information. The court stated: "here, even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information."

This holding illustrates a critical distinction in informational standing doctrine. Not every statute that requires agency action creates individual rights that plaintiffs can enforce in court. The court must examine whether the statute was "designed to protect" the plaintiff's concrete interests and whether it provides a specific entitlement to information. Section 1216's mandatory language—"shall...conduct an investigation"—addresses OSC's obligations but says nothing about what information, if any, must be provided to complainants.

## The Discretionary Nature of Section 1214 Notice Requirements in Section 1216 Investigations

AFL attempted to salvage its second theory by arguing that a section 1216 investigation might trigger section 1214's notice requirements. Section 1214 requires OSC to provide certain notices and information to complainants when investigating prohibited personnel practices. AFL contended that these requirements might apply by extension to section 1216 investigations.

The court rejected this argument by pointing to precedent acknowledging that "OSC's 'decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements' in a section 1216 investigation is 'discretionary.'" The court cited the Sixth Circuit's decision in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, which held that applying section 1214's notice provisions to section 1216 investigations is not mandatory but rather within OSC's discretion.

Because OSC has discretion over whether to provide section 1214 notices during a section 1216 investigation, AFL could not claim an entitlement to such information. And without an entitlement, there is no concrete informational injury. The court concluded that "absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory."

## Why AFL Failed Under Both Ordinary and Relaxed Standing Standards

Before analyzing AFL's specific theories, the court noted that AFL sought to invoke "relaxed standing requirements that apply to cases involving alleged procedural injury." Under this relaxed approach, plaintiffs alleging violation of a procedural right must show that the violation caused injury to some "concrete, particularized interest." The relaxation affects imminence and redressability but not injury in fact or causation.

The court ultimately found that AFL's claims failed under both ordinary and relaxed standards. Even assuming that section 1216 created a procedural right to an investigation, AFL could not identify a concrete interest harmed by the deprivation of that right. AFL's two asserted concrete interests—the FOIA records and the investigation notices—both failed for the reasons discussed above. Because AFL could not establish the requisite concrete interest, the court concluded it "need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive."

The court's analysis demonstrates the relationship between procedural rights and concrete injury. A procedural right alone, without connection to some concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect, cannot support Article III standing. As the court noted, quoting *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, "the 'deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient' to confer Article III standing."

# Disposition and Significance

## The Court's Holding: AFL Lacks Article III Standing

The D.C. Circuit concluded that America First Legal Foundation lacked Article III standing to pursue its claims against the Office of Special Counsel. This holding resolved the case entirely on jurisdictional grounds, without reaching the merits of AFL's statutory arguments. The court found that AFL could not satisfy either of its two standing theories: the informational injury allegedly caused by DOJ's failure to produce records, and the informational injury allegedly caused by OSC's failure to provide notices and updates about an investigation.

Both theories failed for related reasons. The first theory—the core of AFL's claim—suffered from "fatal causation and redressability problems." Any informational injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose records was traceable to DOJ, not OSC. More fundamentally, the causal chain connecting an OSC investigation to DOJ's production of records was too speculative and attenuated. The court explained that this chain would require speculation about choices by multiple actors not before the court: OSC's exercise of enforcement discretion, the Merit Systems Protection Board's potential sanctions, and DOJ's ultimate decision to produce records. The second theory failed because section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no statutory entitlement to receive information. AFL could not point to any legal requirement that OSC provide it with notices or information during a section 1216 investigation.

## Vacatur of the District Court's Judgment and Remand

Having concluded that AFL lacked standing, the court vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This disposition follows directly from the jurisdictional nature of standing doctrine. Article III standing goes to the federal court's power to hear a case; without it, the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction entirely.

The court's use of vacatur is significant. Rather than simply affirming or reversing the district court's decision, the court wiped the lower court's judgment from the books. This makes sense because the district court should not have reached the merits at all—it lacked jurisdiction to do so. The remand with instructions to dismiss ensures that the case ends at the trial court level on proper jurisdictional grounds.

The standard of review merits brief mention. The court noted that it reviews the dismissal de novo, meaning it considers the standing question afresh without deference to the district court's analysis. This is the standard approach for questions of law, including jurisdictional questions.

## The Importance of Addressing Standing Before Reaching the Merits

The court's opinion exemplifies a fundamental principle of federal jurisdiction: courts must address standing before reaching the merits. The opinion opens its analysis by stating that "before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing." This ordering is not a matter of judicial preference but of constitutional command.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment* establishes that federal courts lack authority to resolve the merits of a case when the plaintiff lacks standing. This principle follows from the case-or-controversy limitation in Article III, which restricts the federal judicial power to actual disputes between proper parties. The D.C. Circuit properly treated standing as a threshold question that it was "bound to enforce" even when the parties had not raised it.

Notably, neither party initially addressed standing in this case. The court therefore ordered supplemental briefing on the question. This judicial initiative reflects the court's independent obligation to ensure subject-matter jurisdiction exists before proceeding. The parties' silence on standing did not waive the requirement—indeed, parties cannot waive Article III limitations on federal judicial power.

## Implications for Future Cases Seeking to Compel OSC Investigations

This decision clarifies important limitations on using section 1216 as an alternative enforcement mechanism for FOIA rights. Litigants cannot circumvent FOIA's direct enforcement provisions by petitioning OSC to investigate and then suing to compel that investigation. The court's analysis reveals why this strategy fails as a jurisdictional matter: the connection between an OSC investigation and the ultimate disclosure of records is simply too attenuated to support standing.

The opinion also highlights the distinction between sections 1216 and 1214 of the Civil Service Reform Act. While section 1214—governing prohibited personnel practices—includes provisions for notifying complaining parties about investigation progress, section 1216—governing FOIA violations—contains no comparable notice requirements. AFL attempted to argue that OSC might provide section 1214 notices during a section 1216 investigation, but the court correctly noted that OSC's decision to do so is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to information, AFL could not establish the concrete informational injury needed for standing.

Future litigants should take careful note of the court's skepticism about "procedural rights in vacuo." Citing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, the court emphasized that the deprivation of a procedural right, without some concrete interest affected by that deprivation, is insufficient for Article III standing. Even if section 1216 creates a procedural right to an investigation, AFL needed to show that the denial of that procedure harmed some concrete interest—which it failed to do.

## The Separation-of-Powers Dimension: Courts Cannot Compel Agency Enforcement

Underlying much of the court's standing analysis is a fundamental separation-of-powers principle: courts generally cannot compel agencies to exercise their enforcement discretion. The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, the leading Supreme Court case establishing that agency decisions not to enforce are presumptively unreviewable. This presumption reflects the complex policy and resource considerations involved in enforcement decisions—considerations that agencies, not courts, are institutionally equipped to make.

The court's causation analysis incorporates this principle. AFL's theory required speculation about "how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." Even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, OSC would retain discretion about whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action. The court refused to assume that OSC would take enforcement action following an investigation, just as it refused to assume that the MSPB would sanction DOJ employees or that DOJ would produce records in response.

The court also cited recent Supreme Court authority reinforcing that causation analysis cannot rely on speculation about choices by independent actors not before the court. In *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the Supreme Court rejected standing theories that required assuming how third parties would respond to court rulings. Similarly, in *Haaland v. Brackeen*, the Court explained that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade non-parties. These principles protect courts from being drawn into disputes where their judgments cannot provide meaningful relief.

## Practical Considerations for Litigants Seeking OSC Enforcement of FOIA Rights

Practitioners should understand that this case effectively closes the door on using section 1216 as a litigation strategy for compelling FOIA compliance. FOIA itself contains comprehensive enforcement provisions that allow requesters to sue agencies directly in federal court for wrongful withholding of records. The statute also provides for attorneys' fee awards and, in limited circumstances, referral to OSC for potential disciplinary proceedings. But FOIA's judicial enforcement mechanism remains the primary avenue for relief.

The opinion underscores an important strategic point: even if a statute uses mandatory language like "shall" to describe an agency's duty, this does not automatically create a private right of action or establish standing. Section 1216(a)(3) states that OSC "shall...conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Yet the court found that AFL lacked standing to enforce this provision because it could not demonstrate that an investigation would redress its informational injury.

Litigants should also recognize the limits of "informational injury" as a basis for standing. While courts have recognized that denial of information required by statute can constitute a concrete injury, this requires a statutory entitlement to receive that information. Here, AFL could point to no provision requiring OSC to provide it with information about a section 1216 investigation. The absence of such an entitlement proved fatal to AFL's second standing theory.

# Procedural Posture and Judicial Review Standards

Understanding how this case reached the D.C. Circuit—and what standards govern review at each stage—is essential to following the court's analysis. The procedural history shapes what questions the appellate court could consider and how it approached them. This chapter walks through the case's journey through the courts and explains the standards that controlled each phase of review.

## Motion to Dismiss Stage: Pleading Requirements for Standing

This case reached the D.C. Circuit after the district court dismissed AFL's claims for failure to state a claim under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). At the motion-to-dismiss stage, courts assess whether the plaintiff has alleged enough facts to make its claims plausible, accepting all well-pleaded facts as true. But standing—the plaintiff's qualification to bring suit—operates differently.

Standing is a jurisdictional requirement rooted in Article III of the Constitution. A plaintiff must have suffered an injury that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." The injury must be caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable court decision. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each of these elements. Here, AFL's standing was not seriously challenged below, and the district court reached the merits—ultimately dismissing the case because it concluded that even if OSC had erred legally, the decision to investigate was committed to OSC's discretion.

## De Novo Review Standard on Appeal

When AFL appealed the district court's dismissal, the D.C. Circuit reviewed the decision de novo. De novo review means the appellate court considers the legal questions afresh, giving no deference to the district court's conclusions. The court reviews the dismissal "accepting the factual allegations in the complaint as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor." This standard applies both to the merits of a failure-to-state-a-claim dismissal and to questions of standing.

The de novo standard matters here because it meant the D.C. Circuit was free to reach its own conclusions about whether AFL had adequately pleaded standing—even though the district court had not confronted that issue directly and had instead resolved the case on the merits.

## The Court's Authority to Raise Standing Issues Sua Sponte

Neither party addressed standing in their initial briefs on appeal. The court nonetheless raised the issue on its own motion—described in legal Latin as *sua sponte*—and ordered supplemental briefing. Federal courts have an independent obligation to ensure they have subject-matter jurisdiction before proceeding to the merits. Because standing is jurisdictional, courts must verify it exists even if the parties do not raise the question.

The Supreme Court has emphasized that standing "cannot be inferred argumentatively from the complaint" but must be affirmatively demonstrated. If a court lacks jurisdiction, it cannot proceed to decide the merits. The D.C. Circuit's decision to flag the standing issue reflects this bedrock principle: jurisdiction comes first.

## Supplemental Briefing Ordered by the Court

After identifying standing as a potential problem, the court ordered both parties to submit supplemental briefing on the question. This procedure ensures that the parties have a fair opportunity to address a jurisdictional issue the court has identified before the court decides it. Supplemental briefing is especially appropriate when, as here, the issue was not raised below or in the initial appellate briefs.

The supplemental briefs allowed AFL to articulate its theories of standing and the government to respond. AFL argued that it had suffered two informational injuries: first, from DOJ's failure to produce the requested records; and second, from OSC's failure to provide notices and information during an investigation. The court evaluated these arguments carefully before concluding that neither satisfied Article III requirements.

## Why the Court Addressed Standing Before Reaching the Merits

The court followed the well-established principle that a court must confirm its jurisdiction before addressing the merits of a case. As the Supreme Court held in *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, federal courts must first assure themselves of their jurisdiction because jurisdiction "is the power to declare the law." Without jurisdiction, a court has no authority to issue any binding judgment.

This principle meant the D.C. Circuit could not affirm or reverse the district court's merits reasoning without first confirming that AFL had standing. The court explicitly stated: "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction." The court thus asked the jurisdictional question first and, finding no standing, stopped there—never reaching the merits of whether section 1216 required OSC to investigate.

## The Distinction Between Merits Dismissals and Jurisdictional Dismissals

The procedural disposition here—vacating the district court's judgment and remanding with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction—carries important consequences. The district court had dismissed AFL's claims on the merits for failure to state a claim. A merits dismissal can have preclusive effect, potentially barring future litigation of the same claims under res judicata principles.

By contrast, a jurisdictional dismissal resolves nothing on the merits. The plaintiff remains free to refile if they can later establish jurisdiction—for example, by suffering a new injury that provides standing. The D.C. Circuit's disposition thus converted the district court's merits dismissal into a jurisdictional one. This matters for AFL: the court did not decide whether section 1216 actually requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings. It held only that AFL could not bring this particular lawsuit because AFL could not demonstrate that OSC's refusal to investigate caused AFL a concrete injury that a court order could redress. The merits questions remain open for another day, should a plaintiff with standing pursue them.
