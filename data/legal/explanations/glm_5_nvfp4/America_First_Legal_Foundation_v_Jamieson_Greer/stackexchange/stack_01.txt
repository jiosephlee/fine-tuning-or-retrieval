Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the court vacate the district court's judgment instead of simply affirming the dismissal?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural outcome here. The district court dismissed AFL's case for failure to state a claim, which sounds like a merits dismissal. But the D.C. Circuit vacated that judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Why does this distinction matter? If the result is that AFL loses either way, why does the appeals court bother vacating rather than just affirming? Is there some practical consequence I'm missing?
Answer:
### Answer

The distinction matters because subject-matter jurisdiction is a threshold requirement that federal courts must confirm before exercising any judicial power—including the power to dismiss a case on the merits. The D.C. Circuit could not simply "affirm" a merits-based dismissal when the court below lacked jurisdiction to reach those merits in the first place.

### The Governing Principle: Jurisdiction Comes First

As the court explains, citing *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998), courts must address standing "before reaching the merits" because standing "bear[s] on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction."

This is not mere formalism. Article III of the Constitution limits federal judicial power to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." If a plaintiff lacks standing, there is no case or controversy, and the court has no power to adjudicate anything—including whether the complaint fails to state a claim.

### Why Vacatur, Not Affirmance?

The district court committed legal error by dismissing on the merits (Rule 12(b)(6)) rather than for lack of jurisdiction (Rule 12(b)(1)). The district court actually reached the substantive question of whether § 1216 committed OSC's investigation decision to agency discretion. That was beyond its power if AFL lacked standing.

An affirmance would leave intact a judgment that improperly reached the merits without jurisdiction. The proper corrective is to vacate that judgment entirely, wiping the slate clean, and remand with instructions to enter the correct disposition—a jurisdictional dismissal.

### Practical Consequences of the Distinction

There are real differences between dismissal on the merits versus dismissal for lack of jurisdiction:

1. **Preclusive effect**: A merits dismissal can have claim-preclusive (res judicata) effect, barring future litigation on the same claim. A jurisdictional dismissal does not prejudge the merits and generally does not bar a plaintiff from refiling if they can later establish standing (though that seems unlikely here given the court's analysis).

2. **Appellate precedent**: The district court's substantive conclusions about § 1216—such as its interpretation of OSC's investigative authority—should not stand as valid legal rulings if the court lacked power to issue them.

3. **Proper record**: The vacatur ensures the legal record reflects that the dismissal was for lack of jurisdiction, not because AFL's claim was substantively invalid.

In short: the appellate court couldn't affirm a merits dismissal because the district court never had the power to dismiss on the merits. The only proper disposition was to vacate and remand for a jurisdictional dismissal.
