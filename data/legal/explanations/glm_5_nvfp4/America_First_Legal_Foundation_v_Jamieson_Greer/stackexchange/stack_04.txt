Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does Heckler v. Chaney apply when a statute uses mandatory language like 'shall'?
Question:
The court cites Heckler v. Chaney for the principle that courts generally cannot compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion. But section 1216(a)(3) says OSC 'shall... conduct an investigation' - that sounds mandatory, not discretionary. Does Heckler still apply even when Congress uses mandatory statutory language? Is there a difference between an agency's decision not to investigate at all versus its discretionary choices during an investigation?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is that this opinion does not resolve your question because the court dismisses the case on standing grounds without reaching the merits. However, the opinion's discussion of *Heckler v. Chaney* in the standing analysis is instructive on why mandatory language alone may not eliminate agency discretion.

**The Court Did Not Decide the Merits**

The D.C. Circuit vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of Article III standing. Because the court concluded that AFL lacked standing, it had no occasion to decide whether *Heckler*'s presumption against judicial review of enforcement decisions applies despite Section 1216(a)(3)'s mandatory "shall" language. The court explicitly limited its use of *Heckler* to the standing analysis.

**How the Opinion Uses Heckler**

The court invokes *Heckler* in analyzing whether AFL's claimed injury is redressable—that is, whether a favorable court decision would likely remedy the harm. The court explains that ordering OSC to investigate would still require speculation about "how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." This reasoning suggests that even if Section 1216(a)(3) creates an obligation to investigate, the *manner* of exercising that investigative authority involves discretionary choices that remain unreviewable under *Heckler*.

**The District Court's Merits Reasoning Below**

The district court did reach the question you ask. It held that even though Section 1216(a)(3) uses "shall," Section 1216(c) commits the investigation decision to OSC's discretion because it provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The district court read this "may" language as preserving enforcement discretion, analogous to the discretion inherent in the prohibited personnel practice context. The appellate court's opinion does not endorse or reject this interpretation.

**The Broader Doctrine**

Under *Heckler v. Chaney*, there is a strong presumption that agency decisions not to institute enforcement proceedings are unreviewable. This presumption can be rebutted if Congress has "indicated an intent to circumscribe agency enforcement discretion." However, the presumption operates even when statutory language suggests a duty to act, because courts must still determine what specific enforcement actions are compelled versus what remains discretionary.

**Key Takeaway**

This opinion illustrates a practical consequence of *Heckler*: even when a statute uses mandatory language, the causal chain from a court order to concrete redress may be too speculative because agencies retain discretion over how to conduct investigations and what enforcement actions to pursue. Whether the mandatory "shall" in Section 1216(a)(3) meaningfully limits that discretion remains an open question in this circuit.
