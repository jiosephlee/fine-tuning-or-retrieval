Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why can't AFL's ongoing FOIA litigation against DOJ establish redressability?
Question:
AFL's underlying FOIA lawsuit against DOJ is apparently still ongoing, with DOJ producing records. The court says any informational injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. But if OSC investigated and found wrongdoing, couldn't that influence the FOIA litigation or pressure DOJ to produce more? The court mentions that redressability turns on 'how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors not before the court.' But couldn't an OSC finding of arbitrary withholding be legally relevant in the FOIA case?
Answer:
### Answer

The court's analysis turns on a fundamental principle of Article III standing: a federal court's judgment can only bind parties before it, not separate actors in other proceedings.

**The Redressability Problem**

AFL sued OSC, seeking to compel an investigation. But the injury AFL alleges—the withheld FOIA records—is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. The court explains that any judgment here would bind only OSC; it "would not require DOJ to do anything." The causal chain required to connect an OSC investigation to actual document production involves multiple speculative steps:

1. OSC investigates and *chooses* to pursue corrective or disciplinary action
2. The MSPB *decides* to sanction DOJ employees
3. DOJ *responds* by producing the requested records

As the court notes, this requires "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit" and "speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion." Under *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, causation cannot rely on speculation about "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

**Why the Ongoing FOIA Litigation Doesn't Help**

You suggest an OSC finding of arbitrary withholding might be "legally relevant" in the FOIA case. But the court directly addresses this: "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court'" (quoting *Haaland v. Brackeen*).

Even if OSC investigated and found wrongdoing, that finding would not bind the district court presiding over the FOIA litigation. The FOIA court makes its own determination about whether DOJ's withholding was lawful. An OSC finding might be persuasive, but it cannot compel DOJ to produce records—and Article III redressability requires that a favorable judgment would "very likely" redress the injury, not merely that it might have persuasive value in a separate case.

**The Procedural Rights Exception Doesn't Apply**

AFL invoked the relaxed standing standard for procedural rights, but the court rejected this because section 1216 "creates no entitlement to receive information." Unlike statutes that grant plaintiffs a right to obtain information, section 1216 merely requires OSC to investigate—it doesn't entitle AFL to any particular outcome or disclosure.

In short: AFL's ongoing FOIA case against DOJ cannot establish redressability against OSC because DOJ remains an independent actor, and nothing in the court's judgment here could compel DOJ to act.
