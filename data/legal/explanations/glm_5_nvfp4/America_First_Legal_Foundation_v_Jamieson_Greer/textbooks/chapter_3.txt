Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Article III Standing Doctrine and Procedural Injury

## Article III's Case-or-Controversy Requirement and Its Constitutional Basis

Article III of the United States Constitution limits the federal "judicial Power" to resolving certain "Cases" and "Controversies." This constitutional limitation, found in Article III, Section 2, Clause 1, serves as the foundation for standing doctrine. As the Supreme Court explained in *Allen v. Wright*, standing doctrine flows directly from this case-or-controversy requirement. The purpose of this limitation is to ensure that federal courts address only actual, ongoing disputes between adverse parties, rather than issuing advisory opinions on abstract questions of law.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit confronted a threshold jurisdictional question that the parties had not initially addressed. Before reaching the merits of AFL's claims, the court was obligated to determine whether AFL had satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, which bears directly on whether the district court possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The court invoked the principle from *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment* that courts must address standing before proceeding to the merits. Neither party had initially briefed the standing issue, so the court ordered supplemental briefing—a demonstration of how standing serves as an essential gateway question that courts must resolve regardless of whether the parties raise it.

## The Three Elements of Standing: Injury in Fact, Causation, and Redressability

To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate three core elements: the plaintiff must have suffered an injury that is caused by the defendant and is likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. The Supreme Court articulated this tripartite framework in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, and the D.C. Circuit applied it methodically to AFL's claims. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff need not prove these elements but must allege facts that plausibly support each one.

The court's analysis of AFL's standing illustrates how these three elements operate in practice. AFL needed to show that it suffered a concrete injury, that this injury was fairly traceable to OSC's conduct, and that a favorable court decision would likely remedy the injury. The court examined each of AFL's theories and found them wanting, particularly on causation and redressability grounds. The analysis demonstrates that standing is not a mere formality but a substantive constitutional requirement that filters which disputes federal courts may entertain.

## Requirements for Injury in Fact: Concrete, Particularized, Actual or Imminent

The injury-in-fact requirement demands that a plaintiff demonstrate "an invasion of a legally protected interest" that is both "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." The concreteness requirement ensures that the plaintiff has suffered a real harm rather than an abstract grievance. Particularization requires that the injury affect the plaintiff in a personal and individual way. The actual-or-imminent requirement prevents courts from adjudicating disputes based on speculative or hypothetical future harms.

In this case, AFL attempted to articulate two informational injuries that it claimed were concrete and particularized. First, AFL pointed to DOJ's failure to provide the records requested through FOIA. Second, AFL invoked the notices and information that OSC would have provided had it conducted an investigation under section 1216. The court's analysis of these alleged injuries reveals how the concreteness requirement operates when plaintiffs claim injury from the denial of a statutorily created procedure.

## Procedural Injury Doctrine: When Violation of a Procedural Right Can Confer Standing

AFL grounded its standing argument in its alleged procedural right to an OSC investigation under section 1216. However, the court rejected this approach, citing the Supreme Court's teaching in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer Article III standing. This principle prevents plaintiffs from manufacturing standing simply by citing a statutory requirement that some government action be taken, without showing how the plaintiff is personally harmed by the failure to act.

The procedural injury doctrine addresses a fundamental tension: if a statute requires an agency to take some action, and the agency fails to act, does any citizen have standing to sue? The answer is no. Something more is required—a concrete interest of the plaintiff that is threatened by the procedural violation. The court explained that the procedural right must be "designed to protect their threatened concrete interest." This requirement ensures that plaintiffs invoking procedural injuries are not merely seeking to enforce the law in the abstract but are vindicating their own legitimate stake in the outcome.

## Relaxed Standards for Imminence and Redressability in Procedural Injury Cases

The court invoked an important doctrinal refinement for cases involving procedural injury. When a plaintiff alleges injury from the violation of a procedural statutory right that is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest, courts apply a more relaxed standard for imminence and redressability—but not for injury in fact or causation. The D.C. Circuit drew this principle from its decision in *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education* and the en banc decision in *Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen*.

This relaxation makes practical sense. When a statute requires an agency to follow certain procedures before taking action that might harm the plaintiff, it would be unfair to require the plaintiff to show with precision how the procedural violation caused or would cause specific harm. However, the court emphasized that this relaxation has limits. Plaintiffs still must demonstrate that the procedural violation resulted in injury to a "concrete, particularized interest." AFL's failure to satisfy even these relaxed standards proved fatal to its case.

## Informational Injury: Standing Based on Denial of Information to Which Plaintiff Is Statutorily Entitled

AFL's second standing theory invoked informational injury—the harm suffered when a plaintiff is denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive. The court explained that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the requested information. Citing cases including *FEC v. Akins* and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, the court noted that plaintiffs have standing when they are denied information that a statute requires the government to provide.

This doctrine is distinct from the harm alleged in a typical FOIA case, where the plaintiff seeks specific agency records. Here, AFL was not seeking records from OSC but rather seeking to compel OSC to investigate DOJ's FOIA practices. AFL argued that if OSC conducted an investigation under section 1216, it might provide AFL with notices and information similar to what section 1214 requires for investigations of prohibited personnel practices. However, the court rejected this theory because section 1216 itself creates no entitlement to receive information. Moreover, AFL acknowledged that OSC's decision whether to apply section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, AFL could not establish the concrete informational injury necessary for standing.

## The Requirement That Procedural Rights Must Be Tied to a Concrete Interest

The court's resolution of this case turns on the fundamental principle that procedural rights alone cannot generate standing. AFL invoked section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. But AFL could not identify a concrete interest that the procedural right was designed to protect. The court found both of AFL's proffered interests inadequate. The first—the interest in obtaining records from DOJ—suffered from fatal causation and redressability defects because the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ production of records was too speculative and attenuated. The second—the interest in information from the OSC investigation—failed because no statute entitled AFL to receive such information.

The court's causation analysis is particularly instructive. AFL argued that if OSC had investigated, it might have pursued corrective or disciplinary action, the Merit Systems Protection Board might have sanctioned DOJ employees, and DOJ then might have produced the records. The court rejected this speculative chain, citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the principle that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The court also invoked *Heckler v. Chaney* for the proposition that courts generally may not compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion. Because AFL's standing depended on speculation about decisions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—all involving discretion at each step—AFL could not establish that its injury was caused by OSC or would be redressed by a favorable judgment against OSC. The court therefore concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing, vacated the district court's judgment, and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
