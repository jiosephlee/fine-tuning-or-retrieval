Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # The Statutory Framework Governing OSC's Authority

## OSC's General Authority to Investigate Prohibited Personnel Practices under the Civil Service Reform Act

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial agency within the federal government. Congress established OSC pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act with a mandate to pursue "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The agency's core mission involves investigating what the Act terms "prohibited personnel practices"—improper actions by federal agencies affecting government employees. When OSC uncovers evidence of such practices, it may initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), an administrative tribunal that adjudicates federal employment disputes. The Act empowers OSC to seek two distinct forms of relief from the MSPB: corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, which aims to remedy harm to affected individuals, and disciplinary action under 5 U.S.C. § 1215, which punishes wrongdoing by agency officials.

This dual-track enforcement structure—corrective and disciplinary—forms the backbone of OSC's operational authority. The corrective track focuses on making whole any employee who suffered from a prohibited personnel practice, while the disciplinary track addresses culpable officials through sanctions. Understanding this framework is essential because, as we shall see, Congress extended this same enforcement architecture to certain Freedom of Information Act violations through Section 1216.

## Section 1216(a)(3): Mandatory Investigation of Arbitrary FOIA Withdrawings

Section 1216 expands OSC's jurisdiction beyond traditional personnel matters to encompass certain forms of misconduct by government employees. Most relevant to this case, Section 1216(a)(3) addresses FOIA compliance. The statute provides that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings that violate FOIA's disclosure requirements.

The statutory language deserves careful attention. The word "shall" in Section 1216(a)(3) suggests a mandatory duty: upon receiving an allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, OSC must investigate. This mandatory language became the centerpiece of AFL's legal theory. AFL argued that because the statute uses "shall" rather than "may," OSC lacks discretion to decline investigation when presented with a colorable allegation of arbitrary withholding. The word "arbitrary or capricious" in the statute mirrors the standard from Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), suggesting Congress intended OSC to investigate precisely the type of conduct at issue in this case.

## Section 1216(c): OSC's Authority to Seek Corrective and Disciplinary Action for FOIA Violations

Section 1216(c) specifies what happens after OSC investigates an alleged FOIA violation. It provides that for such violations, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." This provision creates a bridge between FOIA enforcement and OSC's preexisting enforcement machinery.

Several aspects of Section 1216(c) warrant attention. First, the statute uses "may" rather than "shall," suggesting that the decision to pursue enforcement after investigation lies within OSC's discretion. Second, the statute makes Section 1214 and Section 1215—the corrective and disciplinary tracks—available for FOIA violations just as they are for prohibited personnel practices. This means that if OSC elects to pursue enforcement, it can seek both remedies for the aggrieved requester (corrective action) and sanctions against responsible officials (disciplinary action). Third, by referencing the MSPB process, Congress integrated FOIA enforcement into the existing administrative framework rather than creating a separate remedial scheme.

## Section 1214: Procedures for Corrective Actions and Notification Requirements

Section 1214 establishes procedural requirements governing corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices. The statute includes specific provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation. For example, Section 1214(a)(1)(B) through (D) detail various notification requirements that keep complainants informed as OSC processes their allegations.

These notification provisions became relevant to AFL's standing argument. AFL contended that OSC's refusal to investigate deprived AFL of information it would have received had OSC proceeded under Section 1214's procedural requirements. However, as the court noted, Section 1216 investigations are not automatically subject to Section 1214's notification requirements. The relationship between these provisions—whether Section 1214's procedures apply in Section 1216 investigations—remains a contested legal question that factored into the standing analysis.

## Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i): FOIA's Separate Enforcement Provision Requiring Court Order Before OSC Disciplinary Proceedings

FOIA contains its own enforcement mechanism that operates independently of Section 1216. Under Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), OSC must initiate a disciplinary proceeding when three specific conditions are met. First, a court must have ordered production of agency records that were improperly withheld under FOIA. Second, the court must have awarded attorneys' fees against the agency. Third, the court must have "specifically question[ed] whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding."

Only when all three predicates are satisfied does Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) require OSC to "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." This provision represents Congress's decision to tether FOIA enforcement through OSC to prior judicial involvement. The statute creates a gatekeeping role for federal courts: only after a court has found a violation, awarded fees, and specifically questioned the agency's conduct does the mandatory OSC proceeding begin.

The three-predicate structure reveals Congress's careful calibration of this enforcement mechanism. Mere success in FOIA litigation is insufficient; the court must take the additional step of questioning whether the agency's conduct was arbitrary or capricious. This higher threshold suggests Congress intended Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to address only the most egregious FOIA violations—those warranting judicial reproach beyond mere findings of improper withholding.

## The Relationship Between Section 1216 and Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i): Competing Interpretations of OSC's Authority

The central interpretive dispute in this case concerns how Section 1216 relates to Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The parties advanced starkly different readings of these overlapping provisions.

OSC maintained that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) establishes the exclusive trigger for its FOIA enforcement authority. Under this view, OSC may investigate FOIA violations only after a court has satisfied all three predicates—the production order, the fee award, and the specific questioning of arbitrary conduct. Because none of these predicates had occurred in AFL's case—DOJ was producing records through ongoing litigation, and no court had yet questioned arbitrary conduct—OSC concluded it lacked authority to investigate AFL's allegation.

AFL countered that Section 1216(a)(3) operates independently of Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL emphasized the mandatory "shall" language in Section 1216(a)(3), arguing that this provision creates a freestanding obligation on OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, regardless of whether the Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates have been satisfied. Under AFL's reading, the two statutes address different circumstances: Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) mandates OSC action after certain judicial findings, while Section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate allegations even without prior court involvement.

The district court adopted something of a middle position. It concluded that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under Section 1216—a ruling in AFL's favor on the legal question. The court therefore held that OSC's refusal to investigate rested on a legal error. However, the district court ultimately declined to order OSC to investigate, reasoning that Section 1216(c) committed such decisions to agency discretion. The court remanded to OSC for further consideration, and when OSC again declined to investigate, the district court dismissed AFL's remaining claims, prompting this appeal.

As the D.C. Circuit noted, the court did not need to resolve this statutory interpretation question because AFL lacked Article III standing regardless. The jurisdictional defect precluded reaching the merits of whether Section 1216 mandated an investigation. Nevertheless, understanding the parties' competing interpretations remains essential to grasping why this lawsuit proceeded as it did and how the standing question ultimately proved dispositive.
