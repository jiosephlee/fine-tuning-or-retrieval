Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 5: # Disposition and Significance

## The Court's Holding: AFL Lacks Article III Standing

The D.C. Circuit concluded that America First Legal Foundation lacked Article III standing to pursue its claims against the Office of Special Counsel. This holding resolved the case entirely on jurisdictional grounds, without reaching the merits of AFL's statutory arguments. The court found that AFL could not satisfy either of its two standing theories: the informational injury allegedly caused by DOJ's failure to produce records, and the informational injury allegedly caused by OSC's failure to provide notices and updates about an investigation.

Both theories failed for related reasons. The first theory—the core of AFL's claim—suffered from "fatal causation and redressability problems." Any informational injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose records was traceable to DOJ, not OSC. More fundamentally, the causal chain connecting an OSC investigation to DOJ's production of records was too speculative and attenuated. The court explained that this chain would require speculation about choices by multiple actors not before the court: OSC's exercise of enforcement discretion, the Merit Systems Protection Board's potential sanctions, and DOJ's ultimate decision to produce records. The second theory failed because section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no statutory entitlement to receive information. AFL could not point to any legal requirement that OSC provide it with notices or information during a section 1216 investigation.

## Vacatur of the District Court's Judgment and Remand

Having concluded that AFL lacked standing, the court vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This disposition follows directly from the jurisdictional nature of standing doctrine. Article III standing goes to the federal court's power to hear a case; without it, the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction entirely.

The court's use of vacatur is significant. Rather than simply affirming or reversing the district court's decision, the court wiped the lower court's judgment from the books. This makes sense because the district court should not have reached the merits at all—it lacked jurisdiction to do so. The remand with instructions to dismiss ensures that the case ends at the trial court level on proper jurisdictional grounds.

The standard of review merits brief mention. The court noted that it reviews the dismissal de novo, meaning it considers the standing question afresh without deference to the district court's analysis. This is the standard approach for questions of law, including jurisdictional questions.

## The Importance of Addressing Standing Before Reaching the Merits

The court's opinion exemplifies a fundamental principle of federal jurisdiction: courts must address standing before reaching the merits. The opinion opens its analysis by stating that "before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing." This ordering is not a matter of judicial preference but of constitutional command.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment* establishes that federal courts lack authority to resolve the merits of a case when the plaintiff lacks standing. This principle follows from the case-or-controversy limitation in Article III, which restricts the federal judicial power to actual disputes between proper parties. The D.C. Circuit properly treated standing as a threshold question that it was "bound to enforce" even when the parties had not raised it.

Notably, neither party initially addressed standing in this case. The court therefore ordered supplemental briefing on the question. This judicial initiative reflects the court's independent obligation to ensure subject-matter jurisdiction exists before proceeding. The parties' silence on standing did not waive the requirement—indeed, parties cannot waive Article III limitations on federal judicial power.

## Implications for Future Cases Seeking to Compel OSC Investigations

This decision clarifies important limitations on using section 1216 as an alternative enforcement mechanism for FOIA rights. Litigants cannot circumvent FOIA's direct enforcement provisions by petitioning OSC to investigate and then suing to compel that investigation. The court's analysis reveals why this strategy fails as a jurisdictional matter: the connection between an OSC investigation and the ultimate disclosure of records is simply too attenuated to support standing.

The opinion also highlights the distinction between sections 1216 and 1214 of the Civil Service Reform Act. While section 1214—governing prohibited personnel practices—includes provisions for notifying complaining parties about investigation progress, section 1216—governing FOIA violations—contains no comparable notice requirements. AFL attempted to argue that OSC might provide section 1214 notices during a section 1216 investigation, but the court correctly noted that OSC's decision to do so is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to information, AFL could not establish the concrete informational injury needed for standing.

Future litigants should take careful note of the court's skepticism about "procedural rights in vacuo." Citing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, the court emphasized that the deprivation of a procedural right, without some concrete interest affected by that deprivation, is insufficient for Article III standing. Even if section 1216 creates a procedural right to an investigation, AFL needed to show that the denial of that procedure harmed some concrete interest—which it failed to do.

## The Separation-of-Powers Dimension: Courts Cannot Compel Agency Enforcement

Underlying much of the court's standing analysis is a fundamental separation-of-powers principle: courts generally cannot compel agencies to exercise their enforcement discretion. The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, the leading Supreme Court case establishing that agency decisions not to enforce are presumptively unreviewable. This presumption reflects the complex policy and resource considerations involved in enforcement decisions—considerations that agencies, not courts, are institutionally equipped to make.

The court's causation analysis incorporates this principle. AFL's theory required speculation about "how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." Even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, OSC would retain discretion about whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action. The court refused to assume that OSC would take enforcement action following an investigation, just as it refused to assume that the MSPB would sanction DOJ employees or that DOJ would produce records in response.

The court also cited recent Supreme Court authority reinforcing that causation analysis cannot rely on speculation about choices by independent actors not before the court. In *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the Supreme Court rejected standing theories that required assuming how third parties would respond to court rulings. Similarly, in *Haaland v. Brackeen*, the Court explained that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade non-parties. These principles protect courts from being drawn into disputes where their judgments cannot provide meaningful relief.

## Practical Considerations for Litigants Seeking OSC Enforcement of FOIA Rights

Practitioners should understand that this case effectively closes the door on using section 1216 as a litigation strategy for compelling FOIA compliance. FOIA itself contains comprehensive enforcement provisions that allow requesters to sue agencies directly in federal court for wrongful withholding of records. The statute also provides for attorneys' fee awards and, in limited circumstances, referral to OSC for potential disciplinary proceedings. But FOIA's judicial enforcement mechanism remains the primary avenue for relief.

The opinion underscores an important strategic point: even if a statute uses mandatory language like "shall" to describe an agency's duty, this does not automatically create a private right of action or establish standing. Section 1216(a)(3) states that OSC "shall...conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Yet the court found that AFL lacked standing to enforce this provision because it could not demonstrate that an investigation would redress its informational injury.

Litigants should also recognize the limits of "informational injury" as a basis for standing. While courts have recognized that denial of information required by statute can constitute a concrete injury, this requires a statutory entitlement to receive that information. Here, AFL could point to no provision requiring OSC to provide it with information about a section 1216 investigation. The absence of such an entitlement proved fatal to AFL's second standing theory.
