Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # Application: AFL's Standing Theories and the Court's Analysis

Having established the framework for Article III standing, the court turns to apply these principles to AFL's specific claims. This section of the opinion demonstrates how standing doctrine operates in practice and why even plausible grievances may fail to meet constitutional thresholds for federal court jurisdiction.

## AFL's First Theory: Injury from Not Receiving FOIA Records from DOJ

AFL's first theory of standing connects its alleged injury to the underlying FOIA request that prompted this litigation. AFL claimed that because OSC failed to investigate DOJ's allegedly arbitrary FOIA withholding, AFL was denied access to the records it had requested from DOJ. Framing this as an informational injury, AFL argued that OSC's inaction caused it harm that a court order compelling an investigation could redress.

This theory illustrates an important principle in standing analysis: the party who causes an injury is not always the party best positioned to redress it through litigation. AFL sought to sue OSC for an injury—the withholding of records—that DOJ, a separate agency, had directly caused. The court recognized this mismatch as a fundamental problem for AFL's standing theory.

## Causation Problems: Tracing the Injury to DOJ Rather than OSC

The court identified a straightforward causation defect in AFL's first theory: "any informational injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." This statement reflects the requirement that a plaintiff's injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, not merely connected to it in some attenuated sense.

The traceability requirement, sometimes called causation, demands that the plaintiff establish a causal connection between the injury and the conduct complained of. Here, AFL sued OSC, but DOJ was the agency that denied the FOIA request. AFL's theory thus required the court to trace the injury back from DOJ's refusal, through OSC's failure to investigate, to OSC's alleged statutory violation. The court found this connection "too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."

## Redressability Problems: The Speculative Causal Chain Involving OSC, MSPB, and DOJ

Even if OSC had caused AFL's injury in some sense, the court found that AFL could not establish redressability—that a favorable court decision would likely remedy the harm. The court laid out the causal chain that AFL's theory would require:

> If OSC had conducted the assertedly mandatory section 1216 investigation, it might have decided to pursue a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action under section 1215, against offending DOJ employees. Then, the MSPB might have decided to sanction those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Next, DOJ might have decided to produce the requested records.

Each step in this chain involves discretionary decisions by actors who are not parties to the lawsuit. OSC must decide whether to pursue enforcement action; the MSPB must decide whether to impose sanctions; and DOJ must decide whether to produce records in response. The court emphasized that this speculative sequence involves "choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit."

## The Principle That Courts Cannot Rely on Speculation About Independent Actors Not Before the Court

The court grounded its redressability analysis in a fundamental principle of standing doctrine. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court stated that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

This principle serves an important jurisdictional function. Federal courts exist to resolve concrete disputes between adverse parties, not to issue advisory opinions about how third parties might respond to a court's reasoning. When redressability depends on actions by non-parties, a plaintiff cannot demonstrate that a court judgment will actually remedy the alleged harm.

The court further noted that "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" This distinction between binding judgments and persuasive reasoning marks the boundary between justiciable controversies and advisory opinions.

## Heckler v. Chaney and Enforcement Discretion: Courts Generally May Not Compel Agency Action

The court's redressability analysis drew on an additional principle from administrative law. Citing *Heckler v. Chaney*, the court observed that its analysis "requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."

*Heckler v. Chaney* established that an agency's decision not to take enforcement action is presumptively unreviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act. This presumption reflects both the agency's comparative expertise in allocating enforcement resources and the practical reality that enforcement decisions often involve complex policy judgments unsuitable for judicial resolution. The court invoked this principle to highlight another layer of speculation in AFL's theory: even if ordered to investigate, OSC would retain discretion over whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action.

## AFL's Second Theory: Informational Injury from Not Receiving Investigation Notices

AFL's second standing theory shifted focus from the underlying FOIA records to the investigation itself. AFL argued that if OSC conducted a section 1216 investigation, AFL might receive notices and information that OSC provides when investigating "prohibited personnel practices" under section 1214. This theory frames the injury as a denial of information to which AFL claims entitlement.

The court approached this theory through the framework of informational standing. A plaintiff claiming informational injury must show that a statute entitled them to receive the information they were denied. Without such an entitlement, the claimed injury remains abstract and insufficiently concrete.

## Why Section 1216 Does Not Create a Statutory Entitlement to Information

The court found AFL's second theory defective because section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, does not create an entitlement to information. The court stated: "here, even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information."

This holding illustrates a critical distinction in informational standing doctrine. Not every statute that requires agency action creates individual rights that plaintiffs can enforce in court. The court must examine whether the statute was "designed to protect" the plaintiff's concrete interests and whether it provides a specific entitlement to information. Section 1216's mandatory language—"shall...conduct an investigation"—addresses OSC's obligations but says nothing about what information, if any, must be provided to complainants.

## The Discretionary Nature of Section 1214 Notice Requirements in Section 1216 Investigations

AFL attempted to salvage its second theory by arguing that a section 1216 investigation might trigger section 1214's notice requirements. Section 1214 requires OSC to provide certain notices and information to complainants when investigating prohibited personnel practices. AFL contended that these requirements might apply by extension to section 1216 investigations.

The court rejected this argument by pointing to precedent acknowledging that "OSC's 'decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements' in a section 1216 investigation is 'discretionary.'" The court cited the Sixth Circuit's decision in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, which held that applying section 1214's notice provisions to section 1216 investigations is not mandatory but rather within OSC's discretion.

Because OSC has discretion over whether to provide section 1214 notices during a section 1216 investigation, AFL could not claim an entitlement to such information. And without an entitlement, there is no concrete informational injury. The court concluded that "absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory."

## Why AFL Failed Under Both Ordinary and Relaxed Standing Standards

Before analyzing AFL's specific theories, the court noted that AFL sought to invoke "relaxed standing requirements that apply to cases involving alleged procedural injury." Under this relaxed approach, plaintiffs alleging violation of a procedural right must show that the violation caused injury to some "concrete, particularized interest." The relaxation affects imminence and redressability but not injury in fact or causation.

The court ultimately found that AFL's claims failed under both ordinary and relaxed standards. Even assuming that section 1216 created a procedural right to an investigation, AFL could not identify a concrete interest harmed by the deprivation of that right. AFL's two asserted concrete interests—the FOIA records and the investigation notices—both failed for the reasons discussed above. Because AFL could not establish the requisite concrete interest, the court concluded it "need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive."

The court's analysis demonstrates the relationship between procedural rights and concrete injury. A procedural right alone, without connection to some concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect, cannot support Article III standing. As the court noted, quoting *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, "the 'deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient' to confer Article III standing."
