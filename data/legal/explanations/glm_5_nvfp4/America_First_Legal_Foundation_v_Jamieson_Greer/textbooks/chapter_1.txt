Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## The Office of Special Counsel: Purpose and Function Within the Federal Civil Service System

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is an independent investigative and prosecutorial agency within the federal government. Congress established OSC to advance the goal of a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The agency's primary authority comes from the Civil Service Reform Act, which empowers OSC to investigate "prohibited personnel practices"—improper employment actions taken against federal employees—and to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). When OSC identifies a prohibited personnel practice, it may seek corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 (such as reinstatement of a wrongfully terminated employee) or disciplinary action under 5 U.S.C. § 1215 (such as suspension or removal of the responsible official). Section 1214 also includes procedural protections for complainants, including requirements that OSC provide notice about the progress of an investigation.

Beyond personnel matters, Congress has given OSC authority to investigate certain other forms of misconduct by government employees. Most relevant to this case, Section 1216 of the Act addresses violations of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). Specifically, Section 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC "shall . . . conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings that violate FOIA. If OSC finds such a violation, it may pursue corrective or disciplinary action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." This statutory language makes Section 1216 the mechanism by which OSC enforces compliance with FOIA's disclosure requirements through the civil service system.

FOIA itself contains a separate enforcement provision that implicates OSC. Under Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), when a court orders production of improperly withheld records, awards attorneys' fees against the agency, and "specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." This provision creates a mandatory duty for OSC—but only when three specific predicates are satisfied: a court order for production, an award of attorneys' fees, and an express judicial finding questioning arbitrary or capricious conduct. The relationship between this provision and Section 1216's more general investigatory mandate lies at the heart of this dispute.

## AFL's FOIA Request to DOJ and Its Allegations of Arbitrary Withholding

America First Legal Foundation (AFL), a nonprofit organization, submitted a FOIA request to the Department of Justice as part of what the opinion describes as "its effort to investigate judicial recusals." Specifically, AFL sought emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division. According to AFL, the Civil Division responded with what AFL considered a "dubious ground" for denial: the Division claimed it does not search employee emails for responsive records without the employee's consent. AFL viewed this response as evidence of an arbitrary policy that violated FOIA's requirements.

AFL then filed suit against DOJ under FOIA to compel production of the requested records. Importantly, during that separate lawsuit, DOJ began producing responsive records and continued to do so. The opinion notes that "the sufficiency of this production is not at issue" in the present case. Thus, AFL's underlying FOIA request was being satisfied through traditional FOIA litigation against the agency that possessed the records.

## AFL's Complaint to OSC Invoking Section 1216

Separate from its FOIA lawsuit against DOJ, AFL also filed a complaint with OSC. AFL asked OSC to investigate what it alleged was DOJ's "FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent." In support of this request, AFL invoked Section 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall . . . conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. AFL's theory was that Section 1216 creates a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding—regardless of whether the other statutory triggers in FOIA's own enforcement provision had been satisfied.

This argument represented an aggressive reading of Section 1216. If accepted, it would mean that any person who believed an agency had arbitrarily withheld FOIA records could compel OSC to open an investigation, even without first obtaining a court order finding wrongdoing. AFL was essentially asking the court to interpret Section 1216 as creating an independent, self-executing enforcement mechanism parallel to—but not dependent on—the procedures set forth in FOIA itself.

## OSC's Refusal to Investigate Based on Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) Predicates

OSC declined to investigate AFL's allegations. The agency took the position that it lacked authority to investigate because the three predicates required by Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been satisfied. Recall that provision requires a court to have: (1) ordered production of improperly withheld records, (2) awarded attorneys' fees, and (3) specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. None of these conditions had occurred in AFL's FOIA litigation against DOJ.

OSC's position, therefore, was that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) defines the exclusive circumstances under which it must investigate FOIA violations. AFL disagreed, arguing that Section 1216 mandated an investigation into its allegations even if the FOIA enforcement provision's requirements were not met. This dispute presented a pure question of statutory interpretation: Does Section 1216 create an independent obligation to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, or does Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) cabin that authority by specifying the only circumstances triggering an investigation?

## District Court's Ruling: Legal Error by OSC but Discretionary Authority Under Section 1216(c)

AFL filed suit against OSC (then headed by Acting Special Counsel Kerner) in federal district court to compel an investigation. The district court ruled in part for AFL, agreeing with AFL's interpretation of the statutory relationship. The court concluded that "Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216." In other words, OSC had committed legal error by treating the FOIA provision's predicates as limiting its Section 1216 authority.

However, this partial victory for AFL did not translate into the ultimate relief it sought. The district court declined to order OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy. The court reasoned that Section 1216(c)—which provides that OSC may investigate FOIA violations "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved"—committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion. Even though OSC had misinterpreted the scope of its authority, the court concluded that the ultimate decision to investigate remained discretionary rather than mandatory.

Having found that OSC had discretion over whether to investigate, the district court remanded the case to OSC for further consideration. This procedural posture is significant: the court found legal error in OSC's reasoning but nonetheless determined that the statute did not compel the particular remedy AFL sought.

## OSC's Second Refusal After Remand and Final Judgment

On remand, OSC again declined to investigate AFL's allegations. The opinion does not specify the grounds for this second refusal, but the result was clear: OSC, having reconsidered in light of the district court's ruling, still would not open an investigation into DOJ's FOIA practices. Following this second refusal, AFL moved for entry of final judgment in the district court, presumably seeking to preserve its right to appeal the adverse ruling.

The district court dismissed AFL's remaining claims, entering final judgment against AFL. This dismissal ended the litigation in the district court and set the stage for AFL's appeal to the D.C. Circuit. The procedural posture on appeal was a final judgment dismissing AFL's claims for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.

## Appeal and the Court's Decision to Order Supplemental Briefing on Standing

AFL appealed the district court's dismissal to the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. The standard of review for a district court's dismissal is de novo, meaning the appellate court reviews the legal conclusions afresh without deference to the district court's reasoning.

Significantly, neither party initially addressed the question of standing in their appellate briefs. Standing—the requirement that a plaintiff demonstrate a concrete injury causally connected to the defendant's conduct that a favorable court decision can redress—is a jurisdictional prerequisite. Federal courts lack subject-matter jurisdiction over cases where the plaintiff lacks standing. The Supreme Court has held that courts must address standing before reaching the merits of a dispute.

Because standing is jurisdictional and the parties had not briefed the issue, the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing. This order signaled that the court had identified a potential jurisdictional problem that could resolve the case without reaching the merits of the statutory interpretation dispute. Indeed, as the opinion ultimately demonstrates, standing became the dispositive issue—the court never reached the merits of whether Section 1216 mandates an investigation or whether the district court correctly interpreted the statutory scheme. Instead, the court concluded that AFL lacked standing to pursue its claims, requiring dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
