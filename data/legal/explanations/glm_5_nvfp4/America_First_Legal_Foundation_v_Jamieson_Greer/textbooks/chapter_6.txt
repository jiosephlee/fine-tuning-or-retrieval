Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 6: # Procedural Posture and Judicial Review Standards

Understanding how this case reached the D.C. Circuit—and what standards govern review at each stage—is essential to following the court's analysis. The procedural history shapes what questions the appellate court could consider and how it approached them. This chapter walks through the case's journey through the courts and explains the standards that controlled each phase of review.

## Motion to Dismiss Stage: Pleading Requirements for Standing

This case reached the D.C. Circuit after the district court dismissed AFL's claims for failure to state a claim under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). At the motion-to-dismiss stage, courts assess whether the plaintiff has alleged enough facts to make its claims plausible, accepting all well-pleaded facts as true. But standing—the plaintiff's qualification to bring suit—operates differently.

Standing is a jurisdictional requirement rooted in Article III of the Constitution. A plaintiff must have suffered an injury that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." The injury must be caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable court decision. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each of these elements. Here, AFL's standing was not seriously challenged below, and the district court reached the merits—ultimately dismissing the case because it concluded that even if OSC had erred legally, the decision to investigate was committed to OSC's discretion.

## De Novo Review Standard on Appeal

When AFL appealed the district court's dismissal, the D.C. Circuit reviewed the decision de novo. De novo review means the appellate court considers the legal questions afresh, giving no deference to the district court's conclusions. The court reviews the dismissal "accepting the factual allegations in the complaint as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor." This standard applies both to the merits of a failure-to-state-a-claim dismissal and to questions of standing.

The de novo standard matters here because it meant the D.C. Circuit was free to reach its own conclusions about whether AFL had adequately pleaded standing—even though the district court had not confronted that issue directly and had instead resolved the case on the merits.

## The Court's Authority to Raise Standing Issues Sua Sponte

Neither party addressed standing in their initial briefs on appeal. The court nonetheless raised the issue on its own motion—described in legal Latin as *sua sponte*—and ordered supplemental briefing. Federal courts have an independent obligation to ensure they have subject-matter jurisdiction before proceeding to the merits. Because standing is jurisdictional, courts must verify it exists even if the parties do not raise the question.

The Supreme Court has emphasized that standing "cannot be inferred argumentatively from the complaint" but must be affirmatively demonstrated. If a court lacks jurisdiction, it cannot proceed to decide the merits. The D.C. Circuit's decision to flag the standing issue reflects this bedrock principle: jurisdiction comes first.

## Supplemental Briefing Ordered by the Court

After identifying standing as a potential problem, the court ordered both parties to submit supplemental briefing on the question. This procedure ensures that the parties have a fair opportunity to address a jurisdictional issue the court has identified before the court decides it. Supplemental briefing is especially appropriate when, as here, the issue was not raised below or in the initial appellate briefs.

The supplemental briefs allowed AFL to articulate its theories of standing and the government to respond. AFL argued that it had suffered two informational injuries: first, from DOJ's failure to produce the requested records; and second, from OSC's failure to provide notices and information during an investigation. The court evaluated these arguments carefully before concluding that neither satisfied Article III requirements.

## Why the Court Addressed Standing Before Reaching the Merits

The court followed the well-established principle that a court must confirm its jurisdiction before addressing the merits of a case. As the Supreme Court held in *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, federal courts must first assure themselves of their jurisdiction because jurisdiction "is the power to declare the law." Without jurisdiction, a court has no authority to issue any binding judgment.

This principle meant the D.C. Circuit could not affirm or reverse the district court's merits reasoning without first confirming that AFL had standing. The court explicitly stated: "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction." The court thus asked the jurisdictional question first and, finding no standing, stopped there—never reaching the merits of whether section 1216 required OSC to investigate.

## The Distinction Between Merits Dismissals and Jurisdictional Dismissals

The procedural disposition here—vacating the district court's judgment and remanding with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction—carries important consequences. The district court had dismissed AFL's claims on the merits for failure to state a claim. A merits dismissal can have preclusive effect, potentially barring future litigation of the same claims under res judicata principles.

By contrast, a jurisdictional dismissal resolves nothing on the merits. The plaintiff remains free to refile if they can later establish jurisdiction—for example, by suffering a new injury that provides standing. The D.C. Circuit's disposition thus converted the district court's merits dismissal into a jurisdictional one. This matters for AFL: the court did not decide whether section 1216 actually requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings. It held only that AFL could not bring this particular lawsuit because AFL could not demonstrate that OSC's refusal to investigate caused AFL a concrete injury that a court order could redress. The merits questions remain open for another day, should a plaintiff with standing pursue them.
