Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights 'In Vacuo': The D.C. Circuit Clarifies When Mandated Agency Action Creates Judicially Enforceable Rights

The D.C. Circuit's decision last week in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* offers a pointed reminder that statutory commands to agency action—even commands using the word "shall"—do not automatically translate into judicially enforceable rights. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas held that a plaintiff lacks Article III standing to compel an investigation by the Office of Special Counsel under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, even though the statute states that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" of certain FOIA violations. The decision underscores an important limit on the procedural injury doctrine: a plaintiff must connect the asserted procedural right to a concrete interest that the statute is designed to protect.

## The Statutory Scheme and AFL's Challenge

The case arose from a FOIA request that America First Legal Foundation submitted to the Department of Justice. AFL sought emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division as part of an investigation into judicial recusals. DOJ's Civil Division reportedly refused to search employee emails without the employee's consent—a policy AFL viewed as an arbitrary and capricious withholding of information prohibited by FOIA.

AFL had separate litigation pending against DOJ to obtain the records. But AFL also asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." When OSC declined, pointing to another FOIA provision that requires three specific predicates before OSC must investigate, AFL sued to compel the investigation.

The district court agreed with AFL that § 1216 does not require satisfaction of FOIA's three predicates, but it nonetheless dismissed the case, reasoning that OSC retained discretion over whether to apply § 1214's corrective action procedures to a § 1216 investigation. On remand, OSC again declined to investigate, and AFL appealed.

## The Standing Problem

Before reaching the merits, the D.C. Circuit raised a threshold question: Does AFL have Article III standing to pursue these claims? Neither party had addressed the issue, so the court ordered supplemental briefing.

Judge Katsas's opinion walks through the familiar triad of standing requirements—injury, causation, and redressability—but focuses on a wrinkle that arises when plaintiffs claim injury from the violation of a procedural right. The Supreme Court has recognized that when a statute grants a procedural right designed to protect a plaintiff's concrete interests, courts relax—but do not eliminate—the usual requirements for imminence and redressability. Yet the plaintiff must still show that the procedural right is "designed to protect" some "concrete, particularized interest." A procedural right untethered to any such interest—what the Court in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* called a "procedural right in vacuo"—cannot support standing.

AFL offered two theories. First, it claimed that OSC's failure to investigate prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested under FOIA. Second, it asserted an informational interest in receiving notices and updates that might flow from an OSC investigation.

Both theories failed. The court's analysis of the first theory illustrates the causation and redressability problems that arise when plaintiffs try to compel agency enforcement action. OSC's investigation might—or might not—lead to a decision to pursue corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board. The MSPB might—or might not—sanction DOJ employees. DOJ might—or might not—change its FOIA practices in response. This chain requires speculation about choices by actors not before the court, including an agency (DOJ) and an adjudicatory body (the MSPB) that are not parties to this lawsuit. Under *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, such speculation about "the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts" cannot satisfy Article III causation requirements.

AFL's second theory fared no better. The court held that § 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no statutory entitlement to receive information from that investigation. AFL pointed to notice requirements that apply when OSC investigates "prohibited personnel practices" under § 1214, but as the Sixth Circuit held in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, OSC's decision to apply those procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to information, there is no concrete informational injury.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision highlights an important boundary in procedural injury doctrine. Since *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, courts have recognized that plaintiffs claiming injury from the violation of a procedural right get some slack on causation and redressability. But *Summers* and its progeny make clear that this relaxation applies only when the plaintiff identifies a concrete interest that the statutory procedure is designed to protect. The plaintiff in *Summers*, for example, challenged the Forest Service's decision to approve certain salvage timber sales without providing notice and opportunity to comment, but the plaintiff had not identified any particular timber sale that affected its members' concrete interests. The procedural right—to comment—existed "in vacuo."

*America First Legal* applies this principle in a context that arises with some frequency: a plaintiff seeking to compel an agency to investigate or enforce against a third party. The decision reinforces that plaintiffs cannot bootstrap their way into federal court by pointing to statutory language directing agency action. Even when Congress uses the word "shall," the plaintiff must identify some concrete interest—whether informational, physical, economic, or otherwise—that the statutory command is meant to protect, and that interest must be one the plaintiff actually holds.

This limitation has significant implications for litigation involving government accountability and transparency. FOIA itself creates a right to obtain records, but the D.C. Circuit has now made clear that a separate statutory scheme giving OSC authority to investigate FOIA violations does not create a judicially enforceable right for FOIA requesters. The requester's remedy for an unlawful withholding is a FOIA lawsuit against the agency that withheld the records—not a mandamus action to compel a separate agency to investigate the withholding.

## Downstream Effects

The decision may have ripple effects for similar attempts to compel agency investigations under other statutory schemes. Plaintiffs seeking to enforce statutory commands to investigate or take enforcement action will need to carefully identify the concrete interest they are pursuing—not just the abstract interest in having agencies follow the law. And they will need to show that a court order can actually redress the claimed injury, rather than merely setting in motion a chain of discretionary decisions by other actors.

Judge Katsas's opinion also serves as a reminder that standing doctrine, for all its complexity, ultimately serves to enforce the constitutional limits on the judicial power. Federal courts exist to resolve concrete disputes, not to supervise the executive branch's compliance with statutory mandates in the abstract. Even when Congress has spoken in mandatory terms, not every statutory command creates a right enforceable by every affected citizen in federal court.

*America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* thus joins a line of decisions clarifying that the procedural injury doctrine is not a workaround for Article III's case-or-controversy requirement. Procedural rights must be tethered to concrete interests, and plaintiffs seeking to compel agency action must show that judicial intervention can actually address the harm they claim.
