Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Causation Conundrum: D.C. Circuit Applies Recent Supreme Court Precedent to Multi-Agency Enforcement Chains

Standing doctrine has long grappled with how to handle injuries that flow through multiple government actors before reaching the plaintiff. Last week, in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, the D.C. Circuit added a new chapter to this ongoing story—one that practitioners and scholars should read carefully for its application of recent Supreme Court precedent to multi-agency enforcement chains.

The case arose from a familiar frustration: a FOIA request denied, a lawsuit filed, and the requesting party still seeking additional avenues for relief. But the court's standing analysis broke new ground in explaining when speculative causal chains involving multiple agencies and enforcement discretion fall short of Article III requirements.

## The Factual Backdrop

America First Legal Foundation requested emails from the Department of Justice as part of an investigation into judicial recusals. DOJ's Civil Division reportedly denied the request on the ground that it does not search employee emails without the employee's consent—a policy AFL found dubious at best.

AFL did what any sophisticated requester would do: it sued under FOIA. DOJ has been producing records, and the sufficiency of that production was not before the court. But AFL also pursued a parallel track, asking the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552."

OSC declined, reasoning that the three predicates for triggering an investigation under FOIA's own enforcement provision—court-ordered production, an attorneys' fee award, and a judicial finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding—were not satisfied. AFL sued to compel the investigation. The district court agreed that OSC had committed legal error but ultimately dismissed for failure to state a claim, finding that OSC retained discretion over whether to investigate.

The D.C. Circuit never reached the merits. Instead, in an opinion by Judge Katsas, the court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing altogether.

## The Causation Problem

AFL advanced two standing theories, both of which the court rejected. But it is the first theory—that OSC's failure to investigate caused AFL to be denied the records it sought from DOJ—that offers the most instructive analysis for future cases.

The court's reasoning cuts to the heart of what makes multi-agency enforcement chains so problematic for standing purposes. Even assuming section 1216 mandates an investigation upon request (a question the court did not decide), AFL's theory required the court to speculate about a chain of events involving multiple actors not before the court:

First, OSC would have to investigate and decide to pursue corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board. Then the MSPB would have to sanction the responsible DOJ employees. Then DOJ would have to respond to that sanction by producing the requested records.

This chain, the court explained, requires "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit (the MSPB and DOJ)." The court then deployed recent Supreme Court precedent to explain why such speculation defeats standing.

## Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Independent Actors

The D.C. Circuit's application of *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024) is particularly noteworthy. In that case, the Supreme Court held that the plaintiffs lacked standing to challenge FDA's approval of mifepristone because the alleged injuries depended on the independent choices of third parties—doctors who might prescribe the medication and patients who might use it.

Here, the D.C. Circuit extended that logic to government actors. The court quoted *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*'s teaching that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The principle applies with equal force when those independent actors are other agencies.

This extension makes sense. The federal government is not a monolith. Agencies have independent statutory mandates, institutional priorities, and policy preferences. DOJ might have perfectly legitimate reasons to continue litigating its FOIA position even if OSC investigates and the MSPB sanctions individual employees. To assume otherwise is precisely the kind of conjecture that standing doctrine forbids.

## Brackeen and the Limits of Judicial Persuasion

The court also drew on *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) for a more subtle point about redressability. There, the Supreme Court emphasized that redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'"

This principle has significant implications for cases seeking to compel agency investigations. A court order requiring OSC to investigate DOJ's FOIA practices would bind only OSC. It would not—and could not—compel DOJ to produce records or the MSPB to impose sanctions. The fact that such an order might create pressure on those agencies is legally insufficient.

This distinction between binding judgments and persuasive reasoning should give pause to litigants who hope that a favorable ruling in one case will cascade through the federal bureaucracy. Courts are not in the business of issuing advisory opinions that might influence other actors in the executive branch.

## Heckler v. Chaney and Enforcement Discretion

The court's analysis also wove in *Heckler v. Chaney* (1985), the foundational case establishing that agency enforcement decisions are presumptively unreviewable. Even if section 1216 mandates an investigation, the court suggested, OSC's decisions about how to exercise its enforcement discretion—whether to pursue corrective action before the MSPB—would remain within OSC's judgment.

This point is crucial. AFL's standing theory required the court to assume not just that OSC would investigate, but that OSC would take enforcement action, that the MSPB would impose sanctions, and that DOJ would respond by producing records. Each step involves discretion that the court cannot compel.

## The Informational Injury Theory

AFL's alternative standing theory—that it had a right to information generated by the OSC investigation—fared no better. The court explained that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information. Section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no such entitlement.

AFL argued that OSC might choose to provide notices and information under section 1214's procedures. But as the court pointed out, AFL itself acknowledged that OSC's decision to apply section 1214 procedures to a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. You cannot base standing on information you have no right to receive.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision has several important implications for standing doctrine and administrative litigation.

First, it clarifies that *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*'s prohibition on speculating about independent actors applies to other agencies, not just private third parties. This should make courts more skeptical of standing theories that require agencies not before the court to take particular actions.

Second, it reinforces that *Brackeen*'s redressability principle applies with full force in intra-government disputes. A judgment binding one agency does not satisfy redressability when the plaintiff's injury depends on actions by other agencies.

Third, it suggests limits on using statutory mandates for investigation as vehicles for litigation. Even when a statute uses mandatory language—"shall conduct an investigation"—the court's analysis implies that plaintiffs still need to show how the investigation itself would redress a concrete injury.

## Downstream Effects

Practitioners should take note. This decision will make it harder to bring suits seeking to compel agency investigations when the plaintiff's ultimate injury was caused by a different agency. The logic would seem to apply equally to other contexts—for example, suits seeking to compel an agency to investigate alleged violations by another agency.

For scholars, the decision offers a useful data point in the ongoing debate about standing doctrine's rigor. Critics who argue that modern standing doctrine has become too demanding will find support in the court's refusal to accept that OSC investigation would likely lead to document production. Defenders of the doctrine will see the case as a faithful application of fundamental Article III principles.

The decision also highlights an often-overlooked reality of federal administrative law: the government is not a unitary actor. When Congress distributes authority across multiple agencies, it may inadvertently create gaps in judicial review. A plaintiff wronged by Agency A may have no recourse against Agency B, even if Agency B has some supervisory or investigative authority over Agency A.

## Conclusion

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a straightforward application of standing doctrine that nevertheless illuminates important principles. The D.C. Circuit correctly recognized that causation and redressability requirements cannot be satisfied through speculation about how other agencies might respond to an OSC investigation. In doing so, the court applied recent Supreme Court precedent to a new context and provided useful guidance for future cases involving multi-agency enforcement chains.

Whether Congress should create clearer pathways for judicial review in such situations is a policy question beyond the court's purview. But for now, plaintiffs seeking to compel agency investigations should be prepared to show how the investigation itself—not just potential downstream consequences—will redress their injuries. Anything less is, in the court's words, too "speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."
