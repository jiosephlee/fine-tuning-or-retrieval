Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights 'In Vacuo': The D.C. Circuit Clarifies When Mandated Agency Action Creates Judicially Enforceable Rights

The D.C. Circuit's decision last week in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* offers a pointed reminder that statutory commands to agency action—even commands using the word "shall"—do not automatically translate into judicially enforceable rights. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas held that a plaintiff lacks Article III standing to compel an investigation by the Office of Special Counsel under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, even though the statute states that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" of certain FOIA violations. The decision underscores an important limit on the procedural injury doctrine: a plaintiff must connect the asserted procedural right to a concrete interest that the statute is designed to protect.

## The Statutory Scheme and AFL's Challenge

The case arose from a FOIA request that America First Legal Foundation submitted to the Department of Justice. AFL sought emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division as part of an investigation into judicial recusals. DOJ's Civil Division reportedly refused to search employee emails without the employee's consent—a policy AFL viewed as an arbitrary and capricious withholding of information prohibited by FOIA.

AFL had separate litigation pending against DOJ to obtain the records. But AFL also asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." When OSC declined, pointing to another FOIA provision that requires three specific predicates before OSC must investigate, AFL sued to compel the investigation.

The district court agreed with AFL that § 1216 does not require satisfaction of FOIA's three predicates, but it nonetheless dismissed the case, reasoning that OSC retained discretion over whether to apply § 1214's corrective action procedures to a § 1216 investigation. On remand, OSC again declined to investigate, and AFL appealed.

## The Standing Problem

Before reaching the merits, the D.C. Circuit raised a threshold question: Does AFL have Article III standing to pursue these claims? Neither party had addressed the issue, so the court ordered supplemental briefing.

Judge Katsas's opinion walks through the familiar triad of standing requirements—injury, causation, and redressability—but focuses on a wrinkle that arises when plaintiffs claim injury from the violation of a procedural right. The Supreme Court has recognized that when a statute grants a procedural right designed to protect a plaintiff's concrete interests, courts relax—but do not eliminate—the usual requirements for imminence and redressability. Yet the plaintiff must still show that the procedural right is "designed to protect" some "concrete, particularized interest." A procedural right untethered to any such interest—what the Court in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* called a "procedural right in vacuo"—cannot support standing.

AFL offered two theories. First, it claimed that OSC's failure to investigate prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested under FOIA. Second, it asserted an informational interest in receiving notices and updates that might flow from an OSC investigation.

Both theories failed. The court's analysis of the first theory illustrates the causation and redressability problems that arise when plaintiffs try to compel agency enforcement action. OSC's investigation might—or might not—lead to a decision to pursue corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board. The MSPB might—or might not—sanction DOJ employees. DOJ might—or might not—change its FOIA practices in response. This chain requires speculation about choices by actors not before the court, including an agency (DOJ) and an adjudicatory body (the MSPB) that are not parties to this lawsuit. Under *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, such speculation about "the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts" cannot satisfy Article III causation requirements.

AFL's second theory fared no better. The court held that § 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no statutory entitlement to receive information from that investigation. AFL pointed to notice requirements that apply when OSC investigates "prohibited personnel practices" under § 1214, but as the Sixth Circuit held in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, OSC's decision to apply those procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to information, there is no concrete informational injury.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision highlights an important boundary in procedural injury doctrine. Since *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, courts have recognized that plaintiffs claiming injury from the violation of a procedural right get some slack on causation and redressability. But *Summers* and its progeny make clear that this relaxation applies only when the plaintiff identifies a concrete interest that the statutory procedure is designed to protect. The plaintiff in *Summers*, for example, challenged the Forest Service's decision to approve certain salvage timber sales without providing notice and opportunity to comment, but the plaintiff had not identified any particular timber sale that affected its members' concrete interests. The procedural right—to comment—existed "in vacuo."

*America First Legal* applies this principle in a context that arises with some frequency: a plaintiff seeking to compel an agency to investigate or enforce against a third party. The decision reinforces that plaintiffs cannot bootstrap their way into federal court by pointing to statutory language directing agency action. Even when Congress uses the word "shall," the plaintiff must identify some concrete interest—whether informational, physical, economic, or otherwise—that the statutory command is meant to protect, and that interest must be one the plaintiff actually holds.

This limitation has significant implications for litigation involving government accountability and transparency. FOIA itself creates a right to obtain records, but the D.C. Circuit has now made clear that a separate statutory scheme giving OSC authority to investigate FOIA violations does not create a judicially enforceable right for FOIA requesters. The requester's remedy for an unlawful withholding is a FOIA lawsuit against the agency that withheld the records—not a mandamus action to compel a separate agency to investigate the withholding.

## Downstream Effects

The decision may have ripple effects for similar attempts to compel agency investigations under other statutory schemes. Plaintiffs seeking to enforce statutory commands to investigate or take enforcement action will need to carefully identify the concrete interest they are pursuing—not just the abstract interest in having agencies follow the law. And they will need to show that a court order can actually redress the claimed injury, rather than merely setting in motion a chain of discretionary decisions by other actors.

Judge Katsas's opinion also serves as a reminder that standing doctrine, for all its complexity, ultimately serves to enforce the constitutional limits on the judicial power. Federal courts exist to resolve concrete disputes, not to supervise the executive branch's compliance with statutory mandates in the abstract. Even when Congress has spoken in mandatory terms, not every statutory command creates a right enforceable by every affected citizen in federal court.

*America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* thus joins a line of decisions clarifying that the procedural injury doctrine is not a workaround for Article III's case-or-controversy requirement. Procedural rights must be tethered to concrete interests, and plaintiffs seeking to compel agency action must show that judicial intervention can actually address the harm they claim.



# Investigation Without Information: When Does a Mandatory Investigation Create an Entitlement to Know?

The D.C. Circuit's decision last week in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* offers a clarifying lesson on the limits of informational injury standing—a doctrine that has long permitted plaintiffs to sue when statutes entitle them to information they have been denied. Writing for the panel, Judge Katsas drew a sharp distinction between a statutory command that an agency investigate and a statutory entitlement that a plaintiff receive information. That distinction dooms AFL's attempt to compel the Office of Special Counsel to probe the Department of Justice's alleged FOIA violations. But the reasoning has implications far beyond this particular dispute.

## The Statutory Framework at Issue

The Civil Service Reform Act empowers OSC to investigate "prohibited personnel practices" and pursue corrective or disciplinary action. Separately, Section 1216 extends OSC's reach to FOIA violations, directing the agency to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." Critically, Section 1216 further provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved."

AFL's theory was straightforward: Section 1216(a)(3) uses the word "shall," creating a mandatory duty to investigate. When OSC refused to investigate DOJ's alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent, AFL sued to compel that investigation. The district court agreed that OSC had misread the statute but concluded that the decision to investigate remained discretionary under Section 1216(c), a provision permitting OSC to use the same procedures as for prohibited personnel practices.

## The Procedural Right Problem

The standing analysis began with a now-familiar principle: a "procedural right in vacuo" cannot confer Article III standing. Citing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, Judge Katsas explained that the deprivation of a procedural right requires some concrete interest affected by that deprivation. AFL thus needed to identify some concrete harm that OSC's refusal to investigate had caused.

AFL offered two theories. First, it claimed an informational injury stemming from DOJ's failure to produce the requested FOIA records. Second, it asserted an entitlement to notices and information that would flow from an OSC investigation conducted under Section 1214's procedures.

Both theories failed. The first stumbled over causation and redressability. Any informational injury from DOJ's nondisclosure was "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." Moreover, the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ producing records was far too speculative. The court would have to assume that OSC decided to pursue corrective action, that the Merit Systems Protection Board then sanctioned DOJ employees, and that DOJ thereafter changed course and produced the records. This "requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit" as well as speculation about how OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion. The court correctly noted that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

## The Missing Entitlement

The second standing theory presented the more interesting doctrinal question. AFL invoked the informational injury doctrine, which permits standing when a plaintiff is denied information that a "statute entitled him to receive." The classic cases include *FEC v. Akins*, where the Federal Election Campaign Act created a right to receive certain campaign finance information, and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, where the Federal Advisory Committee Act entitled plaintiffs to information about advisory committee meetings.

But here, the court concluded, Section 1216 creates no such entitlement. The statute mandates an investigation but says nothing about providing information to the complainant. AFL protested that OSC could choose to give AFL the notices and information that Section 1214 requires for investigations into prohibited personnel practices. But AFL acknowledged existing precedent holding that OSC's decision whether to follow Section 1214 procedures in a Section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without any alleged entitlement to receive information under Section 1214, AFL could point to no concrete injury.

This distinction is more than semantic. *Akins* and *Public Citizen* involved statutes that specifically entitled plaintiffs to receive information. Section 1216, by contrast, directs OSC to investigate but remains silent on what information, if any, must be shared with the complainant. The court correctly recognized that a statutory command to investigate does not automatically generate an informational entitlement.

## Downstream Implications

The decision reinforces an important boundary in informational injury jurisprudence. Not every statutory directive creates a justiciable right. Plaintiffs invoking *Akins* must identify specific statutory language creating an entitlement to receive information—not merely a statutory duty that might incidentally produce information at some point through an agency's discretionary choices.

The ruling also highlights a broader tension in the law of standing. Agencies frequently have statutory duties that they may or may not fulfill, but turning those duties into privately enforceable rights requires more than a plaintiff's interest in the outcome. Congress must speak clearly to create the entitlement. Here, Section 1216's mandatory "shall" directed OSC's action but did not confer any benefit on AFL that would satisfy Article III's injury requirement.

For watchdog groups seeking to spur agency investigations, the message is clear: the mere fact that a statute says an agency "shall" investigate does not mean that disappointed complainants can sue. Without a concomitant right to receive information—or some other concrete injury flowing from the agency's inaction—the courthouse doors remain closed.

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* thus represents a disciplined application of standing principles to a statute that promised investigation but delivered no enforceable right to know. The court properly declined to expand informational injury doctrine beyond its moorings in statutory text that creates genuine entitlements.



# The Causation Conundrum: D.C. Circuit Applies Recent Supreme Court Precedent to Multi-Agency Enforcement Chains

Standing doctrine has long grappled with how to handle injuries that flow through multiple government actors before reaching the plaintiff. Last week, in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, the D.C. Circuit added a new chapter to this ongoing story—one that practitioners and scholars should read carefully for its application of recent Supreme Court precedent to multi-agency enforcement chains.

The case arose from a familiar frustration: a FOIA request denied, a lawsuit filed, and the requesting party still seeking additional avenues for relief. But the court's standing analysis broke new ground in explaining when speculative causal chains involving multiple agencies and enforcement discretion fall short of Article III requirements.

## The Factual Backdrop

America First Legal Foundation requested emails from the Department of Justice as part of an investigation into judicial recusals. DOJ's Civil Division reportedly denied the request on the ground that it does not search employee emails without the employee's consent—a policy AFL found dubious at best.

AFL did what any sophisticated requester would do: it sued under FOIA. DOJ has been producing records, and the sufficiency of that production was not before the court. But AFL also pursued a parallel track, asking the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552."

OSC declined, reasoning that the three predicates for triggering an investigation under FOIA's own enforcement provision—court-ordered production, an attorneys' fee award, and a judicial finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding—were not satisfied. AFL sued to compel the investigation. The district court agreed that OSC had committed legal error but ultimately dismissed for failure to state a claim, finding that OSC retained discretion over whether to investigate.

The D.C. Circuit never reached the merits. Instead, in an opinion by Judge Katsas, the court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing altogether.

## The Causation Problem

AFL advanced two standing theories, both of which the court rejected. But it is the first theory—that OSC's failure to investigate caused AFL to be denied the records it sought from DOJ—that offers the most instructive analysis for future cases.

The court's reasoning cuts to the heart of what makes multi-agency enforcement chains so problematic for standing purposes. Even assuming section 1216 mandates an investigation upon request (a question the court did not decide), AFL's theory required the court to speculate about a chain of events involving multiple actors not before the court:

First, OSC would have to investigate and decide to pursue corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board. Then the MSPB would have to sanction the responsible DOJ employees. Then DOJ would have to respond to that sanction by producing the requested records.

This chain, the court explained, requires "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit (the MSPB and DOJ)." The court then deployed recent Supreme Court precedent to explain why such speculation defeats standing.

## Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Independent Actors

The D.C. Circuit's application of *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024) is particularly noteworthy. In that case, the Supreme Court held that the plaintiffs lacked standing to challenge FDA's approval of mifepristone because the alleged injuries depended on the independent choices of third parties—doctors who might prescribe the medication and patients who might use it.

Here, the D.C. Circuit extended that logic to government actors. The court quoted *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*'s teaching that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The principle applies with equal force when those independent actors are other agencies.

This extension makes sense. The federal government is not a monolith. Agencies have independent statutory mandates, institutional priorities, and policy preferences. DOJ might have perfectly legitimate reasons to continue litigating its FOIA position even if OSC investigates and the MSPB sanctions individual employees. To assume otherwise is precisely the kind of conjecture that standing doctrine forbids.

## Brackeen and the Limits of Judicial Persuasion

The court also drew on *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) for a more subtle point about redressability. There, the Supreme Court emphasized that redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'"

This principle has significant implications for cases seeking to compel agency investigations. A court order requiring OSC to investigate DOJ's FOIA practices would bind only OSC. It would not—and could not—compel DOJ to produce records or the MSPB to impose sanctions. The fact that such an order might create pressure on those agencies is legally insufficient.

This distinction between binding judgments and persuasive reasoning should give pause to litigants who hope that a favorable ruling in one case will cascade through the federal bureaucracy. Courts are not in the business of issuing advisory opinions that might influence other actors in the executive branch.

## Heckler v. Chaney and Enforcement Discretion

The court's analysis also wove in *Heckler v. Chaney* (1985), the foundational case establishing that agency enforcement decisions are presumptively unreviewable. Even if section 1216 mandates an investigation, the court suggested, OSC's decisions about how to exercise its enforcement discretion—whether to pursue corrective action before the MSPB—would remain within OSC's judgment.

This point is crucial. AFL's standing theory required the court to assume not just that OSC would investigate, but that OSC would take enforcement action, that the MSPB would impose sanctions, and that DOJ would respond by producing records. Each step involves discretion that the court cannot compel.

## The Informational Injury Theory

AFL's alternative standing theory—that it had a right to information generated by the OSC investigation—fared no better. The court explained that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information. Section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no such entitlement.

AFL argued that OSC might choose to provide notices and information under section 1214's procedures. But as the court pointed out, AFL itself acknowledged that OSC's decision to apply section 1214 procedures to a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. You cannot base standing on information you have no right to receive.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision has several important implications for standing doctrine and administrative litigation.

First, it clarifies that *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*'s prohibition on speculating about independent actors applies to other agencies, not just private third parties. This should make courts more skeptical of standing theories that require agencies not before the court to take particular actions.

Second, it reinforces that *Brackeen*'s redressability principle applies with full force in intra-government disputes. A judgment binding one agency does not satisfy redressability when the plaintiff's injury depends on actions by other agencies.

Third, it suggests limits on using statutory mandates for investigation as vehicles for litigation. Even when a statute uses mandatory language—"shall conduct an investigation"—the court's analysis implies that plaintiffs still need to show how the investigation itself would redress a concrete injury.

## Downstream Effects

Practitioners should take note. This decision will make it harder to bring suits seeking to compel agency investigations when the plaintiff's ultimate injury was caused by a different agency. The logic would seem to apply equally to other contexts—for example, suits seeking to compel an agency to investigate alleged violations by another agency.

For scholars, the decision offers a useful data point in the ongoing debate about standing doctrine's rigor. Critics who argue that modern standing doctrine has become too demanding will find support in the court's refusal to accept that OSC investigation would likely lead to document production. Defenders of the doctrine will see the case as a faithful application of fundamental Article III principles.

The decision also highlights an often-overlooked reality of federal administrative law: the government is not a unitary actor. When Congress distributes authority across multiple agencies, it may inadvertently create gaps in judicial review. A plaintiff wronged by Agency A may have no recourse against Agency B, even if Agency B has some supervisory or investigative authority over Agency A.

## Conclusion

*America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a straightforward application of standing doctrine that nevertheless illuminates important principles. The D.C. Circuit correctly recognized that causation and redressability requirements cannot be satisfied through speculation about how other agencies might respond to an OSC investigation. In doing so, the court applied recent Supreme Court precedent to a new context and provided useful guidance for future cases involving multi-agency enforcement chains.

Whether Congress should create clearer pathways for judicial review in such situations is a policy question beyond the court's purview. But for now, plaintiffs seeking to compel agency investigations should be prepared to show how the investigation itself—not just potential downstream consequences—will redress their injuries. Anything less is, in the court's words, too "speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."



# The Unresolved Tension Between Section 1216 and FOIA's Own Enforcement Mechanism

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* arrives at an opportune moment for administrative law watchers, but perhaps leaves the most interesting question unanswered. The case presented a potentially significant clash between two statutory provisions governing Freedom of Information Act enforcement—provisions that appear to speak past each other in ways that could reshape how agencies handle disclosure obligations. Instead of resolving that clash, however, Judge Katsas's opinion for the panel vacated the district court's judgment on Article III standing grounds, leaving the underlying statutory interpretation fight for another day.

The question that went unanswered matters for anyone who litigates FOIA cases or watches executive branch accountability. Section 1216 of the Civil Service Reform Act states that the Office of Special Counsel "shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." The language is mandatory: "shall conduct." Yet FOIA itself contains its own enforcement mechanism, one triggered only after a court has ordered production of records, awarded attorneys' fees, and "specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously." Only then must OSC "promptly initiate a proceeding" to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted.

The tension is obvious. One statute appears to obligate OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding. The other requires three judicial predicates before OSC gets involved. Read one way, Section 1216 gives OSC an independent investigative mandate that operates alongside FOIA's judicial enforcement scheme. Read another way, FOIA's specific enforcement provision cabins or limits OSC's general authority under Section 1216—a classic canon scenario where the specific governs the general.

The district court sided with AFL on the threshold interpretive question. Judge Rudolph Contreras concluded that Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under Section 1216. The court reasoned that OSC's decision not to investigate rested on legal error—the agency had essentially treated FOIA's enforcement mechanism as exhaustive when the statute says nothing of the sort. That interpretation has considerable textual force. Section 1216 speaks in absolute terms, and nothing in its text cross-references or defers to FOIA's enforcement provision.

But the district court nonetheless stopped short of granting AFL the relief it sought. The court concluded that Section 1216(c), which states that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214" for FOIA violations, committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion. The word "may" in that cross-reference, the court held, meant that even if Section 1216(a)(3)'s mandatory language created an obligation to investigate, OSC retained discretion over how and when to exercise its enforcement authority. The court remanded to OSC for further consideration; OSC again declined to investigate; and the district court dismissed the case.

The D.C. Circuit never reached that discretionary analysis, nor the underlying question of whether FOIA's enforcement provision limits Section 1216. Instead, the panel held that AFL lacked Article III standing to pursue its claims. The analysis is characteristically crisp. AFL's first theory—that OSC's refusal to investigate caused AFL's ongoing informational injury from DOJ's failure to produce records—failed for lack of causation and redressability. Any causal connection between an OSC investigation and DOJ's production decisions was simply too speculative. The chain of contingencies involved OSC's investigative choices, the Merit Systems Protection Board's potential disciplinary rulings, and DOJ's response to any such sanctions. As the court noted, citing *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, causation cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court.

AFL's second theory—that it had a concrete informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself—similarly faltered. The court held that Section 1216 creates no statutory entitlement to receive information. AFL pointed to Section 1214's notice requirements, but acknowledged that OSC's decision whether to apply those requirements in a Section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a right to information, there is no informational injury.

The standing analysis is sound, but the dismissal leaves the statutory tension intact. And that tension matters beyond this case. If Section 1216 provides OSC independent authority to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, it creates an alternative enforcement pathway that bypasses FOIA's judicial prerequisites. Requesters could potentially trigger OSC investigations without first prevailing in FOIA litigation—a meaningful change in the enforcement landscape. The district court's interpretation would also give OSC a broader role in policing agency disclosure practices, one that does not depend on courts first finding arbitrary withholding.

The counter-reading has its own appeal. Congress carefully structured FOIA's enforcement scheme. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s three predicates serve as meaningful gatekeepers—they ensure that OSC disciplinary proceedings follow judicial findings of arbitrary conduct, not merely allegations by requesters. Reading Section 1216 to sweep more broadly would effectively nullify those gatekeeping provisions. The specific-over-general canon supports that reading, as does the principle against interpreting statutes to create surplusage.

The ambiguity also raises separation-of-powers considerations. If Section 1216 obligates OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, who gets to define what counts as "arbitrary or capricious"? If OSC can decline to investigate based on its own interpretation of FOIA's substantive standards, the "shall conduct" language becomes something closer to "may conduct at its discretion." The district court's reading of Section 1216(c) essentially reaches that conclusion already.

The practical implications extend beyond FOIA. Section 1216 also empowers OSC to investigate other forms of employee misconduct, including whistleblower retaliation. How courts resolve the tension between mandatory investigation language and discretionary enforcement cross-references could affect the entire scope of OSC's statutory mandate.

For now, the question remains open. The D.C. Circuit's standing ruling means the district court's statutory interpretation—though thoughtful and well-reasoned—carries no precedential weight. Future litigants will need to craft their claims more carefully, perhaps by alleging concrete injuries that flow more directly from OSC's refusal to investigate. Or perhaps Congress will clarify the statutory relationship. Until then, OSC retains considerable discretion to define the scope of its own investigative authority, and requesters who want to compel investigations face a daunting road ahead.
