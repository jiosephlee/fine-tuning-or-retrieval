Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Mootness and Civil Penalties: When Does Full Compensation End a Plaintiff's Stake in the Case?

What happens to a plaintiff's stake in litigation once they've been paid everything they're personally owed? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* tackles this increasingly relevant question at the intersection of Article III standing doctrine and state wage-and-hour enforcement. The opinion raises important questions about whether civil penalties—payable to the government rather than the plaintiff—can sustain a private action after the plaintiff's personal damages have been fully satisfied.

## The Factual and Procedural Background

Bruce Cohen worked as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer for Consilio, a legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. In July 2019, Consilio informed Cohen and other Minnesota-based reviewers that it was eliminating overtime premium pay. Under the new policy, these employees would receive only their base rate for all hours worked, including overtime hours.

Cohen sued in August 2020, alleging violations of three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). He sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Here's where things get interesting. Consilio paid Cohen and similarly situated employees $256,010.01 to cover all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed" and that he had received all liquidated damages to which he was entitled.

What remained unresolved were statutory penalties—$172,080 under the MPWA, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. The parties agreed to let the district court resolve Consilio's summary judgment motion before proceeding further. When the district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, Cohen appealed.

## The Court's Standing Analysis

The Eighth Circuit's handling of Cohen's MFLSA claim is the most doctrinally significant portion of the opinion. Writing for the panel, Judge Kelly raised a jurisdictional concern that neither party had briefed: whether Cohen retained standing to pursue statutory penalties after stipulating that he had received all personal compensation owed to him.

The court acknowledged that civil penalties payable to the government can, under Supreme Court precedent, "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The court cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), which held that civil penalties can serve the goals of encouraging defendants to discontinue current violations and deterring future ones.

But the court noted that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing "for each form of relief that is sought." And the requisite personal interest "must continue throughout its existence"—the mootness doctrine. The critical question became whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remained in effect. If the policy was still operative, Cohen's claim for penalties might still be viable because ongoing unlawful conduct could support standing. But if the policy had been discontinued and "it is absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur," the claim might be moot.

Because the record did not clearly establish whether Consilio's policy remained in place, the court vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction.

## The MPWA and MWTA Claims

On Cohen's other claims, the court was less equivocal. The MPWA claim failed because the statute only authorizes the Commissioner of the Department of Labor and Industry—not private plaintiffs—to seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. The court's textual analysis was straightforward: the statute references penalties only in connection with the Commissioner's enforcement authority. And the Minnesota legislature, the court observed, clearly knew how to authorize private penalty actions when it wished to do so—as it did in §§ 181.13 and 181.14.

The MWTA claim met a different fate. Cohen failed to develop any meaningful argument on appeal regarding his entitlement to penalties under this statute. The court declined to do his work for him, affirming the district court's dismissal.

## The Tension Between Deterrence and Article III

What makes *Cohen* significant is the way it surfaces an unresolved tension in standing doctrine. Laidlaw established that civil penalties payable to the government can provide sufficient redress to sustain citizen suits under environmental statutes. The deterrent effect of penalties, even when paid to the treasury rather than the plaintiff, can constitute a concrete benefit to the plaintiff by preventing future harm.

But wage-and-hour litigation presents a different context. Environmental violations often involve ongoing public harms that affect plaintiffs and non-parties alike. Wage violations, by contrast, typically involve discrete past harms to identifiable employees. Once those employees have been fully compensated, what ongoing interest do they retain in pursuing penalties?

The answer depends on whether the unlawful policy or practice remains in effect. This is the factual question the Eighth Circuit has remanded for the district court to resolve. If Consilio's no-premium-overtime policy remains in place, Cohen might have standing to pursue penalties. But if the policy has been discontinued, his claim may well be moot.

Judge Kobes, in a special concurrence, expressed skepticism about whether Cohen has standing under Laidlaw at all, noting "differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." Neither party briefed the issue, suggesting that the jurisdictional questions caught both sides by surprise.

## Strategic Implications for Defendants and Plaintiffs

The opinion raises important strategic considerations for both sides in wage-and-hour litigation. For defendants, the case suggests a potential path to mooting claims: pay all personal damages owed to named plaintiffs, then demonstrate that the unlawful policy has been discontinued. This approach could eliminate not only the damages claims but also the penalties claims, potentially avoiding class certification altogether.

But the court's remand instruction suggests this strategy isn't automatic. Defendants must demonstrate that the challenged conduct has ceased and that there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. The "voluntary cessation" doctrine means that a defendant's unilateral action to end unlawful conduct does not automatically deprive a court of jurisdiction. The burden rests with the defendant to show that the conduct is genuinely discontinued.

For plaintiffs, the case underscores the importance of preserving standing throughout litigation. Seeking injunctive relief in addition to damages can help maintain a live controversy even after personal damages have been satisfied. Cohen's problem was that he stipulated to full payment of his personal damages without securing a clear record about whether the policy remained in effect—and his request for injunctive relief was deemed waived because he changed theories mid-stream.

## The Broader Stakes

The Eighth Circuit's approach has implications beyond wage-and-hour cases. Many state labor statutes include civil penalties payable to state agencies rather than private plaintiffs. If compensation alone can moot these claims, private enforcement could become significantly more difficult. And if defendants can strategically moot claims by tendering payment to named plaintiffs before class certification, the class action mechanism could be undermined.

Conversely, allowing plaintiffs to pursue penalties after full compensation raises Article III concerns. If penalties serve only deterrent purposes and provide no personal benefit to the plaintiff, the standing inquiry requires careful attention to whether the plaintiff has a sufficient ongoing stake in the outcome.

The district court's resolution of these questions on remand will be worth watching. For now, *Cohen* serves as a reminder that jurisdictional issues can arise at any stage of litigation—and that stipulations about damages, while efficient, can have unintended consequences for the broader remedial goals of wage-and-hour enforcement.
