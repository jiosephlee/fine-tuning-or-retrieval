Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The 'Legislature Knows How' Canon: Parsing Private Enforcement Rights Under Minnesota Wage Law

When an employer fails to pay overtime, employees naturally look to the statutes protecting them and ask: what remedies can I pursue? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* offers a useful lesson in how courts answer that question through the lens of statutory construction—and a reminder that the availability of civil penalties doesn't always extend to private litigants.

The case arose from a straightforward wage dispute. Consilio, a legal document review company, eliminated premium overtime pay for its Minnesota-based hourly attorneys in 2019. Bruce Cohen sued under three Minnesota statutes: the Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Before summary judgment, Consilio paid Cohen and similarly situated employees all the overtime wages and liquidated damages they sought. What remained were Cohen's claims for statutory penalties—approximately $256,000 worth—and injunctive relief.

## The Commissioner-Only Problem

Judge Kelly's majority opinion methodically worked through each claim, but the MPWA analysis is where the court's interpretive methodology shines. Cohen argued that § 181.101 authorized him to seek "average daily wage penalties"—essentially a per-day penalty for each day the employer failed to pay owed wages. The statute's plain language, he contended, supported this reading.

The court disagreed, and it did so through careful attention to what the statute actually says. Section 181.101(a) references the Commissioner of the Department of Labor and Industry as the enforcement officer for penalties. The statute provides that the Commissioner "may charge and collect" penalties after serving a demand for payment, and critically, "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." As the court noted, the statute reserves to employees only the right to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages"—not to collect penalties directly.

## The 'Legislature Knows How' Canon in Action

What makes the opinion particularly instructive is how the court deployed what's known as the "legislature knows how" canon of statutory construction. Minnesota's own Supreme Court has recognized that "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so."

The Eighth Circuit pointed to §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2—provisions addressing discharged employees and those who resign. Both statutes explicitly state that an employee "may charge and collect a penalty." The contrast with § 181.101 is stark. Had the legislature wanted to empower private employees to seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101, it knew how to draft that language. Its absence speaks volumes.

This approach reflects a disciplined form of statutory interpretation that refuses to rewrite statutes under the guise of interpreting them. Courts applying this canon recognize that legislatures make choices, and those choices matter. When a statute creates an enforcement mechanism that routes through an administrative official rather than private litigation, that structural decision deserves respect.

## The Standing Question Lurking Beneath

Perhaps the most intriguing aspect of *Cohen* is what the court declined to resolve. After Cohen stipulated that he'd received all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed, his MFLSA claim for statutory penalties presented a thorny jurisdictional question. The penalties under the MFLSA, unlike liquidated damages, are payable to the Commissioner—not to Cohen personally.

The court flagged a serious standing concern. Under *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates*, a plaintiff must demonstrate standing "for each form of relief that is sought." Civil penalties paid to the government can sometimes satisfy Article III requirements if they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." But the Eighth Circuit wasn't prepared to resolve whether Cohen's claim survived mootness when Consilio had already paid all wages owed.

Instead, the court vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine whether it even had jurisdiction. The move is procedurally cautious—federal courts, after all, must assure themselves of their jurisdiction before proceeding to the merits.

Judge Kobes, concurring specially, expressed skepticism that the standing analysis from *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services* transfers cleanly to this context. He may be right. Environmental statutes like the Clean Water Act expressly authorize citizen suits with penalties flowing to the U.S. Treasury. The MFLSA's enforcement structure differs meaningfully, and neither party briefed the question thoroughly.

## Doctrinal Implications

*Cohen* reinforces an important principle for litigants navigating state wage laws: the existence of statutory penalties doesn't automatically translate into a private right of action. Lawyers drafting complaints would do well to closely examine whether penalties flow to the employee or to a state official. The distinction matters for both liability and standing.

The decision also illustrates how courts approach statutory enforcement schemes more broadly. When a legislature creates an administrative enforcement mechanism alongside a private right of action, the boundaries of each must be respected. Courts won't expand private enforcement authority by implication, even when the underlying policy goals might support it.

For employers facing wage claims, the case offers a reminder that early payment of actual damages owed may substantially reduce exposure. Consilio's decision to pay all wages and liquidated damages before summary judgment narrowed the case to questions of penalties—a strategy that ultimately limited its liability even though it didn't win a complete victory on all claims.

On remand, the district court will need to confront whether Cohen has adequately demonstrated that Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in effect. If the policy has been discontinued and wrongful conduct "could not reasonably be expected to recur," the case may well be moot. But as *Laidlaw* established, a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice doesn't automatically deprive a court of jurisdiction.

The Eighth Circuit's opinion doesn't break new doctrinal ground, but it applies settled principles with care and clarity. That's precisely what makes it worth reading.



# Mootness and Civil Penalties: When Does Full Compensation End a Plaintiff's Stake in the Case?

What happens to a plaintiff's stake in litigation once they've been paid everything they're personally owed? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* tackles this increasingly relevant question at the intersection of Article III standing doctrine and state wage-and-hour enforcement. The opinion raises important questions about whether civil penalties—payable to the government rather than the plaintiff—can sustain a private action after the plaintiff's personal damages have been fully satisfied.

## The Factual and Procedural Background

Bruce Cohen worked as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer for Consilio, a legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. In July 2019, Consilio informed Cohen and other Minnesota-based reviewers that it was eliminating overtime premium pay. Under the new policy, these employees would receive only their base rate for all hours worked, including overtime hours.

Cohen sued in August 2020, alleging violations of three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). He sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Here's where things get interesting. Consilio paid Cohen and similarly situated employees $256,010.01 to cover all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed" and that he had received all liquidated damages to which he was entitled.

What remained unresolved were statutory penalties—$172,080 under the MPWA, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. The parties agreed to let the district court resolve Consilio's summary judgment motion before proceeding further. When the district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, Cohen appealed.

## The Court's Standing Analysis

The Eighth Circuit's handling of Cohen's MFLSA claim is the most doctrinally significant portion of the opinion. Writing for the panel, Judge Kelly raised a jurisdictional concern that neither party had briefed: whether Cohen retained standing to pursue statutory penalties after stipulating that he had received all personal compensation owed to him.

The court acknowledged that civil penalties payable to the government can, under Supreme Court precedent, "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The court cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), which held that civil penalties can serve the goals of encouraging defendants to discontinue current violations and deterring future ones.

But the court noted that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing "for each form of relief that is sought." And the requisite personal interest "must continue throughout its existence"—the mootness doctrine. The critical question became whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remained in effect. If the policy was still operative, Cohen's claim for penalties might still be viable because ongoing unlawful conduct could support standing. But if the policy had been discontinued and "it is absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur," the claim might be moot.

Because the record did not clearly establish whether Consilio's policy remained in place, the court vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction.

## The MPWA and MWTA Claims

On Cohen's other claims, the court was less equivocal. The MPWA claim failed because the statute only authorizes the Commissioner of the Department of Labor and Industry—not private plaintiffs—to seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. The court's textual analysis was straightforward: the statute references penalties only in connection with the Commissioner's enforcement authority. And the Minnesota legislature, the court observed, clearly knew how to authorize private penalty actions when it wished to do so—as it did in §§ 181.13 and 181.14.

The MWTA claim met a different fate. Cohen failed to develop any meaningful argument on appeal regarding his entitlement to penalties under this statute. The court declined to do his work for him, affirming the district court's dismissal.

## The Tension Between Deterrence and Article III

What makes *Cohen* significant is the way it surfaces an unresolved tension in standing doctrine. Laidlaw established that civil penalties payable to the government can provide sufficient redress to sustain citizen suits under environmental statutes. The deterrent effect of penalties, even when paid to the treasury rather than the plaintiff, can constitute a concrete benefit to the plaintiff by preventing future harm.

But wage-and-hour litigation presents a different context. Environmental violations often involve ongoing public harms that affect plaintiffs and non-parties alike. Wage violations, by contrast, typically involve discrete past harms to identifiable employees. Once those employees have been fully compensated, what ongoing interest do they retain in pursuing penalties?

The answer depends on whether the unlawful policy or practice remains in effect. This is the factual question the Eighth Circuit has remanded for the district court to resolve. If Consilio's no-premium-overtime policy remains in place, Cohen might have standing to pursue penalties. But if the policy has been discontinued, his claim may well be moot.

Judge Kobes, in a special concurrence, expressed skepticism about whether Cohen has standing under Laidlaw at all, noting "differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." Neither party briefed the issue, suggesting that the jurisdictional questions caught both sides by surprise.

## Strategic Implications for Defendants and Plaintiffs

The opinion raises important strategic considerations for both sides in wage-and-hour litigation. For defendants, the case suggests a potential path to mooting claims: pay all personal damages owed to named plaintiffs, then demonstrate that the unlawful policy has been discontinued. This approach could eliminate not only the damages claims but also the penalties claims, potentially avoiding class certification altogether.

But the court's remand instruction suggests this strategy isn't automatic. Defendants must demonstrate that the challenged conduct has ceased and that there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. The "voluntary cessation" doctrine means that a defendant's unilateral action to end unlawful conduct does not automatically deprive a court of jurisdiction. The burden rests with the defendant to show that the conduct is genuinely discontinued.

For plaintiffs, the case underscores the importance of preserving standing throughout litigation. Seeking injunctive relief in addition to damages can help maintain a live controversy even after personal damages have been satisfied. Cohen's problem was that he stipulated to full payment of his personal damages without securing a clear record about whether the policy remained in effect—and his request for injunctive relief was deemed waived because he changed theories mid-stream.

## The Broader Stakes

The Eighth Circuit's approach has implications beyond wage-and-hour cases. Many state labor statutes include civil penalties payable to state agencies rather than private plaintiffs. If compensation alone can moot these claims, private enforcement could become significantly more difficult. And if defendants can strategically moot claims by tendering payment to named plaintiffs before class certification, the class action mechanism could be undermined.

Conversely, allowing plaintiffs to pursue penalties after full compensation raises Article III concerns. If penalties serve only deterrent purposes and provide no personal benefit to the plaintiff, the standing inquiry requires careful attention to whether the plaintiff has a sufficient ongoing stake in the outcome.

The district court's resolution of these questions on remand will be worth watching. For now, *Cohen* serves as a reminder that jurisdictional issues can arise at any stage of litigation—and that stipulations about damages, while efficient, can have unintended consequences for the broader remedial goals of wage-and-hour enforcement.



# Procedural Lessons from Cohen v. Consilio: Preserving Claims and Proper Pleading in Wage Litigation

When a plaintiff secures payment of unpaid wages and liquidated damages, one might expect the litigation to wind down. But in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit's October 6, 2025 decision illustrates how the pursuit of statutory penalties and injunctive relief can create thorny procedural problems—and how failing to properly preserve arguments can doom otherwise viable claims.

The case offers a timely reminder that procedural discipline matters as much as substantive rights. Bruce Cohen, an attorney who performed document review for Consilio, challenged the company's elimination of overtime premium pay under three Minnesota statutes. After Consilio paid all contested overtime wages and liquidated damages, Cohen pressed forward seeking roughly $256,000 in statutory penalties across his various claims. The Eighth Circuit affirmed dismissal of most claims, vacated one for potential mootness, and along the way delivered pointed lessons on waiver, standing, and the importance of amending pleadings properly.

## The MWTA Claim: A textbook waiver

Cohen's most avoidable error came in his appeal of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act claim. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio, and on appeal, Cohen complained that the lower court had offered "little to no discussion of this claim." The Eighth Circuit was unsympathetic. Cohen had "stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him" and, critically, "point[ed] to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute."

The court's response was swift: "With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim." Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, the panel reiterated the well-established principle that failure to support an assertion with argument or legal authority constitutes waiver.

This should serve as a cautionary tale for appellate practitioners. The Eighth Circuit will not construct arguments on a litigant's behalf, particularly where—as here—the underlying statute appears silent on the availability of civil penalties to private plaintiffs. Cohen apparently assumed that the general remedial structure of Minnesota's wage laws would carry his claim, but the court demanded statutory citation. When none materialized, the claim evaporated.

## Injunctive relief and the perils of briefing new claims

Cohen's request for injunctive relief met a similar fate, though for a different procedural failing. In his complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. But by the summary judgment stage, he had pivoted to request a different remedy—an order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The district court, unsurprisingly, did not address this new formulation.

The Eighth Circuit refused to consider it either. "New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs," the court explained, citing *InfoDeli, LLC* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). The panel correctly noted that the issue was "not properly before" them.

This holding underscores a fundamental principle of federal litigation: summary judgment is not the time to remodel your claims. Litigants who discover mid-case that they want different relief must use the procedural mechanisms designed for that purpose. Rule 15(a) permits amendment of pleadings, and courts are generally liberal in allowing such amendments early in litigation. But a party cannot simply slide new theories into summary judgment briefing and expect the court—trial or appellate—to engage.

The strategic lesson is clear: if your understanding of what relief you need evolves during discovery, formalize that evolution through an amended pleading. The alternative is losing the claim entirely.

## The MFLSA claim and the standing problem the parties ignored

Perhaps the most doctrinally interesting portion of the decision involves Cohen's MFLSA claim. Here, the parties had stipulated that Cohen received all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. What remained was Cohen's request for statutory penalties—a remedy the parties agreed would be payable to the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry, not to Cohen personally.

The Eighth Circuit, acting sua sponte, flagged "a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue." The court's analysis draws on *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, where the Supreme Court held that civil penalties paid to the government can nonetheless provide redress to private plaintiffs by encouraging defendants to cease violations and deterring future misconduct. But the court emphasized that standing must exist "for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought."

This created a potential mootness problem. If Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy is no longer in effect and there is no reasonable expectation it will recur, Cohen's claim for penalties might well be moot. The court vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment and remanded for jurisdictional fact-finding.

Judge Kobes, concurring specially, expressed skepticism about Cohen's standing under *Laidlaw*, noting that "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." He also flagged that neither party had briefed the issue—a striking omission given the jurisdictional stakes.

The takeaway for practitioners is twofold. First, courts will police standing requirements even when parties do not, particularly where Article III's case-or-controversy requirements are implicated. Second, plaintiffs seeking penalties payable to the government must be prepared to demonstrate an ongoing injury or a reasonable likelihood of recurrence—especially after defendants have paid all direct monetary relief.

## The MPWA claim: Statutory interpretation and legislative intent

The court's affirmance of summary judgment on Cohen's MPWA claim merits brief mention for its clean statutory analysis. Cohen sought "average daily wage penalties" under §§ 181.171 and 181.101. The court held that § 181.101(a) grants enforcement authority over penalties exclusively to the Commissioner, who must first serve a demand for payment and may then collect penalties that are ultimately paid to the employee.

The court's reasoning turns on the statutory text: penalties are available only if the Commissioner "serve[s] a demand" and "may charge and collect" both wages and penalties. The statute expressly reserves to employees only the right to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages"—not penalties. The court bolstered this reading by contrasting § 181.101 with other Minnesota wage statutes that explicitly authorize employees to "charge and collect" penalties themselves.

This comparative-statute approach—showing that the legislature "knew how" to grant penalty authority to employees when it wished to do so—reflects a well-established canon of statutory construction and provides a model for similar wage-and-hour disputes.

## Practical lessons for wage litigation

*Cohen v. Consilio* ultimately reads as a case study in procedural self-sabotage. Cohen secured meaningful monetary relief: Consilio paid over $256,000 to himself and similarly situated employees. But his pursuit of penalties and injunctive relief ran aground on multiple procedural shoals.

For practitioners, the decision offers several lessons. Preserve every argument with cited authority—waiver remains a real threat. Amend pleadings formally rather than shifting theories in summary judgment briefs. And anticipate standing and mootness challenges when the relief sought flows to the government rather than the plaintiff. These principles may seem elementary, but *Cohen* demonstrates how easily they can be forgotten in the heat of litigation.

The remanded MFLSA claim may yet produce additional guidance on when penalties payable to government agencies can sustain private standing. But for now, *Cohen* stands as a reminder that procedural discipline is not mere formalism—it is the difference between preserving claims and watching them disappear.
