Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What does the "legislature knows how to" argument mean in statutory interpretation?
Question:
I'm reading this Eighth Circuit opinion interpreting Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act. The court concludes that employees can't seek penalties under § 181.101 because that statute only references the Commissioner regarding penalties. But the court then compares other Minnesota statutes (like § 181.13) that explicitly give employees the right to collect penalties, saying the legislature 'knew how to' give employees that right if it wanted to. Is this a form of expressio unius? Why does the language in a completely different statute tell us anything about legislative intent for this particular statute? This seems like comparing apples to oranges.
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important statutory interpretation principle that courts employ regularly. The "legislature knows how to" argument is indeed related to *expressio unius*, though it functions as a distinct analytical tool.

**The Doctrine Explained**

The "legislature knows how to" canon permits courts to compare related statutes enacted by the same legislature to infer intentional drafting choices. The logic is straightforward: if the legislature used specific language to achieve a particular result in one statute but omitted that language from a similar statute, the omission was likely deliberate.

As the Eighth Circuit explains, quoting the Minnesota Supreme Court:

> "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so" (citing *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022)).

**Application in This Case**

The court applies this principle by contrasting § 181.101 with §§ 181.13 and 181.14:

- **§ 181.13(a)**: Explicitly provides that a discharged employee "may charge and collect a penalty"
- **§ 181.14, subd. 2**: Similarly gives employees who quit the right to "charge and collect a penalty"
- **§ 181.101**: Contains no such language—it references only the Commissioner's authority to "charge and collect" penalties

The inference is that the Minnesota legislature, when drafting § 181.101, chose not to grant employees the same direct enforcement mechanism it provided elsewhere. If the legislature "wished" to empower employees directly, it "knew how to" do so—it simply declined to do so here.

**Why Different Statutes Matter**

This isn't comparing "apples to oranges" because these provisions are part of the same legislative scheme governing wage payments. The court presumes the legislature acts with knowledge of its own enactments. When the same legislature uses different language across related statutes addressing similar remedial schemes, courts may infer different meanings were intended.

This approach is narrower than pure *expressio unius* because it doesn't rely solely on what's absent within a single statute—it affirmatively demonstrates the legislature's capacity to draft language achieving a particular result when desired.

### Can a plaintiff have Article III standing to seek civil penalties payable to the government?
Question:
In this case, Cohen sought statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act. The court notes these penalties are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen himself. Yet the court cites Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for the proposition that civil penalties 'may afford redress to citizen plaintiffs' through deterrence. I'm confused about how a plaintiff has standing to seek penalties that don't go to them personally. I thought standing required a personal injury that the relief would redress. If the penalties go to the government, how do they redress the plaintiff's injury? What's the theory here?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified a subtle but important question about the redressability prong of Article III standing. The short answer is that civil penalties payable to the government can redress a plaintiff's injury through **deterrence**—by discouraging the defendant from continuing or repeating the unlawful conduct that harmed the plaintiff.

The court in *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* explained this theory: civil penalties "may afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). The redressability requirement is satisfied not because the plaintiff receives the money, but because the penalty gives the defendant a concrete reason to stop the harmful behavior. The plaintiff's injury—the ongoing unlawful conduct—is thus addressed indirectly through deterrence.

There are two key limitations embedded in this doctrine, both reflected in the *Cohen* opinion:

**1. The plaintiff must have a continuing injury or threat of future harm.** The deterrence rationale only works if there is something still to deter. As the court notes, the claim for penalties "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." *Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189. This is why the Eighth Circuit remanded the MFLSA claim—to determine whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in effect. If the policy is gone and there's no realistic chance it returns, there's no ongoing injury to redress, and the claim becomes moot.

**2. The defendant's voluntary cessation doesn't automatically moot the claim.** The court emphasizes that "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice." *Id.* (quoting *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)). The burden is on the defendant to show that the wrongful conduct "could not reasonably be expected to recur."

Judge Kobes' concurrence raises a fair point: there may be meaningful differences between the citizen-suit penalties at issue in *Laidlaw* (a Clean Water Act case) and the state-law statutory penalties here. But as neither party briefed the standing issue, the court left that question open, focusing instead on the potential mootness problem—whether Cohen still has a live controversy given that he's already been paid for his actual damages.

In short: standing to seek government-payable civil penalties exists under *Laidlaw*'s deterrence theory, but it hinges on the plaintiff showing a continuing injury that the penalties could help prevent.

### Does paying a plaintiff's damages make a claim moot if the challenged policy might still be in effect?
Question:
The defendant in this case paid the plaintiff all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. The parties stipulated he'd been paid everything. But Cohen still sought statutory penalties, and the court remanded to determine if the claim is moot because it's unclear whether Consilio's 'violative overtime policy is still in place.' I'm confused about the mootness analysis here. If Cohen has been fully compensated for his personal losses, why does the ongoing policy matter for his penalties claim? What does 'absolutely clear that the wrongful conduct will not recur' mean in practice? Is this about his standing or about the court's ability to grant meaningful relief?
Answer:
**The short answer: This is about Article III mootness—the court's jurisdiction to hear the case. The ongoing policy matters because if the policy remains in effect, there is ongoing unlawful conduct that statutory penalties could deter, which keeps the claim alive. If the policy is definitively discontinued, the claim may be moot because there would be no ongoing injury for the court to redress.**

## Mootness vs. Standing

Your instinct about "the court's ability to grant meaningful relief" is correct. Mootness and standing are related but distinct doctrines. Standing requires a concrete, particularized injury at the outset of litigation. Mootness requires that injury to continue throughout. As the court notes, quoting *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, the "personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)."

Here, Cohen had standing when he filed—he wasn't being paid overtime properly. The question is whether that injury continues now that he's been paid.

## Why the Ongoing Policy Matters for Penalties

The key fact is that **statutory penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally.** So what personal stake does Cohen have in pursuing them?

The court draws directly from *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Even though civil penalties go to the government, they can "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

So Cohen's claim for penalties is **not moot** if Consilio's violative overtime policy "remains in place." The penalties would serve their deterrent function against ongoing unlawful conduct.

Conversely, if Consilio's policy "is no longer in place and it is 'absolutely clear' that its wrongful conduct will not recur," then the penalties claim "may in fact be moot." There would be no ongoing violation to deter.

## "Absolutely Clear" the Wrongful Conduct Will Not Recur

This language comes from the Supreme Court's "voluntary cessation" doctrine. As the court explains, a "defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice."

The defendant bears a heavy burden to show that the allegedly wrongful behavior "could not reasonably be expected to recur." Simply paying Cohen what he was owed doesn't automatically make it "absolutely clear" the policy won't be applied again—to other employees, or even to Cohen if he continues working there. That's why the court remanded: the record doesn't establish whether the policy is still in effect.

## Summary

The remand is necessary because the court cannot grant meaningful relief (statutory penalties serving a deterrent function) unless there is ongoing unlawful conduct. If Consilio has definitively abandoned the overtime policy, the penalties claim may be moot. If the policy continues, Cohen retains his personal stake in litigation—deterring ongoing violations that affect him and similarly situated employees.

### What constitutes 'meaningful argument' to avoid waiver on appeal?
Question:
The court affirmed dismissal of Cohen's MWTA claim because he 'points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties' and provided 'no meaningful argument in support of reversal.' The court cites Milligan v. City of Red Oak saying that unsupported assertions waive the issue. But Cohen did bring this claim in his complaint. How much argument is 'enough' on appeal? Does a party need to cite specific cases and statutory text, or is a general reference to the claim sufficient? If the argument was made below, does it still get waived if not adequately briefed on appeal?
Answer:
### What constitutes 'meaningful argument' to avoid waiver on appeal?

The short answer is that **raising a claim in the complaint does not preserve it for appeal**—you must independently brief and argue each issue on appeal with supporting legal authority.

The court's handling of Cohen's MWTA claim illustrates the waiver standard clearly.

**The Legal Standard**

The Eighth Circuit applies a straightforward rule: an appellate brief must support assertions with both argument *and* legal authority, or the issue is waived. The court cites *Milligan v. City of Red Oak* for this principle:

> "[I]nasmuch as [defendant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." (230 F.3d at 360)

**What Cohen Failed to Do**

Cohen argued on appeal that the district court gave "little to no discussion" of his MWTA claim. But the court rejected this complaint because Cohen himself "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute."

Note the court's specific criticism: Cohen failed to identify *any* statutory basis for the civil penalties he sought. The MWTA claim was for failure to provide notice of a policy change, but Cohen offered no legal argument explaining why this entitled him to penalties.

**The Answer to Your Questions**

1. **How much argument is "enough"?** At minimum, you must explain *why* you should win and support that explanation with legal authority. A general reference to the claim or a bare assertion that the district court erred is insufficient.

2. **Must you cite specific statutory text or cases?** Yes. The court's criticism—that Cohen "points to no statutory authority"—shows that you need to identify the specific legal basis for your requested relief. You cannot simply invoke a statute generally; you must explain how the statutory text or case law supports your position.

3. **Does raising the argument below preserve it?** No. The fact that Cohen properly pleaded the MWTA claim in his complaint was irrelevant to the appeal. The court treated waiver as occurring at the appellate stage due to inadequate briefing. Each stage requires its own advocacy—you don't get credit for arguments made in earlier filings.

**Practical Takeaway**

On appeal, treat every claim as if you're arguing it from scratch. Identify the legal standard, apply it to the facts, and cite specific authority. The appellate court will not construct arguments for you or search the record for support you failed to provide.

### Can a plaintiff change the injunctive relief they're seeking at summary judgment?
Question:
Cohen's complaint sought specific forms of injunctive relief, but in his summary judgment briefing, he requested different relief—asking the court to order Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The court said it 'did not consider it' because the issue wasn't properly before them, citing a rule that 'new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.' I'm confused about when a change in requested relief requires an amended complaint versus when it's just a refinement of an existing claim. Isn't summary judgment exactly the time when parties clarify what relief they're asking for? What's the line between refining your claim and creating a new one?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is that a plaintiff cannot use summary judgment briefing to substantively change the relief they're seeking. The court rejected Cohen's request because he was not merely "clarifying" his claim—he was seeking different injunctive relief than what he had pleaded in his complaint.

**The governing principle: new claims require amended pleadings**

The Eighth Circuit's holding rests on a straightforward procedural rule: parties must raise new claims through amended complaints, not through summary judgment briefing. The court cited *InfoDeli, LLC* for this proposition and referenced Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), which governs amendments to pleadings.

This rule serves important purposes: it ensures the defendant receives proper notice of the claims and relief sought, and it gives the defendant a meaningful opportunity to respond—including through additional discovery if needed. When Cohen attempted to change his requested injunctive relief in his summary judgment opposition, Consilio had no opportunity to respond to this new request through amended pleadings, additional discovery, or a properly framed summary judgment motion.

**The line between refinement and new claim**

The key distinction is whether you are specifying details of an already-pleaded claim or asserting something substantively different:

**Permissible refinement:** Calculating specific damages amounts based on evidence developed in discovery, clarifying the scope of relief already requested, or updating figures to account for additional harm. This type of elaboration typically does not require an amended complaint because the opposing party was already on notice of the general nature of the relief sought.

**New claim:** Asserting a different legal theory, requesting a different category of damages, or—as here—seeking a different form of injunctive relief. The court emphasized that Cohen's complaint sought "certain specific forms of injunctive relief," but in his summary judgment briefing he "change[d] course" to request that Consilio rescind its 2019 overtime policy. This was not clarification; it was a new request.

**Why summary judgment isn't the time to change your request**

Summary judgment is about determining whether there are genuine issues of material fact on the claims *as pleaded*. It presupposes that the pleadings have already defined the contours of the dispute. While parties frequently refine their damage calculations or specify the exact relief they seek as the case develops, they cannot use the summary judgment stage to pivot to substantively different relief.

If Cohen wanted to seek rescission of the 2019 policy, he should have moved to amend his complaint under Rule 15(a), which permits amendment with the opposing party's written consent or the court's leave—and courts should "freely give leave when justice so requires." Instead, he attempted to add this request through his opposition brief, bypassing the procedural safeguards that amendments provide.

### Why would an appellate court vacate and remand for the district court to determine jurisdiction instead of deciding it?
Question:
The Eighth Circuit raised a 'serious concern regarding our jurisdiction' on the MFLSA claim, noting potential mootness issues. Instead of deciding whether the claim is moot, the court vacated the summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction first. Why wouldn't the appellate court just decide the mootness issue itself? If there's a standing or mootness problem, doesn't that mean the plaintiff never properly had a case, or that the court lacks jurisdiction? What's the purpose of sending it back—what facts could the district court develop that would help answer what seems like a legal question?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important procedural question. The short answer is that **mootness often involves factual disputes that the district court is better positioned to resolve**, and appellate courts generally prefer to have a developed record before deciding jurisdictional issues that turn on facts.

### The Mootness Question Here Is Fact-Dependent

The Eighth Circuit didn't have a sufficient record to determine whether Cohen's MFLSA claim is moot. The court explained that a claim for civil penalties "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." The key factual question: **Is Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy still in place?**

The court explicitly noted that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." If the policy continues, Cohen's penalty claim may remain viable because penalties can "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." But if the policy has been abandoned and recurrence is "absolutely clear" not to happen, the claim may be moot.

This is precisely the type of factual dispute that requires development of the record—something district courts do, not appellate courts.

### Appellate Courts Are Not Factfinders

Appellate courts review factual findings for clear error; they don't make factual findings in the first instance. When the record is insufficient to resolve a jurisdictional fact question, the proper course is to remand. The district court can take evidence, hear testimony if necessary, and make findings about whether Consilio's policy is still in effect and whether any cessation is genuine and permanent.

### The Parties Didn't Brief the Issue Adequately

Judge Kobes's concurrence adds that "Neither party briefed the issue" of standing adequately. The district court is the proper forum to develop the factual record and legal arguments on this threshold question.

### Summary

The appellate court remanded because mootness here turns on factual questions—whether the challenged conduct continues—that require factfinding and record development. The district court must determine whether it has jurisdiction before the appellate court can reach the merits of the MFLSA claim.
