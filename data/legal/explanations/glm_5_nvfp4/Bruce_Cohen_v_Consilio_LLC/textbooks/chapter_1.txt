Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## The Parties and Employment Relationship

This case arises from an employment dispute between Bruce Cohen and his employer, Consilio, LLC. Consilio operates as a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Understanding the nature of Consilio's business is important context: legal document review is a specialized service where attorneys examine documents in connection with litigation, typically during the discovery phase. Companies like Consilio employ licensed attorneys on an hourly basis to perform this work.

Cohen began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. This classification matters. Unlike salaried employees who are generally exempt from overtime requirements, hourly employees are typically entitled to overtime compensation when they work beyond a standard workweek. Cohen's status as an "hourly" worker meant that his compensation structure was subject to state wage and hour laws that mandate premium pay for overtime hours.

## The July 2019 Email Announcing the New Overtime Policy

In July 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers that would become the catalyst for this litigation. The email announced a new overtime policy that would take effect in August 2019. The critical feature of this new policy was the elimination of overtime premium pay. Under the revised policy, Cohen and similarly situated employees would be paid "at [their] base pay rate" for all hours worked—including hours that would previously have qualified for overtime premium compensation.

This policy change lies at the heart of Cohen's claims. By eliminating premium overtime pay while continuing to classify these attorneys as hourly employees, Consilio created a potential conflict with Minnesota's wage and hour statutes. The question of whether this policy violated state law became the central dispute in the lawsuit.

## Cohen's August 2020 Lawsuit Asserting Claims Under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA

Cohen filed his lawsuit in August 2020, approximately one year after the new overtime policy went into effect. He brought the case on behalf of himself and a putative class—meaning he sought to represent other similarly situated employees who were subject to the same policy. The complaint asserted violations of three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA).

Each statute serves a distinct protective function. The MPWA, found at Minnesota Statutes §§ 181.101 and 181.171, governs the payment of wages and provides mechanisms for enforcement. The MFLSA, at §§ 177.25 and 177.27, establishes overtime requirements for employees in Minnesota. The MWTA, at §§ 181.032 and 181.171, addresses wage theft and includes notice requirements for employers changing compensation policies. Cohen's claims under these statutes reflected different aspects of the alleged harm: failure to pay required overtime wages, failure to provide proper notice of the policy change, and violations of general wage payment obligations.

As remedies, Cohen sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Liquidated damages are a common remedy in wage and hour cases, designed to compensate employees for the difficulty of proving the exact harm from unpaid wages. Statutory penalties differ from compensatory damages—they are amounts set by statute that serve punitive and deterrent functions rather than compensatory ones.

## Consilio's Payment of Overtime Wages and Liquidated Damages

A significant development occurred during the litigation: Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen had demanded under his MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 in liquidated damages.

This payment fundamentally changed the nature of the dispute. By compensating the unpaid overtime and providing liquidated damages, Consilio effectively eliminated Cohen's claim for monetary relief under the MFLSA—at least as to wages and liquidated damages. However, the payment did not resolve the entire case because Cohen continued to seek statutory penalties under all three statutes and injunctive relief.

## The Parties' Stipulations Regarding Payment

Following Consilio's payment, the parties entered into stipulations—formal agreements about factual matters—that would prove crucial to the appellate court's analysis. The parties stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the MFLSA."

These stipulations narrowed the issues considerably. By agreeing that Cohen had received full compensation for overtime and liquidated damages, the parties removed the question of actual monetary injury from the case. What remained were the disputed penalties and injunctive relief—remedies that present different legal questions than unpaid wage claims.

## The Disputed Penalty Amounts Under Each Statute

Although wages and liquidated damages were paid, the parties could not agree on penalties. Cohen argued he was entitled to substantial sums: $172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio countered that these statutes did not authorize Cohen to obtain penalties.

This dispute highlights an important distinction in statutory remedies. Some statutes authorize private litigants to recover penalties; others reserve enforcement to government officials. The question of whether an individual employee can recover statutory penalties—or whether only a government actor can seek them—became central to the court's analysis on appeal.

## The Parties' Agreement to Proceed by Summary Judgment

The parties agreed on a procedural path forward. They stipulated "that the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants' summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." The parties acknowledged that if the district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, "no claims [would] remain and the case [would] be fully resolved."

This agreement reflects a strategic choice by both parties. By agreeing to resolve the case through summary judgment before further discovery, they sought an efficient determination of the legal questions—primarily whether Cohen could recover penalties under the statutes at issue. Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The parties apparently believed that the key questions were legal rather than factual, making summary judgment the appropriate vehicle for resolution.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment to Consilio on All Claims

The district court granted Consilio's motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen then appealed, challenging the district court's rulings. The Eighth Circuit reviewed the summary judgment decision de novo, meaning the appellate court gave no deference to the district court's conclusions and examined the legal questions anew.

The procedural posture on appeal was unusual because of the payments and stipulations. Cohen was no longer seeking unpaid wages—he had been paid. Instead, he sought penalties and injunctive relief. This circumstance raised threshold questions about whether Cohen still had a live controversy that the court could adjudicate—questions of standing and mootness that would prove significant in the appellate court's analysis.
