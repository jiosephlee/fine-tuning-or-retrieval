Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 4: # The MWTA Claim, Injunctive Relief, and Preservation Issues

## The MWTA Claim: Failure to Provide Statutorily Required Notice of a Policy Change

Cohen's third statutory claim arose under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Specifically, Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy. Under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4), employers must provide employees with certain notices regarding wage arrangements. Cohen contended that when Consilio changed its overtime policy in August 2019—eliminating premium overtime pay—it failed to provide proper notice, and he sought penalties as a result.

This claim faced a significant obstacle from the outset. The parties had already stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit." This stipulation meant that Cohen could no longer claim unpaid wages as damages. Instead, Cohen sought civil penalties under the MWTA as his sole remaining remedy for the alleged notice violation.

## Cohen's Failure to Cite Statutory Authority for Civil Penalties Under the MWTA

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of Cohen's MWTA claim illustrates a fundamental principle of appellate advocacy: a party must provide legal authority supporting each element of its claims. Cohen argued that he was entitled to penalties under the MWTA, but he pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the specific provisions he invoked—§§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4).

The court noted simply that Cohen "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." This omission proved fatal to his claim. The court's approach demonstrates that courts will not construct arguments on behalf of litigants. When a statute creates a private right of action, the plaintiff bears the burden of identifying the statutory provisions that authorize the specific relief sought. If the plaintiff cannot point to language permitting civil penalties, the court will not presume such penalties exist.

This principle is particularly important in statutory interpretation cases. The court's analysis of Cohen's MPWA claim earlier in the opinion demonstrates the proper approach: carefully examining the statutory text to determine whether it authorizes the relief the plaintiff seeks. Having applied that methodology to the MPWA, the court expected Cohen to do the same for the MWTA. His failure to do so meant the court had no basis to reverse the district court's dismissal.

## Waiver Through Inadequate Appellate Briefing: The Milligan Standard

The Eighth Circuit's disposition of the MWTA claim rests on the doctrine of waiver through inadequate briefing. Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), the court stated: "inasmuch as [defendant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it."

The *Milligan* standard reflects a core principle of adversarial litigation. Appellate courts function as arbiters of the arguments parties present, not as independent investigators of potential claims. When a party fails to develop an argument with supporting legal authority, the court treats that argument as abandoned. This doctrine serves several purposes: it ensures that courts decide only issues that have been properly presented and argued, it respects the adversarial process, and it prevents courts from constructing arguments that parties themselves have not made.

Cohen's dissatisfaction with the district court's sparse discussion of his MWTA claim did not cure his waiver. He complained that the district court "included little to no discussion of this claim," but the appellate court responded that Cohen himself had provided "no meaningful argument in support of reversal." The burden was on Cohen, as the appellant, to demonstrate error—not on the court to independently construct a basis for reversal.

## Cohen's Shifting Injunctive Relief Requests from Complaint to Summary Judgment Briefing

Cohen's claim for injunctive relief presents a different procedural problem. In his complaint, Cohen "sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief." However, by the time he filed his summary judgment briefing, he had "change[d] course and request[ed] different relief"—specifically, an order requiring Consilio "to rescind its illegal overtime policy."

This shift created a mismatch between the claims and relief sought in the complaint and those advanced at summary judgment. The complaint defines the scope of the litigation. It provides notice to the defendant of what the plaintiff is seeking and establishes the framework for the parties' discovery and motion practice. When a plaintiff seeks new or different relief at summary judgment that was not included in the complaint, the defendant has had no opportunity to prepare a defense to that claim.

## The District Court's Proper Refusal to Consider Arguments Raised for the First Time in Opposition Briefs

The district court—understandably, in the Eighth Circuit's words—"did not consider" Cohen's new request for injunctive relief. This refusal was not an abuse of discretion but rather a proper application of procedural rules. Federal courts regularly decline to consider arguments or claims raised for the first time in opposition briefs rather than in the pleadings.

The logic behind this rule is straightforward. Litigation proceeds through defined stages: pleading, discovery, dispositive motions, and trial. Each stage serves specific functions. The pleading stage establishes the boundaries of the dispute. If parties could add new claims or requests for relief at any point through motion papers, the pleading requirements would become meaningless, and the opposing party would be unfairly surprised.

The Eighth Circuit has consistently held that new claims cannot be introduced through summary judgment opposition briefs. Citing *InfoDeli, LLC v. Those Characters, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), the court explained that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."

## Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and Proper Procedures for Amending Claims

The court's citation to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) points to the proper mechanism for Cohen to have pursued his new injunctive relief request. Rule 15(a) governs amended and supplemental pleadings. It provides that a party may amend its pleading once as a matter of course within 21 days after serving it, or within 21 days after service of a responsive pleading or a motion under Rule 12(b), (e), or (f). In all other cases, a party may amend its pleading only with the opposing party's written consent or the court's leave.

The rule further states that "the court should freely give leave when justice so requires." This liberal amendment policy reflects the preference for deciding cases on their merits rather than on procedural technicalities. However, the liberal amendment policy does not permit parties to raise new claims through motion practice instead of through the formal amendment process. A party who wishes to seek different relief must move to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a), not simply request that relief in a summary judgment brief.

Cohen did not follow this procedure. He sought new injunctive relief in his summary judgment briefing without first moving to amend his complaint. Because he bypassed the procedural requirements, his request for injunctive relief was not properly before either the district court or the Eighth Circuit.

## The Court's Affirmance of Dismissal of the MWTA and Injunctive Relief Claims

The Eighth Circuit's resolution of these claims was straightforward: "we will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim." The court affirmed the dismissal of both Cohen's MWTA claim and his claim for injunctive relief. In both instances, Cohen's procedural failures—failure to cite statutory authority for the MWTA penalties and failure to properly amend his complaint to seek different injunctive relief—proved fatal to his appeal.

These holdings illustrate an important lesson for law students and practicing attorneys alike. Substantive rights mean little if a litigant fails to follow procedural requirements. The strongest claim will fail if the attorney does not provide legal authority supporting it. The most compelling request for relief will be rejected if it is not properly pleaded. Procedural competence is not a mere technicality—it is essential to effective advocacy.
