Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # The MFLSA Claim: Standing and Mootness in Civil Penalty Actions

## The Unique Nature of Statutory Penalties Payable to the Commissioner

Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) presented a distinct and important jurisdictional puzzle. Unlike his claims for unpaid overtime wages and liquidated damages—which Consilio paid directly to Cohen and other affected employees—the statutory penalties available under the MFLSA operate differently. The parties agreed that any statutory penalties imposed under the MFLSA would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally. This distinction matters because it raises a fundamental question: if Cohen has already received full compensation for all wages and liquidated damages he was owed, and if any additional penalties would go to the government rather than to him, does he retain a sufficient personal stake in the outcome to continue litigating?

This question sits at the intersection of standing doctrine and mootness. The court recognized that civil penalties, even when paid to the government rather than to private plaintiffs, can still serve an important function in private enforcement actions. However, the court also understood that a plaintiff must maintain a concrete interest in the litigation throughout its duration. The unique structure of these penalties—designed to punish and deter rather than compensate—required the court to carefully examine whether Cohen's injury had been fully redressed and whether any ongoing controversy remained.

## Standing Requirements: Demonstrating Injury for Each Claim and Form of Relief Sought

The court began its analysis by reaffirming a foundational principle of federal jurisdiction: "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." This requirement means that standing is not an all-or-nothing proposition. A plaintiff might have standing to pursue one claim but lack standing to pursue another, or might have standing to seek compensatory damages but lack standing to seek injunctive relief or civil penalties.

For Cohen, this principle had significant consequences. He unquestionably had standing to pursue unpaid overtime wages and liquidated damages—he had suffered a direct financial injury when Consilio failed to pay him the premium overtime rate required by law. But once Consilio paid him in full for those amounts, the question became whether he retained standing to pursue statutory penalties that would flow to the Commissioner rather than to himself.

The court's framework for analyzing this question draws directly from the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, which established that civil penalties payable to the government can nevertheless "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The key insight from *Laidlaw* is that penalties serve a deterrent function—they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." This deterrent effect can provide a continuing benefit to plaintiffs, but only when unlawful conduct is ongoing or threatened to recur.

## The Relationship Between Standing at Commencement and Mootness Throughout Litigation

The court articulated an essential relationship between standing and mootness: "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." This principle reflects the Article III requirement that federal courts may only adjudicate actual, ongoing "Cases" and "Controversies." A case that becomes moot—because the dispute has been resolved or the plaintiff's injury has been fully redressed—no longer presents a live controversy and must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

The relationship between standing and mootness operates as follows. At the moment a plaintiff files suit, he must demonstrate three elements: (1) an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized, (2) causation showing the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, and (3) redressability showing a favorable court decision is likely to remedy the injury. If these elements are satisfied, standing exists at the outset. But circumstances may change during the course of litigation. The defendant may stop the challenged conduct, pay the plaintiff all requested damages, or otherwise eliminate the dispute. When this happens, the court must determine whether the case has become moot—whether the plaintiff still retains a personal stake in the outcome.

For Cohen, his case presented a classic mootness question. He had standing when he filed suit because Consilio had failed to pay him overtime wages, causing him direct financial injury. But after Consilio paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed to be owed, the court needed to determine whether his claim for statutory penalties could survive or whether full payment had rendered the controversy moot.

## The Friends of the Earth Framework: Civil Penalties as Redress for Ongoing Unlawful Conduct

The Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.* provides the governing framework for analyzing whether a claim for civil penalties remains live after a defendant has come into compliance. In *Laidlaw*, an environmental organization sued a company for violating Clean Water Act permit limits. By the time the case reached the Supreme Court, the defendant had come into compliance with its permit, and the question was whether the plaintiff's claim for civil penalties was moot.

The Supreme Court held that the claim was not moot because civil penalties serve an important deterrent function. Even though penalties would be paid to the government rather than to the plaintiffs, they could still "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The penalties work by discouraging future violations—they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." This deterrent effect provides a continuing benefit to plaintiffs who remain at risk of future injury from the defendant's unlawful conduct.

However, the *Laidlaw* framework contains an important limitation. Civil penalties only provide this ongoing redress when there is a continuing violation or a threat of future violation. If a defendant has not only ceased its unlawful conduct but made it "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur," then the claim becomes moot. The deterrent effect of penalties becomes irrelevant when there is nothing left to deter.

## Deterrence Value of Penalties When Violations Are Ongoing

The court's reasoning in Cohen's case underscores the functional role that civil penalties play in the legal system. Penalties are not primarily designed to compensate injured plaintiffs—that is the function of compensatory damages and liquidated damages. Instead, penalties serve a punitive and deterrent function. They punish defendants for past violations and create incentives for compliance going forward.

When a defendant's unlawful policy remains in effect, penalties can provide real value to an aggrieved plaintiff even if those penalties are paid to the government. The penalties may compel the defendant to change its behavior, which directly benefits the plaintiff by eliminating the ongoing violation. In this sense, penalties "afford redress" not by putting money in the plaintiff's pocket, but by changing the defendant's conduct to the plaintiff's benefit.

This deterrence rationale only operates, however, when there is ongoing unlawful conduct to deter. If Consilio had already rescinded its challenged overtime policy, and if it were absolutely clear that the policy would never be reinstated, then penalties would serve no deterrent function. There would be no ongoing violation to discourage and no threat of future injury to prevent. In that circumstance, Cohen's claim would be moot because he would no longer have a personal stake in the outcome—his injury would be fully redressed, and there would be no prospect of future injury.

## The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine and the 'Absolutely Clear' Standard for Mootness

The court applied the voluntary cessation doctrine to Cohen's claim. This doctrine addresses the situation where a defendant stops engaging in challenged conduct after litigation has begun. The Supreme Court has held that "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice." This rule prevents defendants from manipulating the judicial process by temporarily stopping unlawful conduct to moot a case, only to resume the same conduct once the lawsuit is dismissed.

Under the voluntary cessation doctrine, a case becomes moot only if it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." This is a demanding standard. The defendant bears the burden of showing that there is no reasonable possibility that it will resume the challenged conduct. Courts consider factors such as whether the cessation was motivated by a genuine change of heart or by litigation pressure, whether the cessation is permanent or temporary, and whether the defendant has taken concrete steps to ensure compliance going forward.

In Cohen's case, the court could not determine whether this demanding standard had been met. The record contained a critical factual dispute that precluded a definitive mootness determination.

## The Factual Dispute About Whether Consilio's Violative Overtime Policy Remains in Effect

The court identified a central factual question that neither party had adequately addressed: whether Consilio's challenged overtime policy remained in effect. The court noted that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." This factual dispute was dispositive of the mootness question.

If Consilio's policy remained in effect—if Consilio was still paying document reviewers their base rate for overtime hours rather than the premium rate required by law—then Cohen's claim for penalties could still serve its deterrent function. Penalties could encourage Consilio to discontinue its unlawful policy and deter it from committing future violations. Under the *Laidlaw* framework, Cohen would retain standing to pursue those penalties because they would afford him redress by potentially changing Consilio's ongoing unlawful conduct.

However, if Consilio had already rescinded the policy, and if it were "absolutely clear" that the policy would not be reinstated, then the deterrent effect of penalties would have no ongoing violation to address. Cohen would have received full compensation for his past injury, and there would be no threat of future injury. His claim would be moot, and the federal court would lack jurisdiction to adjudicate it.

The court emphasized that federal courts are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest." Without a clear factual record on the status of Consilio's policy, the court could not determine whether Cohen retained the requisite continuing interest.

## The Court's Decision to Vacate and Remand for the District Court to Determine Jurisdiction

Faced with this unresolved factual question, the court chose a prudent procedural course. Rather than decide the mootness issue itself, the court vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment on Cohen's MFLSA claim and remanded the case for the district court to determine its jurisdiction in the first instance.

This approach reflects several important principles of judicial administration. First, jurisdiction is a threshold question that must be resolved before a court may reach the merits of a claim. A federal court has an independent obligation to assure itself of its jurisdiction, regardless of whether the parties raise jurisdictional objections. Second, factual questions bearing on jurisdiction are properly resolved by the district court, which can develop the factual record and make credibility determinations. Third, remand allows the district court to consider the jurisdictional question with the benefit of additional briefing and evidence, if necessary.

On remand, the district court was tasked with determining whether Consilio's overtime policy remained in effect and, consequently, whether Cohen's claim for statutory penalties was live or moot. The court's decision preserves Cohen's opportunity to pursue penalties if he can demonstrate an ongoing violation, while respecting the jurisdictional limits imposed by Article III.

The concurrence by Judge Kobes added a separate perspective on the jurisdictional analysis. Judge Kobes expressed skepticism about whether Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties under *Laidlaw*, noting potential differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA. However, because neither party had briefed the standing issue, Judge Kobes agreed that vacating the judgment and remanding was the appropriate course, allowing the district court to address the mootness question first. This concurrence highlights that the jurisdictional questions in this case remain open for the district court to address on remand.
