Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why would an appellate court vacate and remand for the district court to determine jurisdiction instead of deciding it?
Question:
The Eighth Circuit raised a 'serious concern regarding our jurisdiction' on the MFLSA claim, noting potential mootness issues. Instead of deciding whether the claim is moot, the court vacated the summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction first. Why wouldn't the appellate court just decide the mootness issue itself? If there's a standing or mootness problem, doesn't that mean the plaintiff never properly had a case, or that the court lacks jurisdiction? What's the purpose of sending it back—what facts could the district court develop that would help answer what seems like a legal question?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important procedural question. The short answer is that **mootness often involves factual disputes that the district court is better positioned to resolve**, and appellate courts generally prefer to have a developed record before deciding jurisdictional issues that turn on facts.

### The Mootness Question Here Is Fact-Dependent

The Eighth Circuit didn't have a sufficient record to determine whether Cohen's MFLSA claim is moot. The court explained that a claim for civil penalties "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." The key factual question: **Is Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy still in place?**

The court explicitly noted that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." If the policy continues, Cohen's penalty claim may remain viable because penalties can "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." But if the policy has been abandoned and recurrence is "absolutely clear" not to happen, the claim may be moot.

This is precisely the type of factual dispute that requires development of the record—something district courts do, not appellate courts.

### Appellate Courts Are Not Factfinders

Appellate courts review factual findings for clear error; they don't make factual findings in the first instance. When the record is insufficient to resolve a jurisdictional fact question, the proper course is to remand. The district court can take evidence, hear testimony if necessary, and make findings about whether Consilio's policy is still in effect and whether any cessation is genuine and permanent.

### The Parties Didn't Brief the Issue Adequately

Judge Kobes's concurrence adds that "Neither party briefed the issue" of standing adequately. The district court is the proper forum to develop the factual record and legal arguments on this threshold question.

### Summary

The appellate court remanded because mootness here turns on factual questions—whether the challenged conduct continues—that require factfinding and record development. The district court must determine whether it has jurisdiction before the appellate court can reach the merits of the MFLSA claim.
