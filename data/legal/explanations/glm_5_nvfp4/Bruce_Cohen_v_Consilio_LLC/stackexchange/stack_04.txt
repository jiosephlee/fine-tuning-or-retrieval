Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What constitutes 'meaningful argument' to avoid waiver on appeal?
Question:
The court affirmed dismissal of Cohen's MWTA claim because he 'points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties' and provided 'no meaningful argument in support of reversal.' The court cites Milligan v. City of Red Oak saying that unsupported assertions waive the issue. But Cohen did bring this claim in his complaint. How much argument is 'enough' on appeal? Does a party need to cite specific cases and statutory text, or is a general reference to the claim sufficient? If the argument was made below, does it still get waived if not adequately briefed on appeal?
Answer:
### What constitutes 'meaningful argument' to avoid waiver on appeal?

The short answer is that **raising a claim in the complaint does not preserve it for appeal**—you must independently brief and argue each issue on appeal with supporting legal authority.

The court's handling of Cohen's MWTA claim illustrates the waiver standard clearly.

**The Legal Standard**

The Eighth Circuit applies a straightforward rule: an appellate brief must support assertions with both argument *and* legal authority, or the issue is waived. The court cites *Milligan v. City of Red Oak* for this principle:

> "[I]nasmuch as [defendant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." (230 F.3d at 360)

**What Cohen Failed to Do**

Cohen argued on appeal that the district court gave "little to no discussion" of his MWTA claim. But the court rejected this complaint because Cohen himself "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute."

Note the court's specific criticism: Cohen failed to identify *any* statutory basis for the civil penalties he sought. The MWTA claim was for failure to provide notice of a policy change, but Cohen offered no legal argument explaining why this entitled him to penalties.

**The Answer to Your Questions**

1. **How much argument is "enough"?** At minimum, you must explain *why* you should win and support that explanation with legal authority. A general reference to the claim or a bare assertion that the district court erred is insufficient.

2. **Must you cite specific statutory text or cases?** Yes. The court's criticism—that Cohen "points to no statutory authority"—shows that you need to identify the specific legal basis for your requested relief. You cannot simply invoke a statute generally; you must explain how the statutory text or case law supports your position.

3. **Does raising the argument below preserve it?** No. The fact that Cohen properly pleaded the MWTA claim in his complaint was irrelevant to the appeal. The court treated waiver as occurring at the appellate stage due to inadequate briefing. Each stage requires its own advocacy—you don't get credit for arguments made in earlier filings.

**Practical Takeaway**

On appeal, treat every claim as if you're arguing it from scratch. Identify the legal standard, apply it to the facts, and cite specific authority. The appellate court will not construct arguments for you or search the record for support you failed to provide.
