Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a plaintiff have Article III standing to seek civil penalties payable to the government?
Question:
In this case, Cohen sought statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act. The court notes these penalties are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen himself. Yet the court cites Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for the proposition that civil penalties 'may afford redress to citizen plaintiffs' through deterrence. I'm confused about how a plaintiff has standing to seek penalties that don't go to them personally. I thought standing required a personal injury that the relief would redress. If the penalties go to the government, how do they redress the plaintiff's injury? What's the theory here?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified a subtle but important question about the redressability prong of Article III standing. The short answer is that civil penalties payable to the government can redress a plaintiff's injury through **deterrence**—by discouraging the defendant from continuing or repeating the unlawful conduct that harmed the plaintiff.

The court in *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* explained this theory: civil penalties "may afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). The redressability requirement is satisfied not because the plaintiff receives the money, but because the penalty gives the defendant a concrete reason to stop the harmful behavior. The plaintiff's injury—the ongoing unlawful conduct—is thus addressed indirectly through deterrence.

There are two key limitations embedded in this doctrine, both reflected in the *Cohen* opinion:

**1. The plaintiff must have a continuing injury or threat of future harm.** The deterrence rationale only works if there is something still to deter. As the court notes, the claim for penalties "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." *Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189. This is why the Eighth Circuit remanded the MFLSA claim—to determine whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in effect. If the policy is gone and there's no realistic chance it returns, there's no ongoing injury to redress, and the claim becomes moot.

**2. The defendant's voluntary cessation doesn't automatically moot the claim.** The court emphasizes that "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice." *Id.* (quoting *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)). The burden is on the defendant to show that the wrongful conduct "could not reasonably be expected to recur."

Judge Kobes' concurrence raises a fair point: there may be meaningful differences between the citizen-suit penalties at issue in *Laidlaw* (a Clean Water Act case) and the state-law statutory penalties here. But as neither party briefed the standing issue, the court left that question open, focusing instead on the potential mootness problem—whether Cohen still has a live controversy given that he's already been paid for his actual damages.

In short: standing to seek government-payable civil penalties exists under *Laidlaw*'s deterrence theory, but it hinges on the plaintiff showing a continuing injury that the penalties could help prevent.
