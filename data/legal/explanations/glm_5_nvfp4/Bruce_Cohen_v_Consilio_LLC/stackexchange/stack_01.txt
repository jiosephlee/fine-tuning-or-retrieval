Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What does the "legislature knows how to" argument mean in statutory interpretation?
Question:
I'm reading this Eighth Circuit opinion interpreting Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act. The court concludes that employees can't seek penalties under § 181.101 because that statute only references the Commissioner regarding penalties. But the court then compares other Minnesota statutes (like § 181.13) that explicitly give employees the right to collect penalties, saying the legislature 'knew how to' give employees that right if it wanted to. Is this a form of expressio unius? Why does the language in a completely different statute tell us anything about legislative intent for this particular statute? This seems like comparing apples to oranges.
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important statutory interpretation principle that courts employ regularly. The "legislature knows how to" argument is indeed related to *expressio unius*, though it functions as a distinct analytical tool.

**The Doctrine Explained**

The "legislature knows how to" canon permits courts to compare related statutes enacted by the same legislature to infer intentional drafting choices. The logic is straightforward: if the legislature used specific language to achieve a particular result in one statute but omitted that language from a similar statute, the omission was likely deliberate.

As the Eighth Circuit explains, quoting the Minnesota Supreme Court:

> "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so" (citing *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022)).

**Application in This Case**

The court applies this principle by contrasting § 181.101 with §§ 181.13 and 181.14:

- **§ 181.13(a)**: Explicitly provides that a discharged employee "may charge and collect a penalty"
- **§ 181.14, subd. 2**: Similarly gives employees who quit the right to "charge and collect a penalty"
- **§ 181.101**: Contains no such language—it references only the Commissioner's authority to "charge and collect" penalties

The inference is that the Minnesota legislature, when drafting § 181.101, chose not to grant employees the same direct enforcement mechanism it provided elsewhere. If the legislature "wished" to empower employees directly, it "knew how to" do so—it simply declined to do so here.

**Why Different Statutes Matter**

This isn't comparing "apples to oranges" because these provisions are part of the same legislative scheme governing wage payments. The court presumes the legislature acts with knowledge of its own enactments. When the same legislature uses different language across related statutes addressing similar remedial schemes, courts may infer different meanings were intended.

This approach is narrower than pure *expressio unius* because it doesn't rely solely on what's absent within a single statute—it affirmatively demonstrates the legislature's capacity to draft language achieving a particular result when desired.
