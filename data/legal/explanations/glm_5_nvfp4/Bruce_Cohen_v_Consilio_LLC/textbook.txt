Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## The Parties and Employment Relationship

This case arises from an employment dispute between Bruce Cohen and his employer, Consilio, LLC. Consilio operates as a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Understanding the nature of Consilio's business is important context: legal document review is a specialized service where attorneys examine documents in connection with litigation, typically during the discovery phase. Companies like Consilio employ licensed attorneys on an hourly basis to perform this work.

Cohen began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. This classification matters. Unlike salaried employees who are generally exempt from overtime requirements, hourly employees are typically entitled to overtime compensation when they work beyond a standard workweek. Cohen's status as an "hourly" worker meant that his compensation structure was subject to state wage and hour laws that mandate premium pay for overtime hours.

## The July 2019 Email Announcing the New Overtime Policy

In July 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers that would become the catalyst for this litigation. The email announced a new overtime policy that would take effect in August 2019. The critical feature of this new policy was the elimination of overtime premium pay. Under the revised policy, Cohen and similarly situated employees would be paid "at [their] base pay rate" for all hours worked—including hours that would previously have qualified for overtime premium compensation.

This policy change lies at the heart of Cohen's claims. By eliminating premium overtime pay while continuing to classify these attorneys as hourly employees, Consilio created a potential conflict with Minnesota's wage and hour statutes. The question of whether this policy violated state law became the central dispute in the lawsuit.

## Cohen's August 2020 Lawsuit Asserting Claims Under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA

Cohen filed his lawsuit in August 2020, approximately one year after the new overtime policy went into effect. He brought the case on behalf of himself and a putative class—meaning he sought to represent other similarly situated employees who were subject to the same policy. The complaint asserted violations of three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA).

Each statute serves a distinct protective function. The MPWA, found at Minnesota Statutes §§ 181.101 and 181.171, governs the payment of wages and provides mechanisms for enforcement. The MFLSA, at §§ 177.25 and 177.27, establishes overtime requirements for employees in Minnesota. The MWTA, at §§ 181.032 and 181.171, addresses wage theft and includes notice requirements for employers changing compensation policies. Cohen's claims under these statutes reflected different aspects of the alleged harm: failure to pay required overtime wages, failure to provide proper notice of the policy change, and violations of general wage payment obligations.

As remedies, Cohen sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Liquidated damages are a common remedy in wage and hour cases, designed to compensate employees for the difficulty of proving the exact harm from unpaid wages. Statutory penalties differ from compensatory damages—they are amounts set by statute that serve punitive and deterrent functions rather than compensatory ones.

## Consilio's Payment of Overtime Wages and Liquidated Damages

A significant development occurred during the litigation: Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen had demanded under his MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 in liquidated damages.

This payment fundamentally changed the nature of the dispute. By compensating the unpaid overtime and providing liquidated damages, Consilio effectively eliminated Cohen's claim for monetary relief under the MFLSA—at least as to wages and liquidated damages. However, the payment did not resolve the entire case because Cohen continued to seek statutory penalties under all three statutes and injunctive relief.

## The Parties' Stipulations Regarding Payment

Following Consilio's payment, the parties entered into stipulations—formal agreements about factual matters—that would prove crucial to the appellate court's analysis. The parties stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the MFLSA."

These stipulations narrowed the issues considerably. By agreeing that Cohen had received full compensation for overtime and liquidated damages, the parties removed the question of actual monetary injury from the case. What remained were the disputed penalties and injunctive relief—remedies that present different legal questions than unpaid wage claims.

## The Disputed Penalty Amounts Under Each Statute

Although wages and liquidated damages were paid, the parties could not agree on penalties. Cohen argued he was entitled to substantial sums: $172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio countered that these statutes did not authorize Cohen to obtain penalties.

This dispute highlights an important distinction in statutory remedies. Some statutes authorize private litigants to recover penalties; others reserve enforcement to government officials. The question of whether an individual employee can recover statutory penalties—or whether only a government actor can seek them—became central to the court's analysis on appeal.

## The Parties' Agreement to Proceed by Summary Judgment

The parties agreed on a procedural path forward. They stipulated "that the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants' summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." The parties acknowledged that if the district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, "no claims [would] remain and the case [would] be fully resolved."

This agreement reflects a strategic choice by both parties. By agreeing to resolve the case through summary judgment before further discovery, they sought an efficient determination of the legal questions—primarily whether Cohen could recover penalties under the statutes at issue. Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The parties apparently believed that the key questions were legal rather than factual, making summary judgment the appropriate vehicle for resolution.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment to Consilio on All Claims

The district court granted Consilio's motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen then appealed, challenging the district court's rulings. The Eighth Circuit reviewed the summary judgment decision de novo, meaning the appellate court gave no deference to the district court's conclusions and examined the legal questions anew.

The procedural posture on appeal was unusual because of the payments and stipulations. Cohen was no longer seeking unpaid wages—he had been paid. Instead, he sought penalties and injunctive relief. This circumstance raised threshold questions about whether Cohen still had a live controversy that the court could adjudicate—questions of standing and mootness that would prove significant in the appellate court's analysis.

# The MPWA Claim: Statutory Interpretation of Penalty Provisions

## The statutory framework: Minn. Stat. §§ 181.171 and 181.101

The court's analysis of Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) begins with the interplay between two statutory provisions. Section 181.171 establishes a private right of action, providing that "a person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations" of certain enumerated provisions, including § 181.101. When an employer "is found to have violated" such a provision, the statute makes the employer "liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Importantly, the statute also provides that an employer who violates these provisions is liable to the aggrieved party for "civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." This qualifying phrase—"provided for in the section violated"—becomes central to the court's analysis. It means that before an employee can recover penalties, there must actually be penalties authorized in the specific provision that the employer allegedly violated.

Cohen brought his claim under § 181.101, alleging that Consilio violated that section and that he was therefore entitled to "average daily wage penalties." The question before the court was whether § 181.101 actually provides for such penalties in a manner that makes them available to private plaintiffs like Cohen. Answering this question required the court to engage in careful statutory interpretation, examining the text of § 181.101 to determine what remedies it authorizes and to whom those remedies are available.

## Cohen's argument that the plain language of § 181.101 authorizes employees to seek penalties

Cohen argued that the "plain language and unambiguous meaning" of § 181.101 authorized employees to seek average daily wage penalties. This argument invokes a fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts must give effect to that language as written, assuming it manifests legislative intent. Cohen's position was that the statute's text, read naturally, created a right for employees to pursue these penalties directly against their employers.

The court rejected this reading. While § 181.171 does create a private cause of action for violations of § 181.101, the penalties available depend on what § 181.101 itself provides. The court's task was therefore to examine § 181.101 to determine whether it actually makes penalties available to private employees—or whether it channels those penalties through a different mechanism.

## The court's textual analysis showing penalties are available only through the Commissioner

The court's analysis of § 181.101(a) revealed a structural feature of the statute that undermined Cohen's position. The provision references the Commissioner—not private employees—regarding penalties. Under the statute, penalties become available only if the Commissioner first "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." If the employer fails to make payment within ten days of that demand, "the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." The statute further specifies that "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned."

This textual analysis showed that the penalty mechanism in § 181.101 operates through administrative enforcement by the Commissioner, not through private litigation. The Commissioner serves as the gatekeeper: the penalty process begins with the Commissioner's demand, and the Commissioner is the actor who "may charge and collect" the penalty. While the penalty ultimately benefits the employee—the collected money must be paid to the employee—the statutory pathway runs through the Commissioner's office, not through a private civil action.

## The role of the Commissioner under § 181.101(a): serving demands and collecting penalties

The court's reading of § 181.101(a) highlighted the Commissioner's active role throughout the penalty process. The Commissioner initiates the process by serving a demand for payment on behalf of an employee. The Commissioner then has authority to charge and collect both unpaid wages and penalties if the employer fails to comply within ten days. The statute explicitly directs that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the affected employee.

This administrative structure reflects a deliberate legislative choice about how penalty enforcement should work under this particular provision. The Commissioner acts as an intermediary who investigates demands, determines whether payment is owed, and enforces collection. This contrasts with statutes that directly authorize private parties to seek penalties in court. Under § 181.101, the employee benefits from penalties collected, but the employee does not control the penalty process.

## The statute's reservation to employees of the right to 'prosecut[e] a claim for wages'

Crucially, § 181.101(a) contains language that explicitly addresses what employees may do independently. The statute provides that "this section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages." It further states that the section "provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times."

The court understood this reservation as defining the scope of the employee's independent enforcement authority. What is reserved to the employee is the ability to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages"—not a claim for penalties. Had the legislature intended to reserve to employees the right to pursue penalties directly, it could have included penalties in this reservation. Instead, it limited the reservation to claims for wages, suggesting that penalty enforcement was intentionally left to the Commissioner.

This interpretive principle—expressio unius est exclusio alterius (the expression of one thing is the exclusion of another)—supports the court's reading. By specifying that employees may prosecute claims for wages, the statute implicitly excludes penalties from the employee's independent enforcement authority.

## Comparative statutory analysis: §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which explicitly authorize employees to collect penalties

The court reinforced its interpretation by comparing § 181.101 to other provisions within the MPWA. Sections 181.13(a) and 181.14, subdivision 2, address situations involving discharged employees and employees who resign, respectively. Both provisions explicitly state that an employee "may charge and collect a penalty" in addition to unpaid wages. In these sections, the legislature used direct language authorizing the employee—not the Commissioner—to pursue penalties.

This comparison illuminated a significant difference in legislative drafting. Where the legislature wanted employees to have direct authority to collect penalties, it knew how to say so explicitly. Sections 181.13 and 181.14 demonstrate that when the legislature intends to empower employees with penalty enforcement authority, it uses clear language granting that power. The absence of similar language in § 181.101 is therefore meaningful.

## The 'legislature knows how' canon of statutory construction

This comparative approach reflects a canon of statutory construction sometimes called the "legislature knows how" principle. As the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Lagasse v. Horton*, the legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the legislature "knows how" to accomplish a particular objective when it wishes to do so. When the legislature has demonstrated its ability to draft language achieving a specific result, courts may infer that the absence of such language in another statute reflects a deliberate choice rather than oversight.

Here, the legislature demonstrated in §§ 181.13 and 181.14 that it knew how to authorize employees to collect penalties directly. The fact that § 181.101 lacks similar language suggests the legislature made a different choice for that provision—channeling penalty enforcement through the Commissioner rather than through private action. This comparative analysis strengthened the court's conclusion that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101.

## Affirmance of the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MPWA claim

Based on this analysis, the court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment on Cohen's MPWA claim. Because the court concluded that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, Cohen could not establish that he was entitled to the penalties he sought. The district court therefore correctly ruled that Consilio was entitled to judgment as a matter of law on this claim.

The opinion cited *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, a district court decision that had reached the same conclusion about § 181.101(a): "the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee." The Eighth Circuit's analysis thus aligned with how at least one district court had already interpreted this provision, bringing clarity to a question of Minnesota statutory law. The court's methodical approach to statutory interpretation—examining text, structure, and comparative provisions—provides a model for how courts determine the scope of private enforcement rights under statutory schemes.

# The MFLSA Claim: Standing and Mootness in Civil Penalty Actions

## The Unique Nature of Statutory Penalties Payable to the Commissioner

Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) presented a distinct and important jurisdictional puzzle. Unlike his claims for unpaid overtime wages and liquidated damages—which Consilio paid directly to Cohen and other affected employees—the statutory penalties available under the MFLSA operate differently. The parties agreed that any statutory penalties imposed under the MFLSA would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally. This distinction matters because it raises a fundamental question: if Cohen has already received full compensation for all wages and liquidated damages he was owed, and if any additional penalties would go to the government rather than to him, does he retain a sufficient personal stake in the outcome to continue litigating?

This question sits at the intersection of standing doctrine and mootness. The court recognized that civil penalties, even when paid to the government rather than to private plaintiffs, can still serve an important function in private enforcement actions. However, the court also understood that a plaintiff must maintain a concrete interest in the litigation throughout its duration. The unique structure of these penalties—designed to punish and deter rather than compensate—required the court to carefully examine whether Cohen's injury had been fully redressed and whether any ongoing controversy remained.

## Standing Requirements: Demonstrating Injury for Each Claim and Form of Relief Sought

The court began its analysis by reaffirming a foundational principle of federal jurisdiction: "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." This requirement means that standing is not an all-or-nothing proposition. A plaintiff might have standing to pursue one claim but lack standing to pursue another, or might have standing to seek compensatory damages but lack standing to seek injunctive relief or civil penalties.

For Cohen, this principle had significant consequences. He unquestionably had standing to pursue unpaid overtime wages and liquidated damages—he had suffered a direct financial injury when Consilio failed to pay him the premium overtime rate required by law. But once Consilio paid him in full for those amounts, the question became whether he retained standing to pursue statutory penalties that would flow to the Commissioner rather than to himself.

The court's framework for analyzing this question draws directly from the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, which established that civil penalties payable to the government can nevertheless "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The key insight from *Laidlaw* is that penalties serve a deterrent function—they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." This deterrent effect can provide a continuing benefit to plaintiffs, but only when unlawful conduct is ongoing or threatened to recur.

## The Relationship Between Standing at Commencement and Mootness Throughout Litigation

The court articulated an essential relationship between standing and mootness: "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." This principle reflects the Article III requirement that federal courts may only adjudicate actual, ongoing "Cases" and "Controversies." A case that becomes moot—because the dispute has been resolved or the plaintiff's injury has been fully redressed—no longer presents a live controversy and must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

The relationship between standing and mootness operates as follows. At the moment a plaintiff files suit, he must demonstrate three elements: (1) an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized, (2) causation showing the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, and (3) redressability showing a favorable court decision is likely to remedy the injury. If these elements are satisfied, standing exists at the outset. But circumstances may change during the course of litigation. The defendant may stop the challenged conduct, pay the plaintiff all requested damages, or otherwise eliminate the dispute. When this happens, the court must determine whether the case has become moot—whether the plaintiff still retains a personal stake in the outcome.

For Cohen, his case presented a classic mootness question. He had standing when he filed suit because Consilio had failed to pay him overtime wages, causing him direct financial injury. But after Consilio paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed to be owed, the court needed to determine whether his claim for statutory penalties could survive or whether full payment had rendered the controversy moot.

## The Friends of the Earth Framework: Civil Penalties as Redress for Ongoing Unlawful Conduct

The Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.* provides the governing framework for analyzing whether a claim for civil penalties remains live after a defendant has come into compliance. In *Laidlaw*, an environmental organization sued a company for violating Clean Water Act permit limits. By the time the case reached the Supreme Court, the defendant had come into compliance with its permit, and the question was whether the plaintiff's claim for civil penalties was moot.

The Supreme Court held that the claim was not moot because civil penalties serve an important deterrent function. Even though penalties would be paid to the government rather than to the plaintiffs, they could still "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The penalties work by discouraging future violations—they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." This deterrent effect provides a continuing benefit to plaintiffs who remain at risk of future injury from the defendant's unlawful conduct.

However, the *Laidlaw* framework contains an important limitation. Civil penalties only provide this ongoing redress when there is a continuing violation or a threat of future violation. If a defendant has not only ceased its unlawful conduct but made it "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur," then the claim becomes moot. The deterrent effect of penalties becomes irrelevant when there is nothing left to deter.

## Deterrence Value of Penalties When Violations Are Ongoing

The court's reasoning in Cohen's case underscores the functional role that civil penalties play in the legal system. Penalties are not primarily designed to compensate injured plaintiffs—that is the function of compensatory damages and liquidated damages. Instead, penalties serve a punitive and deterrent function. They punish defendants for past violations and create incentives for compliance going forward.

When a defendant's unlawful policy remains in effect, penalties can provide real value to an aggrieved plaintiff even if those penalties are paid to the government. The penalties may compel the defendant to change its behavior, which directly benefits the plaintiff by eliminating the ongoing violation. In this sense, penalties "afford redress" not by putting money in the plaintiff's pocket, but by changing the defendant's conduct to the plaintiff's benefit.

This deterrence rationale only operates, however, when there is ongoing unlawful conduct to deter. If Consilio had already rescinded its challenged overtime policy, and if it were absolutely clear that the policy would never be reinstated, then penalties would serve no deterrent function. There would be no ongoing violation to discourage and no threat of future injury to prevent. In that circumstance, Cohen's claim would be moot because he would no longer have a personal stake in the outcome—his injury would be fully redressed, and there would be no prospect of future injury.

## The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine and the 'Absolutely Clear' Standard for Mootness

The court applied the voluntary cessation doctrine to Cohen's claim. This doctrine addresses the situation where a defendant stops engaging in challenged conduct after litigation has begun. The Supreme Court has held that "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice." This rule prevents defendants from manipulating the judicial process by temporarily stopping unlawful conduct to moot a case, only to resume the same conduct once the lawsuit is dismissed.

Under the voluntary cessation doctrine, a case becomes moot only if it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." This is a demanding standard. The defendant bears the burden of showing that there is no reasonable possibility that it will resume the challenged conduct. Courts consider factors such as whether the cessation was motivated by a genuine change of heart or by litigation pressure, whether the cessation is permanent or temporary, and whether the defendant has taken concrete steps to ensure compliance going forward.

In Cohen's case, the court could not determine whether this demanding standard had been met. The record contained a critical factual dispute that precluded a definitive mootness determination.

## The Factual Dispute About Whether Consilio's Violative Overtime Policy Remains in Effect

The court identified a central factual question that neither party had adequately addressed: whether Consilio's challenged overtime policy remained in effect. The court noted that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." This factual dispute was dispositive of the mootness question.

If Consilio's policy remained in effect—if Consilio was still paying document reviewers their base rate for overtime hours rather than the premium rate required by law—then Cohen's claim for penalties could still serve its deterrent function. Penalties could encourage Consilio to discontinue its unlawful policy and deter it from committing future violations. Under the *Laidlaw* framework, Cohen would retain standing to pursue those penalties because they would afford him redress by potentially changing Consilio's ongoing unlawful conduct.

However, if Consilio had already rescinded the policy, and if it were "absolutely clear" that the policy would not be reinstated, then the deterrent effect of penalties would have no ongoing violation to address. Cohen would have received full compensation for his past injury, and there would be no threat of future injury. His claim would be moot, and the federal court would lack jurisdiction to adjudicate it.

The court emphasized that federal courts are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest." Without a clear factual record on the status of Consilio's policy, the court could not determine whether Cohen retained the requisite continuing interest.

## The Court's Decision to Vacate and Remand for the District Court to Determine Jurisdiction

Faced with this unresolved factual question, the court chose a prudent procedural course. Rather than decide the mootness issue itself, the court vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment on Cohen's MFLSA claim and remanded the case for the district court to determine its jurisdiction in the first instance.

This approach reflects several important principles of judicial administration. First, jurisdiction is a threshold question that must be resolved before a court may reach the merits of a claim. A federal court has an independent obligation to assure itself of its jurisdiction, regardless of whether the parties raise jurisdictional objections. Second, factual questions bearing on jurisdiction are properly resolved by the district court, which can develop the factual record and make credibility determinations. Third, remand allows the district court to consider the jurisdictional question with the benefit of additional briefing and evidence, if necessary.

On remand, the district court was tasked with determining whether Consilio's overtime policy remained in effect and, consequently, whether Cohen's claim for statutory penalties was live or moot. The court's decision preserves Cohen's opportunity to pursue penalties if he can demonstrate an ongoing violation, while respecting the jurisdictional limits imposed by Article III.

The concurrence by Judge Kobes added a separate perspective on the jurisdictional analysis. Judge Kobes expressed skepticism about whether Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties under *Laidlaw*, noting potential differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA. However, because neither party had briefed the standing issue, Judge Kobes agreed that vacating the judgment and remanding was the appropriate course, allowing the district court to address the mootness question first. This concurrence highlights that the jurisdictional questions in this case remain open for the district court to address on remand.

# The MWTA Claim, Injunctive Relief, and Preservation Issues

## The MWTA Claim: Failure to Provide Statutorily Required Notice of a Policy Change

Cohen's third statutory claim arose under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Specifically, Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy. Under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4), employers must provide employees with certain notices regarding wage arrangements. Cohen contended that when Consilio changed its overtime policy in August 2019—eliminating premium overtime pay—it failed to provide proper notice, and he sought penalties as a result.

This claim faced a significant obstacle from the outset. The parties had already stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit." This stipulation meant that Cohen could no longer claim unpaid wages as damages. Instead, Cohen sought civil penalties under the MWTA as his sole remaining remedy for the alleged notice violation.

## Cohen's Failure to Cite Statutory Authority for Civil Penalties Under the MWTA

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of Cohen's MWTA claim illustrates a fundamental principle of appellate advocacy: a party must provide legal authority supporting each element of its claims. Cohen argued that he was entitled to penalties under the MWTA, but he pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the specific provisions he invoked—§§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4).

The court noted simply that Cohen "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." This omission proved fatal to his claim. The court's approach demonstrates that courts will not construct arguments on behalf of litigants. When a statute creates a private right of action, the plaintiff bears the burden of identifying the statutory provisions that authorize the specific relief sought. If the plaintiff cannot point to language permitting civil penalties, the court will not presume such penalties exist.

This principle is particularly important in statutory interpretation cases. The court's analysis of Cohen's MPWA claim earlier in the opinion demonstrates the proper approach: carefully examining the statutory text to determine whether it authorizes the relief the plaintiff seeks. Having applied that methodology to the MPWA, the court expected Cohen to do the same for the MWTA. His failure to do so meant the court had no basis to reverse the district court's dismissal.

## Waiver Through Inadequate Appellate Briefing: The Milligan Standard

The Eighth Circuit's disposition of the MWTA claim rests on the doctrine of waiver through inadequate briefing. Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), the court stated: "inasmuch as [defendant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it."

The *Milligan* standard reflects a core principle of adversarial litigation. Appellate courts function as arbiters of the arguments parties present, not as independent investigators of potential claims. When a party fails to develop an argument with supporting legal authority, the court treats that argument as abandoned. This doctrine serves several purposes: it ensures that courts decide only issues that have been properly presented and argued, it respects the adversarial process, and it prevents courts from constructing arguments that parties themselves have not made.

Cohen's dissatisfaction with the district court's sparse discussion of his MWTA claim did not cure his waiver. He complained that the district court "included little to no discussion of this claim," but the appellate court responded that Cohen himself had provided "no meaningful argument in support of reversal." The burden was on Cohen, as the appellant, to demonstrate error—not on the court to independently construct a basis for reversal.

## Cohen's Shifting Injunctive Relief Requests from Complaint to Summary Judgment Briefing

Cohen's claim for injunctive relief presents a different procedural problem. In his complaint, Cohen "sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief." However, by the time he filed his summary judgment briefing, he had "change[d] course and request[ed] different relief"—specifically, an order requiring Consilio "to rescind its illegal overtime policy."

This shift created a mismatch between the claims and relief sought in the complaint and those advanced at summary judgment. The complaint defines the scope of the litigation. It provides notice to the defendant of what the plaintiff is seeking and establishes the framework for the parties' discovery and motion practice. When a plaintiff seeks new or different relief at summary judgment that was not included in the complaint, the defendant has had no opportunity to prepare a defense to that claim.

## The District Court's Proper Refusal to Consider Arguments Raised for the First Time in Opposition Briefs

The district court—understandably, in the Eighth Circuit's words—"did not consider" Cohen's new request for injunctive relief. This refusal was not an abuse of discretion but rather a proper application of procedural rules. Federal courts regularly decline to consider arguments or claims raised for the first time in opposition briefs rather than in the pleadings.

The logic behind this rule is straightforward. Litigation proceeds through defined stages: pleading, discovery, dispositive motions, and trial. Each stage serves specific functions. The pleading stage establishes the boundaries of the dispute. If parties could add new claims or requests for relief at any point through motion papers, the pleading requirements would become meaningless, and the opposing party would be unfairly surprised.

The Eighth Circuit has consistently held that new claims cannot be introduced through summary judgment opposition briefs. Citing *InfoDeli, LLC v. Those Characters, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), the court explained that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."

## Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and Proper Procedures for Amending Claims

The court's citation to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) points to the proper mechanism for Cohen to have pursued his new injunctive relief request. Rule 15(a) governs amended and supplemental pleadings. It provides that a party may amend its pleading once as a matter of course within 21 days after serving it, or within 21 days after service of a responsive pleading or a motion under Rule 12(b), (e), or (f). In all other cases, a party may amend its pleading only with the opposing party's written consent or the court's leave.

The rule further states that "the court should freely give leave when justice so requires." This liberal amendment policy reflects the preference for deciding cases on their merits rather than on procedural technicalities. However, the liberal amendment policy does not permit parties to raise new claims through motion practice instead of through the formal amendment process. A party who wishes to seek different relief must move to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a), not simply request that relief in a summary judgment brief.

Cohen did not follow this procedure. He sought new injunctive relief in his summary judgment briefing without first moving to amend his complaint. Because he bypassed the procedural requirements, his request for injunctive relief was not properly before either the district court or the Eighth Circuit.

## The Court's Affirmance of Dismissal of the MWTA and Injunctive Relief Claims

The Eighth Circuit's resolution of these claims was straightforward: "we will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim." The court affirmed the dismissal of both Cohen's MWTA claim and his claim for injunctive relief. In both instances, Cohen's procedural failures—failure to cite statutory authority for the MWTA penalties and failure to properly amend his complaint to seek different injunctive relief—proved fatal to his appeal.

These holdings illustrate an important lesson for law students and practicing attorneys alike. Substantive rights mean little if a litigant fails to follow procedural requirements. The strongest claim will fail if the attorney does not provide legal authority supporting it. The most compelling request for relief will be rejected if it is not properly pleaded. Procedural competence is not a mere technicality—it is essential to effective advocacy.
