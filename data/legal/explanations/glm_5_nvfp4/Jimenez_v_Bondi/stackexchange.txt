Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What level of harm is required to constitute "persecution" under the INA, and why don't threats alone qualify?
Question:
I'm reading the Tenth Circuit's decision in Jimenez v. Bondi where the court held that repeated threats at gunpoint, combined with psychological harm, did not constitute past persecution. The court says threats alone are insufficient and that the harm must be "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." But the applicants were held at gunpoint and told they would be killed. The court also mentions they consider incidents collectively under the cumulative effects doctrine. How much harm is actually required? Does physical injury have to occur? What makes threats cross the line into persecution?
Answer:
### Answer

The Tenth Circuit's decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates a key principle in asylum law: **persecution requires more than threats, even when those threats are frightening and repeated.**

Here's the legal framework the court applies:

## The Standard: "More Than Restrictions or Threats"

The INA does not define "persecution," but the Tenth Circuit (like other circuits) has established that persecution requires harm that is "offensive" and "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." The court cites *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004).

This means there's a threshold: threats—even death threats—fall below that line.

## Why Threats Alone Don't Qualify

The court states directly: **"threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution."** The court does consider threats as part of the cumulative analysis, but they cannot carry a persecution claim by themselves.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners experienced:
- Repeated threatening phone calls
- A threatening brochure ("death to snitches")
- Being held at gunpoint and told to leave the neighborhood
- Armed men threatening to kill them
- A man grabbing their son's shoulder and covering his mouth

Yet the court held this did not rise to persecution. The key factor: **the petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm.**

## Physical Harm Not Required, But...

The court acknowledges that physical harm is *not* required—the harm must simply be "more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." However, the court notes it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

The court compares *Jimenez* to cases with more severe facts that still failed:
- *Carias-Mejia*: threats with machetes and six robberies were insufficient because the threats were "contingent on compliance with a demand"
- *Chhetri*: four telephone threats and an in-person threat did not constitute persecution

## What Would Cross the Line?

The decision suggests persecution requires actual infliction of harm—not just its threat. The harm must be:
1. **Offensive suffering** inflicted on those who differ in a protected way, AND
2. **More than restrictions or threats** to life and liberty

Being held at gunpoint was terrifying, but since the men ultimately left without physically injuring anyone, the court viewed this as an escalated threat rather than persecution. The psychological harm (panic attacks, sleeplessness) stemming from threats was likewise insufficient.

## The Cumulative Effects Doctrine

The court applies the cumulative effects doctrine—looking at incidents collectively rather than in isolation. But even aggregating all incidents, the harm did not cross the threshold. The doctrine elevates multiple incidents to persecution only when the combined harm reaches the necessary level of severity—not simply because there were many incidents.

**Bottom line:** Under Tenth Circuit precedent, persecution requires the *actual infliction* of offensive harm. Threats create fear, but fear alone—even substantial psychological trauma from genuine danger—does not satisfy the legal standard for persecution.

### How does the "mixed-motive" analysis work for establishing nexus in asylum cases?
Question:
The court in Jimenez found that the persecutors had multiple motives - Jimenez's professional involvement in the property dispute AND his political opinion. The court said the political opinion was "tangential and subordinate" to the main reason (threatening Don Rafa's revenue and security). The opinion cites that a protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for persecution, not just a minor role. I'm confused about how courts determine which motive is "central" when persecutors mention multiple reasons during the threats. Here, the gunmen called them "snitches for the police" and mentioned they were "in politics" - so why wasn't political opinion considered central?
Answer:
### Answer

The mixed-motive analysis recognizes that persecutors can have multiple reasons for targeting someone, but the protected ground must be **"at least one central reason"** for the persecution—not merely incidental, tangential, or subordinate.

---

### The Legal Framework

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), an asylum applicant must establish that a protected ground (such as political opinion) was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. As the Tenth Circuit explained in *Jimenez*, quoting *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*:

> "That is, Petitioners' political opinion 'cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm' or play 'a minor role' in their past treatment or fears of future mistreatment."

In mixed-motive cases, the persecutor may have multiple motivations, but **at least one protected ground must be a central reason** for the harm. The court's task is to determine whether the protected ground is a primary driver or merely a secondary factor.

---

### How the Court Applied the Analysis

The court looked at the **totality of the circumstances** to determine what truly motivated the persecutors—not just what they said during the threats.

**Key factors the court considered:**

1. **Timing:** Before Jimenez's confrontation with Don Rafa over the property dispute, Petitioners received political threats but continued campaigning undeterred. There was no evidence Don Rafa was behind those earlier threats. The *escalated* threats began **only after** Jimenez investigated Don Rafa's property.

2. **Specific content of threats:** During the confrontation, the gunmen accused Jimenez of being a "snitch for the prosecutor's office" and being "responsible for searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property." When they returned, they explicitly stated "the order came from Don Rafa."

3. **Causal connection:** The property dispute directly threatened Don Rafa's "revenue and security"—his criminal enterprise operating on the premises. This personal and professional conflict was the predominant motive.

---

### Why "In Politics" Didn't Make Political Opinion Central

The persecutors' references to Petitioners being "snitches" who were "in politics" did not transform political opinion into a central reason. The court explained that the political element was merely **"tangential and subordinate"** to the primary motive: Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's criminal operations.

The court noted that merely mentioning a protected ground during threats does not establish nexus—the protected characteristic must be a genuine *reason for* the persecution, not just a descriptor used incidentally.

---

### The Practical Takeaway

In mixed-motive cases, courts examine:

- **When** the persecution began relative to the protected activity
- **What** specifically triggered the persecutor's actions
- **Whether** the protected ground is a primary driver or merely coincidental

Here, the property dispute was the "but-for" cause of the escalated harm, making the political opinion subordinate—even though politics was mentioned during the threats.

### What constitutes evidence that a government is "unable or unwilling" to protect asylum applicants?
Question:
The court held that Petitioners failed to show the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The opinion emphasizes that they only spoke to two individual police officers and didn't report to higher authorities, even though Jimenez had law enforcement connections through his work. The court says "a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" doesn't prove government inability. But the officer told them nothing could be done and that police were "contributing to the problem." How much evidence of government complicity or failure is actually required? Does an applicant need to exhaust all reporting options before proving futility?
Answer:
### Answer

The "unable or unwilling" standard requires evidence of **systemic government failure**, not merely isolated incidents of police unhelpfulness. The Tenth Circuit's analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi* clarifies what evidence is insufficient—and what an applicant must show to meet this burden.

---

### The Legal Standard

The court explains that the unable-or-unwilling analysis "considers evidence about a government's response to an applicant's past persecution." Two factors weigh heavily:

1. **Whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors**; and
2. **Whether the government offered protection to the applicant** (e.g., protective custody).

Critically, the government must have **notice and an opportunity to respond**. As the court states: *"Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped."*

---

### Why Petitioners' Evidence Fell Short

Petitioners argued that a police officer told them "nothing could be done" and that police were "contributing to the problem." The court rejected this for several reasons:

**1. Isolated officer statements don't prove government-wide inability.**  
The court cites *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* for the principle that *"we often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect its people."* A single conversation with an unhelpful officer is insufficient to demonstrate government inability.

**2. Petitioners failed to provide adequate notice to authorities.**  
Jimenez spoke only to "two individual police officers" and never escalated the matter. The court notes that *"there is nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government's position."* Notably, Jimenez had law enforcement connections through his work at the National Prosecutor's office, yet he never used those contacts to report the threats.

**3. The record contradicted claims of government complicity.**  
Petitioners argued their persecutors were "closely aligned" with the government. But the court pointed out that *"the government was aware of the property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there."* This evidence directly undermined the claim that authorities were unwilling to act.

---

### Must an Applicant Exhaust All Reporting Options?

**No, but the futility exception has limits.**  
The court acknowledges that *"an applicant may satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous."* However, the applicant bears the burden of proving futility.

Here, Petitioners' futility argument failed because: (1) they had professional connections to law enforcement they never used, and (2) the record showed the government was actively investigating the very criminal activity at issue. As the court notes, *"without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond."*

---

### Key Takeaway

To prove a government is "unable or unwilling" to protect an asylum applicant, evidence must demonstrate **systemic failure or complicity**, not isolated incidents of police indifference. A single dismissive officer—even one suggesting corruption—does not satisfy the standard without additional evidence that higher authorities would likewise fail to respond.

### How does the "substantial evidence" standard of review constrain appellate courts in asylum cases?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly states that the BIA's findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This seems like an extremely deferential standard. The court says it's not their prerogative to "reweigh the evidence." I'm struggling to understand when an appellate court can actually reverse BIA factual findings under this standard. Is this essentially a rubber-stamp review? What would have to be in the record for the court to find the evidence "compels" a contrary conclusion?
Answer:
### Answer

You're right to notice that this is an extremely deferential standard—but it is not quite a "rubber stamp." The substantial evidence standard is deliberately designed to reserve fact-finding for the agency, not the reviewing court. However, the standard does have teeth, and the opinion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates both its limits and how a petitioner might meet it.

**The Standard: "Compelled to Conclude to the Contrary"**

As the court states, the BIA's factual findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This language comes from *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder* and is the Tenth Circuit's formulation of the substantial evidence standard used across immigration cases.

This means the court cannot reverse simply because it would have weighed the evidence differently. The court explicitly states: "it is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ's [or the BIA's] decision." The question is not whether the court agrees with the BIA, but whether the BIA's conclusion falls within the bounds of reasonableness given the record.

**When Can the Court Reverse?**

Reversal is appropriate only when the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable fact-finder could reach the BIA's conclusion. The court must be *compelled* to a contrary result—meaning the record leaves no room for reasonable disagreement. This is a high bar, but not impossible to meet.

The opinion shows what does *not* clear this bar. Consider the nexus analysis: Petitioners argued they were targeted for their political opinion. The BIA found instead that they were targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa's criminal enterprise. The court noted that:

- Petitioners continued political activities undeterred after initial threats
- The escalation coincided with Jimenez's property dispute with Don Rafa
- Threats referenced Jimenez's role in searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property

Given these facts, the BIA's conclusion that political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate" is supported by substantial evidence—even if another fact-finder might have weighed the evidence differently. The court cannot substitute its own judgment.

**What Would "Compel" a Contrary Finding?**

To compel reversal, a petitioner would need to show that the record contains overwhelming, uncontradicted evidence supporting their claim, with no reasonable basis for the BIA's finding. Hypothetically, this might occur if:

1. **The BIA ignored undisputed facts**: If the record showed Petitioners reported threats to multiple government agencies and all ignored them, and the BIA nonetheless found they failed to seek protection, that finding might be unreasonable.

2. **The BIA's finding is internally contradictory**: If the BIA found Petitioners credible but then rejected their testimony without explanation, reversal might be compelled.

3. **The evidence is entirely one-sided**: If threats explicitly and exclusively referenced political activity—with no mention of any other motive—and the BIA still found no nexus, a court might find the evidence compels a contrary conclusion.

The opinion hints at this in the government protection analysis. The court notes that Petitioners spoke only to "two individual police officers" and "there is nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government's position." Citing *Osorio-Morales*, the court distinguishes "individual failures" from "systemic" government inability to protect. If Petitioners had shown systemic government complicity—rather than isolated police apathy—the BIA's finding might not have survived review.

**Conclusion**

Substantial evidence review is highly deferential but not illusory. The court can reverse when the record leaves no reasonable path to the BIA's conclusion. The *Jimenez* opinion demonstrates that petitioners rarely meet this standard because the BIA's findings usually have some foothold in the record—even if another fact-finder would have reached a different result. The standard reserves fact-finding for the agency, ensuring courts do not become de facto immigration judges.

### What is the relationship between asylum and withholding of removal standards?
Question:
At the end of the opinion, the court states that "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." I know withholding of removal has a "more likely than not" standard compared to asylum's "well-founded fear" standard. But are there other differences? And can someone qualify for withholding even if they don't qualify for asylum, or are the requirements essentially the same except for the burden of proof?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is: **you cannot qualify for withholding of removal if you don't qualify for asylum** (at least on the persecution analysis), because withholding requires a higher burden of proof on the same underlying elements.

Here's the relationship between the two standards as explained in *Jimenez v. Bondi*:

## The Core Distinction: Burden of Proof

The opinion articulates the fundamental relationship clearly:

> "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal."

This "necessarily fail" language tells you that **withholding's standard is a strict superset of asylum's standard**. If you cannot meet the lower bar, you cannot clear the higher one.

The opinion (quoting *Carias-Mejia*) establishes that:
- **Asylum** requires a "well-founded fear" of persecution
- **Withholding of removal** requires showing persecution is "more likely than not"

The "more likely than not" standard is substantially higher—you must demonstrate a greater than 50% probability of persecution.

## The Substantive Elements Are the Same

Both forms of relief require the applicant to prove the **same underlying elements**. The opinion walks through these requirements for asylum:

1. **Persecution**: Harm that "entail[s] more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty"
2. **Nexus**: The persecution must be "on account of" a statutorily protected ground (race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion), meaning the protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution
3. **Government inability or unwillingness**: The government must be unable or unwilling to control the persecutors
4. **Internal relocation**: The applicant must show they cannot safely relocate within their home country

These same elements apply to withholding of removal—the difference lies entirely in how strongly you must prove them.

## One Additional Distinction: Discretionary vs. Mandatory

The opinion notes that "asylum is a **discretionary form of relief** which may be granted by the Attorney General to eligible noncitizens."

While the opinion doesn't explicitly contrast this with withholding, this is a key doctrinal difference worth understanding: asylum is discretionary (even if eligible, the government may deny it), whereas withholding of removal—if the higher standard is met—is **mandatory**.

## Conclusion

To answer your question directly: **No, you cannot qualify for withholding of removal if you fail to qualify for asylum on the merits.** The court's reasoning makes this explicit—failing the lower "well-founded fear" standard necessarily means failing the higher "more likely than not" standard. The requirements are not "essentially the same except for the burden of proof" in a trivial sense; rather, the **entirety** of withholding's requirements subsume asylum's requirements, plus a higher burden of proof.

### When can an applicant successfully argue that internal relocation within their home country is not reasonable?
Question:
The court found that Petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia because they stayed with Jimenez's mother in the countryside for three weeks unharmed, and the threats specifically told them to leave their "neighborhood" not the country. Petitioners argued relocation was unworkable due to administrative burdens and their persecutors' ties to the president. The court dismissed these as "hypothetical hardships." What factors actually make internal relocation unreasonable? Does the persecutor's geographic reach matter, or is it purely about whether the applicant personally can find safety somewhere in the country?
Answer:
## When Internal Relocation Is Unreasonable

The court in *Jimenez v. Bondi* applies the established framework for internal relocation analysis under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i). Under this regulation, the government can rebut a presumption of future persecution by showing the applicant "could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant's country."

### The Legal Standard: Reasonableness

The critical question is whether relocation would be **reasonable**—not merely whether the applicant could physically survive elsewhere. The analysis turns on whether the applicant can "safely and reasonably" relocate, as the court emphasizes.

Based on *Jimenez*, courts assess several concrete factors:

**1. Geographic scope of the threat.** The court found significant that the persecutors told Petitioners to leave their "neighborhood," not the country. This geographic limitation suggested the threat was localized, making relocation to other parts of Colombia viable.

**2. Demonstrated safety elsewhere in the country.** Petitioners stayed with family in the Bogota countryside for three weeks without incident. The court treated this as actual evidence that alternative locations provided safety.

**3. Safety of similarly situated family members.** The court noted that Petitioners' family and relatives "continue to live in Colombia unharmed" and that Jimenez's father "still works for the criminal justice system." This undercut claims that the persecutors posed a nationwide threat.

### What Does NOT Render Relocation Unreasonable

The court rejected several arguments as insufficient:

- **Hypothetical hardships**: Petitioners argued family members might be targeted due to their own political opinions, but the court refused to grant relief based on speculation about what "may" happen.

- **Administrative and economic burdens**: The court dismissed arguments about practical difficulties of relocation as "haphazardly briefed" and insufficient.

- **Alleged government ties to persecutors**: Petitioners claimed Don Rafa was "politically aligned" with Colombia's president. But the court found the record contradicted this—the government had actually *attempted* searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property. The court requires "evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures" to prove government inability to protect.

### The Persecutor's Geographic Reach Matters Significantly

Your instinct is correct: the persecutor's geographic reach is central to the analysis. If Petitioners had shown Don Rafa had influence extending throughout Colombia, or that his political connections made him untouchable nationwide, relocation would be unreasonable.

The problem for Petitioners was evidentiary. The court noted:
- The threats were neighborhood-specific
- They successfully stayed elsewhere unharmed
- The government had taken some action against Don Rafa's property
- Family members lived safely elsewhere

### Summary

Internal relocation is unreasonable when the applicant demonstrates that:
1. The persecutor has nationwide reach or influence
2. The government is unwilling or unable to protect anywhere in the country (requiring evidence of *systemic* failure, not isolated police apathy)
3. Practical obstacles render relocation genuinely infeasible (though bare assertions of "administrative burdens" are insufficient)

The burden is on the applicant to prove these elements. Speculative or hypothetical dangers do not suffice.

### Can past political activity that predates the actual persecution establish nexus to a protected ground?
Question:
The court noted that before Jimenez's confrontation with Don Rafa, the family received threats related to their Liberal Party membership and continued campaigning despite a colleague being murdered. But the court found the escalated threats came only after the property dispute. Does prior political persecution that's unrelated to the main persecutor count for establishing nexus? The court said there was no evidence Don Rafa was behind the initial political threats - so if a different group persecuted someone politically first, does that help establish that later persecution by someone else was also politically motivated?
Answer:
### Answer

**Short answer: No, not in this case.** The court found that prior political persecution by unknown actors does not establish nexus for later persecution by a different actor where the evidence shows the later persecution was motivated by something else entirely.

---

### The Nexus Requirement

To establish nexus, an asylum applicant must show that a protected ground—here, political opinion—was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). The protected ground cannot be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012).

A persecutor can have mixed motives, but at least one protected ground must be a *central* reason—not merely tangential.

---

### Why the Prior Political Threats Didn't Establish Nexus

The court acknowledged that before Jimenez's confrontation with Don Rafa, the family received threatening phone calls related to their Liberal Party membership, and a colleague was murdered. But this did not save their nexus argument for two key reasons:

**1. No evidence connecting Don Rafa to the initial political threats.**

> "Petitioners do not point to any evidence in the record that suggests Don Rafa was behind those initial politically affiliated threats."

The initial threats came from an unidentified source. The later, escalated threats came from Don Rafa's associates. Without evidence linking Don Rafa to the earlier political threats, the prior persecution is legally irrelevant to establishing *his* motive.

**2. The timing and content showed a different motive.**

The court emphasized that the escalated threats began *only after* Jimenez became involved in the property dispute with Don Rafa:

> "In fact, Petitioners only began receiving escalated threats after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa, and after Don Rafa became aware that Jimenez was investigating his rented property."

During the confrontations, the perpetrators accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and held him responsible for searches and seizures at the property—explicitly tying the threats to his interference with Don Rafa's criminal operations, not his political activity.

---

### The Key Principle

The court's reasoning illustrates a fundamental point about nexus: you must show that *the specific persecutor* was motivated by a protected ground. Past persecution by one actor for political reasons does not automatically imbue later persecution by a different actor with political motive.

Here, the court found substantial evidence that Don Rafa targeted Jimenez because he "threatened Don Rafa's revenue and security, not because of their political opinion." The political opinion element was, at most, "merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm."

---

### Bottom Line

Prior political activity or persecution *can* be relevant to nexus—if it helps establish that the persecutor at issue acted on account of that protected ground. But where, as here, there's no evidence linking the persecutor to the earlier threats and the timeline shows the persecution escalated for unrelated reasons, the prior political activity carries little weight. The focus remains on *this* persecutor's *actual* motives.
