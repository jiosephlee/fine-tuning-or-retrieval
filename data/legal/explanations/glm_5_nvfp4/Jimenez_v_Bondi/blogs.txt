Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Mixed Motives and the Nexus Requirement: When Political Opinion Is Merely 'Tangential'

The Tenth Circuit's decision last week in *Jimenez v. Bondi* offers a instructive example of how courts grapple with one of the more difficult questions in asylum law: when a persecutor has multiple motives for targeting a victim, how do adjudicators determine whether a protected ground—such as political opinion—was "at least one central reason" for the persecution? The answer, as the court's analysis demonstrates, often turns on close factual examination of timing, language, and the precise nature of the threat.

## The Facts and the Framework

Wilson Jimenez and his family, Colombian nationals, petitioned for asylum based on a campaign of intimidation they attributed to their political activities. Jimenez was a member of the Colombian Liberal Party and had actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a rival to Colombia's current president Gustavo Petro. The family had received threatening phone calls, a colleague had been murdered, and eventually armed men confronted Jimenez at his furniture factory, threatened his family, and attempted to grab his young son.

But there was a wrinkle. During the same period, Jimenez—a provisionally licensed attorney—had agreed to help a client with a property dispute. The tenant turned out to be Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa," a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group politically aligned with President Petro. Jimenez's intervention threatened Don Rafa's criminal operation. The threats against Jimenez escalated dramatically only after this confrontation.

The Tenth Circuit, affirming the Board of Immigration Appeals, concluded that Jimenez's political opinion was at most incidental to his persecution. The real motive, the court found, was retaliation for Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's criminal enterprise.

## Disentangling Mixed Motives

The nexus requirement sits at the heart of asylum eligibility. Congress codified the "one central reason" standard in the REAL ID Act of 2005, establishing that a protected ground cannot be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." But persecutors are complicated actors. They can harbor multiple animating grievances simultaneously. The law recognizes this reality while demanding that a protected ground remain central, not peripheral.

Here, the court's analysis turned largely on temporal evidence. The Jimenez family had received politically tinged threats before the property dispute—but they continued campaigning undeterred. Only after Jimenez tangled with Don Rafa did the threats escalate to armed confrontation and direct threats to kill. The content of the threats also proved revealing: the assailants called Jimenez a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for searches at Don Rafa's property. The court reasonably inferred that Don Rafa's primary concern was protecting his revenue stream, not advancing Petro's political agenda.

This is a fact-intensive inquiry, and the court properly gave substantial deference to the BIA's findings. But the decision also illustrates how timing evidence can become dispositive in mixed-motive cases. Applicants who suffered harm before any non-political dispute arose have a stronger claim; those whose persecution escalates only after a personal or professional conflict may struggle to establish that politics, rather than retaliation, drove the persecution.

## The Snitch Label and Imputed Political Opinion

One subtle aspect of the decision deserves attention. Jimenez argued that the label "snitch"—invoked by his persecutors—reflected an imputed political opinion opposing government corruption. The court rejected this argument without extensive analysis, noting only that the evidence showed Jimenez was targeted for threatening Don Rafa's "revenue and security."

This touches on an ongoing doctrinal question: when does an imputed characteristic become a protected ground? The Tenth Circuit has previously recognized that an imputed political opinion can support asylum eligibility. But the mere fact that persecutors use political language doesn't transform a personal vendetta into political persecution. Don Rafa may have believed Jimenez was cooperating with authorities, but that belief stemmed from Jimenez's legal work on a specific property dispute, not from any actual or perceived political ideology. The court's parsimonious approach to imputed political opinion claims seems sound—otherwise, any persecutor could retroactively politicize a purely personal conflict by invoking political rhetoric.

## What This Means for Future Litigants

*Jimenez* reinforces several practical lessons for asylum practitioners. First, nexus arguments require evidence beyond the applicant's subjective belief about why they were targeted. Courts will look to what the persecutors said and did, when threats escalated, and whether non-political disputes provided an alternative explanation.

Second, the decision highlights the challenge of proving that local police indifference reflects government-wide inability or unwillingness to protect. The court emphasized that Jimenez never formally reported the threats, instead relying on informal conversations with patrol officers. For applicants from countries with localized corruption, this creates a dilemma: reporting may be dangerous, but failure to report undermines the unable-or-unwilling showing. The Tenth Circuit has suggested that applicants should at least attempt to seek protection through official channels or document why doing so would be futile.

Finally, the case underscores that safe internal relocation remains a viable defense for the government. The court noted that Jimenez's family continued to live safely in Colombia, that the threats were neighborhood-specific, and that the persecutors told Jimenez to leave the area—not the country. When the harm is geographically bounded, applicants face an uphill battle proving that relocation would be unreasonable.

## The Bottom Line

*Jimenez v. Bondi* doesn't break new doctrinal ground, but it illustrates how courts apply established principles in messy factual contexts. The line between a central motive and a tangential one often depends on granular factual details that emerge only at the merits hearing. For asylum applicants with complex persecution narratives involving both political activity and personal disputes, the case serves as a reminder that the burden of proving nexus remains heavy—and that the government will seize on any alternative explanation for why the persecutor came calling.



# The Cumulative Harm Doctrine's Limits: Threats, Psychological Injury, and the Persecution Threshold

The Tenth Circuit's recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* offers a stark illustration of the high bar asylum seekers must clear to establish past persecution—even when the threats against them are repeated, explicit, and backed by violence against others. The court's October 7 opinion denies review of a Colombian family's asylum claims, holding that their experiences, taken together, did not rise to the level of persecution under the Immigration and Nationality Act. In doing so, the decision reinforces a restrictive application of the cumulative harm doctrine and clarifies the boundaries of what constitutes persecution when physical injury is absent.

## A Fact Pattern of Escalating Danger

The petitioners' circumstances were undeniably frightening. Wilson Jimenez, a law student and Liberal Party activist in Colombia, received threatening phone calls about his political work beginning in May 2022. A fellow party member was kidnapped and murdered during this period. But the threats escalated dramatically after Jimenez became involved in a property dispute on behalf of a client—where he discovered that the occupant was Rafael "Don Rafa" Montanez Lancheros, a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader with political connections.

What followed reads like a thriller: a threatening brochure appearing at the family furniture factory; two men holding Jimenez at gunpoint while calling him a "snitch"; those same men returning weeks later to assault an employee and demand the family's location; and finally, a stranger grabbing Jimenez's young son outside his school and telling the boy to "run to your dad." The family fled to the United States shortly thereafter.

The immigration judge found the petitioners credible. Yet the IJ still denied asylum, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed.

## The Cumulative Harm Doctrine and Its Limits

The central question was whether these incidents, considered collectively, constituted persecution. The Tenth Circuit has long held that courts must evaluate the cumulative effect of multiple incidents rather than examining each in isolation. But as *Jimenez* demonstrates, even a substantial accumulation of threatening conduct may fall short.

Judge Tymkovich, writing for the court, acknowledged that the petitioners endured "unfortunate circumstances" and that the events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling." Yet the court held that threats alone—even repeated, credible threats from violent actors—do not satisfy the persecution standard. The petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm and no lasting physical injury. Their psychological injuries, including panic attacks and sleeplessness, were insufficient to bridge the gap.

The decision relies heavily on precedent holding that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." The court noted that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." This categorical approach to threats represents a significant doctrinal limitation on cumulative harm analysis: no matter how many threats accumulate, and no matter how genuine the fear they produce, the persecution threshold remains unmet without physical harm or equivalent suffering.

This framework creates something of a paradox. The petitioners experienced what most reasonable people would call terrifying persecution: armed confrontations, explicit death threats, and a direct physical approach to their child. Yet under existing Tenth Circuit precedent, the absence of actual physical injury to the petitioners themselves proved fatal to their claim. The court cited unpublished decisions involving machete threats and six robberies that still failed to meet the standard—suggesting that only the most severe physical violence will suffice.

## Nexus and the Mixed-Motive Problem

The court's nexus analysis compounds the difficulty for asylum seekers in similar positions. Even if the petitioners had established persecution, the court held that their political opinion was merely "tangential and subordinate" to the primary motive: Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's criminal enterprise.

The court acknowledged that persecutors can have mixed motives, but emphasized that the protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution—not merely incidental. Here, the timeline was telling: the petitioners had continued their political activities despite earlier threats, and the escalation occurred only after Jimenez confronted Don Rafa. The threats referenced Jimenez as a "snitch for the prosecutor's office" and a "snitch for the police"—language the court interpreted as evidencing personal retaliation rather than political targeting.

This analysis illustrates the challenge of establishing political opinion claims when the persecutor has multiple plausible motivations. The court's willingness to credit the property dispute as the "predominant motive" reflects a demanding standard for applicants to demonstrate that their protected characteristic is truly central to the persecution.

## Government Protection and the Relocation Alternative

The court's treatment of the government protection and internal relocation issues further narrowed the path to relief. On government protection, the court emphasized that the petitioners made only minimal efforts to report the threats—speaking briefly with two local police officers who told them to avoid trouble. The court found this insufficient to demonstrate that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, particularly given that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of Don Rafa's property.

Notably, the court discounted the officers' dismissive responses as reflecting "individual" failures rather than systemic government inability. This distinction between isolated police apathy and government-wide incapacity represents another significant hurdle for asylum applicants, especially those threatened by actors with alleged political connections.

On relocation, the court noted that the family had safely stayed with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before departing for the United States. The threats had directed them to leave the neighborhood, not the country. Combined with evidence that family members remained unharmed in Colombia, the court found internal relocation both feasible and reasonable.

## Doctrinal Implications

*Jimenez* reinforces several key principles that will shape future asylum litigation in the Tenth Circuit. First, it establishes that the cumulative harm doctrine has a ceiling: threats, no matter how numerous or menacing, cannot alone constitute persecution without physical harm. Second, it signals skepticism toward claims of government inability to protect where the applicant has not made serious efforts to involve higher authorities. Third, it demonstrates a rigorous approach to nexus analysis in mixed-motive cases, requiring clear evidence that the protected ground is central rather than merely present.

The decision also raises broader questions about the purposes of asylum law. The statutory framework protects those persecuted on account of protected characteristics, but the court's reasoning suggests that the form that persecution takes matters as much as the motive. A person targeted for their political beliefs who receives only threats may have no claim, while someone physically assaulted for a different reason might qualify. Whether this distinction properly serves the humanitarian goals of asylum law is a question that will continue to divide courts and scholars.

For practitioners, *Jimenez* underscores the critical importance of documenting any physical harm, however minor, and establishing a clear temporal and causal connection between protected activities and persecution. In a legal regime where threats are categorically insufficient, the line between persecution and mere harassment remains sharply drawn—and for petitioners like the Jimenez family, that line may mean the difference between safety and return to danger.



# The Reporting Requirement: Individual Police Failures vs. Systemic Government Inability

The Tenth Circuit's recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* highlights a persistent tension in asylum law: how applicants can prove that a foreign government is "unable or unwilling" to protect them when they never meaningfully asked for that protection in the first place. The court's analysis clarifies that conversations with individual police officers—no matter how discouraging—cannot substitute for evidence of systemic government failure, and it illustrates how evidence of government anti-crime efforts can cut against claims of state complicity.

## The Facts and the Fatal Gap

Wilson Jimenez and his family faced genuinely frightening circumstances. After Jimenez, a provisional lawyer and Liberal Party activist, intervened in a property dispute involving a local narco-trafficking leader known as "Don Rafa," the threats came quickly and escalated steadily. Armed men showed up at his furniture factory, demanded to know his whereabouts, and ultimately threatened to kill him and his family. One man even grabbed Jimenez's young son outside his school.

But when it came to seeking government protection, the record was thin. Jimenez spoke to one police officer who had warned him to stay away from the disputed property. Later, after the incidents escalated, he spoke to a patrol officer who told him nothing could be done and that officers were "contributing to the problem." That was essentially the sum total of his outreach to Colombian authorities—despite having worked as an intern in the National Prosecutor's office and having a father who worked in the criminal justice system.

## The "Unable or Unwilling" Standard and the Notice Requirement

The court's analysis of the "unable or unwilling" prong is instructive. As Judge Tymkovich explains, the analysis "considers evidence about a government's response to an applicant's past persecution." Two factors weigh heavily: whether the government tried to stop the persecutors, and whether it offered protection to the applicant.

Crucially, the court emphasized that the government must have notice of the persecution and an opportunity to respond. "Without such notice," the court reasoned, "there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." This creates what amounts to a reporting requirement: applicants generally must have alerted authorities to their persecution, unless they can show that doing so would have been futile or dangerous.

The court stopped short of calling the failure to report fatal to an asylum claim, acknowledging that "a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors," but "is not necessarily fatal." The applicant can still satisfy the requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.

## Individual Apathy vs. Systemic Failure

This is where the court's distinction between individual and systemic government failure becomes critical. Petitioners argued that the officers' dismissive responses demonstrated the government's inability or unwillingness to protect them. The court rejected this reasoning, citing the Seventh Circuit's observation that "we often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect its people."

The court's analysis here is sound but highlights an Catch-22 that many asylum applicants face. On one hand, a single unhelpful officer cannot prove systemic government failure. On the other hand, applicants who have already received a brush-off from local police may reasonably fear that escalating their complaints will only expose them to more danger. The court's framework requires applicants to navigate between these obstacles by showing that reporting would have been futile—no easy task given that it requires proving a counterfactual about how the government would have responded.

## Evidence of Government Efforts Cuts Against Complicity

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of the decision is how the court treated evidence that the Colombian government had actually attempted to address criminal activity at Don Rafa's property. Petitioners argued that their persecutors were "closely aligned and associated" with the Colombian government, given Don Rafa's political connections to President Gustavo Petro. But the court noted that the government had "attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct" at the property.

This evidence cut against claims of government complicity in precisely the way the "unable or unwilling" analysis contemplates. If the government is actively trying to stop a persecutor—even if those efforts have been unsuccessful—that weighs heavily against finding that the government is unwilling to protect the applicant. The court's reasoning suggests that applicants who allege government alignment with their persecutors must grapple with any evidence of law enforcement action against those same actors.

## The Practical Burden

The decision underscores the practical burden placed on asylum applicants to demonstrate governmental inability or unwillingness to protect them. Petitioners who have faced threats from powerful criminal actors face an evidentiary gauntlet: they must show that they reported the persecution, or that reporting would have been futile, and they must produce evidence of systemic government failure rather than isolated police indifference.

For applicants like the Jimenez family, who may have reasonably feared that going to authorities would only endanger them further, this burden is formidable. The court's analysis suggests that mere fear of futility may not be enough—applicants need evidence that reporting would have been pointless. In cases involving corruption or organized crime, such evidence may be difficult to obtain without exposing oneself to additional danger.

## Implications for Future Cases

*Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a reminder that the "unable or unwilling" analysis remains a significant hurdle for asylum applicants, particularly those fleeing private actors rather than government persecution. The decision reinforces that applicants should document their attempts to seek government protection and should be prepared to explain why additional efforts would have been futile.

The case also highlights a strategic consideration for practitioners: evidence of government law enforcement efforts against the persecutor can undermine claims of state complicity. Applicants would be well-served to anticipate such evidence and explain why those efforts were ineffective or insincere.

Ultimately, the Tenth Circuit's decision reflects the difficult balance that asylum law strikes between offering protection to those fleeing persecution and requiring applicants to show that their home government cannot or will not protect them. For applicants fleeing powerful non-state actors, that balance may often tip toward denial.
