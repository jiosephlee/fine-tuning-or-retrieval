Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What level of harm is required to constitute "persecution" under the INA, and why don't threats alone qualify?
Question:
I'm reading the Tenth Circuit's decision in Jimenez v. Bondi where the court held that repeated threats at gunpoint, combined with psychological harm, did not constitute past persecution. The court says threats alone are insufficient and that the harm must be "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." But the applicants were held at gunpoint and told they would be killed. The court also mentions they consider incidents collectively under the cumulative effects doctrine. How much harm is actually required? Does physical injury have to occur? What makes threats cross the line into persecution?
Answer:
### Answer

The Tenth Circuit's decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates a key principle in asylum law: **persecution requires more than threats, even when those threats are frightening and repeated.**

Here's the legal framework the court applies:

## The Standard: "More Than Restrictions or Threats"

The INA does not define "persecution," but the Tenth Circuit (like other circuits) has established that persecution requires harm that is "offensive" and "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." The court cites *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004).

This means there's a threshold: threats—even death threats—fall below that line.

## Why Threats Alone Don't Qualify

The court states directly: **"threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution."** The court does consider threats as part of the cumulative analysis, but they cannot carry a persecution claim by themselves.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners experienced:
- Repeated threatening phone calls
- A threatening brochure ("death to snitches")
- Being held at gunpoint and told to leave the neighborhood
- Armed men threatening to kill them
- A man grabbing their son's shoulder and covering his mouth

Yet the court held this did not rise to persecution. The key factor: **the petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm.**

## Physical Harm Not Required, But...

The court acknowledges that physical harm is *not* required—the harm must simply be "more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." However, the court notes it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

The court compares *Jimenez* to cases with more severe facts that still failed:
- *Carias-Mejia*: threats with machetes and six robberies were insufficient because the threats were "contingent on compliance with a demand"
- *Chhetri*: four telephone threats and an in-person threat did not constitute persecution

## What Would Cross the Line?

The decision suggests persecution requires actual infliction of harm—not just its threat. The harm must be:
1. **Offensive suffering** inflicted on those who differ in a protected way, AND
2. **More than restrictions or threats** to life and liberty

Being held at gunpoint was terrifying, but since the men ultimately left without physically injuring anyone, the court viewed this as an escalated threat rather than persecution. The psychological harm (panic attacks, sleeplessness) stemming from threats was likewise insufficient.

## The Cumulative Effects Doctrine

The court applies the cumulative effects doctrine—looking at incidents collectively rather than in isolation. But even aggregating all incidents, the harm did not cross the threshold. The doctrine elevates multiple incidents to persecution only when the combined harm reaches the necessary level of severity—not simply because there were many incidents.

**Bottom line:** Under Tenth Circuit precedent, persecution requires the *actual infliction* of offensive harm. Threats create fear, but fear alone—even substantial psychological trauma from genuine danger—does not satisfy the legal standard for persecution.
