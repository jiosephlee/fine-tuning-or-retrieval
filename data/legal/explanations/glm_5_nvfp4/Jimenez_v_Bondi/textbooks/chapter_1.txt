Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Introduction and Factual Background

This chapter introduces the petitioners and the events in Colombia that gave rise to their asylum claim. Understanding the factual background is essential because asylum law requires applicants to connect their experiences of harm to specific legal standards. The court's analysis of whether petitioners qualify for asylum turns entirely on how the facts measure against these standards. As you read the factual account, consider how each incident might—or might not—satisfy the elements of an asylum claim: persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and government inability or unwillingness to protect.

## The Petitioners: Wilson Jimenez, Angelica Parada Duarte, and Their Minor Sons

The petitioners in this case are a family of four from Bogota, Colombia. Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, are the lead applicants, and their two minor sons are included in the proceedings. The family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota before relocating to the United States in December 2022.

In immigration proceedings, family members often file consolidated applications. Here, the minor children proceeded as "rider respondents" and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez's asylum application. This procedural arrangement means the children's claims depend on the success of the primary applicant's claim rather than standing independently. The court's analysis therefore focuses primarily on Jimenez's and Duarte's experiences, with the children's status tied to the outcome of the parents' case.

## Political Involvement: Membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and Campaigning for Federico Gutierrez

The petitioners had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012. During the 2022 Colombian presidential election, they actively supported Federico Gutierrez, a presidential candidate. Their political involvement went beyond passive membership: they engaged in door-to-door canvassing in local neighborhoods to solicit votes for their candidate.

This factual detail matters because asylum law protects individuals who face persecution "on account of political opinion." To qualify for protection, an applicant must show that their political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the persecution they suffered. The petitioners' active campaigning establishes that they held—and openly expressed—a political opinion. The question for the court becomes whether the harm they suffered was motivated by that political opinion or by something else entirely.

## Initial Threats Related to Political Activity: Phone Calls and the Murder of a Colleague

Beginning around late May 2022, Jimenez started receiving threatening phone calls regarding his involvement with the Liberal Party. These calls came once or twice a week. Other Liberal Party leaders and colleagues received similar threats. In early June 2022, the situation escalated dramatically: a colleague who had reported threats he received was kidnapped and murdered.

Despite these alarming developments, the petitioners continued their political campaigning. This detail is significant for the court's legal analysis. When applicants claim persecution based on political opinion, courts examine whether the persecutors' motives relate to the applicants' political activities. The petitioners argue that these early threats—connected to their party work—demonstrate they were targeted for their political beliefs.

## Jimenez's Professional Background: Law Student, Provisional License, and Work with the National Prosecutor's Office

In 2022, Jimenez held a provisional law license and was a law student. He also worked as an intern for Colombia's National Prosecutor's office, where he assisted victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. This professional background becomes relevant because it established Jimenez's connection to law enforcement and the criminal justice system.

The court's nexus analysis examines whether the persecution was motivated by political opinion or by Jimenez's professional role. Persecutors can have mixed motives, but the protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for the harm. Jimenez's work with the prosecutor's office provides an alternative explanation for why he might have been targeted—one that the court must consider alongside his political activities.

## The Property Dispute: Jimenez's Representation of a Client Against "Don Rafa"

In June 2022, a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez for legal help with a property dispute. Her tenant had not paid rent for over a year, and she wanted Jimenez to negotiate with him about restitution and vacating the property. When Jimenez visited the property to negotiate, the tenant refused and told him not to "look for trouble."

Jimenez later discovered that the tenant was actually the client's ex-husband—Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa." Don Rafa was a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas who were politically aligned with Colombia's current president, Gustavo Petro. This discovery transformed a routine property dispute into a confrontation with a powerful criminal figure.

The timing of events becomes crucial to the legal analysis. The petitioners had received threats related to their political activities before this property dispute. But the most serious incidents—the gunpoint confrontation, the assault on their employee, and the attempted abduction of their son—all occurred after Jimenez's involvement with Don Rafa. The court must determine which of these two factors—political opinion or the property dispute—was the central reason for the persecution.

## Discovery of Criminal Activity: The Sealed Property Used for Narco-Trafficking, Prostitution, and Reports of Kidnapping and Murder

During his visit to the property, Jimenez observed that the third floor had been sealed off by the health department. His investigation revealed that the property had been closed because it was being used as a day-rental for migrants and as a base for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The premises were also linked to reports of kidnapping and murder. Law enforcement had attempted several searches and seizures at the property but had been unsuccessful. A local police officer warned Jimenez to stay away and not seek problems.

This discovery raises the stakes of the property dispute considerably. Jimenez had stumbled onto evidence of serious criminal enterprise, and Don Rafa apparently believed Jimenez was responsible for the law enforcement attention. The court's nexus analysis turns on whether Don Rafa targeted Jimenez because of his political opinion or because Jimenez had threatened Don Rafa's criminal operations.

## Escalating Threats and Incidents: The Threatening Brochure, Gunpoint Confrontation, Assault on Employee, and Attempted Abduction of Jimenez's Son

After Jimenez's confrontation with Don Rafa, the threats escalated dramatically. Later in June 2022, Jimenez received a threatening brochure at his factory that stated "death to snitches from Liberal Party." Jimenez discarded it. But the following month, two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at his factory and told him to leave the neighborhood. One accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for the searches at Don Rafa's property.

About two months later, the same two men returned to the factory while the petitioners were away. They assaulted an employee and, at gunpoint, demanded to know where the petitioners were. The men again called them "snitches for the police" who were involved in politics. They threatened to kill the petitioners if seen again, stating the order came from Don Rafa.

Days later, Jimenez witnessed a man grab his younger son outside the boy's school. The man put his arm around the boy, covered his mouth as he tried to escape, and told him to "run to your dad." Jimenez intervened, and the family fled to his mother's home in the Bogota countryside before ultimately leaving for the United States.

These facts present the core question for the court's persecution analysis. The petitioners argue these incidents collectively constitute persecution. The court must decide whether threats, emotional harm, and this attempted abduction—without proven physical injury—rise to the legal standard for persecution.

## Limited Government Response: Informal Contacts with Police and the Decision to Flee

Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez reported the incidents to a police patrol officer. The officer responded that nothing could be done and that police officers were "contributing to the problem." The officer advised the family to leave. That is what they did.

This factual detail is essential to the court's analysis of whether the Colombian government was "unable or unwilling" to protect the petitioners. Asylum applicants must show that their home government cannot or will not control their persecutors. The petitioners point to the officer's dismissive response as evidence of government complicity. The court, however, must determine whether one officer's statement reflects the government's official position or constitutes an isolated instance of police apathy. The court must also consider whether the petitioners gave the government a meaningful opportunity to respond by making formal reports through proper channels—particularly given Jimenez's own connections to law enforcement through his work with the prosecutor's office.
