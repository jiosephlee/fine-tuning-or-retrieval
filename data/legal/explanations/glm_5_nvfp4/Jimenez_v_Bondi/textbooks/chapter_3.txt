Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Past Persecution: Definition and Application

## Persecution Undefined in the INA: Judicial Interpretation of the Term

The Immigration and Nationality Act defines who qualifies as a "refugee" eligible for asylum, but it does not define what constitutes "persecution." This statutory silence requires courts to give meaning to the term through common law adjudication. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit applies its established interpretation: persecution requires "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive." This formulation, drawn from *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, establishes that persecution is a concept with real substance—it cannot be satisfied by mere mistreatment or unpleasant circumstances. The court's approach illustrates how federal courts fill gaps in immigration statutes, developing interpretive frameworks that applicants must satisfy.

The undefined nature of persecution means each case turns on its particular facts. As the court notes, "Whether a noncitizen has proved past private persecution in their home country—the overarching issue here—is a fact question." This factual determination receives deference on appeal under the substantial evidence standard, meaning the BIA's findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." The absence of a statutory definition thus creates a flexible but demanding standard that courts apply case by case.

## The Harm Threshold: More Than 'Restrictions or Threats to Life and Liberty'

The court articulates a critical threshold requirement: persecution "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This language establishes that not all harmful or frightening experiences qualify. The standard captures a qualitative difference between persecution and lesser forms of mistreatment. Restrictions on movement, employment difficulties, harassment, and even threats of serious harm do not automatically satisfy the persecution inquiry.

This threshold serves an important gatekeeping function in asylum law. If any adverse treatment could constitute persecution, the refugee definition would expand beyond its intended scope. The court's formulation requires applicants to show harm that rises above the routine difficulties, dangers, and unpleasantness that many people face in their home countries. The offensive nature of the harm—directed at someone because of a protected characteristic—must be accompanied by a sufficient level of severity.

## Cumulative Harm Doctrine: Considering Multiple Incidents Collectively

A key doctrinal tool in persecution analysis is the cumulative harm doctrine. As the court explains, "We do not look at each incident in isolation, but instead consider them collectively, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." This doctrine recognizes that while no single incident might independently rise to the level of persecution, the aggregate effect of repeated mistreatment might cross the threshold.

The cumulative approach prevents a fragmented analysis that would artificially minimize an applicant's experience. Someone who faces a series of threatening phone calls, an intimidating encounter, and a later assault might have experienced persecution even if each individual incident, viewed alone, would be insufficient. The doctrine is particularly important in cases involving a campaign of harassment or intimidation, where the persecutor's strategy involves creating a climate of fear through multiple acts rather than a single dramatic event.

However, *Jimenez* demonstrates that cumulative harm analysis does not guarantee a favorable finding. Even viewing the petitioners' experiences collectively—threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, being held at gunpoint, the assault on their employee, and the frightening incident with their son—the court concluded the harm "does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA." The cumulative doctrine makes the analysis more complete but does not lower the substantive standard.

## Role of Threats in Persecution Analysis: Threats Alone Are Insufficient

The court directly addresses a recurring issue in asylum cases: whether threats constitute persecution. Citing *Karki v. Holder*, the court states plainly that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." This rule holds even when threats are serious and frightening. The court acknowledges that it considers threats as part of the cumulative analysis, but they cannot independently satisfy the persecution requirement.

This principle reflects a practical distinction between words and actions. A threat expresses an intention to cause harm, but it does not itself inflict the suffering that persecution requires. The distinction matters because many asylum applicants have genuinely feared for their safety based on threats, yet the law requires more than fear to establish refugee status.

The petitioners in *Jimenez* experienced multiple forms of threats: weekly threatening phone calls about their political involvement, a brochure stating "death to snitches from Liberal Party," and direct verbal threats from the men who held Jimenez at gunpoint. While these threats contributed to the overall picture of mistreatment, the court treated them as part of the analysis rather than as persecution itself. The rule that threats alone are insufficient thus shaped the court's assessment of each incident's significance.

## Physical Harm vs. Psychological or Emotional Harm: What Qualifies as Persecution

*Jimenez* addresses whether non-physical harm can satisfy the persecution standard. The petitioners argued that they suffered "lasting emotional and psychological harm," including sleeplessness and panic attacks in their son. The court acknowledges that "physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong," citing *Karki*. This acknowledgment reflects that persecution can take forms beyond physical violence—a campaign of severe psychological abuse might constitute persecution even without physical contact.

However, the court maintains that the harm must be sufficiently severe. The psychological effects described by the petitioners—fear, anxiety, sleep disruption—while genuine and distressing, did not cross the required threshold. The court notes that "such fears stemming from their received threats are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution." This application shows that while psychological harm is theoretically sufficient, courts apply a demanding standard. The suffering must be offensive in a way that parallels the severity of physical persecution.

Importantly, the court observes that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." This statement suggests that while the door is theoretically open for such claims, successful ones are exceptional. Applicants relying on psychological harm face a steep climb to show that their experience meets the persecution standard.

## Comparison to Precedent: Cases Involving More Extreme Harm That Were Still Insufficient

The court strengthens its analysis by comparing the petitioners' circumstances to prior cases where asylum was denied. At oral argument, petitioners' counsel acknowledged that Tenth Circuit precedent has rejected applications "in cases involving more extreme fact patterns of inflicted harm." The court cites examples: *Chhetri v. Rosen* found that "four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat" did not constitute persecution. *Carias-Mejia v. Garland* held that threats with machetes and six robberies were insufficient because "the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand."

These comparisons serve two functions. First, they demonstrate the consistency of the court's approach—the petitioners' harm does not meet the standard because less has been insufficient in other cases. Second, they illustrate what does not qualify, helping future applicants and adjudicators understand the boundaries of persecution. The *Carias-Mejia* reference is particularly telling: if being robbed six times and threatened with machetes does not constitute persecution, the petitioners' experiences—while genuinely frightening—face an uphill battle.

The court's use of precedent exemplifies how common law reasoning operates in immigration adjudication. Each decision builds on prior ones, creating a body of law that defines persecution through accumulated examples rather than abstract formula.

## Application to Petitioners: Why the Threats and Emotional Harm, Even in Aggregate, Did Not Constitute Past Persecution

The court's application of these principles to the petitioners' claims demonstrates how persecution analysis works in practice. The petitioners presented multiple incidents: threatening phone calls beginning in May 2022, a threatening brochure, being held at gunpoint by two men who demanded they leave the neighborhood, the assault on their employee and explicit death threats from the same men, and the incident where a man grabbed their son. They also pointed to the psychological effects—fear, inability to sleep, panic attacks.

Applying the cumulative harm doctrine, the court considered these incidents collectively rather than dismissing them individually. Yet even in aggregate, the court found the harm did not rise to persecution. The threats, while frightening, remained threats. The physical confrontation where Jimenez was held at gunpoint did not result in physical injury. The assault on the employee was serious but happened to a third party, not the petitioners themselves. The incident with the son was deeply alarming but ended without physical harm.

The court concluded: "Given these facts and circumstances, we find substantial evidence to support the BIA's finding that Petitioners' harm, even in the aggregate, does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA." This conclusion reflects the demanding nature of the persecution standard and the deferential substantial evidence review.

## The Significance of Petitioners' Concession That They Suffered No Physical Harm

A telling aspect of *Jimenez* is the petitioners' concession. At the merits hearing before the immigration judge, they "conceded . . . they were not physically harmed." In their opening brief to the Tenth Circuit, they similarly noted "they experienced no lasting physical harm." These concessions significantly shaped the court's analysis.

While the court acknowledges that physical harm is not strictly necessary for persecution, the absence of any physical injury makes the petitioners' case harder. They asked the court to find persecution based on threats and psychological effects alone—a category where the court has rarely granted relief. The concession eliminated the possibility of arguing that their physical experiences—being held at gunpoint or grabbed—caused actual physical harm, forcing them to rely on the more difficult argument that psychological harm sufficed.

This aspect of the decision illustrates the strategic importance of factual concessions in immigration litigation. Concessions about what happened or did not happen can narrow the available legal arguments. The petitioners' acknowledgment of no physical harm effectively required them to clear a higher bar: showing that threats and emotional harm alone met the persecution standard, a showing the Tenth Circuit has rarely accepted.
