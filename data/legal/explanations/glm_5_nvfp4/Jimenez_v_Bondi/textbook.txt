Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Introduction and Factual Background

This chapter introduces the petitioners and the events in Colombia that gave rise to their asylum claim. Understanding the factual background is essential because asylum law requires applicants to connect their experiences of harm to specific legal standards. The court's analysis of whether petitioners qualify for asylum turns entirely on how the facts measure against these standards. As you read the factual account, consider how each incident might—or might not—satisfy the elements of an asylum claim: persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and government inability or unwillingness to protect.

## The Petitioners: Wilson Jimenez, Angelica Parada Duarte, and Their Minor Sons

The petitioners in this case are a family of four from Bogota, Colombia. Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, are the lead applicants, and their two minor sons are included in the proceedings. The family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota before relocating to the United States in December 2022.

In immigration proceedings, family members often file consolidated applications. Here, the minor children proceeded as "rider respondents" and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez's asylum application. This procedural arrangement means the children's claims depend on the success of the primary applicant's claim rather than standing independently. The court's analysis therefore focuses primarily on Jimenez's and Duarte's experiences, with the children's status tied to the outcome of the parents' case.

## Political Involvement: Membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and Campaigning for Federico Gutierrez

The petitioners had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012. During the 2022 Colombian presidential election, they actively supported Federico Gutierrez, a presidential candidate. Their political involvement went beyond passive membership: they engaged in door-to-door canvassing in local neighborhoods to solicit votes for their candidate.

This factual detail matters because asylum law protects individuals who face persecution "on account of political opinion." To qualify for protection, an applicant must show that their political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the persecution they suffered. The petitioners' active campaigning establishes that they held—and openly expressed—a political opinion. The question for the court becomes whether the harm they suffered was motivated by that political opinion or by something else entirely.

## Initial Threats Related to Political Activity: Phone Calls and the Murder of a Colleague

Beginning around late May 2022, Jimenez started receiving threatening phone calls regarding his involvement with the Liberal Party. These calls came once or twice a week. Other Liberal Party leaders and colleagues received similar threats. In early June 2022, the situation escalated dramatically: a colleague who had reported threats he received was kidnapped and murdered.

Despite these alarming developments, the petitioners continued their political campaigning. This detail is significant for the court's legal analysis. When applicants claim persecution based on political opinion, courts examine whether the persecutors' motives relate to the applicants' political activities. The petitioners argue that these early threats—connected to their party work—demonstrate they were targeted for their political beliefs.

## Jimenez's Professional Background: Law Student, Provisional License, and Work with the National Prosecutor's Office

In 2022, Jimenez held a provisional law license and was a law student. He also worked as an intern for Colombia's National Prosecutor's office, where he assisted victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. This professional background becomes relevant because it established Jimenez's connection to law enforcement and the criminal justice system.

The court's nexus analysis examines whether the persecution was motivated by political opinion or by Jimenez's professional role. Persecutors can have mixed motives, but the protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for the harm. Jimenez's work with the prosecutor's office provides an alternative explanation for why he might have been targeted—one that the court must consider alongside his political activities.

## The Property Dispute: Jimenez's Representation of a Client Against "Don Rafa"

In June 2022, a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez for legal help with a property dispute. Her tenant had not paid rent for over a year, and she wanted Jimenez to negotiate with him about restitution and vacating the property. When Jimenez visited the property to negotiate, the tenant refused and told him not to "look for trouble."

Jimenez later discovered that the tenant was actually the client's ex-husband—Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa." Don Rafa was a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas who were politically aligned with Colombia's current president, Gustavo Petro. This discovery transformed a routine property dispute into a confrontation with a powerful criminal figure.

The timing of events becomes crucial to the legal analysis. The petitioners had received threats related to their political activities before this property dispute. But the most serious incidents—the gunpoint confrontation, the assault on their employee, and the attempted abduction of their son—all occurred after Jimenez's involvement with Don Rafa. The court must determine which of these two factors—political opinion or the property dispute—was the central reason for the persecution.

## Discovery of Criminal Activity: The Sealed Property Used for Narco-Trafficking, Prostitution, and Reports of Kidnapping and Murder

During his visit to the property, Jimenez observed that the third floor had been sealed off by the health department. His investigation revealed that the property had been closed because it was being used as a day-rental for migrants and as a base for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The premises were also linked to reports of kidnapping and murder. Law enforcement had attempted several searches and seizures at the property but had been unsuccessful. A local police officer warned Jimenez to stay away and not seek problems.

This discovery raises the stakes of the property dispute considerably. Jimenez had stumbled onto evidence of serious criminal enterprise, and Don Rafa apparently believed Jimenez was responsible for the law enforcement attention. The court's nexus analysis turns on whether Don Rafa targeted Jimenez because of his political opinion or because Jimenez had threatened Don Rafa's criminal operations.

## Escalating Threats and Incidents: The Threatening Brochure, Gunpoint Confrontation, Assault on Employee, and Attempted Abduction of Jimenez's Son

After Jimenez's confrontation with Don Rafa, the threats escalated dramatically. Later in June 2022, Jimenez received a threatening brochure at his factory that stated "death to snitches from Liberal Party." Jimenez discarded it. But the following month, two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at his factory and told him to leave the neighborhood. One accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for the searches at Don Rafa's property.

About two months later, the same two men returned to the factory while the petitioners were away. They assaulted an employee and, at gunpoint, demanded to know where the petitioners were. The men again called them "snitches for the police" who were involved in politics. They threatened to kill the petitioners if seen again, stating the order came from Don Rafa.

Days later, Jimenez witnessed a man grab his younger son outside the boy's school. The man put his arm around the boy, covered his mouth as he tried to escape, and told him to "run to your dad." Jimenez intervened, and the family fled to his mother's home in the Bogota countryside before ultimately leaving for the United States.

These facts present the core question for the court's persecution analysis. The petitioners argue these incidents collectively constitute persecution. The court must decide whether threats, emotional harm, and this attempted abduction—without proven physical injury—rise to the legal standard for persecution.

## Limited Government Response: Informal Contacts with Police and the Decision to Flee

Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez reported the incidents to a police patrol officer. The officer responded that nothing could be done and that police officers were "contributing to the problem." The officer advised the family to leave. That is what they did.

This factual detail is essential to the court's analysis of whether the Colombian government was "unable or unwilling" to protect the petitioners. Asylum applicants must show that their home government cannot or will not control their persecutors. The petitioners point to the officer's dismissive response as evidence of government complicity. The court, however, must determine whether one officer's statement reflects the government's official position or constitutes an isolated instance of police apathy. The court must also consider whether the petitioners gave the government a meaningful opportunity to respond by making formal reports through proper channels—particularly given Jimenez's own connections to law enforcement through his work with the prosecutor's office.

# Procedural History and the Legal Framework for Asylum

## Procedural Posture: Notices to Appear, Proceedings Before the Immigration Judge, and Appeal to the BIA

The procedural journey in immigration cases follows a distinctive path that differs from typical federal litigation. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Petitioners—Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons—entered the United States in December 2022 without being admitted or paroled by an immigration officer. Three months after their arrival, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) served each family member with an individual Notice to Appear. The Notice to Appear is the charging document in removal proceedings; it specifies the legal grounds under which DHS seeks to remove a noncitizen from the United States. Here, DHS charged the Petitioners as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).

In August 2023, the Petitioners admitted the truth of DHS's factual allegations. The Immigration Judge (IJ) then charged them as inadmissible and directed their removal to Colombia. At this point, Petitioners applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). The minor children proceeded as "rider respondents" and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez's asylum application, meaning their claims depended on the success of the principal applicant's claim.

## The IJ's Decision: Credibility Finding but Denial on Four Grounds

The Immigration Judge conducted a merits hearing where Petitioners presented testimony and other evidence in support of their applications. The IJ found Petitioners to be credible—that is, the IJ accepted their testimony as truthful. Credibility findings are critical in immigration proceedings because they determine the evidentiary foundation upon which the adjudicator evaluates the legal claims. A positive credibility finding means the factfinder accepts the applicant's account of events as reliable.

Despite finding Petitioners credible, the IJ denied their applications on four independent grounds. First, the IJ concluded that the harm Petitioners suffered did not amount to "past persecution" under the statute. Second, the IJ found that Petitioners failed to establish a "nexus"—a causal connection—between the harm they suffered and any statutorily protected ground. Third, the IJ determined that internal relocation within Colombia was reasonably available to Petitioners. Fourth, the IJ concluded that Petitioners had not shown the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them from their persecutors. The IJ also separately denied protection under the CAT. Each of these four grounds was independently sufficient to deny asylum.

## The BIA's Affirmance and Dismissal of Appeal

Petitioners appealed the IJ's decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the highest administrative body in the immigration court system. The BIA functions as an appellate body that reviews IJ decisions for legal and factual errors. A single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ's decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected Petitioners' due process arguments that the IJ had violated their right to counsel.

The BIA's decision constituted a "final order of removal," which is the prerequisite for judicial review. Only after the BIA issues a final order may a petitioner seek review in the federal courts of appeals. Petitioners then filed a petition for review in the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit.

## Standard of Review: Substantial Evidence Standard and Review of the Administrative Record

Federal courts exercise jurisdiction over petitions for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. The Tenth Circuit's review of asylum claims is cabined by important limitations. First, the court's review is "limited to the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." The court does not take new evidence or conduct a trial de novo; it examines only what was before the immigration authorities.

Second, because a single BIA member affirmed the IJ's decision, the court reviews the BIA's decision as the final agency action. However, the court may "consult the IJ's more complete explanation of those same grounds" when the BIA has adopted or relied upon the IJ's reasoning. This approach recognizes that the BIA often affirms IJ decisions while leaving the IJ's detailed findings undisturbed.

The most important constraint on judicial review is the "substantial evidence" standard that governs factual findings. Under this highly deferential standard, the BIA's findings of fact are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This is an extraordinarily demanding standard for petitioners. The court does not ask whether it would have reached the same conclusion as the BIA; rather, the court asks whether any reasonable factfinder was *compelled* to reach a different conclusion. If the evidence supports two competing interpretations, the agency's interpretation prevails.

## Statutory Framework: Asylum Eligibility Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)

The court began its analysis by identifying the governing statutory provisions. Asylum is "a discretionary form of relief which may be granted by the Attorney General to eligible noncitizens." The key word is "discretionary"—even if an applicant establishes eligibility, the Attorney General retains discretion whether to grant asylum.

To be eligible for asylum, an applicant must prove "by a preponderance of the evidence" that he or she is a "refugee." The preponderance standard means that it is more likely than not that the applicant qualifies as a refugee. This burden of proof rests entirely on the applicant.

## Definition of 'Refugee' Under 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)

The INA defines "refugee" as "any person who is outside any country of such person's nationality . . . who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion."

This definition contains multiple requirements that the applicant must satisfy. The applicant must be outside his or her country of nationality. The applicant must be unable or unwilling to return to that country. This unwillingness must stem from persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. Critically, this persecution must be "on account of" one of five enumerated grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. These five grounds are the "statutorily protected grounds" that animate the entire asylum analysis.

## The Three Elements of an Asylum Claim: Persecution, Nexus to a Protected Ground, and Government Unable or Unwilling to Control Persecutors

The court distilled the refugee definition into three elements that an applicant must prove: "(1) an incident, or incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control."

The first element—persecution—requires the infliction of suffering or harm that goes beyond mere restrictions or threats. The court noted that persecution is undefined in the INA, but case law establishes that it "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." The court evaluates incidents collectively rather than in isolation, as "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution."

The second element—nexus—requires that the persecution be motivated "on account of" a protected ground. The applicant's protected characteristic must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution. It cannot be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." This requirement distinguishes legitimate asylum claims from situations where harm is motivated by purely personal disputes or criminal motives unrelated to protected characteristics.

The third element—government inability or unwillingness to control persecutors—applies when private actors, rather than government officials, perpetrate the persecution. The applicant must show that the government cannot or will not protect its citizens from the private persecutors. This element acknowledges that refugees may flee not only state-sponsored persecution but also persecution that the state fails to prevent.

## The Presumption Arising from Past Persecution and the Relationship Between Asylum and Withholding of Removal

An applicant can establish refugee status in two ways. First, the applicant can demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution. Second, the applicant can show that they suffered past persecution, which "gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution." This presumption shifts the burden to the government to demonstrate either that the applicant could safely relocate within their home country or that conditions have fundamentally changed such that the fear is no longer well-founded.

The court also addressed the relationship between asylum and withholding of removal. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief that carries greater benefits, including the possibility of permanent residence and eventual citizenship. Withholding of removal, by contrast, is mandatory if the applicant meets the applicable standard—but that standard is higher than asylum. An applicant for withholding must demonstrate a "clear probability" of persecution, meaning it is "more likely than not" they would face persecution. This is a higher burden than the "well-founded fear" standard for asylum, which the Supreme Court has described as requiring only a one-in-ten chance of persecution.

Because withholding of removal requires meeting a higher standard than asylum, "a failure to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards necessarily means the applicant cannot meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." The court applied this principle to conclude that Petitioners' failure on their asylum claim was dispositive of their withholding claim as well.

# Past Persecution: Definition and Application

## Persecution Undefined in the INA: Judicial Interpretation of the Term

The Immigration and Nationality Act defines who qualifies as a "refugee" eligible for asylum, but it does not define what constitutes "persecution." This statutory silence requires courts to give meaning to the term through common law adjudication. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit applies its established interpretation: persecution requires "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive." This formulation, drawn from *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, establishes that persecution is a concept with real substance—it cannot be satisfied by mere mistreatment or unpleasant circumstances. The court's approach illustrates how federal courts fill gaps in immigration statutes, developing interpretive frameworks that applicants must satisfy.

The undefined nature of persecution means each case turns on its particular facts. As the court notes, "Whether a noncitizen has proved past private persecution in their home country—the overarching issue here—is a fact question." This factual determination receives deference on appeal under the substantial evidence standard, meaning the BIA's findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." The absence of a statutory definition thus creates a flexible but demanding standard that courts apply case by case.

## The Harm Threshold: More Than 'Restrictions or Threats to Life and Liberty'

The court articulates a critical threshold requirement: persecution "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This language establishes that not all harmful or frightening experiences qualify. The standard captures a qualitative difference between persecution and lesser forms of mistreatment. Restrictions on movement, employment difficulties, harassment, and even threats of serious harm do not automatically satisfy the persecution inquiry.

This threshold serves an important gatekeeping function in asylum law. If any adverse treatment could constitute persecution, the refugee definition would expand beyond its intended scope. The court's formulation requires applicants to show harm that rises above the routine difficulties, dangers, and unpleasantness that many people face in their home countries. The offensive nature of the harm—directed at someone because of a protected characteristic—must be accompanied by a sufficient level of severity.

## Cumulative Harm Doctrine: Considering Multiple Incidents Collectively

A key doctrinal tool in persecution analysis is the cumulative harm doctrine. As the court explains, "We do not look at each incident in isolation, but instead consider them collectively, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." This doctrine recognizes that while no single incident might independently rise to the level of persecution, the aggregate effect of repeated mistreatment might cross the threshold.

The cumulative approach prevents a fragmented analysis that would artificially minimize an applicant's experience. Someone who faces a series of threatening phone calls, an intimidating encounter, and a later assault might have experienced persecution even if each individual incident, viewed alone, would be insufficient. The doctrine is particularly important in cases involving a campaign of harassment or intimidation, where the persecutor's strategy involves creating a climate of fear through multiple acts rather than a single dramatic event.

However, *Jimenez* demonstrates that cumulative harm analysis does not guarantee a favorable finding. Even viewing the petitioners' experiences collectively—threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, being held at gunpoint, the assault on their employee, and the frightening incident with their son—the court concluded the harm "does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA." The cumulative doctrine makes the analysis more complete but does not lower the substantive standard.

## Role of Threats in Persecution Analysis: Threats Alone Are Insufficient

The court directly addresses a recurring issue in asylum cases: whether threats constitute persecution. Citing *Karki v. Holder*, the court states plainly that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." This rule holds even when threats are serious and frightening. The court acknowledges that it considers threats as part of the cumulative analysis, but they cannot independently satisfy the persecution requirement.

This principle reflects a practical distinction between words and actions. A threat expresses an intention to cause harm, but it does not itself inflict the suffering that persecution requires. The distinction matters because many asylum applicants have genuinely feared for their safety based on threats, yet the law requires more than fear to establish refugee status.

The petitioners in *Jimenez* experienced multiple forms of threats: weekly threatening phone calls about their political involvement, a brochure stating "death to snitches from Liberal Party," and direct verbal threats from the men who held Jimenez at gunpoint. While these threats contributed to the overall picture of mistreatment, the court treated them as part of the analysis rather than as persecution itself. The rule that threats alone are insufficient thus shaped the court's assessment of each incident's significance.

## Physical Harm vs. Psychological or Emotional Harm: What Qualifies as Persecution

*Jimenez* addresses whether non-physical harm can satisfy the persecution standard. The petitioners argued that they suffered "lasting emotional and psychological harm," including sleeplessness and panic attacks in their son. The court acknowledges that "physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong," citing *Karki*. This acknowledgment reflects that persecution can take forms beyond physical violence—a campaign of severe psychological abuse might constitute persecution even without physical contact.

However, the court maintains that the harm must be sufficiently severe. The psychological effects described by the petitioners—fear, anxiety, sleep disruption—while genuine and distressing, did not cross the required threshold. The court notes that "such fears stemming from their received threats are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution." This application shows that while psychological harm is theoretically sufficient, courts apply a demanding standard. The suffering must be offensive in a way that parallels the severity of physical persecution.

Importantly, the court observes that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." This statement suggests that while the door is theoretically open for such claims, successful ones are exceptional. Applicants relying on psychological harm face a steep climb to show that their experience meets the persecution standard.

## Comparison to Precedent: Cases Involving More Extreme Harm That Were Still Insufficient

The court strengthens its analysis by comparing the petitioners' circumstances to prior cases where asylum was denied. At oral argument, petitioners' counsel acknowledged that Tenth Circuit precedent has rejected applications "in cases involving more extreme fact patterns of inflicted harm." The court cites examples: *Chhetri v. Rosen* found that "four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat" did not constitute persecution. *Carias-Mejia v. Garland* held that threats with machetes and six robberies were insufficient because "the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand."

These comparisons serve two functions. First, they demonstrate the consistency of the court's approach—the petitioners' harm does not meet the standard because less has been insufficient in other cases. Second, they illustrate what does not qualify, helping future applicants and adjudicators understand the boundaries of persecution. The *Carias-Mejia* reference is particularly telling: if being robbed six times and threatened with machetes does not constitute persecution, the petitioners' experiences—while genuinely frightening—face an uphill battle.

The court's use of precedent exemplifies how common law reasoning operates in immigration adjudication. Each decision builds on prior ones, creating a body of law that defines persecution through accumulated examples rather than abstract formula.

## Application to Petitioners: Why the Threats and Emotional Harm, Even in Aggregate, Did Not Constitute Past Persecution

The court's application of these principles to the petitioners' claims demonstrates how persecution analysis works in practice. The petitioners presented multiple incidents: threatening phone calls beginning in May 2022, a threatening brochure, being held at gunpoint by two men who demanded they leave the neighborhood, the assault on their employee and explicit death threats from the same men, and the incident where a man grabbed their son. They also pointed to the psychological effects—fear, inability to sleep, panic attacks.

Applying the cumulative harm doctrine, the court considered these incidents collectively rather than dismissing them individually. Yet even in aggregate, the court found the harm did not rise to persecution. The threats, while frightening, remained threats. The physical confrontation where Jimenez was held at gunpoint did not result in physical injury. The assault on the employee was serious but happened to a third party, not the petitioners themselves. The incident with the son was deeply alarming but ended without physical harm.

The court concluded: "Given these facts and circumstances, we find substantial evidence to support the BIA's finding that Petitioners' harm, even in the aggregate, does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA." This conclusion reflects the demanding nature of the persecution standard and the deferential substantial evidence review.

## The Significance of Petitioners' Concession That They Suffered No Physical Harm

A telling aspect of *Jimenez* is the petitioners' concession. At the merits hearing before the immigration judge, they "conceded . . . they were not physically harmed." In their opening brief to the Tenth Circuit, they similarly noted "they experienced no lasting physical harm." These concessions significantly shaped the court's analysis.

While the court acknowledges that physical harm is not strictly necessary for persecution, the absence of any physical injury makes the petitioners' case harder. They asked the court to find persecution based on threats and psychological effects alone—a category where the court has rarely granted relief. The concession eliminated the possibility of arguing that their physical experiences—being held at gunpoint or grabbed—caused actual physical harm, forcing them to rely on the more difficult argument that psychological harm sufficed.

This aspect of the decision illustrates the strategic importance of factual concessions in immigration litigation. Concessions about what happened or did not happen can narrow the available legal arguments. The petitioners' acknowledgment of no physical harm effectively required them to clear a higher bar: showing that threats and emotional harm alone met the persecution standard, a showing the Tenth Circuit has rarely accepted.

# Nexus to a Protected Ground

## The Nexus Requirement: Connecting Persecution to Race, Religion, Nationality, Membership in a Particular Social Group, or Political Opinion

Establishing that an applicant suffered harm is only the first step in proving eligibility for asylum. The Immigration and Nationality Act requires that the persecution be "on account of" a statutorily protected ground: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. This is the nexus requirement—the critical link between the harm suffered and the reason why the persecutor inflicted it. Without this connection, even severe mistreatment cannot form the basis for asylum relief. The statute does not protect individuals from all violence or threats; it protects those who are targeted because of who they are or what they believe.

The nexus requirement serves as a gatekeeping function, distinguishing between those who suffer harm for personal or random reasons and those whom Congress intended to protect through refugee law. An applicant who is robbed by a criminal, threatened by a business competitor, or targeted by a neighbor over a personal dispute has not suffered persecution "on account of" a protected ground, regardless of how frightening the experience may have been. The harm must be causally connected to one of the five protected characteristics listed in the statute.

## Political Opinion as a Protected Ground in This Case

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Petitioners argued that they were persecuted because of their political opinion. Specifically, they were members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and had actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a candidate in Colombia's 2022 presidential election who ran against the eventual winner, Gustavo Petro. The Petitioners went door-to-door seeking votes and received threatening phone calls regarding their involvement with the Liberal Party. Other Liberal Party leaders and colleagues also received threats, and one colleague was kidnapped and murdered.

The Petitioners contended that Don Rafa—the leader of a narco-trafficking group who was politically aligned with President Petro—targeted them because they had supported a rival political candidate. They argued that their political activism and party membership formed the basis for the persecution they suffered, including threatening brochures, armed confrontations, and threats against their children. Political opinion has long been recognized as a protected ground under refugee law, encompassing both actual opinions that the persecutor attributes to the victim and opinions the persecutor believes the victim to hold.

## The 'At Least One Central Reason' Standard: Political Opinion Cannot Be Incidental, Tangential, Superficial, or Subordinate

The Tenth Circuit applied the statutory standard that a protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution. This language, added to the INA in 2005, codified existing case law while raising the bar for nexus determinations. The protected characteristic cannot be merely incidental or tangential to the persecutor's motivation. As the court explained, quoting *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, the political opinion "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" or play "a minor role" in the mistreatment.

This standard requires more than showing that the persecutor was aware of the victim's political affiliation. The applicant must demonstrate that the political opinion was a genuine driver of the persecution—a central motivation rather than a background fact. The standard reflects Congress's intent to provide asylum to those genuinely targeted for their beliefs, not to those whose political affiliations happen to coexist with other reasons for persecution. The "at least one central reason" formulation makes clear that the protected ground must occupy a significant place in the persecutor's motivation, even if other factors also contribute.

## Mixed-Motive Persecution: When Persecutors Have Multiple Motivations

The court acknowledged that persecutors can have multiple motives for targeting someone. In mixed-motive cases, the persecutor may be driven by a combination of protected and unprotected reasons. The legal question becomes whether at least one protected ground is a "central reason" among the various motivations. The court need not find that the protected ground was the sole or even primary motivation—only that it was central to the persecutor's decision to inflict harm.

This recognition of mixed motives reflects the messy reality of human conflict. A persecutor might target someone both because of a personal grudge and because of their political beliefs, or both because of a business dispute and their religion. The statute does not require purity of motive on the persecutor's part. But it does require that the protected characteristic be more than pretextual or peripheral. In such cases, courts must carefully examine the evidence to determine what truly drove the persecutor's actions.

## The Timeline of Threats: Distinction Between Initial Political Threats and Later Escalation

A crucial aspect of the court's analysis was the timeline of the threats against the Petitioners. The court noted that before Jimenez's encounter with Don Rafa, the family had received threatening phone calls once or twice a week regarding their Liberal Party involvement. A colleague had been kidnapped and murdered despite reporting threats he received. Yet the Petitioners were "undeterred and continued to campaign."

Critically, the court observed that the Petitioners did not point to any evidence that Don Rafa was behind those initial politically affiliated threats. The escalation began only after Jimenez became involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa's ex-husband tenant. The threatening brochure, the armed confrontation at the furniture factory, the assault on the employee, and the incident with the Petitioners' son all occurred after Don Rafa became aware that Jimenez was investigating his property. This temporal pattern undermined the claim that political opinion was a central reason for the persecution.

## Evidence of Motive: The Accusers' Statements About Jimenez Being a 'Snitch' and Interfering with Don Rafa's Property

The statements made by the persecutors provided telling evidence of their true motivation. During the confrontation at the furniture factory, one of the armed men accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and held him responsible for the searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property. When the same men returned and assaulted an employee, they again called the Petitioners "snitches for the police" who were "in politics," and stated that the order came from Don Rafa.

These statements revealed that the persecutors viewed Jimenez as a threat to Don Rafa's criminal operations, not primarily as a political opponent. The term "snitch" refers to someone who reports criminal activity to authorities—exactly what Jimenez had done by investigating the property used for narco-trafficking. The men's references to "searches and seizures" confirmed that their grievance stemmed from law enforcement actions against Don Rafa's enterprise. Even when the persecutors mentioned politics, they did so in conjunction with accusations of snitching, suggesting politics was incidental to their primary complaint.

## The BIA's Finding: Jimenez Threatened Don Rafa's Revenue and Security, Not His Political Interests

The BIA and IJ found that Petitioners were "primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa's revenue and security, not because of their political opinion." This factual finding was critical to the court's analysis. Jimenez, working as a provisional lawyer, had visited a property where Don Rafa's associate was conducting illegal activities including narco-trafficking and prostitution. Jimenez's inquiries threatened to expose these operations and potentially cut into Don Rafa's profits.

The court noted that the government had already attempted several searches and seizures at the property. Jimenez's involvement—as someone who worked with the prosecutor's office and was now asking questions—represented an additional threat to Don Rafa's criminal enterprise. The BIA's finding that this was the "predominant motive" behind the threats received substantial evidence deference from the Tenth Circuit. Under the substantial evidence standard of review, the court could not reweigh the evidence but could only determine whether any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to reach a contrary conclusion.

## Why Political Opinion Was 'Tangential and Subordinate' to the Primary Motive for Harm

The court concluded that Petitioners' political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm." Several factors supported this determination. First, the Petitioners had continued their political activities despite receiving initial threats, suggesting those threats were not serious enough to drive them from the country. The escalation occurred only after Jimenez's professional involvement with Don Rafa's property. Second, the persecutors' statements focused on Jimenez's role as a "snitch" interfering with criminal operations, with political affiliation mentioned only secondarily.

Third, Don Rafa's status as someone "politically aligned with" President Petro did not transform a dispute over criminal activities into political persecution. Alignment with a political party does not mean that all actions taken by that person are politically motivated. Don Rafa was motivated by threats to his revenue stream and his security—the property was used for day-rentals to migrants and as a base for narco-trafficking. The Petitioners' involvement in opposition politics may have made them more visible targets, but it was not a central reason for their persecution. The nexus between their harm and their political opinion was too attenuated to satisfy the statutory requirement.

# Government Protection and Internal Relocation

This chapter examines the final two elements of the asylum analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi*: whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners, and whether they could safely relocate within Colombia. These requirements serve as critical safeguards in asylum law, ensuring that protection is reserved for those who truly have no recourse in their home countries.

## The 'Unable or Unwilling to Control' Standard: Government Response to Persecution

An asylum applicant must prove that persecution was committed "by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control." This requirement recognizes that asylum is fundamentally about the home country's failure to protect its own citizens. As the Tenth Circuit explained, the analysis "considers evidence about a government's response to an applicant's past persecution."

Two categories of information carry significant weight in this inquiry. First, courts examine whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors. Second, courts consider whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing them in protective custody. The central question is whether the government has demonstrated a genuine inability or unwillingness to address the threat facing the applicant.

This standard reflects a core principle of asylum law: international protection serves as a last resort when national protection has failed. An applicant cannot simply bypass their home government's protective mechanisms without first giving that government a meaningful opportunity to help.

## The Notice Requirement: Why Reporting to Authorities Matters

Intertwined with the government protection analysis is an inquiry into whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond. As the court emphasized, "[w]ithout such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped."

Accordingly, "a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government." A failure to report can significantly undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. Without such reporting, the administrative record remains silent on how authorities would have responded, leaving courts to speculate rather than evaluate actual government conduct.

However, the failure to report is not necessarily fatal to an asylum claim. An applicant may satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. This futility exception acknowledges that in some circumstances, expecting an applicant to seek government assistance would be unreasonable or even life-threatening.

## Evidence of Government Response: Attempted Searches and Seizures at Don Rafa's Property

In *Jimenez*, the record contained evidence that directly contradicted the petitioners' claims about government inaction. The government was aware of Don Rafa's property and "had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there." This evidence demonstrated that Colombian authorities were not merely aware of the criminal activity but had taken active, if ultimately unsuccessful, steps to address it.

This fact proved significant because it undercut the petitioners' argument that their persecutors were "closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government." Had Don Rafa enjoyed the kind of government protection the petitioners alleged, law enforcement would not have been conducting raids on his property. The court found that "the evidence counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government and would be unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors."

The case illustrates how evidence of government action—even unsuccessful action—can undermine a claim of government inability or unwillingness. The standard does not require perfect protection, only that the government is genuinely attempting to exercise its protective function.

## Individual vs. Systemic Government Failure: Why Conversations with Individual Officers Are Insufficient

The petitioners in *Jimenez* testified that they spoke to two individual police officers who told them they should not seek trouble and that nothing could be done. The court found this evidence insufficient to establish government inability or unwillingness.

Quoting the Seventh Circuit's decision in *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the Tenth Circuit explained that courts "often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is 'unwilling or unable' to protect its people." While police apathy can indicate a broader problem, "a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim."

This distinction between individual and systemic failure serves an important function. Individual officers may be corrupt, incompetent, or simply overworked, but their conduct does not necessarily reflect official government policy or capacity. Asylum applicants must demonstrate something more systemic—a pattern of government non-response or active complicity—rather than isolated incidents of unhelpful police conduct.

The court also noted that the petitioners "did not seek help outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts." Jimenez himself worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office, and his father still worked for the criminal justice system. These connections provided additional avenues for seeking protection that the petitioners never pursued.

## The Futility Exception: When Reporting Would Be Dangerous or Pointless—And Why It Didn't Apply Here

The petitioners argued that contacting the government for help would have been futile and dangerous because their persecutors were closely aligned with the Colombian government. This argument invoked the futility exception to the notice requirement.

The court rejected this argument as unsupported by the record. The key problem was that the evidence actually suggested the opposite: the government had attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property, demonstrating that authorities were not aligned with him. As the court explained, citing *Chhetri v. Rosen*, the petitioners could not show that the Colombian government was, or would be, unable or unwilling to protect them had it known about the threats.

The futility exception remains a viable path for applicants who genuinely cannot safely approach their government. However, *Jimenez* illustrates that applicants must provide concrete evidence supporting a futility claim. Speculative fears or conclusory assertions about government corruption are insufficient. The record must demonstrate that reporting would genuinely have been pointless or dangerous under the specific circumstances.

## Safe Internal Relocation: The Government's Rebuttal Option Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)

Even if an applicant establishes eligibility for asylum, the government may rebut that eligibility through an alternative argument: that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their home country. Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), the government may prevent an asylum application by showing by a preponderance of the evidence either: (1) there has been a "fundamental change" in circumstances such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution, or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating within the country.

This internal relocation doctrine recognizes that asylum should be a last resort. If an applicant can live safely elsewhere in their home country, international protection may be unnecessary. The doctrine asks whether the threat facing the applicant is truly nationwide or whether it is localized to a particular region or city.

The analysis involves both feasibility and reasonableness. Courts consider whether the applicant could physically relocate to another area and whether such relocation would be reasonable under the circumstances, taking into account factors such as family ties, employment opportunities, and living conditions.

## Evidence Supporting Relocation: Family Remaining Unharmed in Colombia, Temporary Safe Stay with Jimenez's Mother

In *Jimenez*, substantial evidence supported the finding that the petitioners could reasonably relocate within Colombia. The court identified several key facts from the record.

First, the petitioners' family and relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed. Jimenez's father still worked for the criminal justice system. The ongoing presence and safety of family members in the country undermined any claim that the petitioners faced a nationwide threat.

Second, and particularly significant, the petitioners had "safely lived with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to relocate to the United States." This temporary stay demonstrated that safe havens existed within Colombia. The petitioners had already successfully availed themselves of one such location before choosing to leave the country entirely.

The petitioners argued that relocation was unworkable due to administrative and economic burdens, Colombia's judicial infrastructure, and their persecutors' ties to the president. They also suggested that their relatives might face danger due to their own political opinions. The court found these arguments insufficient, noting that the court "cannot grant asylum based on such hypothetical hardships."

## The Significance of the Threat to 'Leave the Neighborhood' Rather Than Leave the Country

A telling detail in the record shaped the court's relocation analysis: when the men threatened the petitioners, "they told them to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country."

This distinction proved meaningful. The threats were geographically specific—targeting the petitioners' presence in a particular area of Bogota where Don Rafa operated his criminal enterprise. The threateners did not demand that the family flee Colombia entirely, only that they leave the neighborhood where the property dispute had originated.

The court found this fact significant in supporting the conclusion that "Petitioners can mitigate any potential for future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia." The nature of the threats themselves suggested that the danger was localized rather than nationwide, making internal relocation a viable option.

This aspect of *Jimenez* illustrates how the specific content and context of threats can inform the relocation analysis. Not all threats are created equal; their geographic scope and specific demands matter in assessing whether an applicant truly needs international protection or could find safety elsewhere within their home country.
