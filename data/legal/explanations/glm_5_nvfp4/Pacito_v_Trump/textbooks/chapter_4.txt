Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Standards for Stay Pending Appeal and the Court's Disposition

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stay Pending Appeal

When a district court issues an order granting preliminary injunctive relief, the losing party may seek a "stay pending appeal"—an order from the appellate court that temporarily suspends the district court's injunction while the appeal proceeds. The Supreme Court established the governing standard in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), articulating four factors that courts must weigh: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likely success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other interested parties; and (4) where the public interest lies.

These factors are not weighted equally. The first two—likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 434. This hierarchical structure makes sense: if an applicant cannot show a reasonable chance of prevailing on appeal, the balance of hardships becomes largely irrelevant. Conversely, without a showing of irreparable harm, the applicant could simply wait for the appeal to run its course. The standard is demanding because a stay pending appeal represents an extraordinary intervention by an appellate court into the ongoing proceedings of a coordinate branch.

In *Pacito*, the government sought a stay of the district court's preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of Executive Order 14163 and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugee services. The Ninth Circuit applied the *Nken* framework to determine whether to grant relief pending resolution of the merits appeal.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits as the 'Most Critical' Factor

The court's analysis of the first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—revealed a divided outcome. On the question of the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions under Executive Order 14163, the court found the government likely to prevail. The panel relied heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which upheld a presidential proclamation restricting entry from several countries under the same statutory provision at issue here: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f).

Section 1182(f) provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." Quoting *Hawaii*, the court emphasized that the statute "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." The panel concluded that "the sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f) is a presidential finding of detriment, and President Trump had satisfied that requirement.

Critically, the court rejected any "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This deferential standard reflects separation-of-powers concerns: when Congress delegates broad authority to the executive branch in matters of immigration and national security, courts generally do not second-guess the President's policy judgments. The court thus determined the government was likely to succeed in defending the validity of Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions.

However, the court reached the opposite conclusion regarding the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges to the termination of funding for refugee resettlement services. The court indicated that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." This split determination on the first *Nken* factor set the stage for the court's bifurcated disposition.

## Irreparable Injury Analysis for Both the Government and the Plaintiffs

The irreparable injury inquiry examines whether the party seeking a stay faces harm that cannot be adequately remedied by monetary damages or other compensatory relief after the appeal is resolved. In *Nken*, the Supreme Court stressed that this factor and likelihood of success are "the most critical" because without irreparable injury, there is no urgency warranting appellate intervention.

For the government, the court implicitly accepted that the executive branch would suffer institutional injury from having to implement a refugee admissions program that the President had determined to be detrimental to national interests. This type of injury—being compelled to act contrary to lawful policy judgments—qualifies as irreparable because it cannot be undone after the fact. Once refugees are admitted, they cannot be "un-admitted," and the executive branch's authority over entry decisions would be undermined.

For the plaintiffs, the organizational appellants and individual refugees, the irreparable injury calculus was different. The organizations faced termination of cooperative agreements that provided essential funding for their operations. Refugees already admitted to the United States risked losing access to statutorily mandated reception and placement services—services designed to help them achieve economic self-sufficiency and navigate their first months in a new country. The court recognized that denial of these services could cause hardship that compensation after the fact could not adequately remedy.

Judge Lee's partial dissent took issue with the majority's analysis of the statutory obligations. He argued that Section 1522 is permissive, not mandatory, and therefore the government faced no legal duty that could justify injunctive relief. This disagreement about the nature of the statutory obligation informed the divergent views on whether irreparable injury existed on the services question.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest Considerations in Immigration and Foreign Affairs

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—substantial injury to other parties and the public interest—converge in cases involving immigration and foreign affairs. Courts recognize that executive decisions in these domains implicate "both domestic and foreign interests of the United States" that are entrusted to the President's "broad discretion." *Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 683–84.

The court in *Pacito* found that these factors favored the government with respect to the admissions suspension. The President's determination that refugee admissions would be detrimental to national interests carried substantial weight. Courts are generally reluctant to override executive judgments about national security and foreign policy, particularly when Congress has delegated broad authority to the executive branch. The panel accordingly concluded that the balance of equities and public interest supported staying the injunction as to admissions.

The calculus shifted, however, regarding resettlement services for refugees already admitted. Congress had enacted specific statutory requirements in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 directing the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide certain services "to the extent of available appropriations." These provisions reflected a congressional judgment that the United States owes certain obligations to individuals it has already admitted as refugees. The public interest in fulfilling statutory commitments to vulnerable populations who have already been granted entry provided a counterweight to executive discretion.

This distinction—between those seeking admission and those already admitted—proved crucial to the court's ultimate disposition. The balance of equities analysis recognized that different considerations apply to different categories of affected individuals.

## The Court's Bifurcated Disposition: Staying the Injunction on Admissions While Reinstating Cooperative Agreements for Resettlement Services

Having applied the *Nken* factors, the court issued a carefully calibrated order. The district court's preliminary injunctions were stayed "in their entirety, except as noted below." The effect was to allow the government to implement Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions while simultaneously requiring compliance with statutory obligations to refugees already in the United States.

This bifurcated approach reflected the court's split determination on likelihood of success on the merits. Because the government was likely to prevail on the validity of the admissions suspension under § 1182(f), the court stayed the injunction blocking that aspect of Executive Order 14163. The President's statutory authority to suspend entry of a class of aliens was clear and entitled to deference.

Conversely, because the government was not likely to prevail on the APA challenges to defunding resettlement services, the court declined to stay that portion of the district court's orders. The statutory framework in § 1522 imposed obligations that the executive branch could not unilaterally set aside through funding termination. The court thus maintained injunctive relief as to the services component, ensuring that statutory mandates would continue to be fulfilled while the appeal proceeded.

Judge Lee dissented from this aspect of the order. He would have stayed the district court's injunctions in their entirety, reading § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandate. This interpretive disagreement—whether statutory language creates a duty or merely permits action—represents a fundamental question of statutory construction that would presumably be addressed in the forthcoming merits opinion.

## The Order to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements for Refugees Already Admitted to the United States

The court's order included specific directives regarding implementation. The government was "directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This requirement extended to "the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

The court grounded this directive in the statutory text of § 1522. Several subsections imposed specific obligations: § 1522(a)(1)(A) required the Director to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance designed to promote economic self-sufficiency. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) required the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children during interim periods. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) required the Secretary of Health and Human Services to develop alternative projects providing interim support, medical services, and case management for recently resettled refugees.

The court noted that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for fiscal year 2025, though this amount was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The existence of appropriated funds triggered the "to the extent of available appropriations" language in § 1522(a), suggesting that spending authority existed.

Significantly, the court highlighted the government's own uncertainty about its ability to provide mandated services. At oral argument, the government "advised the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services"—despite having represented to the district court that it was actively preparing a request for proposals. This gap between representation and reality strengthened the court's conclusion that reinstatement of existing cooperative agreements was necessary to fulfill statutory obligations.

## The Limited Carveout and Its Practical Implications for Resettlement Agencies

The procedural history revealed an earlier attempt to craft a limited exception to the stay. The March 25, 2025 order had created a "limited carveout from the stay" for individuals who were "conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." The motions panel issued subsequent orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025 clarifying this carveout.

This earlier approach attempted to distinguish between refugees at different stages of the admissions process—those who had received conditional approval before the executive order versus those whose applications remained pending. The practical effect was to allow entry for certain refugees whose cases had advanced substantially through the vetting process.

However, the panel's current order vacated the March 25 stay "as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025" and replaced it with a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal. This procedural reset meant that the earlier carveout framework was superseded. The new order instead drew the line between admissions (which could be suspended) and services for those already admitted (which must continue).

For the organizational plaintiffs—the three resettlement agencies that had cooperative agreements with the State Department—the order had immediate practical implications. The agencies were entitled to reinstatement of their agreements for purposes of serving refugees already present in the United States. This restored their ability to provide the reception and placement services that constitute their core mission, at least for that population.

The practical effect of the order was to maintain a functional refugee resettlement infrastructure for individuals already admitted while allowing the executive branch to implement its policy judgment regarding new admissions. The court thus navigated between competing considerations—preserving statutory obligations to refugees who had already entered the country while deferring to the President's authority over entry decisions.
