Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 32: # The Stay Pending Appeal Analysis and Final Order

## The Nken Four-Factor Test for Stay Pending Appeal

When a party seeks to pause a district court's order while an appeal proceeds, the party requests a "stay pending appeal." The governing standard comes from *Nken v. Holder*, in which the Supreme Court articulated a four-factor test that courts must apply. The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito* began its analysis by reciting these four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies.

A stay pending appeal is distinct from an injunction. While an injunction grants or denies substantive relief, a stay merely suspends a lower court's order during appellate review. The stay preserves the status quo—or in some cases, restores a prior status quo—so that the appellate court can deliberate without the underlying controversy becoming moot or causing irreversible harm. The *Nken* Court emphasized that the first two factors—the likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." This hierarchy makes sense: if an applicant cannot show both a real chance of winning and a real risk of serious harm, a court should not intervene before deciding the appeal.

In this case, the government sought a stay of the district court's preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of Executive Order 14163 and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugee services. The Ninth Circuit's task was to determine whether the government met the *Nken* standard. The court's analysis would reveal that the factors do not always point uniformly in one direction—different aspects of a single case may yield different conclusions under the same test.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Different Conclusions for Different Claims

The first *Nken* factor required the court to assess the government's likelihood of success on appeal. The panel's analysis revealed a crucial insight: when a single case involves multiple legal claims, the likelihood-of-success inquiry must be conducted claim by claim. The court reached opposite conclusions on two different aspects of the plaintiffs' challenge.

On the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, the court found the government likely to succeed. The court's reasoning centered on statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which authorizes the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens when he "finds that their entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court emphasized that this statutory language "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power" over entry restrictions. The "sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f) is that the President make the required finding of detriment—which President Trump had done. The court concluded it could not "engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This portion of the opinion illustrates the concept of judicial deference to executive branch determinations in certain statutory contexts. When Congress uses language granting broad discretion to the President, courts will not second-guess the policy merits of the executive's decision. The panel treated the President's finding of detriment as conclusive for purposes of the stay analysis.

On the Administrative Procedure Act challenge to the defunding of refugee resettlement services, however, the court reached the opposite conclusion. The government was not likely to succeed on this claim. The court identified statutory mandates in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that require the government to provide certain "reception and placement" services to refugees after their admission. The court quoted several subsections detailing specific obligations: employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and special provisions for unaccompanied minors. These statutory provisions, the court suggested, create duties that the executive branch cannot simply ignore through executive order or funding suspension.

The court's bifurcated analysis demonstrates that the first *Nken* factor is not a binary assessment of who will win the entire appeal. Rather, a court must examine each distinct legal claim. This granular approach allows a court to grant a stay in part while denying it in part—a possibility that *Nken* itself contemplates but that the *Pacito* opinion illustrates concretely.

## Irreparable Injury, Balance of Equities, and Public Interest

After finding the government likely to succeed on the admissions question but unlikely to succeed on the services question, the court addressed the remaining *Nken* factors. The opinion's treatment of these factors is notably compressed. The court stated that the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President, favor the United States as well."

This terse analysis reflects several principles. First, when the first two factors strongly favor one party, the balance of equities and public interest often align in the same direction. Second, in cases involving immigration and national security, courts traditionally give substantial weight to the executive branch's assessment of the public interest. The court quoted *Trump v. Hawaii*'s reference to "broad discretion" conferred on the President in this domain.

The opinion does not separately analyze harm to the plaintiffs—the refugees and resettlement organizations who would be affected by the executive order. This omission likely reflects the court's conclusion that the government was unlikely to succeed on the services claim. Because the court ultimately ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees, the balance-of-equities analysis for that portion of the case necessarily favored the plaintiffs. The court's reasoning implies that the government's interest in controlling admissions outweighed plaintiffs' interests, but plaintiffs' interest in receiving statutorily mandated services outweighed the government's interest in terminating those services.

The panel's approach illustrates how the *Nken* factors interact. The four-factor test is not a simple checklist. The factors may pull in different directions, and the weight given to each depends heavily on the nature of the underlying claims.

## The Order to Reinstatute Cooperative Agreements for Admitted Refugees

Having concluded that the government was unlikely to succeed on the APA claim regarding resettlement services, the court issued a specific remedial order. The government was "directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

This order raises an important question about the scope of interim relief on appeal. A stay pending appeal typically maintains the status quo; it does not grant affirmative relief. Yet the court's order reinstated terminated cooperative agreements—an affirmative remedy. The justification for this order lies in the court's assessment that § 1522 creates mandatory obligations. If the government has a statutory duty to provide services, and if those services have historically been provided through cooperative agreements, then reinstating those agreements is necessary to preserve the status quo of statutory compliance.

The court grounded its order in factual findings about the government's uncertainty. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency," but at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government admitted it was "unaware whether any progress had been made." This uncertainty about the executive branch's ability to fulfill its statutory obligations supported the court's decision to restore the pre-existing cooperative agreements rather than simply stay the district court's order.

The court's reference to appropriations is also noteworthy. The opinion recites that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, reduced by $800 million through rescission. This funding context supported the conclusion that the statutory obligations under § 1522 were not merely aspirational—Congress had provided resources for their implementation.

## The Distinction Between Staying the Injunction on Admissions Versus Services

The panel's final order reflects a calibrated approach that distinguishes between the executive order's admissions suspension and its services termination. The district court's preliminary injunctions were "stayed in their entirety, except as noted below"—with the "except" referring to the order to reinstate cooperative agreements for admitted refugees.

This bifurcated disposition makes conceptual sense given the court's legal analysis. The admissions suspension, the court held, was likely within the President's statutory authority under § 1182(f). The district court had enjoined that suspension, so staying the injunction allowed the suspension to take effect. The services termination, by contrast, was likely ultra vires—beyond the executive branch's authority—because § 1522 mandates certain assistance. The district court had ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements to fulfill those mandates, and the Ninth Circuit required the government to continue providing services.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent illuminates the dividing line. Judge Lee agreed with the majority that the President had authority to impose the admissions moratorium; he would have stayed the injunction in full. But Judge Lee disagreed with the majority's interpretation of § 1522. In his view, the statute's title—"Authorization for programs"—indicated that the provisions were permissive rather than mandatory. The word "authorization," he argued, signals congressional permission for the executive to act, not a congressional command that it must act. The statutory text repeatedly states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance, which Judge Lee read as confirming discretion.

The majority's contrary reading treated § 1522(a) as imposing conditions on how the government must act if it provides assistance, while the specific authorization language in subsections (b) through (e) still leaves room for executive discretion. Judge Lee's dissent highlights an interpretive debate that the forthcoming merits opinion will presumably address: when does "shall" impose an obligation, and when does "authorized" language negate that obligation?

The distinction drawn in the panel order has practical significance. The admissions suspension applies broadly—refugees outside the United States may not enter under USRAP. But refugees already admitted remain entitled to reception and placement services. The court's order thus creates two categories of affected persons: those seeking entry (subject to the suspension) and those already present (entitled to services). This distinction reflects the court's understanding of the statutory architecture: Congress gave the President broad power over entry, but Congress also imposed specific obligations regarding the treatment of refugees after admission.
