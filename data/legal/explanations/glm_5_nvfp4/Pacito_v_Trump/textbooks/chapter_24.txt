Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 24: # Background and Context

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." This executive order invoked two statutory provisions—8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)—as the legal foundation for its directives. Section 1182(f) is particularly significant: it provides that whenever the President finds that the entry of any class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

Relying on this authority, Executive Order 14163 made a presidential determination "that entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The order then directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings. The scope of this suspension is comprehensive: it applies to refugees seeking admission under the entire USRAP framework. The government's legal position, which the Ninth Circuit references, is that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to presidential judgment and vests the President with "ample power to impose entry restrictions" beyond those specifically enumerated elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of Foreign Aid Funding

A separate executive order, issued on the same day as Executive Order 14163, also affected the refugee program. Executive Order No. 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," directed the Department of State to suspend foreign aid funding pending review. On January 24, 2025, four days after the order's issuance, the State Department acted pursuant to this directive to suspend all funding for the USRAP program.

Critically, this funding suspension extended beyond overseas operations. It encompassed funding for "domestic resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." This aspect of the funding freeze would become central to the litigation, as plaintiffs argued that defunding services for refugees already in the United States violated statutory mandates. The dual executive orders thus operated on two distinct tracks: EO 14163 addressed the admission of new refugees, while EO 14169's implementation cut off funding for services supporting refugees who had already been admitted.

## The Plaintiffs: Admitted Refugees, Conditionally Approved Refugees, and Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs in this case fall into three categories, each representing different interests affected by the executive orders. The first group consists of "refugees who were previously admitted to the United States." These individuals had already lawfully entered the country under the refugee program but faced the termination of resettlement services that Congress had mandated. Their interest is in continuing services to support their integration and self-sufficiency.

The second group comprises refugees who "have been approved for resettlement to the United States but remain outside the country." These individuals had completed the approval process and were awaiting admission when the executive order suspended the program. Their legal interest centers on whether the government could halt their entry after they had already been conditionally approved—a question the prior motions panel addressed by carving out this group from the initial stay.

The third group of plaintiffs consists of "three organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." These organizational plaintiffs had direct contractual relationships with the government; the State Department terminated those cooperative agreements on February 26, 2025. Their standing derives from the direct economic and operational harm caused by the funding termination, in addition to their interest in carrying out their organizational missions.

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions and Their Scope

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, alleging violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The Refugee Act, passed by Congress in 1980, established "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The plaintiffs argued that Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated this statutory framework and that the defunding of USRAP violated various APA provisions.

The district court responded with two preliminary injunctions. On February 25, the court orally issued its first preliminary injunction, prohibiting enforcement of Executive Order 14163's provisions suspending refugee admissions. The following day, February 26, the government terminated every cooperative agreement providing reception and placement services domestically and nearly all agreements for overseas processing. The district court then issued a written order on February 28 to "define the scope" of its preliminary injunction, explicitly specifying that the injunction also "prohibits the suspension of USRAP funding." This order (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) thus extended judicial protection to the funding streams that enabled resettlement services.

On March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), this time ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. This second injunction was practical in nature: it sought to restore the operational capacity of the resettlement organizations that the government had defunded. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, bringing both injunctions before the Ninth Circuit.

## Prior Ninth Circuit Orders and the Procedural Posture Leading to This Decision

The procedural history of this appeal involves multiple orders from the Ninth Circuit, reflecting the expedited and evolving nature of the dispute. The government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part on March 25. In that order, the panel quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to presidential authority in immigration matters. However, the panel denied the stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—refugees who had already received conditional approval before the executive order would continue to receive protection from the injunction.

The motions panel issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying the scope of this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk to place the appeal on the next available calendar for oral argument. Before the scheduled argument on September 19, however, the district court issued an order on July 14 establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. This prompted the government to file an "emergency motion to enforce, clarify or amend the stay order" on July 17.

The Ninth Circuit responded on July 18 by issuing an "administrative stay" of the district court's July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is a temporary measure that preserves the status quo while the court considers the merits of a motion. The court then scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis for September 3, 2025. The September 12, 2025 order—the subject of this chapter—represents the court's ruling following that argument. The court vacated both its administrative stay of July 18 and its prior stay of March 25 (as amended), replacing them with a new stay pending appeal. The court also partially granted and partially denied the government's requests, staying the district court's injunctions in their entirety except as specified in the order itself—most significantly, requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States.
