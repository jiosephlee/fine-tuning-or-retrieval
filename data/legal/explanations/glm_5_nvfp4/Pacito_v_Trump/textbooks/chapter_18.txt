Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 18: # Introduction and Case Overview

## The Significance of Pacito v. Trump in the Landscape of Executive Immigration Authority

*Pacito v. Trump* arrives at the intersection of some of the most contested questions in modern constitutional law: the scope of presidential power over immigration, the limits of judicial review over national security determinations, and the extent to which Congress can constrain executive action through statutory mandates. The case arises from Executive Order No. 14163, issued on January 20, 2025, in which President Trump invoked his authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of all refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). The President determined that such entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—the statutory trigger for suspension authority under § 1182(f).

This opinion is significant because it represents an early appellate application of the Supreme Court's landmark decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which upheld the travel ban executive order under the same statutory provision. The Ninth Circuit explicitly quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." In doing so, the panel signals that courts must not conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" when the President acts under this statute. This deferential standard effectively insulates the executive's determination from meaningful judicial scrutiny, provided the President makes the requisite finding of detriment.

At the same time, *Pacito* introduces a wrinkle: the court refuses to defer entirely to the executive branch when it comes to statutory obligations regarding refugees *already admitted* to the United States. The opinion thus draws a line between the President's broad authority to exclude aliens at the border and Congress's power to mandate how the executive treats noncitizens once they have been lawfully admitted. This distinction—between exclusion and treatment—may prove consequential for future litigation involving executive immigration policy.

## The Intersection of Refugee Law, Administrative Law, and Separation of Powers

The opinion sits at the crossroads of three distinct bodies of law, each carrying its own doctrines and standards of review. First, refugee law is governed by the Refugee Act of 1980, which Congress enacted to bring U.S. law into compliance with international refugee conventions. The Refugee Act created a statutory framework for refugee admissions and resettlement, codified across multiple provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The plaintiffs in *Pacito* argue that Executive Order 14163 violates the Refugee Act by suspending admissions in a manner inconsistent with Congress's statutory scheme.

Second, administrative law principles come into play through the plaintiffs' claims under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq., provides the default framework for judicial review of agency action. Under the APA, a reviewing court must set aside agency action that is "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law." 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The plaintiffs contend that the executive branch's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations violated APA procedures or exceeded statutory authority.

Third, separation of powers questions pervade the opinion. The court must determine whether the President has exceeded his statutory authority under § 1182(f), whether Congress has imposed mandatory obligations through § 1522 that constrain executive discretion, and how courts should navigate the "political question" of foreign policy and national security when reviewing executive immigration action. The opinion's split disposition—staying the injunction as to admissions but reinstating funding as to resettlement services—reflects the court's attempt to honor both executive authority and congressional mandates.

## The Procedural Context: An Interim Order Pending Appeal

Understanding the procedural posture of *Pacito* is essential to reading the opinion correctly. The Ninth Circuit has not yet issued a merits decision. Instead, this document is an order granting a stay pending appeal—a provisional remedy that maintains the status quo while the appellate court considers the underlying appeal. A stay "pending appeal" freezes the district court's order (here, the preliminary injunctions) until the appeal is resolved.

The standards for granting a stay come from *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), which requires courts to consider four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likely success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The first two factors, the court notes, are "the most critical." This procedural context matters because the court's analysis is necessarily predictive: the court is assessing *likelihood* of success, not finally resolving the merits. The court can (and does) indicate how it leans, but a merits opinion "will issue in due course."

The opinion also vacates prior stay orders that had created a "limited carveout" for refugees who were conditionally approved for admission before January 20, 2025. By vacating these prior stays and issuing a new stay "in its entirety," the panel simplifies the procedural landscape—but also reveals the evolving nature of interim orders in complex litigation.

## Overview of the Key Legal Issues and the Court's Approach

The opinion addresses two central legal issues, each governed by different statutes and standards.

**Issue One: The Validity of the Admission Suspension Under § 1182(f).** The court concludes that the government is likely to succeed on its argument that Executive Order 14163 falls within the President's § 1182(f) authority. The statute provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation... suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." The court applies *Trump v. Hawaii* deferentially, holding that the President's determination of detriment satisfies the statutory prerequisite and that the order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." On this issue, the court stays the district court's injunction—meaning the suspension of refugee admissions may proceed.

**Issue Two: The Legality of Terminating Resettlement Services Under § 1522.** Here the court reaches the opposite conclusion. Section 1522 of Title 8 addresses "programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." The court reads certain provisions of § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations on the executive branch to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees who have already been admitted. Notably, the court focuses on language directing that the Director "shall" provide specific services "to the extent of available appropriations." Because the government could not confirm whether it had secured alternative providers for these mandated services, the court directs the government to reinstate the terminated cooperative agreements necessary to fulfill its statutory obligations.

The result is a split ruling: the President's admission suspension stands, but the funding termination for resettlement services is partially enjoined.

## Roadmap of the Opinion Structure: From Background to Stay Analysis to the Split Decision

The opinion follows a logical structure that students should track carefully:

**Background Section.** The court first establishes the factual and procedural foundation: the executive orders, the statutory framework, the plaintiffs' identity and claims, and the district court proceedings. This section is largely undisputed and provides the necessary context for the legal analysis.

**Discussion Section.** The court then applies the *Nken* stay factors. The analysis bifurcates based on the two legal issues. For the admission suspension, the court finds the government likely to succeed based on deference under *Hawaii*. For the APA challenge to the funding termination, the court finds the government not likely to succeed because § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations that the executive cannot simply ignore. The court's disposition follows from this split: stay the injunction as to admissions, but require reinstatement of cooperative agreements for resettlement services.

**Judge Lee's Partial Concurrence and Partial Dissent.** Judge Lee agrees with the majority on the admission issue but dissents from the conclusion that § 1522 mandates funding. His dissent rests on a textual analysis of § 1522's title ("Authorization for programs...") and the permissive language ("authorized") found in the funding subsections. This disagreement over statutory interpretation—whether "shall" creates a mandate or merely sets conditions on discretionary authority—represents a classic divide in statutory construction.

Students should pay attention to how each section builds toward the court's ultimate holding. The background provides facts; the discussion applies standards; the split disposition reflects the court's attempt to balance competing legal obligations. Judge Lee's separate opinion highlights the reasonable disagreements that can arise even when judges apply the same legal framework.
