Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 26: # Statutory Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Services vs. Discretionary Authorization

## Overview of § 1522 and the Refugee Act of 1980 Framework

The Refugee Act of 1980, passed as an amendment to the Immigration and Nationality Act, established the framework for the admission and resettlement of refugees in the United States. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act sets forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." Within this framework, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs the domestic resettlement services that the federal government provides to refugees after their admission into the United States.

Section 1522 sits at the heart of the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*. The organizational plaintiffs—nonprofit agencies that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide resettlement services—challenged the government's termination of those agreements under the Administrative Procedure Act. The threshold question before the Ninth Circuit was whether § 1522 creates mandatory obligations that the government must fulfill, or whether it merely authorizes discretionary programs that the executive branch may choose to fund or not. This question of statutory interpretation determines whether the government's actions violated the APA, which prohibits agency action that is "not in accordance with law."

The majority and dissent reached opposite conclusions on this question, employing different tools of statutory interpretation. Their disagreement illustrates how courts wrestle with ambiguous statutory language and the significance of textual clues like section titles, mandatory versus permissive verbs, and statutory structure.

## The Majority's Reading: 'Shall' Language Creates Mandatory Duties

The majority concluded that the government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" because § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations to provide certain resettlement services. The opinion begins its analysis with § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall," "to the extent of available appropriations," provide several categories of assistance.

The use of "shall" is significant in statutory interpretation. The word "shall" is generally presumed to impose a mandatory duty rather than granting discretionary authority. Here, the majority read § 1522(a)(1)(A) as requiring the Director to make available employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunities for women refugees—to the extent Congress has appropriated funds for these purposes. The majority also noted that these services must be provided "as quickly as possible" to achieve economic self-sufficiency, suggesting congressional intent that refugees receive prompt assistance.

Crucially, the majority observed that Congress appropriated approximately $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 (though reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025). The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" thus operates as a limiting principle: the government's obligation extends only as far as Congress has provided funding. But once appropriations exist, the majority concluded, the statute requires the government to fulfill its statutory duties.

The majority further noted that § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) permits these services to be provided through "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The organizational plaintiffs held such cooperative agreements, which the government terminated in February 2025. Given the government's uncertainty at oral argument about whether it could secure alternative providers, the majority ordered the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

## Key Provisions: § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The majority relied on three specific provisions to support its reading of § 1522 as creating mandatory obligations.

First, § 1522(a)(1)(A) establishes the baseline obligations for refugee services. This provision requires the Director to provide four categories of assistance: "(i) make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement," "(ii) provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training," "(iii) insure that cash assistance is made available to refugees," and "(iv) insure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction." Each of these mandatory commands is modified by the prefatory language limiting the obligation "to the extent of available appropriations." The majority understood this structure as creating a binding duty tied to congressional funding decisions.

Second, the majority pointed to § 1522(d)(2)(B), which addresses unaccompanied refugee minors. This provision states that "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." The majority found this language significant because it imposes a direct obligation on the Director to take responsibility for unaccompanied minors during the interim period before placement. The "if necessary" qualifier, in the majority's view, does not eliminate the mandatory nature of the duty but rather specifies when that duty is triggered.

Third, § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary of Health and Human Services to "develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." Again, the majority read "shall develop and implement" as creating a mandatory obligation, with "as needed" merely defining the scope of services required.

Together, these provisions persuaded the majority that Congress intended to require the executive branch to provide resettlement services when refugees have been admitted and appropriations are available. The government's termination of cooperative agreements, coupled with its uncertainty about replacement providers, suggested that it was failing to meet these statutory obligations.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: Significance of 'Authorization' in Section Title

Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, offered a fundamentally different interpretation of § 1522. In his view, "the provision is most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate." Judge Lee began with a textualist starting point: the section's title.

Section 1522's title reads: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee emphasized that "authorization" is permissive, not mandatory. He cited Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "authorization" as "permission to do something" or "the official document granting such permission." This definitional approach reflects the principle that statutory titles and headings can inform interpretation, particularly when they are part of the enacted statute rather than merely editorial additions.

Judge Lee observed that the specific funding provisions throughout § 1522 consistently use permissive language. Sections 1522(b) through (e)—which provide funding for various refugee services including "Program of initial resettlement" and "Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees"—all state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. In Judge Lee's reading, this repeated use of "authorized" rather than "required" or "shall" signals Congress's intent to grant discretion to the executive branch.

This interpretive dispute illustrates how courts must weigh competing textual signals. The majority focused on the "shall" language in § 1522(a), while Judge Lee focused on the section title and the "authorized" language in the substantive funding provisions. Both sides claimed that their reading reflected the statute's "natural" meaning, demonstrating how reasonable judges can disagree about which textual indicators should control.

## Structural Interpretation: § 1522(a) as Conditions on Discretionary Funding

Judge Lee's dissent offered a structural interpretation that sought to reconcile the "shall" language in § 1522(a) with the permissive language elsewhere in the section. He argued that § 1522(a) does not independently mandate funding but rather imposes mandatory conditions that apply *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretion under the funding provisions.

The key textual clue, according to Judge Lee, is that § 1522(a) is titled "Conditions and consideration." This title suggests that subsection (a) establishes requirements that govern how programs must operate, not whether they must operate. Judge Lee pointed to the qualifying phrase in § 1522(a): "In providing assistance under this section." This phrase, he argued, signals that the mandatory "shall" language applies only when the government is already "providing assistance"—that is, when it has chosen to exercise the discretion granted in the subsequent subsections.

Under this reading, the structure of § 1522 works as follows: subsections (b) through (e) authorize the executive branch to establish and fund refugee programs. If the government elects to provide such assistance, then subsection (a) imposes mandatory "conditions and considerations" that must be followed. For example, if the Director exercises discretion to fund employment training under § 1522(c), then § 1522(a)(1)(A)(i) requires that such training aim to "achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible."

Judge Lee applied similar reasoning to the provisions the majority cited. Subsection (d)(2)(B)'s requirement that the Director assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors only applies "if necessary," and it "drips with deference to the executive branch." Subsection (e)(7)(A)'s requirement that the Secretary "develop and implement" projects applies only "as needed," leaving the "metes and bounds of the programs" to executive discretion. In Judge Lee's view, these qualifiers undermine any claim of a firm congressional mandate.

## The Role of Appropriations in Determining the Scope of Obligations

Both the majority and the dissent grappled with the significance of the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" in § 1522(a)(1)(A). This phrase is central to the dispute because it links statutory obligations to congressional funding decisions, but the two opinions understood that link differently.

For the majority, appropriations operate as an enabling condition: once Congress appropriates funds, the government has a duty to provide the services described in § 1522. The majority noted that Congress appropriated billions of dollars for refugee assistance for FY 2025, which meant that appropriations were "available" and the government's obligations were triggered. The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" merely limits the scope of services to what Congress has funded; it does not eliminate the underlying duty.

Judge Lee offered a different perspective. In his view, the appropriations language is consistent with § 1522 being an authorization statute—a type of legislation that permits spending but does not require it. He argued that "Section 1522 authorizes the executive branch to craft programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow. It does not require that those programs operate." Under this reading, appropriations are a necessary but not sufficient condition for services to be provided; the executive retains discretion over whether to actually establish programs even when funding exists.

This disagreement has practical implications that go beyond this case. If § 1522 creates mandatory obligations, then refugees admitted to the United States have enforceable legal rights to receive certain services, and agencies can be sued under the APA for failing to provide them. If § 1522 merely authorizes discretionary programs, then the executive branch has greater flexibility to reduce or eliminate services, subject only to political accountability rather than judicial review.

The majority's order addressed these practical concerns by directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to refugees "who have been admitted to the United States." This limitation reflects the majority's recognition that the government's obligations under § 1522 apply specifically to those refugees who have already been admitted—an important distinction given that the court simultaneously stayed the injunction blocking the suspension of new refugee admissions.
