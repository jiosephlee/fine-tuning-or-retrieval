Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 33: # Judge Lee's Partial Dissent and Institutional Implications

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent provides an important counterpoint to the majority's analysis, illustrating how reasonable jurists can reach divergent conclusions when interpreting the same statutory text. Her opinion highlights fundamental questions about the nature of statutory obligations and the proper role of the judiciary in overseeing executive branch programs.

## Judge Lee's Agreement on Presidential Admissions Authority

Judge Lee begins by establishing common ground with the majority. She agrees that the President possesses "vast discretion and deference in immigration matters" and that the executive order suspending refugee admissions falls within that authority. On this issue, she would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety," meaning she would allow the President's suspension of refugee admissions to proceed unhindered by the preliminary injunction.

This agreement reflects the Supreme Court's holdings in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which the majority cited for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. Judge Lee accepts that framework without reservation. Her partial concurrence thus joins the majority in recognizing broad executive power over the admission of aliens at the border—a power the court treats as largely immune from searching judicial review.

Where Judge Lee departs from the majority is on the question of services for refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. This distinction—between those seeking admission and those already present—forms the fault line between concurrence and dissent.

## The Dissent's Interpretation of § 1522: Authorization Rather Than Mandate

Judge Lee's central argument is that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates discretionary authority, not a mandatory obligation. She reaches this conclusion through careful attention to the statutory text and structure.

First, Judge Lee points to the section's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, she explains that "authorization" means "permission to do something"—a permissive grant of power, not a command. Under traditional canons of statutory construction, titles and section headings can inform interpretation of ambiguous provisions. Judge Lee treats the title as a strong signal that Congress intended to permit, not compel, executive action.

Second, she examines the operative language in the funding provisions. The specific subsections providing for different refugee services—such as programs for initial resettlement and cash assistance—state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. Judge Lee reads this language as confirming the permissive nature of the statute. The repeated use of "authorized" throughout subsections (b) through (e) reinforces her view that Congress gave the executive branch discretion over whether to fund these programs at all.

The majority, by contrast, had pointed to § 1522(a), which uses the word "shall" and references services the Director must provide "to the extent of available appropriations." Judge Lee offers a competing reading: the "shall" language in subsection (a) is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section." In her view, this creates a conditional structure—if the government chooses to provide assistance under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b) through (e), then certain conditions and considerations apply. The "shall" does not independently require the government to act; it merely specifies what must happen if the government does act.

## Methodological Disagreement over Statutory Construction

The clash between the majority and Judge Lee illustrates a classic problem in statutory interpretation: determining when "shall" creates a mandatory duty versus when it operates within a broader permissive framework.

The majority emphasizes the word "shall" in § 1522(a) and the mandatory language in subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which require the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors and the Secretary to develop support projects. Under this reading, Congress created specific entitlements that the executive must fulfill.

Judge Lee counters that these provisions cannot be read in isolation. She argues that the overall structure of § 1522—including its title, the "authorized" language in the funding provisions, and the conditional framing of subsection (a)—demonstrates that Congress left the ultimate decision to the executive branch. She also notes that subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume responsibility only "if necessary" and authorizes rather than commands decisions about immediate care. Similarly, subsection (e)(7)(A) leaves the "metes and bounds" of support programs to executive discretion.

Judge Lee also raises a factual point: the record is unclear whether any unaccompanied refugee children are actually at issue in this case. This observation underscores her broader concern that the majority may be extending judicial mandates beyond what the statute requires and what the facts support.

## Separation of Powers Concerns Regarding Judicial Mandates on Executive Programs

Judge Lee's dissent carries significant separation of powers implications. Her core institutional argument is that courts exceed their proper role when they mandate executive action that Congress has merely authorized.

If § 1522 is permissive, then the executive branch has discretion to determine whether and how to provide resettlement services. A court that orders the reinstatement of cooperative agreements has effectively converted a grant of discretion into a judicially enforced obligation. Judge Lee warns that "our court is in no position to mandate them."

This concern connects to broader principles of executive discretion in foreign affairs and immigration. Judge Lee's approach would leave the political branches—the President and Congress—to negotiate the scope and funding of refugee programs. Her dissent reflects a view that judicial intervention in such matters risks disrupting the constitutional allocation of powers.

The majority, implicitly, takes a different view: when Congress enacts specific statutory provisions protecting refugees who have already been admitted, the judiciary has a role in ensuring those provisions are enforced. The majority's order reinstating cooperative agreements reflects this enforcement orientation.

## The Broader Significance of the Majority-Dissent Split

The split in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates that reasonable judges can disagree on fundamental questions of statutory interpretation, even when applying the same textual tools to the same provision. The majority and Judge Lee both rely on the text of § 1522, its structure, and relevant canons of construction—yet reach opposite conclusions about whether the statute requires or merely permits the provision of reception and placement services.

This disagreement has immediate practical consequences. The majority's order requires the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted to the United States, ensuring they receive services Congress has funded. Judge Lee would have stayed the injunctions in their entirety, leaving the executive free to terminate all such agreements.

The practical outcome thus represents a compromise: the President's suspension of new refugee admissions may proceed (as both the majority and Judge Lee agree), but services for already-admitted refugees must continue (as only the majority holds). The panel has split the difference between two visions of executive power and statutory obligation.

The panel's statement that "[a]n opinion on the merits will issue in due course" signals that this order is an interim measure. The reasoning underlying the split will be elaborated in a subsequent written opinion, potentially providing fuller analysis of the interpretive questions Judge Lee's dissent raises. For now, the order illustrates the tensions inherent in judicial review of executive immigration policy—and the difficult line courts must walk between enforcing statutory mandates and respecting executive discretion.
