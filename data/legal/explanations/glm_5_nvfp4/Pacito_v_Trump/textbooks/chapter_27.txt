Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 27: # The Stay Pending Appeal Framework

When a district court issues a preliminary injunction, the losing party often seeks immediate relief from the appellate court rather than waiting for the full appeal to be resolved. The procedural mechanism for such relief is a "stay pending appeal"—a temporary freeze of the lower court's order while the appeal proceeds. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit confronted a request to stay two preliminary injunctions that had blocked key aspects of the Trump administration's refugee policy. The court's analysis provides a clear illustration of how the stay pending appeal framework operates and how courts can reach different conclusions about different aspects of a single case.

## Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stay Pending Appeal

The Supreme Court established the governing standard for stays pending appeal in *Nken v. Holder*. The test requires courts to weigh four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Importantly, the Court emphasized that the first two factors—the likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are "the most critical."

This framework is discretionary and fact-intensive. Courts do not mechanically tally the factors; rather, they engage in a balancing exercise. The "strong showing" language for the first factor signals that a stay applicant faces a higher burden than a party seeking a preliminary injunction. The applicant must demonstrate more than a mere possibility of success—it must show a substantial case on the merits. The reasoning behind this elevated burden is that a stay pending appeal upsets the status quo established by the district court's careful consideration of the case. The appellate court must therefore have solid justification for intervening before the appeal is fully briefed and argued.

The *Nken* test reflects competing institutional concerns. On one hand, appellate courts must respect the district court's reasoned judgment and the presumption against disturbing its orders. On the other hand, if the district court has likely erred and the appellant faces serious irreparable harm, the appellate court has an obligation to provide interim relief. The balancing of these concerns is inherently case-specific, which is why courts often reach nuanced, fact-dependent conclusions.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Divergence Between Admissions and Services Claims

The most significant aspect of *Pacito* is the court's divergent analysis of two distinct legal claims: the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions and the challenge to the termination of resettlement services funding. The court reached opposite conclusions on the likelihood of success for these claims, illustrating how the same statutory scheme can support different outcomes for different issues.

For the admissions suspension claim, the court found the government likely to succeed. The executive order rested on authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." The court quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that this provision "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." The "sole prerequisite" under the statute is that the President find that entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court concluded that President Trump had "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement" through Executive Order 14163. Because the statutory language was clear and the executive order did not exceed textual limits, the government had a strong likelihood of success on this claim.

The services claim required a different analysis. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the federal government has obligations regarding refugee resettlement services. The court examined several subsections of this provision. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to make available employment training, provide English language instruction, ensure cash assistance is available, and guarantee equal opportunity for women. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) provides that for unaccompanied refugee minors, the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary." Section 1522(e)(7)(A) states that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for refugees in their first thirty-six months in the United States.

Congress had appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million through later rescission. These appropriations meant the "to the extent of available appropriations" limitation in § 1522(a)(1)(A) was satisfied. Yet at oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency to provide reception and placement services, despite having represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals." This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill statutory obligations led the court to conclude that the government was not likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges to the funding termination.

Judge Lee's partial dissent provides an important contrast. He argued that § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signals that the provision is permissive, not mandatory. He cited the dictionary definition of "authorization" as "permission to do something." He further noted that the specific funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance, using permissive rather than mandatory language. In Judge Lee's reading, § 1522(a)'s use of "shall" merely imposes conditions that apply *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretion to fund refugee programs. This interpretive dispute highlights how statutory construction can determine the outcome of the stay analysis.

## Irreparable Injury Considerations

The irreparable injury factor asks whether the party seeking the stay faces harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or corrected after the appeal. Traditional examples include loss of constitutional rights, permanent loss of business opportunities, or environmental damage. Courts consider whether the harm is imminent, whether it is certain to occur, and whether the injury is truly irreparable.

For the admissions suspension, the court's analysis of irreparable injury was implicit in its conclusion that the government was likely to succeed. The court emphasized that the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States" favored the government, given the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in immigration matters under *Hawaii*. The government's interest in controlling admissions and conducting foreign policy was sufficient to satisfy the irreparable injury requirement for this portion of the stay.

For the services claim, the court focused on the consequences for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. These individuals had no control over the government's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations. Without the reception and placement services historically provided through these agreements, admitted refugees would face immediate hardship in securing basic necessities—employment training, language instruction, and cash assistance. The court's eventual order to reinstate cooperative agreements reflects its concern that failure to provide these services would cause irreparable harm to a vulnerable population that Congress had specifically intended to protect.

The organizational plaintiffs—the three resettlement organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department—also faced irreparable injury. Their contracts were terminated, their ability to fulfill their mission was eliminated, and their relationship with the refugee population they served was disrupted. This harm extended beyond mere financial loss to encompass the destruction of ongoing relationships and programs.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—the balance of equities and the public interest—often merge in practice. Courts consider who wins and who loses from the stay and what outcome best serves broader societal interests. These factors can sometimes cut in different directions, requiring careful calibration.

In *Pacito*, the court found that the balance of equities and public interest favored different outcomes for different claims. For the admissions suspension, the court deferred to the President's assessment of what serves the national interest. *Trump v. Hawaii* established that courts should not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" for entry restrictions under § 1182(f). The public interest in controlling borders and determining who may enter the country weighed heavily in favor of staying the injunction against the admissions suspension.

For the services claim, however, the public interest cut the other way. Congress had spoken through the Refugee Act of 1980, creating statutory obligations for the treatment of refugees who have been admitted. The public interest in the executive branch complying with statutory mandates, and in ensuring that vulnerable individuals receive the assistance Congress has provided, supported maintaining some level of services. The court's concern was not that the administration had made a policy choice about funding levels, but that it had terminated cooperative agreements without any clear plan for providing the services that the statute may require.

The court's practical solution—staying the injunction against the admissions suspension while reinstating cooperative agreements for services to already-admitted refugees—represents an attempt to respect both the President's broad authority over admissions and Congress's specific directives regarding treatment of admitted refugees. This bifurcated approach demonstrates how courts can craft stays that are responsive to the specific legal issues and equities at stake in each portion of a complex case.

## The Court's Practical Disposition: Reinstating Cooperative Agreements for Admitted Refugees

The court's order reflects a pragmatic approach to the stay pending appeal. Rather than granting or denying the stay in its entirety, the court crafted a tailored remedy that addressed its concerns about compliance with statutory obligations while respecting executive authority. The court vacated its prior stays and the district court's preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below." The exception required the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

This order is significant for several reasons. First, it limited the services mandate to refugees who have already been admitted—not those approved for resettlement but still abroad. This distinction tracks the statutory language: § 1522 governs "domestic resettlement," implying it applies to those who have already entered. Second, the order required the government to provide "the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements," providing concrete guidance about what services must be provided. Third, the court explicitly stated that it was not resolving the merits of the appeal, reserving its full reasoning "for reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow."

The court's willingness to order reinstatement of cooperative agreements at the stay stage is notable. Typically, stays preserve the status quo rather than mandate affirmative action. But the court concluded that the status quo could not simply be frozen—refugees who had been admitted were already in the United States and required immediate assistance. The cooperative agreements had been terminated after the litigation began, and reinstatement was necessary to restore a functioning system for providing the services that Congress had mandated.

Judge Lee's partial dissent underscores the contentious nature of this remedy. In his view, § 1522 creates no legal duty to provide services, meaning the court had no authority to order the government to reinstate cooperative agreements. He would have stayed the district court's injunctions "in their entirety," giving the executive branch complete discretion over both admissions and services. This disagreement reflects a deeper divide over statutory interpretation and the respective roles of Congress and the President in refugee policy.

The *Pacito* order illustrates that stay decisions can have immediate, practical consequences even before an appellate court decides the merits. The court's determination that the government was unlikely to succeed on the services claim led to immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements, ensuring continuity of services for admitted refugees while the appeal proceeds. Conversely, the court's conclusion that the government was likely to succeed on the admissions claim meant that the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions could continue during the appeal. This combination demonstrates the *Nken* framework in action: a four-factor balancing test that allows courts to provide tailored interim relief in complex cases involving multiple legal issues and competing interests.
