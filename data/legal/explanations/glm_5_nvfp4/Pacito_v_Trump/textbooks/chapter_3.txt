Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # Statutory Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligations or Discretionary Authorization?

The central legal dispute in *Pacito v. Trump* concerns whether the federal government is legally required to provide resettlement services to refugees already admitted to the United States, or whether such services are discretionary. This question turns on the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a provision of the Refugee Act of 1980. The panel majority and Judge Lee reach opposite conclusions about what this statute commands, illustrating how statutory interpretation can turn on close readings of textual cues—including section titles, verb forms, and the relationship between different subsections.

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and the statutory framework for refugee resettlement services

The Refugee Act of 1980 establishes the legal framework for how the United States handles refugee admissions and resettlement. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act sets forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The statute is codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act, including 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which addresses domestic resettlement services.

Section 1522 provides the statutory basis for what are commonly called "reception and placement" services—assistance provided to refugees after they arrive in the United States. These services include employment training and placement, English language instruction, cash assistance, and medical services. Under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), these services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." Historically, the State Department has entered into cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations to deliver these services. The question before the court was whether, having admitted refugees, the government is obligated to continue providing these services, or whether it may terminate them at its discretion.

## Majority's reading of § 1522: mandatory duties triggered by refugee admission

The majority concludes that the government is "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding the termination of resettlement services. In reaching this conclusion, the court interprets § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations on the government. The majority reads the statute to require that certain services be provided to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The majority's interpretation rests on several textual foundations. First, the court notes that § 1522 establishes requirements for services that must be provided "to refugees after their admission into the United States." This framing suggests that admission triggers statutory obligations. The court quotes extensively from the statute's operative language, emphasizing provisions that use the word "shall"—a term courts often interpret as mandatory rather than permissive.

## Key statutory language: 'shall,' 'to the extent of available appropriations,' and specific service mandates

The majority's analysis centers on the language of § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement shall, "to the extent of available appropriations," provide several categories of assistance. These include: making available resources for employment training and placement to achieve economic self-sufficiency; providing English language training; ensuring cash assistance is available; and ensuring women have equal opportunities to participate in training. The use of "shall" signals to the majority that these are mandatory duties.

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" functions as a limit on the government's obligation, not as a grant of discretion. To the majority, this language means Congress obligated the government to provide these services when funds are available—it does not give the executive branch discretion to withhold services that Congress has funded.

The majority also highlights § 1522(d)(2)(B), which addresses unaccompanied refugee minors. This provision states that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary" during interim periods before placement. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, providing interim support, medical services, and case management "as needed."

The majority further notes that Congress has appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The existence of these appropriations, combined with the mandatory language of the statute, suggests to the majority that Congress intended for these services to continue. The government's admission at oral argument that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies" to provide reception and placement services reinforces the majority's concern that the government may be failing to fulfill statutory obligations.

## Judge Lee's dissent: the significance of 'Authorization' in the section title

Judge Lee offers a fundamentally different reading of § 1522, one that begins with the section's title. Section 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee emphasizes the word "Authorization," citing the Black's Law Dictionary definition: "permission to do something" or an "official document granting such permission." To Judge Lee, this title signals that the section is permissive rather than mandatory—it grants the executive branch permission to act but does not compel action.

This approach reflects a standard principle of statutory interpretation: that section titles can inform the meaning of statutory text. While titles are not controlling, courts may consult them to resolve ambiguities or confirm textual readings. Judge Lee argues that the word "Authorization" in the title establishes the overall character of § 1522 as granting discretionary authority rather than imposing mandatory duties.

## Use of 'is authorized' language in subsections (b) through (e) as indicators of discretion

Building on the significance of the section title, Judge Lee points to the language used in the specific funding provisions—subsections (b) through (e). Each of these subsections states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance. For example, the subsections on initial resettlement and on cash and medical assistance use this permissive phrasing. To Judge Lee, the repeated use of "is authorized" throughout the operative provisions confirms that Congress granted discretion to the executive branch over whether and how to fund these programs.

This reasoning draws on a basic canon of statutory interpretation: courts generally presume that "may" or "is authorized" creates discretionary authority, while "shall" creates mandatory obligations. Judge Lee argues that the consistent use of authorization language across the specific funding provisions demonstrates Congress's intent to give the executive branch flexibility in administering refugee services.

## The interplay between § 1522(a)'s conditions and the discretionary funding provisions

The majority and Judge Lee fundamentally disagree about how to read the relationship between § 1522(a) and the funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e). Section 1522(a) is titled "Conditions and consideration" and uses the word "shall." The majority relies on this language to find mandatory obligations.

Judge Lee offers a competing interpretation. He argues that § 1522(a)'s mandatory language is qualified by the introductory phrase: "In providing assistance under this section." This phrase, to Judge Lee, means that the "conditions and considerations" in § 1522(a) apply only if the government chooses to provide assistance under the discretionary funding provisions. In other words, if the government exercises its discretion to fund refugee programs, it must meet certain conditions—such as providing employment training aimed at achieving economic self-sufficiency. But the government may also choose not to fund such programs at all.

This interpretive dispute illustrates a common challenge in statutory interpretation: determining how different provisions within a section relate to each other. The majority reads § 1522(a) as independently mandating services. Judge Lee reads § 1522(a) as subordinate to the discretionary funding provisions—setting conditions that apply only if the government chooses to act.

## The interpretive significance of subsection (d)(2)(B) for unaccompanied minors and (e)(7)(A) for interim support

The majority and dissent also differ in their readings of specific provisions addressing unaccompanied minors and interim support—provisions that both sides acknowledge use the word "shall."

Judge Lee addresses § 1522(d)(2)(B), which concerns unaccompanied refugee children. While acknowledging that this provision uses "shall," Judge Lee notes that it requires the Director to assume responsibility only "if necessary" and authorizes the Director "to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." Judge Lee argues that these qualifications—"if necessary" and the authorization language—inject executive discretion into what might otherwise appear mandatory. Moreover, Judge Lee notes that the record does not establish whether any unaccompanied refugee children are actually at issue in this case.

Similarly, Judge Lee reads § 1522(e)(7)(A), which requires the Secretary to develop projects for cash and medical support "as needed," as leaving the scope and nature of such programs to executive discretion. The phrase "as needed" suggests that the executive branch determines when such projects are necessary and what form they should take.

The majority, by contrast, reads these provisions as evidence of mandatory duties. The fact that Congress used "shall" in these specific contexts—even if qualified by phrases like "if necessary" or "as needed"—demonstrates that Congress knew how to impose obligations when it wished to do so. To the majority, these provisions reinforce the broader reading of § 1522 as creating duties, not merely granting permission.

The practical outcome of this interpretive divide is significant. The majority's reading leads to an order requiring the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Judge Lee's reading would have resulted in a complete stay of the district court's injunctions, leaving the government free to terminate services entirely. The split illustrates how different approaches to statutory interpretation—emphasizing different textual cues and reading provisions in different relationships to each other—can produce dramatically different practical consequences.
