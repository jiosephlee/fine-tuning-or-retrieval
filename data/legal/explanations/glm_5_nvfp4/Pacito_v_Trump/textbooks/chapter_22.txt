Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 22: # The Stay Pending Appeal Framework and Application

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test

When a party seeks to pause a lower court's order while an appeal proceeds, it requests a "stay pending appeal." The Ninth Circuit applied the well-established four-factor framework from *Nken v. Holder* to evaluate the government's request. Understanding this framework is essential because stays represent a form of interim relief that can dramatically affect the real-world operation of judicial decisions long before an appeal is resolved.

The four factors are: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." This makes intuitive sense: a stay is an extraordinary remedy that freezes the status quo, and courts are reluctant to grant such relief unless the applicant can show both that they have a strong case and that they face serious harm without immediate intervention.

A stay pending appeal differs from an injunction. An injunction resolves the merits of a claim (at least preliminarily), while a stay merely suspends the effect of a lower court ruling during appellate review. The stay preserves the status quo and prevents irreparable harm while the appellate court deliberates. Because the district court had already issued preliminary injunctions against the government, the government bore the burden of demonstrating that these injunctions should be frozen pending resolution of the appeal.

The *Nken* framework operates as a sliding scale—the factors do not require equal weight, and courts may calibrate their analysis accordingly. A particularly strong showing on one factor may compensate for a weaker showing on another, though the "most critical" factors of merits and irreparable injury must still be adequately established.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Different Conclusions for Different Claims

The court's analysis of the first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—reveals how the same case can present multiple distinct legal claims requiring separate analysis. The court reached opposite conclusions on the two main categories of claims, and this divergence shaped the ultimate structure of the stay.

On the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, the court found the government likely to prevail. The analysis centered on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." Quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court emphasized that this statutory language "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions."

The court's analysis illustrates an important principle of statutory interpretation in the immigration context: when Congress grants the President broad authority over admission of aliens, courts apply significant deference and do not conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The President's finding that refugee entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States" satisfied the sole prerequisite in § 1182(f). The court thus concluded the executive order did not exceed any textual limit on presidential authority.

However, on the plaintiffs' challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act regarding refugee resettlement services, the court reached the opposite conclusion. The government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." The court's analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1522—which governs domestic refugee resettlement—reveals how different statutory schemes invite different interpretive approaches. Unlike § 1182(f)'s broad grant of presidential discretion, § 1522 contains specific mandates using mandatory language like "shall." For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) mandates that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied refugee minors. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects" providing interim support and medical services.

The court noted that Congress had appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—approximately $3.178 billion for FY 2025, reduced by $800 million via rescission. The statutory structure therefore created obligations on the executive branch that could not simply be disregarded through executive action.

This split outcome on the first factor illustrates a crucial point for understanding stays: the "likelihood of success" analysis can yield different results for different claims within the same case. The court's approach demonstrated that a stay can be granted in part and denied in part, matching the distinct legal issues presented.

## Irreparable Injury: Considerations for the Government and Plaintiffs

The second *Nken* factor—irreparable injury—requires the stay applicant to demonstrate harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or other legal relief after the fact. The court's analysis of this factor operated differently for the admissions suspension versus the services termination.

For the admissions suspension, the court's treatment of irreparable injury was relatively brief, folded into its discussion of the "remaining factors" that "touch upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States." This brevity reflects a practical reality: when a court has already found a strong likelihood of success on the merits, the irreparable injury analysis may require less extensive discussion, particularly where the government invokes its authority over foreign affairs and national security.

The government's interest in controlling the admission of aliens into the United States has long been recognized as falling within the executive's constitutional and statutory domain. When the executive branch determines that entry of a class of aliens would be detrimental to national interests, courts have been reluctant to second-guess that determination. The irreparable injury here consisted of the harm to the government's ability to implement its chosen immigration policy during the potentially lengthy pendency of appeal—a policy determination the court had already deemed likely lawful.

For the services termination, the irreparable injury analysis shifted. The plaintiffs—refugees already admitted to the United States and the organizations that serve them—faced the immediate loss of statutorily-mandated services. The court was particularly troubled by the government's uncertainty about its ability to provide required services. At oral argument, the government admitted it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency to provide reception and placement services, despite having told the district court it was "actively preparing" such a request. This uncertainty created a risk that refugees lawfully present in the United States would be deprived of services Congress had mandated, creating irreparable harm that could not be undone by later appellate rulings.

The court's concern about the government's admitted uncertainty demonstrates an important aspect of irreparable injury analysis: courts consider not just the theoretical availability of relief, but the practical reality of whether plaintiffs can actually receive what statute entitles them to receive.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest in the Immigration Context

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—balance of equities and public interest—often merge in cases involving the federal government, because the government can invoke the public interest as a component of its own interests. The court's analysis in *Pacito* illustrates how immigration cases present unique considerations at this stage of the inquiry.

The court noted that "the remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President," favored the government with respect to the admissions suspension. This formulation reflects the principle that the political branches—Congress and the President—possess primary authority over immigration policy. When the executive branch makes determinations about the admission of aliens, courts give substantial weight to the executive's assessment of the national interest.

However, this deference has limits. The public interest also includes fidelity to statutory mandates enacted by Congress. When Congress has created specific obligations for the treatment of refugees—such as the requirements in § 1522 for reception and placement services—the public interest includes enforcement of those statutory duties. The court's recognition that the government was unlikely to succeed on its APA claims meant that the public interest favored maintaining required services for refugees already admitted.

The organizational plaintiffs—resettlement agencies with cooperative agreements—also had distinct equities. The government had terminated their cooperative agreements, which the district court ordered reinstated. These organizations had relied on continued performance of these agreements and faced disruption from sudden termination. The balance of equities thus required consideration of the reliance interests of organizations that had built operations around providing federally-mandated services.

The immigration context also implicates foreign relations considerations. The executive branch has unique competence in assessing how refugee policy affects relations with other nations and international perceptions of the United States. This factor reinforced the court's deference to executive determinations about admission policy. But foreign policy concerns did not entirely displace domestic statutory obligations—Congress had made the policy judgment that admitted refugees should receive certain services, and the executive could not simply disregard that judgment.

## The Court's Application: Stay on Admissions, Reinstatement on Services

The court's ultimate disposition exemplifies how appellate courts tailor interim relief to match their analysis of each claim. Rather than granting or denying the stay in toto, the court issued a split ruling that stayed the district court's injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below."

On the admissions suspension, the court stayed the preliminary injunctions that had prohibited enforcement of Executive Order 14163. This meant the government could resume implementing its suspension of refugee admissions under USRAP while the appeal proceeded. The court concluded that the government was likely to succeed on its argument that § 1182(f) authorized the President's determination, and that the balance of equities and public interest favored allowing the executive to implement its chosen admission policy.

On the resettlement services issue, the court took the opposite approach. Because the government was unlikely to prevail on the APA claims regarding services, and because of the government's admitted uncertainty about providing required services, the court "directed [the government] to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This reinstatement was limited to "refugees who have been admitted"—that is, those already lawfully present in the country—and included "the usual and customary services" provided under prior cooperative agreements.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent highlights the interpretive disagreement at the heart of the services issue. Judge Lee read § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs"—as indicating that the statute was permissive rather than mandatory. Under this view, "authorization" grants permission but does not compel action. Judge Lee also emphasized that the specific funding provisions in § 1522(b)-(e) use the phrase "Director is authorized," which he interpreted as discretionary language.

The majority's contrary reading focused on the mandatory "shall" language in § 1522(a) and specific subsections like (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A). This interpretive dispute—between reading the statute as authorization versus mandate—represents a fundamental disagreement about statutory construction that would presumably be addressed more fully in the merits opinion.

For students of appellate procedure, the decision demonstrates several practical points. First, stays pending appeal are neither all-or-nothing propositions nor mere procedural formalities—they can be carefully calibrated to preserve the status quo in light of the court's preliminary assessment of the merits. Second, the stay determination itself can reveal the court's tentative views on substantive legal questions, providing a preview of the eventual merits opinion. Third, even in areas of substantial executive discretion, such as immigration, statutory limits remain enforceable through judicial review. The President's broad authority over admission of aliens does not extinguish Congress's separate authority to mandate how those already admitted must be treated.
