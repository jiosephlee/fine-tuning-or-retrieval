Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 31: # The Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligation or Discretionary Authorization?

## Overview of § 1522 and its placement within the Refugee Act of 1980

The Refugee Act of 1980, passed as an amendment to the Immigration and Nationality Act, establishes the legal framework for the admission and resettlement of refugees in the United States. Section 1522 of Title 8 sits at the heart of the domestic resettlement scheme. Titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," this provision governs the services the federal government provides to refugees after they have been admitted to the United States. The statute addresses employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, medical services, and special protections for unaccompanied refugee minors.

The plaintiffs in *Pacito* challenged the government's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), arguing that § 1522 creates statutory duties that the executive branch cannot simply abandon. The government countered that § 1522 is permissive—it authorizes but does not mandate any particular level of service. This dispute presented the Ninth Circuit with a fundamental question of statutory interpretation: does § 1522 create legally enforceable obligations, or does it merely grant discretionary authority to the executive branch?

The panel's split on this question illustrates how courts approach statutory text, structure, and context when determining congressional intent. The majority and the dissent both claimed fidelity to the text but reached opposite conclusions about what that text requires.

## The majority's interpretation: 'shall' language and mandatory duties

The majority focused its analysis on the mandatory language scattered throughout § 1522. The court began with § 1522(a)(1)(A), which directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to accomplish four specified objectives: providing employment training and placement, offering English language training, ensuring cash assistance is available, and guaranteeing women equal participation opportunities. The majority read these directives as imposing binding obligations, tempered only by the availability of appropriations.

The court reinforced its interpretation by pointing to other subsections that use similarly mandatory language. Under § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for unaccompanied refugee minors during interim periods before placement. Under § 1522(e)(7)(A), the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects" providing interim support, medical services, and case management for recently resettled refugees. The majority treated these "shall" provisions as creating statutory duties that the government cannot simply disregard.

The court buttressed its reading with practical evidence from the record. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" for a new resettlement agency, yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill statutory obligations reinforced the majority's conclusion that the APA challenge had merit. Accordingly, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements sufficient to provide the services § 1522 describes.

## Judge Lee's dissent: significance of 'Authorization' in the section title

Judge Lee opened his dissent with a lesson in statutory interpretation: the title of a statutory provision can illuminate congressional intent. Section 1522's heading reads "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee fastened onto the word "authorization," defining it in accordance with Black's Law Dictionary as "permission to do something." From this starting point, he argued that the section permits but does not compel government action. Congress "allows action in § 1522," he wrote; "it does not require it."

This approach reflects a broader principle of statutory construction: courts should read statutes as a coherent whole, and section titles provide context for interpreting specific provisions within that section. Judge Lee contended that a section labeled "authorization" should presumptively be understood as granting discretionary power rather than imposing mandatory duties. The majority never directly addressed this textual clue from the section heading.

Judge Lee's interpretive move demonstrates how judges can reach different conclusions even when examining the same statutory text. Where the majority saw "shall" language as the key to unlocking congressional intent, Judge Lee saw the section title as the controlling signal that those mandatory phrases should be read in a discretionary context.

## The role of 'is authorized' language in subsections (b) through (e)

Judge Lee deepened his analysis by examining the operative language in the funding provisions themselves. Subsections (b) through (e) of § 1522—which provide for the initial resettlement program, project grants and contracts, cash assistance, and medical assistance—all state that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance. Judge Lee emphasized that this language appears in the provisions that actually appropriate funds and establish programs. By contrast, the "shall" language in § 1522(a) appears in what he called a "conditions and consideration" provision that only applies if the government chooses to exercise its authorized discretion.

This structural argument carries weight in statutory interpretation. If Congress intended to mandate services, Judge Lee reasoned, why would the operative funding provisions use permissive language? The pattern suggests that Congress gave the executive branch discretion over whether to establish programs, while specifying certain conditions that apply if the government chooses to do so. The majority did not directly grapple with this structural point, instead treating the "shall" language in § 1522(a) as independent mandates.

The disagreement illustrates how courts must weigh competing textual signals. Both sides cited the statute's actual language; neither invented words that do not appear. The conflict arose from which textual cues the judges treated as more significant—the "shall" language cited by the majority or the "is authorized" language emphasized in the dissent.

## The interplay between § 1522(a) conditions and discretionary funding provisions

The heart of the interpretive dispute concerned how § 1522(a) relates to the funding provisions that follow. The majority read § 1522(a) as creating standalone obligations: the Director "shall" provide employment training, English instruction, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women, subject only to the "extent of available appropriations." The appropriations qualifier, in the majority's view, merely limited the government's obligations to what Congress had funded—not eliminated them entirely.

Judge Lee offered a competing framework. He read § 1522(a) as a conditional provision that applies only "[i]n providing assistance under this section"—that is, under the discretionary funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e). In his reading, Congress authorized the executive to establish programs, and if the executive chose to do so, it had to comply with certain conditions. The "shall" language thus created mandatory standards for any programs the government decided to run, but did not compel the government to run those programs in the first place.

This dispute mirrors a classic tension in statutory interpretation: whether to read provisions as independent commands or as interlocking parts of a unified scheme. The majority's approach isolated the "shall" language and treated it as self-executing. Judge Lee's approach read the statute structurally, treating the enabling language in subsections (b) through (e) as the operative grant of power, with § 1522(a) supplying conditions that attach only when that power is exercised.

## The practical consequences of each interpretation

The stakes of this interpretive disagreement extended beyond abstract legal theory to the lived experiences of refugees already admitted to the United States. Under the majority's reading, the government remained obligated to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had already arrived, even after the executive order suspended new admissions. The court's order directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements meant that resettlement organizations could continue providing the "usual and customary services" refugees had historically received.

Under Judge Lee's reading, by contrast, the government bore no such ongoing obligation. If § 1522 is purely discretionary, the executive branch could terminate funding and rely on whatever alternative mechanisms it deemed appropriate—even if, as the record suggested, no clear alternative was yet in place. The practical effect of Judge Lee's approach would be to stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, leaving recently admitted refugees without guaranteed federal support.

The split also signals what may happen when the court issues its merits opinion. The majority's clear indication that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" strongly suggests the final opinion will hold that § 1522 creates enforceable statutory duties. Judge Lee's dissent provides a roadmap for potential Supreme Court review, should the government seek to challenge the Ninth Circuit's interpretation. For law students, the case offers a vivid illustration of how courts resolve statutory ambiguities—and how the choice between competing interpretive frameworks can produce dramatically different outcomes for the people affected by those decisions.
