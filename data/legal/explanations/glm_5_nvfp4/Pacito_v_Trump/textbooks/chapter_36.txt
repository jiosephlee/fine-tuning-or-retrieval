Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 36: # The Dispute Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligations or Discretionary Authority?

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and the Framework for Domestic Resettlement Services

The Refugee Act of 1980 established the legal framework governing how the United States receives and resettles refugees. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act sets forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." Within this statutory scheme, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs what the majority opinion terms "reception and placement" services—domestic resettlement assistance provided to refugees after their admission into the United States. The section addresses services such as employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and medical support. A central question in *Pacito v. Trump* is whether § 1522 obligates the executive branch to provide these services to refugees who have already been admitted, or whether the statute merely authorizes—but does not require—the government to do so.

The organizational plaintiffs in this case had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide these resettlement services. The government terminated these agreements following Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee admissions program. The plaintiffs argued, and the district court agreed, that these terminations violated the Administrative Procedure Act because the statute requires the government to provide such services. The government contended that it has discretion over whether to continue funding these programs.

## The Majority's Interpretation: "Shall" Language Creates Mandatory Duties for Admitted Refugees

The majority approaches the statutory interpretation question by examining the text of § 1522. The panel concludes that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" because the statute imposes mandatory obligations. Central to this reasoning is the presence of the word "shall" in key provisions—a term courts traditionally interpret as creating mandatory duties rather than granting discretionary authority.

The majority's analysis emphasizes that once refugees have been admitted to the United States, statutory obligations attach. The court notes that for fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated over $3 billion for migration and refugee assistance, though this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million. The existence of appropriations, combined with the statutory language, suggests to the majority that Congress intended for the executive branch to actually carry out these programs, not merely have the option to do so.

The practical context reinforces this interpretation. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing new agencies to provide reception and placement services. This uncertainty about the government's ability to provide statutorily described services bolsters the majority's conclusion that the statute requires their provision—otherwise, admitted refugees would be left without the support Congress mandated.

## Key Provisions Analyzed: § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The majority opinion examines three specific provisions to support its reading of mandatory obligations. First, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women to participate in programs. The majority reads this provision as creating duties that attach once refugees are admitted, subject only to the limitation of available appropriations.

Second, § 1522(d)(2)(B) addresses unaccompanied refugee minors. It provides that during any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." The majority reads this as imposing an obligation on the Director to provide care and support for unaccompanied minors during transitional periods.

Third, § 1522(e)(7)(A) provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." Again, the mandatory "shall" language signals to the majority that Congress required the development and implementation of these support projects for recently resettled refugees.

The majority notes that these services may be provided through cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies, a mechanism the government has historically employed. The court therefore directs the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: The Significance of "Authorization" in the Section Title

Judge Lee offers a fundamentally different reading of the same statutory text. His analysis begins with the section's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, Judge Lee notes that "authorization" is defined as "permission to do something"—a permissive, not mandatory, term. This framing suggests Congress granted the executive branch discretion over whether to implement these programs.

For Judge Lee, the title provides important interpretive guidance. When Congress intends to create mandatory obligations, it typically uses language that clearly communicates that intent. The word "authorization" in the section heading signals that Congress was permitting the executive branch to act, not commanding it to do so. This approach reflects a broader principle of statutory interpretation: courts should read statutory language in context, and section titles can inform the meaning of substantive provisions.

This interpretive dispute illustrates how the same statutory text can yield different conclusions depending on which textual signals judges find most significant. The majority emphasizes the word "shall" in subsections (a), (d), and (e). Judge Lee emphasizes the word "authorization" in the section title. Both readings have textual support, but they lead to opposite conclusions about whether the government must provide resettlement services.

## The Structural Argument: Subsections (b) Through (e) Use "Is Authorized" Language

Judge Lee's dissent also relies on the structural organization of § 1522. He observes that the specific funding provisions—subsections (b) through (e)—use the phrase "is authorized" rather than "shall." These subsections address the program of initial resettlement, project grants and contracts, cash and medical assistance, and other specific services. The consistent use of "authorized" language throughout these operative provisions suggests to Judge Lee that Congress granted the executive branch discretion over whether to fund these programs.

This structural argument highlights an important principle of statutory interpretation: courts should read individual provisions in light of the broader statutory scheme. If Congress intended to mandate refugee services, Judge Lee reasons, the funding provisions would use mandatory language like "shall provide" or "must fund." Instead, they consistently use the permissive "is authorized," indicating that the executive branch has discretion over these programs.

Judge Lee contrasts this with statutes that genuinely create spending mandates—those typically use "shall" in the operative funding provisions. The structure of § 1522, with discretionary language in the substantive funding provisions, reinforces his reading that Congress authorized but did not require the government to provide these services.

## How § 1522(a) Functions as Conditions on Discretionary Funding Decisions

The crux of the interpretive dispute centers on how to read § 1522(a), which does use "shall" language, in relation to the rest of the section. The majority reads § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing mandatory duties. Judge Lee reads it differently: as establishing conditions that apply only if the government chooses to exercise its discretionary authority under subsections (b) through (e).

Judge Lee points to the opening phrase of § 1522(a): "In providing assistance under this section..." This qualifier, he argues, signals that the "shall" requirements in § 1522(a) apply only when the government is already providing assistance. In other words, § 1522(a) sets forth mandatory conditions and considerations that govern how discretionary programs must operate if the government chooses to implement them. For example, if the Director exercises discretion to fund employment training under § 1522(c), § 1522(a) requires that such training be designed to achieve economic self-sufficiency as quickly as possible.

Under this reading, the "shall" language in § 1522(a)(1)(A), § 1522(d)(2)(B), and § 1522(e)(7)(A) does not independently create obligations. Rather, the requirements in these provisions assume that the government is already operating programs under the discretionary authority granted in subsections (b) through (e). The "to the extent of available appropriations" phrase in § 1522(a)(1)(A) similarly suggests to Judge Lee that the provision governs how funds must be used if appropriated, not that Congress created an entitlement to funding.

Judge Lee also addresses the specific provisions the majority cites. As for § 1522(d)(2)(B), he notes that the provision requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors only when "necessary," and authorizes the Director "to make necessary decisions"—language that preserves executive discretion. Moreover, he questions whether the record even establishes that unaccompanied refugee minors are at issue in this case. As for § 1522(e)(7)(A), he reads the phrase "as needed" as similarly deferring to executive judgment about what services are required.

This interpretive dispute over § 1522 remains unresolved pending the full opinion on the merits. The panel's order granting a partial stay while directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees reflects the majority's preliminary conclusion that at least some statutory obligations exist. Judge Lee's dissent signals that the full merits opinion will need to address these statutory interpretation questions more thoroughly. The case thus presents a classic example of how courts grapple with ambiguous statutory text and competing canons of construction in the immigration context, where executive authority and statutory obligations often intersect.
