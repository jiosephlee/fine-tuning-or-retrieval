Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 23: # The Court's Order and Judge Lee's Partial Dissent

## The Order's Practical Requirements: Reinstating Cooperative Agreements

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* produces an unusual outcome: the government largely prevails on the question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions, yet it must still take affirmative action to reinstate certain funding mechanisms. Understanding this result requires careful attention to the procedural posture and the court's application of the stay factors.

The court begins by vacating its prior stays—the administrative stay issued July 18, 2025, and the earlier stay issued March 25, 2025 (as amended in April and May). These prior orders had temporarily frozen the district court's preliminary injunctions while the appeal was being briefed. By vacating these stays, the court might seem to be restoring the status quo from the district court. But that is not what happens. Instead, the court issues a new stay pending appeal—one that has a different scope than the prior stays.

The court stays the district court's February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below." The "except as noted below" carveout is critical. The court directs the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This is not merely a denial of the government's request for a complete stay; it is an affirmative requirement that the government act. The order specifies that the services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

This aspect of the order illustrates an important feature of stay litigation. A stay is typically understood as a suspension of a lower court's order—maintaining the status quo while the appeal proceeds. Here, however, the court uses its stay authority not simply to freeze proceedings, but to require the government to take steps it had previously refused to take. The court justifies this approach through its analysis of the Administrative Procedure Act claims, finding that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of those claims. The practical effect is that while the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions remains in effect, the funding for services to refugees already in the country must continue.

## The Distinction Between Refugees Already Admitted Versus Those Awaiting Admission

Central to the court's reasoning is a distinction between two categories of individuals: refugees who have already been admitted to the United States, and refugees who have been approved for resettlement but remain outside the country. This distinction drives the different treatment of the two main issues in the case.

For refugees awaiting admission, the court gives the President's suspension order full effect. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court concludes that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and that the President has satisfied the statute's sole prerequisite—finding that entry of the covered class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court emphasizes that it cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Under this highly deferential standard, the executive order's suspension of admissions is likely to be upheld.

For refugees already admitted, however, the court reaches a different conclusion. The court reads 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing statutory obligations to provide "reception and placement services" to refugees "after their admission into the United States." The statute requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide services including employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. The statute authorizes these services to be provided through cooperative agreements with nonprofit organizations—the very agreements the State Department terminated in February 2025.

The court expresses concern about the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide these services. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency," but at oral argument, the government was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies." This uncertainty, combined with the court's reading of the statutory mandates in § 1522, leads the court to require reinstatement of the cooperative agreements—at least for refugees already in the country.

This bifurcated approach reflects the court's attempt to navigate between two competing legal frameworks. The President's authority over admission decisions under § 1182(f) is nearly plenary, governed by a standard of extreme deference. But once individuals have been admitted, different statutory provisions apply—provisions that, in the court's view, create concrete obligations. The distinction between "entry" and "post-entry" rights is well established in immigration law, and the court's order draws on that tradition.

## Judge Lee's Agreement on Presidential Authority to Suspend Admissions

Judge Lee concurs with the majority's conclusion that the President has authority to suspend refugee admissions. This agreement reflects the Supreme Court's holding in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which established that § 1182(f) grants the President broad power to suspend the entry of any class of aliens when the President finds that their entry would be detrimental to national interests. Judge Lee accepts that this authority extends to the refugee context, notwithstanding the specific statutory framework created by the Refugee Act of 1980.

Judge Lee's agreement on this point underscores that the dissent is narrow in scope. Judge Lee would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety"—meaning Judge Lee would not require the government to provide services even to refugees already admitted. The disagreement, therefore, is not about the scope of presidential power over admissions, but rather about whether statutory obligations persist after admission has occurred.

This alignment on the admissions question also reveals something about the procedural posture. The majority and dissent both apply the stay factors from *Nken v. Holder*, and both conclude that the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. The split emerges only when the court turns to the APA claims concerning funding for resettlement services—specifically, the interpretation of § 1522.

## Judge Lee's Dissenting Interpretation of § 1522

Judge Lee's dissent centers on a fundamental question of statutory interpretation: Does § 1522 create mandatory obligations, or does it merely authorize discretionary programs? The answer to this question determines whether the court can properly order the executive branch to continue funding resettlement services.

Judge Lee begins with the statute's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Drawing on Black's Law Dictionary, Judge Lee argues that "authorization" is permissive—it means "permission to do something," not a command to act. This is a textualist argument, using the statutory title as evidence of congressional intent.

The dissent then examines the substantive provisions of § 1522, particularly subsections (b) through (e), which describe specific refugee service programs. Each of these provisions states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance. For Judge Lee, this language signals congressional choice to permit but not require executive action. If Congress had wanted to mandate these services, Judge Lee argues, it would have used the word "shall."

The majority, by contrast, focuses on § 1522(a), which uses mandatory language. The Director "shall"—"to the extent of available appropriations"—provide employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and ensure equal opportunity for women. Judge Lee offers a different reading of this language. The phrase "In providing assistance under this section" qualifies the obligations in § 1522(a), Judge Lee argues. In other words, the mandatory conditions apply only if the Director has already chosen to provide assistance under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b) through (e). This creates a conditional structure: if you fund programs, you must do so in accordance with certain conditions. But you are not required to fund programs in the first place.

Judge Lee also addresses two provisions that the majority cites as evidence of mandatory obligations: § 1522(d)(2)(B), concerning unaccompanied refugee children, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), concerning interim support projects. Judge Lee reads these provisions as retaining executive discretion. Subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children only "if necessary," and the determination of necessity lies with the executive. Subsection (e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to develop projects "as needed"—again, in Judge Lee's view, a standard that leaves room for executive judgment.

This disagreement illustrates a recurring tension in statutory interpretation: how to read statutes that mix permissive and mandatory language. When Congress uses both "authorized" and "shall" in the same statutory scheme, courts must determine how these terms relate to each other. Judge Lee reads the authorization language as the governing structural principle, with the mandatory language serving as conditional requirements. The majority, presumably, reads the mandatory language as independently operative—creating obligations that exist so long as appropriations are available.

## The Broader Separation of Powers Implications of the Majority-Dissent Split

The disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee reflects deeper questions about the separation of powers and the role of courts in reviewing executive action. When Congress enacts a statute that creates a federal program, but the executive branch decides not to implement that program, can the courts order implementation? The answer depends on whether the statute creates judicially enforceable rights—a question that sits at the intersection of statutory interpretation, administrative law, and constitutional structure.

Judge Lee's dissent articulates a vision of executive discretion that echoes themes from the "political question" doctrine and unitary executive theory. If Congress has only "authorized" the executive to act, then the decision whether to act belongs to the political branches, not the judiciary. Judge Lee frames this as a matter of institutional competence: "the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services, our court is in no position to mandate them." This suggests that even if the court believes providing services would be good policy, the court lacks authority to order it.

The majority's approach, by contrast, reflects the traditional role of courts in enforcing statutory obligations. If Congress has mandated certain services through validly enacted legislation, and those services are not being provided, then a court may order the executive branch to comply. This reflects a view of checks and balances in which the judiciary ensures that the executive does not disregard statutory commands.

The separation of powers question becomes more complex given the context: immigration and refugee policy, areas where executive power is traditionally at its apex. The majority acknowledges that the President has broad authority over admission decisions, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*. But the majority distinguishes between admission (where executive power is vast) and post-admission services (where statutory obligations may apply). This line-drawing exercise—determining where plenary executive power ends and statutory obligations begin—implicates fundamental questions about the scope of judicial review in immigration cases.

Both sides invoke text, but they read the same text differently. Both sides claim fidelity to congressional intent, but they disagree about what Congress intended. This pattern is familiar in statutory interpretation cases, and it underscores that textualism alone rarely resolves hard cases. Judges must make choices about how to read statutory language, and those choices often reflect deeper commitments about institutional roles and the proper balance of power among the branches of government.
