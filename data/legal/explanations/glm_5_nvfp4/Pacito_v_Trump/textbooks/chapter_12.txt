Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 12: # Key Takeaways and the Pending Merits Opinion

## The Immediate Practical Effect of the Stay Order

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* produces a bifurcated outcome with significant real-world consequences. As a practical matter, the stay order allows the executive branch to implement the refugee admission suspension while simultaneously compelling the government to maintain certain services for refugees already admitted to the United States. This means the government may block new refugees from entering the country, but must continue providing reception and placement services to those refugees who have already been admitted.

Understanding what a "stay pending appeal" actually does is essential. A stay does not reverse the district court's injunction; rather, it presses pause on the injunction's operation while the appellate court considers the appeal. Here, the Ninth Circuit lifted its previous stays and issued a new stay that freezes the district court's orders—except for the portion requiring continuation of reception and placement services. The order explicitly directs the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

The practical effect is that refugee resettlement organizations—like the three organizational plaintiffs—must have their cooperative agreements reinstated for the limited purpose of serving already-admitted refugees. The court's frustration with the government's uncertainty about providing these services is palpable: despite representing on March 10 that it was "actively preparing" to secure a new agency, the government could not confirm any progress at the September oral argument. This gap between representation and reality appears to have influenced the court's decision to maintain the service requirement.

## What the Court Left Undecided Pending the Merits Opinion

The panel was explicit that this order is interim in nature. The opening sentence announces that "[a]n opinion will issue in due course," and the discussion section reiterates that "[a]n opinion on the merits will issue in due course." This procedural posture is important for students to understand: the court has made preliminary determinations about likelihood of success on the merits—the standard for issuing a stay—but has not issued final rulings on the legal questions.

On the admission suspension, the panel expressed confidence in the government's likely success, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. The court signaled that it views the President's authority as broad and that judicial inquiry into the President's justifications is limited. However, even here, the court has not definitively ruled; it has only determined that the government has made a "strong showing" of likely success under the first *Nken* factor.

On the APA challenge to the funding termination, the court reached the opposite preliminary conclusion but provided limited analysis. The order states that "[f]or reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow," the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim. The court's discussion of § 1522's statutory language and the government's uncertainty about service provision offers hints about the merits reasoning, but the full analysis awaits the opinion to come.

The dissent from Judge Lee further highlights what remains contested. Judge Lee's argument that § 1522 is permissive rather than mandatory—a question of statutory interpretation—will presumably be addressed in the merits opinion. The panel majority and the dissent have staked out competing readings of the same statutory text, and the forthcoming opinion will need to explain which interpretation prevails and why.

## The Significance of This Order as Precedent for Future Refugee Policy Disputes

While an interim stay order carries less precedential weight than a full merits opinion, *Pacito* nonetheless signals important principles for future litigation over executive refugee policy. The order reinforces *Trump v. Hawaii*'s teaching that § 1182(f) grants the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens. Future litigants challenging presidential suspension of refugee admissions under this statute face a steep climb; the Ninth Circuit's citation to *Hawaii* suggests that courts will not probe the President's stated justifications with any degree of rigor.

At the same time, the order draws a line: executive authority over admission does not necessarily translate into authority to terminate all associated services. The court's analysis suggests that once refugees are admitted, different statutory provisions govern their treatment. This distinction—between admission and post-admission treatment—may prove significant in future disputes. The Refugee Act of 1980, as described in *Negusie v. Holder*, establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees," and the court appears to read that ongoing treatment obligation as surviving a suspension of new admissions.

The order also demonstrates that procedural history matters. The government's representation that it was preparing to secure alternative service providers, followed by its inability to confirm progress, created a factual record that undercut its position. Future government litigants would be wise to ensure that representations to courts about plans and preparations can be substantiated.

## How the Bifurcated Analysis Reflects Broader Tensions in Immigration Law

*Pacito* exemplifies a fundamental tension in immigration law: the divide between the political branches' plenary power over admission and the competing imperative to protect the rights of noncitizens once present within the United States. The panel's willingness to stay the admission suspension while maintaining the service requirement mirrors this doctrinal boundary.

The admission decision receives deference so profound that it borders on non-justiciability. The court quotes *Hawaii* for the principle that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This deference reflects the longstanding doctrine that the political branches—and particularly the executive—possess near-exclusive authority over who may enter the nation. The "detrimental to the interests of the United States" finding in § 1182(f) requires only that the President make such a finding; it does not require that courts evaluate whether the finding is correct.

Yet once individuals have been admitted, the legal landscape shifts. The court's analysis of § 1522 proceeds without reference to the deference that governed the admission question. Instead, the court engages in traditional statutory interpretation, parsing the text to determine congressional intent. This shift reflects the principle that noncitizens physically present in the United States enjoy greater legal protections than those seeking entry. The organizational plaintiffs' cooperative agreements, and the services those agreements fund for admitted refugees, occupy this more protected legal space.

Judge Lee's dissent identifies this tension and resolves it differently. By reading § 1522 as permissive, Judge Lee would extend executive discretion beyond admission to encompass service provision. The majority's reading preserves a sphere of statutory protection for admitted refugees. This disagreement reflects competing visions of where the line between admission and treatment should be drawn, and how much deference the executive deserves in each domain.

## The Institutional Relationship Between District Courts and Courts of Appeals in Immigration Cases

The procedural history of *Pacito* illustrates the complex interplay between district courts and courts of appeals in fast-moving immigration litigation. The district court here acted quickly: issuing an oral preliminary injunction on February 25, a written order on February 28, and a second preliminary injunction on March 24. Each order sought to preserve the status quo and prevent irreparable harm to refugees and resettlement organizations.

The Ninth Circuit, in turn, moved rapidly to stay portions of those injunctions. The March 25 order, issued by a motions panel, partially stayed the district court's injunction. Subsequent orders on April 21 and May 9 clarified the "limited carveout" for refugees conditionally approved before January 20. The July 18 administrative stay briefly froze the district court's enforcement framework. This back-and-forth—district court injunction, partial stay, clarification, administrative stay—demonstrates how emergency litigation proceeds in the immigration context.

The standard for appellate review of preliminary injunctions differs from the standard district courts apply in issuing them. The district court applied the traditional preliminary injunction factors, concluding that plaintiffs were likely to succeed and would suffer irreparable harm absent relief. The Ninth Circuit, reviewing the government's stay motion, applied the *Nken* factors, which mirror the preliminary injunction standard but from the movant's perspective. This procedural posture means that the appellate court was not reviewing the district court's decision for abuse of discretion, but rather making an independent assessment of whether a stay was warranted.

The district court's July 14 order, establishing an enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate judge, represents an effort to monitor compliance with its injunction. The Ninth Circuit's initial administrative stay of that order, followed by the decision to lift the stay on the service provision component, shows how appellate courts can cabin district court oversight in complex institutional litigation.

## The Potential Implications for Future Challenges to Executive Immigration Actions

*Pacito* offers a roadmap for both litigants and courts in future challenges to executive immigration actions. For challengers, the opinion suggests that claims based on statutory interpretation of post-admission obligations may succeed even when admission-related claims fail. Plaintiffs who can frame their claims around statutory mandates—like the § 1522 requirements—may find more judicial receptiveness than those challenging executive admission decisions.

For the executive branch, the opinion signals that representations to courts about plans and preparations will be scrutinized. The government's inability to confirm progress on securing alternative service providers weakened its position and may have influenced the court's decision to maintain the service requirement. Future administrations would be well advised either to have concrete plans in place before representing them to courts or to acknowledge uncertainty honestly.

The disagreement between the panel majority and Judge Lee over § 1522's interpretation highlights a broader question: how should courts read statutes that confer both authority and funding? The majority reads "shall" provisions as imposing obligations; Judge Lee reads the overall statutory structure as permissive. This question of statutory interpretation—when "shall" means "must" versus when it operates within a broader permissive scheme—will likely recur in immigration litigation and beyond.

Finally, the opinion reinforces that executive power, while vast in immigration matters, is not unlimited. The court's willingness to compel reinstatement of cooperative agreements demonstrates that even in a highly deferential doctrinal environment, courts will enforce statutory limits. The scope of those limits, and the precise boundary between permissible executive discretion and mandatory statutory duty, await clarification in the forthcoming merits opinion.
