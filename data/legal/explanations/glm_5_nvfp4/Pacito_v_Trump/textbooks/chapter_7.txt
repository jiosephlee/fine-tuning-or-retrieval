Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 7: # The Legislative Framework: The Refugee Act of 1980 and Its Interaction with INA Provisions

## Historical Background and Purpose of the Refugee Act of 1980

The Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102, represents a watershed moment in U.S. immigration law. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act established "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009). Before 1980, refugee admissions were governed by a patchwork of ad hoc provisions that often responded to specific geopolitical crises. The Refugee Act regularized this process, creating a systematic framework for both the admission of refugees and their subsequent resettlement within the United States.

The Act amended the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) by adding new provisions codified throughout Title 8 of the U.S. Code. These amendments created two interconnected but distinct systems: one governing the admission of refugees into the country, and another governing the services provided to refugees after they have been admitted. This structural duality lies at the heart of the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*, as the parties and judges disagree about whether Congress intended these two systems to operate independently or in tandem.

## The Placement of Refugee Provisions Within the Immigration and Nationality Act Structure

Understanding *Pacito* requires locating the relevant statutory provisions within the broader INA framework. The INA is codified in Title 8 of the U.S. Code, beginning at section 1101. The provisions at issue in this case span different sections of Title 8, reflecting the two-track system Congress created.

The admission-related provisions appear in Chapter 12 of Title 8, which governs "Immigration and Nationality." Specifically, 8 U.S.C. § 1182 enumerates classes of aliens ineligible for visas or admission, while § 1185 governs entry and departure controls. Section 1182(f), invoked by President Trump in Executive Order No. 14163, authorizes the President to suspend the entry of aliens when the President "finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States."

The resettlement provisions appear in a different subchapter altogether—Subchapter II of Chapter 12, which addresses "Immigration"—with 8 U.S.C. § 1522 specifically addressing "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." This structural separation between admission provisions (in § 1182) and resettlement provisions (in § 1522) informs the interpretive dispute between the majority and the dissent.

## The Relationship Between Admission Provisions and Resettlement Provisions

The central statutory tension in *Pacito* concerns the relationship between § 1182(f), which governs entry suspensions, and § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services. The executive order at issue suspended "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP" pursuant to § 1182(f). The government argued that this suspension authority necessarily extended to terminating the funding for resettlement services, because refugees who cannot enter cannot receive domestic resettlement services.

The panel majority implicitly rejected this connection. While agreeing that § 1182(f) grants the President broad authority to suspend refugee admissions—a point both the majority and Judge Lee's partial dissent accepted—the majority drew a line at services for refugees already admitted to the United States. In the court's view, the statutory duty to provide resettlement services under § 1522 continues to apply to refugees who have already been admitted, regardless of whether the President has suspended future admissions under § 1182(f).

Judge Lee's partial dissent would have treated the two provisions more holistically, staying the injunctions in their entirety. In his view, because § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs" rather than "Requirement," the provision is permissive rather than mandatory. This interpretive dispute illustrates how the structural relationship between admission and resettlement provisions remains contested even within the same panel.

## Congressional Intent Regarding the Balance Between Executive Discretion and Mandatory Protections

The Refugee Act reflects Congress's attempt to balance executive flexibility in admission decisions with substantive protections for admitted refugees. This balance becomes apparent when examining the contrasting language across the statutory provisions.

Section 1182(f) uses open-ended language: the President "may" suspend entry when he "finds" that such entry "would be detrimental." As the Supreme Court observed in *Trump v. Hawaii*, this language "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions." 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018). The sole prerequisite is that the President make the requisite finding—a low threshold the Court described as "undoubtedly fulfilled" by a presidential determination.

Section 1522, by contrast, uses mandatory language alongside permissive language in ways that generated interpretive disagreement on the panel. The majority focused on the word "shall" in subsections (a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A), reading these provisions as imposing duties on the government to provide specified services. The majority noted that § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance.

Judge Lee's partial dissent emphasized the statute's title—"Authorization for programs"—and the repeated use of the word "authorized" throughout subsections (b) through (e). In his view, § 1522(a) merely sets forth "conditions and considerations" that apply *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretionary authority under the subsequent subsections. This interpretive divide demonstrates how the same statutory text can support competing readings of congressional intent regarding the balance between discretion and obligation.

## The Role of the Office of Refugee Resettlement and the State Department

The Refugee Act divides implementation responsibilities between two executive agencies, a structure the opinion discusses in analyzing the government's obligations. The Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), located within the Department of Health and Human Services, bears primary responsibility for domestic resettlement services. Section 1522 specifically directs the ORR Director to carry out the functions described in that provision.

The State Department, meanwhile, has historically administered the overseas processing of refugee applications. The organizational plaintiffs in *Pacito* held cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide "overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." This division of labor between ORR (domestic services) and State Department (overseas processing and initial reception) reflects the two-track system Congress created.

Critically, both agencies operate through cooperative agreements with private nonprofit organizations rather than providing services directly. Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) authorizes services to be provided through "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The State Department's termination of these cooperative agreements—which the government represented would be replaced by a "new resettlement agency"—triggered much of the present litigation. At oral argument, however, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers.

## How Appropriations Legislation Interacts with Statutory Mandates

The opinion highlights an important feature of refugee resettlement law: the interaction between statutory mandates and the appropriations process. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) qualifies its mandatory language with the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations." This phrase, central to the interpretive dispute, could be read either as a limit on the government's obligation (services are only required to the extent Congress has funded them) or as a trigger (once appropriations are available, the duties take effect).

The opinion notes that for fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, though this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025. The existence of some appropriation suggests that funds remain "available" within the meaning of § 1522(a)(1)(A), even if reduced from initial levels. Judge Lee's dissent, however, suggests that the "to the extent of available appropriations" language merely reinforces that § 1522 operates at executive discretion—if Congress appropriates funds, the executive may use them, but is not required to do so.

This appropriations dimension connects to a practical concern the majority raised: the government's uncertainty about its ability to provide statutorily mandated services. The court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted. This remedy suggests the majority read the appropriations clause not as defeating the statutory duty, but as ensuring the duty could be fulfilled once Congress has provided funding.

The interpretive questions the panel divides on—whether § 1522 creates mandatory obligations or permissive authorizations, whether the "shall" language overrides the "authorization" framing, and how appropriations interact with duties—demonstrate that the Refugee Act's framework continues to generate disputes about the proper balance between executive discretion and congressional mandates. Understanding this legislative framework is essential to following the merits opinion that will follow this interim order.
