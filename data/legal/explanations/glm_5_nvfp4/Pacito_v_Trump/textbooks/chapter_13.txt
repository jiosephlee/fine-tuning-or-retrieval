Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 13: # Practical Skills: Analyzing Stay Pending Appeal Motions

## How to Structure a Stay Pending Appeal Motion Under Nken Factors

When a district court issues a preliminary injunction, the losing party often seeks a stay pending appeal—a temporary order that freezes the injunction while the appellate court considers the case. The Supreme Court established the governing framework in *Nken v. Holder*, requiring courts to weigh four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The first two factors are "the most critical."

In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit applied this framework to the government's request to stay two preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of Executive Order 14163. The court's analysis demonstrates how practitioners should organize stay motions: lead with likelihood of success, because a strong merits showing can anchor the entire analysis. Here, the court concluded the government was "likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions" under § 1182(f), quoting *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that the statute "exudes deference" to presidential entry restrictions.

Critically, the court then turned to the APA claims—a separate merits question where the government's likelihood of success was less certain. The court found that "for reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow," the government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding statutory mandates under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. This split merits ruling—strong on the executive order, weaker on the funding termination—produced a partial stay, illustrating how stay motions can succeed on some issues while failing on others.

When structuring a stay motion, practitioners should address each *Nken* factor sequentially but recognize their interdependence. A powerful showing on the merits may compensate for weaker irreparable harm arguments, while significant harm to the opposing party might require an even stronger merits showing. The *Pacito* court's approach shows that courts will make nuanced, issue-by-issue assessments rather than all-or-nothing rulings.

## Strategic Considerations for Both Government and Plaintiffs in Stay Litigation

Stay litigation moves quickly and often occurs before full briefing on the merits. In *Pacito*, the timeline was compressed: the Executive Order issued January 20, 2025; the district court entered preliminary injunctions in February and March; and the Ninth Circuit had already issued multiple stay-related orders by the time of oral argument on September 3. This expedited schedule shapes strategic choices for both sides.

For the government, one strategic priority is establishing deference. The *Pacito* court emphasized that it could not engage in "'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications'" and that "'[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President "find[]" that the entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."'" By framing the appeal around broad statutory authority and executive discretion, the government positioned itself for success on the entry suspension question.

However, the government's strategy revealed a vulnerability: representations made to courts can constrain later positions. The court noted that "the government advised the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was 'actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.'" At oral argument, however, "the government advised the panel . . . that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency." This gap between representation and reality undermined the government's position on the APA claims, suggesting it could not fulfill statutory mandates even if it wanted to.

For plaintiffs, the strategic focus is preserving the status quo. Plaintiffs won preliminary injunctions below, so a stay reverses their victory. Their best arguments often focus on irreparable harm and the third *Nken* factor—injury to the parties. Here, plaintiffs included refugees who had been admitted to the United States and organizations that provided resettlement services under cooperative agreements. The court's order partially accommodated these interests by directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

A key strategic lesson: parties should anticipate that stay orders may create hybrid outcomes. The *Pacito* court stayed the injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below," while simultaneously directing specific performance on the cooperative agreements. Neither side got everything, but both preserved core interests for the merits appeal.

## The Importance of the Record in Demonstrating Irreparable Harm

The second *Nken* factor—irreparable injury—requires more than speculation. Courts look to the record for concrete evidence of harm that cannot be undone through monetary damages or later relief. *Pacito* illustrates how factual uncertainty in the record can drive outcomes.

On the government's side, the court accepted that enforcing the preliminary injunctions pending appeal would cause irreparable harm by requiring continued operation of a refugee admissions program the President had determined was "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court's reference to "broad discretion" conferred on the President and "domestic and foreign interests" suggests that executive branch judgments about national security and foreign policy are entitled to particular weight in the irreparable harm analysis for immigration cases.

For plaintiffs, the record revealed a critical gap: the government had terminated all cooperative agreements but had no clear plan to replace them. When asked at oral argument, the government could not confirm progress on securing new agencies to provide services. This record failure directly supported the court's order requiring reinstatement of the cooperative agreements. The court noted it was acting "in light of the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522."

Practitioners should ensure the record documents specific harms with supporting evidence. Affidavits, declarations, and documents showing the practical consequences of a stay (or its denial) give courts concrete footing. Vague assertions of harm are easily rejected. Conversely, admissions or uncertainty—like the government's inability to report progress on new contracts—can become the factual basis for court-ordered relief.

The *Pacito* order demonstrates that the record on irreparable harm is not static. Events occurring after the district court's injunction—such as representations to the court about future plans, or the failure to fulfill those plans—can become part of the appellate court's stay analysis. Practitioners should monitor developments and be prepared to update the court on material changes.

## How Courts Weigh Public Interest Arguments in Immigration Cases

The fourth *Nken* factor—public interest—takes on distinctive dimensions in immigration cases. Courts recognize that immigration policy implicates foreign relations, national security, and humanitarian concerns, often pulling in different directions.

In *Pacito*, the court's public interest analysis was succinct but telling: "The remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President, favor the United States as well." This formulation subordinates the court's independent judgment to the executive's assessment of national interest. The court did not attempt to weigh for itself whether suspending the refugee program served the public interest; instead, it deferred to the President's determination.

This deference has strategic implications. When the government argues public interest in immigration cases, it should anchor those arguments in executive branch determinations, citing the President's statutory authority and foreign policy expertise. Courts are generally reluctant to second-guess such judgments. As the court observed, citing *Hawaii*, the statutory language "exudes deference."

However, *Pacito* also shows limits to this deference. Even while deferring to the President on entry restrictions, the court still ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted. The public interest analysis thus balanced presidential discretion against competing interests: refugees legally present in the United States who needed services Congress had authorized by statute. The public interest, in other words, includes faithful execution of statutory mandates as much as deference to presidential policy judgments.

For plaintiffs, public interest arguments should focus on the harms from non-enforcement of statutory protections, the rule of law, and humanitarian interests Congress has codified. While courts may defer to the President on who may enter, they may be less deferential regarding obligations to those already admitted.

## The Relationship Between the Stay Standard and the Preliminary Injunction Standard

Stay pending appeal and preliminary injunction analyses share common elements but serve different functions. A preliminary injunction freezes proceedings pending trial; a stay pending appeal freezes a lower court order pending appellate review. This difference affects how courts apply the relevant standards.

Both analyses ask about likelihood of success on the merits. But for a stay, the question is whether the appellant is likely to succeed in overturning the order below. The *Pacito* court's merits analysis demonstrates this: it assessed whether the government would prevail on appeal of the district court's injunction. The court concluded the government was likely to succeed on the Executive Order's validity but not on the APA challenge—leading to a partial stay.

Both analyses also consider irreparable harm, but the focus shifts. For a preliminary injunction, courts ask whether the moving party will suffer irreparable harm without injunctive relief. For a stay, courts ask whether the appellant will suffer irreparable harm from enforcement of the order being appealed. In *Pacito*, the government argued it would be irreparably harmed by being forced to continue a refugee program it had determined was detrimental. The court accepted this, staying the injunctions barring Executive Order enforcement.

The relationship between the standards also affects the standard of review. The Ninth Circuit reviews preliminary injunctions for abuse of discretion but may review underlying legal conclusions de novo. This means a stay motion gives the appellate court an early opportunity to signal how it will treat legal questions on appeal. The *Pacito* court's conclusion that the government was "likely to prevail" on § 1182(f) strongly suggests the merits opinion will reach the same result on that issue.

Practitioners should recognize that a stay motion is essentially a preview of the merits appeal. Arguments that persuade the motions panel on likelihood of success often persuade the merits panel as well. Conversely, a loss on the stay motion does not end the case, but it signals an uphill battle on the merits.

## Drafting Effective Stay Orders: Lessons from the Court's Approach

The *Pacito* order provides a model for drafting stay orders that are clear, practical, and enforceable. Several drafting lessons emerge.

First, the order clearly states what is stayed and what is not. The court specified that "the district court's orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety, except as noted below." This prevents confusion about what relief remains in effect.

Second, the order provides specific direction on the unstayed portion rather than leaving implementation to speculation. The court directed the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." The order further specified that "such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This level of detail gives parties clear marching orders.

Third, the order explains the court's reasoning. The court briefly addressed each *Nken* factor, citing precedent and explaining why the factors favored a stay on the Executive Order but not on the APA claims. This reasoned approach facilitates compliance and provides a roadmap for future proceedings. Judge Lee's partial dissent illustrates how even disagreement with the outcome benefits from clear reasoning—his argument that § 1522 is an authorization, not a mandate, sets up the issue for the merits opinion.

Fourth, the order acknowledges procedural context. The court noted that briefing was complete and oral argument heard, the case was "submitted for decision," and "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course." This signals to the parties that the stay order is an interim measure, not the final word.

Finally, the order vacates prior stay-related orders, ensuring there is no conflicting authority. The court vacated the administrative stay of July 18 and the stay of March 25 "as amended on April 21 and May 9." This housekeeping prevents confusion about which order governs.
