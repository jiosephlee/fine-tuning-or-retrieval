Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 17: # Conclusion and Study Questions

## Summary of the Court's Key Holdings

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* represents a mixed outcome for the parties, with the court granting the government's request for a stay in substantial part but preserving certain protections for refugees already admitted to the United States. The holdings emerge from the court's application of the four-factor stay standard established in *Nken v. Holder*.

On the central question of Executive Order 14163's validity, the court found the government likely to succeed on the merits. The court applied *Trump v. Hawaii*'s interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), emphasizing that the statute "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power" to suspend the entry of any class of aliens he determines would be detrimental to the national interest. The court concluded that the President's finding of detrimentality satisfied the statute's "sole prerequisite" and that the executive order did not exceed any textual limit on presidential authority. Accordingly, the district court's preliminary injunction prohibiting enforcement of the admission suspension was stayed in its entirety.

However, the court reached a different conclusion regarding the government's obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority determined that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges concerning reception and placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. The court read § 1522 as creating statutory mandates for the provision of certain services, including employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors. The court found significant the government's representation that it was "actively preparing" to secure new resettlement agencies yet its inability at oral argument to confirm any progress. The court therefore directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide these mandated services to admitted refugees.

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part. He agreed that the President has authority to suspend refugee admissions but disagreed with the majority's interpretation of § 1522. In his view, the statute's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement"—signals permissive rather than mandatory language. Judge Lee argued that the "shall" language in § 1522(a) is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section," meaning the mandatory conditions apply only if the government chooses to fund programs in the first instance. He would have stayed the district court's injunctions in their entirety.

## The Broader Significance for Immigration Law and Separation of Powers

This order illustrates the continuing tension between broad executive authority over immigration and statutory constraints on executive action. The court's contrasting treatment of the admission suspension and the funding termination reveals an important doctrinal boundary: while the political branches possess sweeping power over the admission of foreign nationals, once Congress has created statutory entitlements and appropriated funds, the executive may not simply ignore those mandates.

The decision also demonstrates the limits of deference. *Trump v. Hawaii* established that courts should not conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" under § 1182(f). Yet this deference has boundaries. The majority did not extend similar deference to the government's interpretation of its obligations under § 1522. The court engaged in careful statutory interpretation of the refugee resettlement provisions, weighing the text, structure, and practical implications of the government's position. This suggests that while courts may show substantial deference to the political branches on questions of admission, they maintain their role as interpreters of statutory rights and obligations.

The procedural posture matters. This was a stay decision, applying a likelihood-of-success standard rather than issuing a final merits ruling. The court was not definitively resolving the underlying claims but rather assessing the parties' relative positions for purposes of interim relief. The court's reasoning signals where it is likely headed on the merits, but a formal opinion will follow.

## Unresolved Questions Awaiting the Merits Opinion

Several significant questions remain open. The court stated it would issue "an opinion on the merits... in due course." This merits opinion will need to address several issues left unresolved by the stay order.

First, the scope of § 1522's mandates remains uncertain. The majority and Judge Lee disagree on whether the statute authorizes or requires services. The merits opinion will need to conduct a fuller statutory analysis, potentially addressing the significance of the "to the extent of available appropriations" language and the interaction between the statute's title and its operative provisions.

Second, the relationship between the admission suspension and funding obligations requires clarification. The government's position appears to be that suspending admissions under § 1182(f) effectively nullifies any ongoing obligations under § 1522. The court's order suggests a different view: that obligations to already-admitted refugees persist regardless of changes to admission policy.

Third, the APA claims require fuller development. The stay order addressed only whether the government was likely to prevail; it did not analyze the elements of the APA claims or the appropriate remedies if violations are found.

Fourth, the practical implementation of the court's directive to reinstate cooperative agreements raises questions. The order requires reinstatement of agreements "necessary" to provide services, but does not specify which agreements satisfy this standard or how the government should proceed if the original cooperative partners are unwilling or unable to resume operations.

## Study Questions for Further Analysis

1. **Statutory Interpretation**: Judge Lee relies heavily on the title of § 1522—"Authorization for programs"—as evidence that the provision is permissive rather than mandatory. How much weight should courts give to statutory titles when interpreting operative text? Does the presence of both "authorized" and "shall" language in the same statute create an internal tension, and how should courts resolve such tensions?

2. **Deference Doctrines**: The court applies highly deferential review to the President's determination under § 1182(f), citing *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that courts cannot conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness" of the President's justifications. Yet the court does not extend similar deference to the government's interpretation of § 1522. Is this distinction consistent? What distinguishes questions that warrant deference from those that do not?

3. **The Stay Standard**: Under *Nken*, the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." In this case, the court found the government likely to succeed on one issue and unlikely to succeed on another. How does this mixed assessment affect the stay analysis? If the movant is likely to succeed on some issues but not others, should the court grant a partial stay, or must it weigh the relative importance of the different issues?

4. **The Temporal Dimension**: The court distinguishes between refugees seeking admission (whose entry the government may suspend) and refugees already admitted (to whom the government may owe ongoing obligations). What is the legal significance of this temporal boundary? Does admission create rights that the executive cannot subsequently withdraw?

5. **Separation of Powers**: The government argues that the executive has broad discretion over immigration and foreign affairs. The court accepts this as to admission but not as to funding. What does this reveal about the constitutional allocation of power between Congress and the President? Can Congress use spending statutes to constrain executive discretion in areas of presidential authority?

6. **Remedies**: The court directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements. What authority does a court have to order specific performance of government contracts? How should courts structure remedies when statutory violations continue but the government expresses uncertainty about its ability to comply?

## Suggested Additional Reading and Related Cases

- **Trump v. Hawaii**, 585 U.S. 667 (2018): The foundational case for understanding judicial deference to presidential authority under § 1182(f) and the scope of the President's power to suspend the entry of alien classes.

- **Nken v. Holder**, 556 U.S. 418 (2009): The Supreme Court's articulation of the four-factor standard governing stays pending appeal.

- **Negusie v. Holder**, 555 U.S. 511 (2009): Cited by the plaintiffs for the proposition that the Refugee Act of 1980 establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons."

- **Refugee Act of 1980**, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified at 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.): The principal statute governing refugee admissions and resettlement, including the provisions at issue in this case.

- **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)**: The provision authorizing the President to suspend the entry of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."

- **8 U.S.C. § 1522**: The provision governing domestic refugee resettlement programs and services, whose interpretation divided the panel.

- **Administrative Procedure Act**, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq.: The statute under which plaintiffs challenged the termination of refugee funding, alleging agency action contrary to law.
