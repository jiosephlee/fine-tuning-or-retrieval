Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 34: # Case Background and Procedural History

Understanding any appellate opinion requires a firm grasp of how the case arrived before the court. This chapter walks through the factual foundation and procedural timeline of *Pacito v. Trump*, introducing the executive actions that precipitated the lawsuit, the parties who brought the challenge, the legal claims they asserted, and the series of judicial rulings that preceded this Ninth Circuit order.

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked two statutory provisions—8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)—as the legal basis for suspending refugee admissions. Section 1182(f) is a broad delegation of authority to the President regarding immigration: "Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

Executive Order 14163 made the requisite "finding" under § 1182(f): the President determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Based on this finding, Section 3(a) of the order directed that refugee entry under USRAP "be suspended" pending further findings. The statutory framework matters because, as the Ninth Circuit would later observe, § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests the executive branch with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions.

## Executive Order 14169 and the Suspension of USRAP Funding

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the USRAP program. This action was purportedly taken pursuant to a different executive order—Executive Order No. 14169, entitled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." Although the funding suspension arose under a separate order, its practical effect was to halt not only the overseas processing of refugees but also the domestic resettlement services provided to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. The funding suspension thus expanded the scope of the conflict beyond the admission of new refugees to include the treatment of those already present in the country.

## The Plaintiffs: Refugees and Resettlement Organizations with Cooperative Agreements

The plaintiffs in this case fall into two categories. The first group consists of individual refugees: some were previously admitted to the United States, while others had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. The second group comprises three organizations that had entered into "cooperative agreements" with the State Department. These agreements authorized the organizations to provide two types of services: overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and resettlement services for refugees who had been admitted to the United States. The organizational plaintiffs thus stood in a direct contractual relationship with the federal government, which the government would later terminate.

## Claims Under the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, advancing two principal legal theories. First, they alleged that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980. The Refugee Act, passed as an amendment to the Immigration and Nationality Act, establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons," as the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder* (2009). The plaintiffs argued that the executive order conflicted with the statutory scheme Congress created for refugee admissions.

Second, the plaintiffs contended that the defunding of USRAP violated provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA governs the procedures by which federal agencies act and provides a mechanism for judicial review of agency actions. While the Refugee Act challenge targeted the President's authority to suspend admissions, the APA claim focused on the procedural and substantive legality of the funding termination—a distinction that would prove significant on appeal.

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions and Their Scope

The litigation moved quickly in the district court. On February 25, 2025, following an oral ruling, the district court issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of Sections 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. A preliminary injunction is an interim remedy that preserves the status quo while litigation proceeds; it requires the moving party to show a likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, and that the balance of equities and public interest favor injunctive relief.

The following day, February 26, the government responded by terminating every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States, as well as all but one of the agreements for USRAP processing support abroad. This termination came after the district court had already ruled orally against the executive order, suggesting a deliberate effort to advance the policy even while judicial review was underway.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order defining the scope of its preliminary injunction, specifying that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. Then, on March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, bringing both injunctions before the Ninth Circuit.

## Ninth Circuit Procedural History: Partial Stay, Clarifications, and Administrative Stay

Once the government appealed, it sought a "stay pending appeal"—a judicial order that suspends the effect of the lower court's injunction while the appellate court reviews the case. On March 25, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018), the panel observed that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and stayed the district court's injunction regarding refugee admissions. However, the panel denied the stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status" before January 20, 2025. This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—certain refugees with conditional approval would still benefit from the injunction during the appeal.

The Ninth Circuit issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying the scope of this carveout. The motions panel also directed full briefing and calendared the case for oral argument.

While the appeal was pending, the district court on July 14 issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee compliance. The government filed an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order on July 17. The following day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an "administrative stay"—a temporary measure preserving the status quo while the court considered the emergency motion. The court then scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis for September 3, 2025. The order we are now examining was issued on September 12, 2025, following that oral argument.
