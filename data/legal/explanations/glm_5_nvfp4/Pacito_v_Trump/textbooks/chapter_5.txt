Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 5: # The Separation of Powers Dimension

## Congressional Power versus Executive Discretion in Immigration Policy

The Pacito v. Trump order presents a fundamental separation of powers question: when Congress legislates in the immigration field, to what extent can the President override or ignore statutory commands by invoking his constitutional and statutory authority over entry and foreign affairs? The court's resolution of this question reveals a split between the President's authority to exclude noncitizens from the country and Congress's authority to mandate how the executive branch treats those already admitted.

The government's primary argument rests on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the statutory provision authorizing the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Citing Trump v. Hawaii, the panel majority acknowledged that this provision "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act." The court emphasized that under § 1182(f), the "sole prerequisite" is that the President "find" that entry of the covered aliens would be detrimental—a finding the court would not second-guess. This reasoning reflects the traditional judicial reluctance to intrude upon the political branches' authority over admission of noncitizens, a domain where executive discretion has long been considered at its zenith.

However, the court drew a critical distinction between the President's authority to suspend entry and his authority to terminate services for refugees already admitted. This distinction tracks the fundamental difference between exclusion—denying entry at the border—and treatment—determining what obligations the government owes to those within its jurisdiction. The majority found that while the President could suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f), Congress had created mandatory obligations for the treatment of admitted refugees through the Refugee Act of 1980, specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The separation of powers analysis thus turned on whether Congress, through legislation, had constrained executive discretion in a manner that the President could not override through executive order.

## The Role of Appropriations in Determining Executive Obligations

A secondary but significant separation of powers question concerns the relationship between congressional appropriations and executive obligations. The majority opinion noted that § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to perform certain functions "to the extent of available appropriations." The court also observed that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, though this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025.

The presence of appropriations language in the statute introduces complexity into the separation of powers analysis. On one hand, appropriations represent Congress's power of the purse—its constitutional authority to control federal spending. On the other hand, the "to the extent of available appropriations" language might suggest that Congress made the executive's obligations contingent on funding, thereby giving the executive discretion to decline services by manipulating funding mechanisms. The majority's order implicitly rejected this interpretation, treating the appropriations language as addressing the scope of services rather than their existence. The court ordered the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide services, suggesting that Congress's appropriation of funds created a corresponding duty to actually provide the services the statute describes.

Judge Lee's dissent took a different view of the appropriations question. In his reading, § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signaled that Congress was merely permitting rather than mandating executive action. The dissent emphasized that authorization is "permissive, not mandatory," defining it as "permission to do something." Under this interpretation, the existence of appropriations does not create an obligation to spend; rather, it creates permission to spend if the executive chooses to operate the programs. This reading would preserve maximum executive discretion, treating congressional appropriations as enabling rather than constraining executive action.

## Judicial Competence to Review National Security and Foreign Policy Determinations

The court's approach to the President's national security justifications illuminates another dimension of separation of powers: the institutional competence of courts to review executive determinations in sensitive domains. The majority quoted Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" when the President acts under § 1182(f). This deference reflects judicial recognition that national security and foreign policy determinations involve considerations—diplomatic relations, intelligence assessments, geopolitical strategy—for which courts lack both expertise and institutional legitimacy.

The court's refusal to question the President's finding that refugee entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States" exemplifies the political question doctrine's practical operation, even when not formally invoked. The court treated the President's determination under § 1182(f) as effectively unreviewable, requiring only that the statutory prerequisite—a presidential finding—be satisfied. The majority thus deferred to the executive branch on the admission suspension while simultaneously asserting judicial authority to enforce statutory mandates for treatment of admitted refugees.

This bifurcated approach suggests a judicial theory of separated spheres: the political branches have plenary authority over admission decisions, but once Congress legislates how the government must treat persons within U.S. territory, the judiciary has an obligation to enforce those statutory commands. The court thus navigated between two separation of powers extremes—total judicial abstention and aggressive judicial oversight—by deferring where executive authority is constitutionally and statutorily at its peak, while asserting judicial authority where Congress has created justiciable legal standards.

## The Debate over Whether Courts Can Mandate Executive Branch Program Operations

The heart of the separation of powers dispute in Pacito v. Trump centers on whether a court can order the executive branch to operate a statutory program that the executive wishes to terminate. The majority's order directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements represents a significant assertion of judicial authority. The court did not merely prohibit executive action; it affirmatively required the executive to enter into or reinstate contracts with private organizations to provide federally funded services.

The government's uncertainty about its ability to provide services appears to have influenced the majority's decision. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing new agencies to provide reception and placement services, despite having represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals." This practical gap between the government's stated intentions and its actual capacity to fulfill statutory obligations created a factual basis for judicial intervention. The court framed its order as ensuring that the government could meet its statutory duties, not as usurping policy-making authority.

Judge Lee's dissent articulated a competing institutional perspective. He argued that Section 1522 "authorizes the executive branch to craft programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow" but "does not require that those programs operate." In his view, "the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services," and therefore the court was "in no position to mandate them." This position reflects a broader theory of executive discretion: when Congress authorizes but does not mandate executive action, courts lack any basis to compel the executive to act. The dissent thus raised a foundational question about the nature of statutory obligations and the judicial role in enforcing them.

## The Practical Implications of Ordering Reinstatement of Terminated Cooperative Agreements

The court's remedy—ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements—raises practical separation of powers concerns about judicial management of executive operations. Cooperative agreements are a specific type of legal instrument used by federal agencies to accomplish public purposes in collaboration with non-governmental partners. Unlike contracts, which are used when the agency's principal purpose is to acquire goods or services, cooperative agreements involve substantial agency involvement in the funded activity. By ordering reinstatement, the court effectively directed the State Department to restore particular contractual relationships with particular organizations.

This remedial order intersects with separation of powers concerns in several ways. First, it intrudes on executive discretion over procurement and contracting decisions—decisions traditionally committed to agency expertise. Second, it requires the executive to spend appropriated funds in a specific manner, potentially conflicting with the executive's interpretation of its statutory discretion. Third, it creates ongoing judicial supervision of executive branch performance, as the court's reference to "usual and customary services" suggests that future disputes about service adequacy may return to the court.

The government's response to this order—whether it can effectively reinstate agreements, whether the organizations can resume operations, whether funding mechanisms can be restored—will test the practical limits of judicial authority. If the executive refuses or claims inability to comply, the court may face the question of whether it can enforce its order through contempt sanctions or other coercive measures—a scenario that would intensify the separation of powers conflict.

## The Broader Significance of the Majority-Dissent Split on Statutory Interpretation Methodology

The disagreement between the majority and dissent reflects a deeper methodological divide in statutory interpretation that carries separation of powers implications. The majority focused on operative statutory language: the use of "shall" in § 1522(a) and specific subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A). Under traditional canons of interpretation, "shall" creates mandatory obligations, while "may" creates discretionary authority. The majority's reading treated these textual commands as binding on the executive, regardless of the broader statutory context.

The dissent employed a competing interpretive methodology, beginning with the statute's title—"Authorization for programs"—and reading the operative language in light of this framing device. Judge Lee argued that the title signals permissive rather than mandatory intent and that the "authorized" language in subsections (b) through (e) confirms that Congress granted discretion rather than imposed duties. The dissent also employed structural reasoning, arguing that § 1522(a)'s "shall" language is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section"—meaning that the mandatory conditions apply only if the government chooses to provide assistance in the first instance.

This methodological split has separation of powers dimensions because different interpretive approaches allocate power differently between the branches. A text-focused approach that emphasizes "shall" as mandatory tends to enforce congressional constraints on executive discretion. An approach that emphasizes statutory titles, structure, and authorization language tends to preserve executive flexibility. The majority's methodology expanded judicial authority to enforce statutory obligations against the executive, while the dissent's methodology would have preserved executive discretion and limited judicial intervention.

The practical stakes of this methodological disagreement extend beyond this case. Future disputes over the executive's obligations under authorization statutes—across immigration, environmental law, social welfare programs, and other domains—will depend on which interpretive approach courts adopt. The Pacito v. Trump order, though interim, signals that at least some courts will read seemingly discretionary authorization statutes as creating enforceable obligations when combined with mandatory language and congressional appropriations, a position with significant implications for the balance of power among the three branches.
