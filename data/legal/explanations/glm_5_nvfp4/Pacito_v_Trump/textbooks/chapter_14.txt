Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 14: # Comparative Analysis: This Case and Trump v. Hawaii

This chapter examines how the Ninth Circuit relied upon and distinguished the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). Understanding this relationship is essential to reading the opinion, because the court explicitly anchored its reasoning in *Hawaii*'s interpretation of the presidential authority at issue. At the same time, the court recognized limits to what *Hawaii* resolved, leading to a partial stay rather than a complete victory for the government.

## The Factual Similarities and Differences: The Travel Ban versus Refugee Admissions Suspension

Both *Hawaii* and this case involved executive orders issued by President Trump that invoked statutory authority to restrict the entry of non-citizens into the United States. In *Hawaii*, the President issued Executive Order No. 13,780, which suspended entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. In this case, Executive Order No. 14163 suspended the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). Both executive orders rested primarily on the President's authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which permits the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."

The court noted this common statutory foundation explicitly, quoting *Hawaii*'s observation that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." This deferential standard from *Hawaii* carried significant weight in the court's analysis of the suspension of refugee admissions.

A critical difference between the cases, however, lay in what the government did beyond suspending entry. In *Hawaii*, the challenged action was the entry suspension itself. Here, the government not only suspended refugee admissions but also terminated all funding for domestic resettlement services—cutting off support for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This additional action created a separate legal question that *Hawaii* did not address. The court thus had to determine whether *Hawaii*'s reasoning about entry restrictions extended to the termination of statutorily mandated services for those already admitted.

## How the Ninth Circuit Applied the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The Ninth Circuit applied *Hawaii*'s deferential standard to uphold the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions. Quoting *Hawaii* directly, the court stated that it could not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The court emphasized that "the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" Finding that the President had "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement," the court concluded the government was likely to succeed on the merits of this portion of the challenge.

The court also adopted *Hawaii*'s characterization of the statutory text, noting that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear" and that the executive order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." This language mirrors *Hawaii*'s holding that § 1182(f) grants the President broad discretion to impose entry restrictions. The court thus treated *Hawaii* as controlling precedent on the question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f).

However, the court did not extend this deference to the separate question of domestic resettlement services. The court distinguished between the President's authority to control entry—which *Hawaii* addressed—and statutory obligations that attach after admission. This distinction reveals an important boundary to *Hawaii*'s reach: the "exudes deference" standard governs entry decisions, but it does not automatically sweep in all subsequent governmental actions related to the affected class of aliens.

## The Absence of Constitutional Claims in This Case versus the Establishment Clause Claim in Hawaii

A significant difference between the two cases is the nature of the legal challenges. In *Hawaii*, the plaintiffs raised a constitutional claim under the Establishment Clause, arguing that the travel ban was motivated by anti-Muslim animus. The Supreme Court's majority opinion rejected this claim, holding that the President had provided sufficient justification for the proclamation and that the court should not inquire into the President's motives.

This case did not involve an Establishment Clause challenge or any other constitutional claim. The plaintiffs' challenge was purely statutory: they argued that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of USRAP violated the Administrative Procedure Act. The absence of constitutional claims simplified the analysis in one sense—there was no need to apply the heightened scrutiny that might accompany a claim of religious discrimination. But the statutory framework presented its own interpretive difficulties.

Without a constitutional claim, the court's analysis turned entirely on statutory construction: what did § 1182(f) permit, and what did 8 U.S.C. § 1522 require? The court could not rely on the political question or justiciability doctrines that sometimes limit judicial review of executive actions in the immigration context. Instead, the court had to interpret specific statutory provisions—a task that led it to different conclusions on different parts of the challenge.

## The Statutory Basis: § 1182(f) in Both Cases

Section 1182(f) was the pivotal statutory provision in both *Hawaii* and this case. The statute provides:

> "Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

In *Hawaii*, the Supreme Court held that this language "exudes deference" and grants the President "ample power" to suspend entry. The Court emphasized the breadth of the phrase "any class of aliens" and the lack of any limiting principle beyond the President's finding of detrimental impact.

The Ninth Circuit applied this interpretation directly. The court quoted *Hawaii*'s statement that § 1182(f) vests the President with authority "to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." The court concluded that the suspension of refugee admissions fell squarely within this authority. The President had made the required finding—that entry of refugees under USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—and the statute imposed no other substantive limitation on that exercise of discretion.

This portion of the opinion demonstrates how a precedent interpreting statutory authority can constrain later courts. The Ninth Circuit treated *Hawaii*'s interpretation of § 1182(f) as binding, even if it might have reached a different conclusion as an original matter. The court's duty was to apply the Supreme Court's construction, not to reexamine whether that construction was correct.

## Whether Hawaii Resolved the Question of Mandatory Services under § 1522

A central question in reading this opinion is whether *Hawaii* resolved the issue of domestic resettlement services. The Ninth Circuit concluded that it did not. *Hawaii* addressed the President's authority to suspend entry; it did not address any statutory obligations to provide services to refugees who had already been admitted.

The court analyzed 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which provides for domestic resettlement services, independently of *Hawaii*. The court cited several provisions within § 1522 that use mandatory language ("shall") and impose specific obligations on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement. For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary of Health and Human Services to develop support projects for recently resettled refugees.

Judge Lee's partial dissent challenged this interpretation. Judge Lee emphasized that the title of § 1522 uses the word "Authorization"—which she defined as permissive rather than mandatory. She argued that the mandatory language in subsection (a) is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section," meaning that the obligations attach only if the government chooses to provide assistance. In her view, the statute authorizes but does not mandate funding.

This interpretive disagreement illustrates an important point about precedent: *Hawaii* did not answer every question about the scope of executive power over refugee policy. The Supreme Court in *Hawaii* resolved the validity of entry suspensions under § 1182(f), but it did not interpret the separate provisions governing domestic resettlement services. The Ninth Circuit thus had to engage in independent statutory construction on this issue, leading to a different outcome than the government sought.

## What Hawaii Did and Did Not Decide about the Scope of Presidential Authority over Refugees

Understanding the scope of *Hawaii*'s holding is essential to reading this opinion. *Hawaii* decided that the President has broad authority under § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of any class of aliens, including the nationals of designated countries. The Court held that courts should not second-guess the President's finding that entry would be detrimental to the national interest.

*Hawaii* did not decide several questions that arose in this case. First, it did not address whether the President may suspend entry of refugees specifically, though the Ninth Circuit had little difficulty extending *Hawaii*'s reasoning to that context. Second, and more importantly, *Hawaii* did not address whether statutory obligations to provide services persist even after the President suspends entry of additional members of the class.

The court thus bifurcated its analysis. It applied *Hawaii* to uphold the admission suspension, but it looked elsewhere—to the text and structure of § 1522—to assess the funding termination. This approach reflects a basic principle of precedent: courts apply controlling authority to the questions it resolved and engage in independent analysis on questions left open.

The outcome was a partial stay of the district court's injunction. The court stayed the portions of the injunction that blocked the admission suspension, giving the government a victory on that issue. But the court declined to stay the portions of the injunction that required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for resettlement services—services that the court found were statutorily mandated for refugees already admitted. The court even ordered the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services, a remedy that went beyond simply denying the stay.

This split result illustrates how a single case can both follow and distinguish a precedent. The Ninth Circuit followed *Hawaii* where it found *Hawaii* controlling but recognized that *Hawaii*'s reasoning about entry restrictions did not extend to post-admission obligations. The opinion thus teaches students to read precedents carefully, identifying not only what they hold but also what they leave unresolved.
