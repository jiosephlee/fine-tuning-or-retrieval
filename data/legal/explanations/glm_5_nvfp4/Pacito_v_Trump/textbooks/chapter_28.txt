Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 28: # Judge Lee's Partial Dissent and Separation of Powers Implications

## Judge Lee's Agreement on Presidential Authority Over Admissions

Judge Lee's partial dissent begins with an important point of consensus: he agrees with the majority that the President possesses broad authority to suspend refugee admissions. This agreement reflects the Supreme Court's holding in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which established that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and grants him "ample power to impose entry restrictions" beyond those specifically enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act. Judge Lee would therefore stay the district court's injunction in its entirety as it relates to the admission of refugees.

This portion of the dissent demonstrates a fundamental principle of immigration law: the political branches—and particularly the President—wield near-plenary power over admissions decisions. Judge Lee recognizes that courts "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications'" for entry suspensions. Once the President makes the requisite finding under § 1182(f) that entry of a class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States," judicial review is highly constrained. The majority and dissent thus align on the scope of presidential power over the border.

The split emerges solely on the question of domestic resettlement services—what happens to refugees already admitted or in transit when the suspension takes effect. This division transforms the case from a challenge to presidential admissions authority into a dispute over statutory interpretation and the judicial role in enforcing statutory mandates against the executive branch.

## The Dissent's Statutory Interpretation Methodology

Judge Lee's dissent employs a systematic approach to statutory interpretation that begins with the text's structure and proceeds through its provisions in order. His methodology offers a clear framework for understanding how courts parse ambiguous statutory language.

The dissent opens with a textualist observation: the title of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee emphasizes the word "authorization," defining it through Black's Law Dictionary as "permission to do something." Under this reading, the statute permits the executive branch to act but does not compel action. This starting point reflects the canon that statutory titles can inform interpretation, though they are not controlling.

Judge Lee then surveys the operative provisions of § 1522, noting that subsections (b) through (e)—which actually provide funding for different refugee services—all use permissive language. These provisions state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance for initial resettlement, cash assistance, medical assistance, and other services. The repeated use of "authorized" rather than "shall" or "must" signals congressional intent to grant discretion rather than impose obligations.

The majority had relied on § 1522(a), which employs mandatory language: the Director "shall" make available resources for employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Judge Lee counters by examining the provision's qualifying phrase: "In providing assistance under this section." He argues this language conditions the mandatory obligations on the government's decision to provide assistance in the first instance. In other words, § 1522(a) specifies what the government must do *if* it chooses to fund refugee programs—but it does not require that funding to occur.

This interpretive dispute illustrates a common scenario in administrative law: determining whether "shall" language creates an individual entitlement or simply structures executive discretion. Judge Lee reads the statutory scheme as creating a conditional mandate—the government has discretion whether to operate resettlement programs, but if it does so, it must meet certain conditions. The majority reads the same provisions as creating affirmative obligations to provide services to refugees who have been admitted, given that Congress has appropriated funds for that purpose.

Judge Lee also addresses the majority's invocation of specific subsections dealing with unaccompanied minors and interim support. He notes that § 1522(d)(2)(B), which requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children "if necessary," still invokes executive discretion through the necessity qualifier. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A)'s command that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" is tempered by the phrase "as needed"—leaving the determination of need to executive judgment.

## The Separation of Powers Implications of Judicial Mandates on Executive Programs

The heart of Judge Lee's dissent concerns the proper role of the judiciary in ordering the executive branch to administer statutory programs. This question implicates core separation of powers principles that animate much of administrative law.

When a court orders an executive agency to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements and provide specific services, it intrudes upon the executive's constitutional role to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." U.S. Const. art. II, § 3. Judge Lee's dissent can be read as a warning against judicial overreach: if Congress did not clearly mandate the provision of services, the court has no authority to impose such a requirement through an injunction.

This concern reflects the "clear statement" principle that runs through separation of powers jurisprudence. When a statute is interpreted to constrain executive discretion in significant ways, courts often demand textual clarity. Judge Lee's interpretation—reading § 1522 as permissive—preserves maximum executive flexibility. The majority's reading—finding mandatory obligations—transforms judicial power into a tool for compelling executive action.

The appropriations context heightens these separation of powers concerns. Congress appropriated funds for refugee assistance, but appropriations alone do not mandate expenditure. Under the Constitution's Appropriations Clause, no money can be drawn from the Treasury "but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 7. However, appropriations typically authorize rather than require spending—they permit the executive to spend up to a certain amount, leaving discretion about whether and how to use those funds. The majority implicitly reads the Refugee Act as creating a statutory entitlement to services, converting appropriated funds into mandatory spending. Judge Lee resists this conversion.

The dissent also raises practical concerns about judicial competence. Courts lack expertise in managing refugee resettlement programs, negotiating cooperative agreements with service providers, or balancing the many factors that inform executive decisions about resource allocation. By ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements, the majority effectively assumes management authority over an executive program—a role for which courts are ill-suited.

## The Practical and Institutional Consequences of the Majority-Dissent Split

The division between the majority and Judge Lee produces two sharply different outcomes with real-world consequences for refugees, resettlement organizations, and the executive branch.

Under the majority's approach, the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. This provides continuity of services for vulnerable populations who have already been through the admissions process and are legally present in the country. Resettlement organizations can continue their work, at least pending the full merits decision. Refugees receive the support services—employment training, English instruction, case management—that facilitate integration and self-sufficiency.

Under Judge Lee's approach, the district court's injunctions would be stayed entirely, meaning the executive order suspending admissions and the funding termination would both go into effect. The President's policy judgment about refugee admissions and services would govern without judicial interference. This preserves executive flexibility and avoids what Judge Lee sees as judicial commandeering of executive functions.

The split also signals deeper institutional tensions that the full opinion must address. The motions panel that first heard this case created a "limited carveout" protecting refugees who had been conditionally approved for admission before January 20, 2025. The majority's order extends further, mandating services for those already admitted. This progression suggests the court is struggling to identify where presidential authority ends and statutory obligations begin.

For the law student, this case illustrates how preliminary injunctions and stays operate as battlegrounds for substantive disputes. The court's stay order—which is supposed to be a temporary measure preserving the status quo pending appeal—effectively resolves the merits of the APA claim regarding resettlement services. Judge Lee's dissent highlights this anomaly: by determining that the government is "not likely to prevail" on the APA claims, the majority has prejudged the merits before full briefing and argument on those issues.

The practical outcome reflects this preliminary posture. The court orders reinstatement of cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This narrow tailoring attempts to minimize judicial interference while still enforcing what the majority sees as statutory obligations. The order defers to the executive branch to determine which cooperative agreements are necessary and what services fall within "usual and customary" practice—preserving some executive discretion within the judicial mandate.

Judge Lee's dissent serves as a reminder that even interim orders can have lasting consequences. The cooperative agreements must be reinstated. Services must be provided. Organizational plaintiffs must resume operations. These practical effects may be difficult to reverse if the full opinion ultimately sides with Judge Lee's interpretation. The case thus demonstrates how the timing and sequencing of judicial intervention can shape outcomes as much as the legal reasoning itself.
