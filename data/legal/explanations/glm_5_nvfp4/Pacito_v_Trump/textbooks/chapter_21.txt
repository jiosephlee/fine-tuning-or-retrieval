Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 21: # The Dispute Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligation or Discretionary Authorization?

## Overview of the Refugee Act of 1980 and § 1522

The Refugee Act of 1980 represents Congress's comprehensive statutory framework for the treatment of refugees and displaced persons. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." At the heart of this case lies a fundamental question of statutory interpretation: does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 require the executive branch to provide resettlement services to refugees, or does it merely permit such services at the government's discretion?

Section 1522 governs domestic resettlement assistance for refugees who have been admitted to the United States. The statute addresses how the federal government provides what are commonly called "reception and placement" services—support designed to help refugees achieve economic self-sufficiency, acquire English language skills, and access cash and medical assistance during their initial resettlement period. These services may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies, a structure the United States has historically employed by partnering with resettlement organizations.

The interpretive dispute arises from competing readings of the statute's text and structure. The majority and dissent approach the same statutory provisions with different methodological emphases—one focusing on mandatory language within specific subsections, the other on the permissive framing of the provision as a whole. This chapter examines how each side constructs its argument and what tools of statutory interpretation each employs.

## The Majority's Interpretation: 'Shall' Language Creates Mandatory Obligations

The majority concludes that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges because § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations. In reaching this conclusion, the panel emphasizes the statute's use of the word "shall"—the traditional language of statutory command.

The court's analysis begins with the principle that when Congress uses "shall," it ordinarily imposes a duty rather than granting discretion. The majority reads § 1522 as establishing binding obligations on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, an office within the Department of Health and Human Services. Under this reading, the government cannot simply decline to provide the services Congress has specified; the statute makes those services a legal entitlement for refugees who meet the eligibility criteria.

This interpretation matters because it determines whether the executive branch's termination of cooperative agreements violated governing law. If § 1522 creates mandatory obligations, then the government's failure to provide reception and placement services through any mechanism—whether existing cooperative agreements or new contracts—would constitute a statutory violation. The majority's reasoning thus sets the stage for its remedial order directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the mandated services.

## The Majority's Reliance on Specific Subsections (a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The majority builds its interpretation on several specific textual provisions within § 1522. First, the court cites § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director "shall"—"to the extent of available appropriations"—make available resources for employment training, provide English language training, ensure cash assistance is available, and guarantee women equal participation opportunities. The majority appears to read this language as creating affirmative duties, with the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" establishing the funding condition rather than converting the obligations into discretionary options.

The court further relies on § 1522(d)(2)(B), addressing unaccompanied refugee minors. This provision states that during interim periods before placement, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." The mandatory "shall" coupled with financial responsibility suggests to the majority that Congress created a non-discretionary duty in at least some circumstances.

Finally, the majority points to § 1522(e)(7)(A), which provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months." This language directs the Secretary to act, suggesting another mandatory obligation. The majority also notes that Congress has appropriated substantial funds—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million—for migration and refugee assistance, indicating that appropriations exist to support these statutory obligations.

The majority observes that the government represented it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" for a new resettlement agency but then could not confirm at oral argument whether any progress had been made. This practical uncertainty about the government's ability to provide statutorily mandated services reinforces the court's conclusion that the government is unlikely to prevail on the APA challenge concerning funding termination.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: The Significance of 'Authorization' in the Section Title

Judge Lee opens his partial dissent with a different starting point: the statute's title. Section 1522 is entitled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." For Judge Lee, the word "authorization" carries decisive interpretive weight. Citing Black's Law Dictionary, he notes that authorization means "permission to do something"—permissive language, not mandatory command.

This approach reflects a standard principle of statutory interpretation: courts should consider the statute's title as part of the interpretive inquiry. While section titles cannot override clear statutory text, they can inform how ambiguous provisions should be read. Judge Lee argues that Congress labeled this provision an authorization, signaling its intent to permit rather than require the described programs.

Judge Lee further notes that the specific funding provisions in § 1522(b) through (e)—including the "Program of initial resettlement" and "Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees"—all state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. The repeated use of "authorized" rather than "required" or "shall" in these operative funding subsections reinforces his view that Congress granted discretion rather than imposed duties. This pattern of permissive language throughout the section's substantive provisions forms the foundation of the dissent's structural argument.

## The Dissent's Structural Argument: § 1522(a) Imposes Conditions If Funding Is Provided

The dissent's central structural claim is that § 1522(a) does not independently mandate services but instead establishes "conditions and considerations" that apply if the government exercises its discretion to fund refugee programs. Judge Lee reads the phrase "In providing assistance under this section" as qualifying everything that follows in § 1522(a). Under this reading, the "shall" language operates only once the government chooses to act under the discretionary funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e).

To illustrate this interpretation, Judge Lee offers an example: if the government decides to fund employment training under § 1522(c), then § 1522(a) requires that such training be designed "to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible." The mandatory language thus governs how programs operate, not whether they must operate. This reading preserves the "authorization" framework of the statute's title while giving effect to the "shall" language in § 1522(a).

Judge Lee also addresses the specific subsections the majority relies upon. He argues that § 1522(d)(2)(B), dealing with unaccompanied minors, imposes responsibility on the Director only when "necessary"—a condition that invokes executive judgment. Similarly, he reads § 1522(e)(7)(A)'s requirement that the Secretary "develop and implement" projects as leaving the "metes and bounds" of such programs to executive discretion. Moreover, he questions whether any unaccompanied refugee children are even at issue in this case, suggesting the provision may be irrelevant to the present dispute.

## The Practical Implications of Each Interpretation for Executive Discretion

The interpretive dispute carries significant consequences for the separation of powers and executive discretion in immigration policy. Under the majority's reading, Congress has created enforceable statutory entitlements for refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Once refugees lawfully enter the country, the executive branch must provide certain reception and placement services, subject only to available appropriations. The government cannot terminate funding mechanisms without ensuring alternative means of delivering these mandatory services.

Under Judge Lee's reading, by contrast, Congress has granted the executive branch broad discretion over whether to provide domestic resettlement services at all. The President, having determined that continued refugee admissions would be detrimental to national interests, may suspend not only entry but also the support services that accompany resettlement. The statute's authorization framework, in this view, permits but does not compel executive action.

The practical stakes are reflected in the court's order. The majority directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. Judge Lee would stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, leaving the executive branch free to terminate both admissions and services pending appeal. The difference between these outcomes turns entirely on whether § 1522 speaks in the language of obligation or permission—and whether courts or the political branches decide what services, if any, admitted refugees must receive.
