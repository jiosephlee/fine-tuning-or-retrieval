Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 30: # Presidential Authority Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

## Text and Scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The principal statutory authority invoked by Executive Order No. 14163 is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision that has become central to debates over the scope of presidential power over immigration. The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* provides an opportunity to examine how this statute operates and how courts interpret its broad language.

Section 1182(f) provides in relevant part that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The Ninth Circuit's recitation of this language highlights several structural features that shape its interpretation.

First, the statute is triggered by a presidential "find[ing]"—a determination that entry of a particular class of aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests. Second, the statute grants the President authority to act "by proclamation," suggesting a formal executive action. Third, the President may act "for such period as he shall deem necessary," indicating temporal discretion. Fourth, the President may "suspend the entry" of aliens or "impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

The breadth of this language is striking. The statute refers to "any aliens or any class of aliens," permitting the President to act categorically rather than on an individualized basis. The phrase "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate" confers substantial discretion over the substance of those restrictions. Importantly, the statute operates as an additional grant of authority—the Ninth Circuit noted that it "vests [the President] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]."

In *Pacito*, President Trump invoked this authority to suspend the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). The Executive Order determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The legal question before the Ninth Circuit was whether this invocation of § 1182(f) was valid and, more immediately, whether the government was likely to succeed on the merits of that claim for purposes of a stay pending appeal.

## Trump v. Hawaii and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard of Review

The governing precedent for evaluating presidential action under § 1182(f) is the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). The Ninth Circuit's analysis is deeply informed by this decision, and understanding *Hawaii* is essential to understanding the court's reasoning in *Pacito*.

*Trump v. Hawaii* arose from Presidential Proclamation No. 9645, which restricted entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. The proclamation was issued under the authority of § 1182(f), among other statutory provisions. Challengers argued that the proclamation exceeded the President's statutory authority and violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.

The Supreme Court upheld the proclamation. Writing for the majority, Chief Justice Roberts articulated a highly deferential standard of review for presidential actions under § 1182(f). The Court held that the statute "exudes deference" to the President and emphasized that the text vests the President with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions. This language—"exudes deference"—became a touchstone for the Ninth Circuit's analysis in *Pacito*.

The *Hawaii* Court explained that § 1182(f) grants the President authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens he finds detrimental to U.S. interests. The Court rejected the argument that the statute should be read more narrowly, holding that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear" and that the proclamation did "not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." This clear-statement approach means that courts will not read implicit limitations into the statute's broad grant of power.

Critically, the *Hawaii* Court addressed the scope of judicial review over presidential findings under § 1182(f). The Court held that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This formulation establishes a narrow role for judicial scrutiny of the President's "detrimental" finding. Courts will examine whether the President has made the required finding, but they will not second-guess the substantive basis for that finding or probe the adequacy of the President's reasoning.

The Ninth Circuit's stay order in *Pacito* applies this framework directly. Quoting *Hawaii*, the court stated that it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" The court treated *Hawaii* as establishing both the governing standard—extreme deference—and the proper methodology—look to the text of the statute rather than the quality of the executive's reasoning.

## The President's 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests' Finding and Its Sufficiency

The Ninth Circuit's analysis of the President's "detrimental" finding illustrates how *Hawaii* operates in practice. The court focused on whether the President satisfied the statutory prerequisite, not whether the finding was well-supported or persuasive.

Under § 1182(f), the "sole prerequisite" is "that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The Ninth Circuit quoted this formulation from *Hawaii* and applied it to Executive Order No. 14163. The Executive Order stated that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." This statement, the court concluded, satisfied the statutory requirement: "The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here."

Note what the court did not do. The court did not examine the factual basis for the President's determination. The court did not ask whether refugees actually pose a detriment to U.S. interests. The court did not evaluate whether the President's stated reasons were genuine or pretextual. The court simply confirmed that the President made the requisite finding, and that finding was facially sufficient under the statute.

This approach follows directly from *Hawaii*. In that case, the Supreme Court rejected the argument that courts should look beyond the proclamation's text to assess whether the President's stated national security justifications were credible. The Court held that the proclamation "is expressly premised on legitimate purposes" and that "the text says nothing about religion." The Court declined to investigate whether these stated purposes were pretextual, emphasizing that the statutory framework grants the President broad discretion.

The Ninth Circuit's reasoning in *Pacito* reflects this same approach. Once the President has made the statutory finding, the court's inquiry is essentially complete. The President has "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement" by stating that refugee entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The court's use of the word "undoubtedly" signals the deference that *Hawaii* requires.

This aspect of the decision has significant practical implications. A President seeking to suspend the entry of any class of aliens need only state that such entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The President does not need to provide detailed factual support, and challengers cannot prevail by showing that the finding is factually weak or poorly reasoned. As long as the President makes the finding, the statutory prerequisite is satisfied.

## The Limited Role of Judicial Review in Immigration and National Security

The Ninth Circuit's analysis reflects broader principles governing judicial review of executive action in the immigration and national security contexts. These principles limit the role of courts even when individual rights are at stake.

The court's reference to the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in immigration matters echoes a long-standing theme in Supreme Court jurisprudence. Immigration policy is understood to involve foreign policy and national security considerations that are committed to the political branches. Courts have traditionally been reluctant to second-guess executive judgments in these areas, recognizing that the President and Congress have institutional advantages in assessing threats and formulating responses.

The Ninth Circuit applied this principle in its analysis of the stay factors. After concluding that the government was likely to succeed on the merits of the § 1182(f) issue, the court addressed the remaining stay factors—"touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States"—by noting that these interests are determined by the "broad discretion" conferred on the President. This formulation treats the President's assessment of the public interest as controlling for purposes of the stay analysis.

The limited role of judicial review is particularly pronounced when the executive invokes national security justifications. In *Hawaii*, the Supreme Court noted that the proclamation was "expressly premised on legitimate purposes" including "the preclusion of 'detrimental' aliens" and "the 'legitimate purpose' of 'obtain[ing] additional information' about nationals of particular countries." The Court stated that it took "no issue with" the plaintiffs' claim that the President has "broad discretion" over immigration and national security matters.

The practical effect of these principles in *Pacito* is that the plaintiffs' challenge to the refugee admission suspension faced an uphill battle. The President's determination that refugee entry would be detrimental was effectively unreviewable under *Hawaii*'s standard. The court could not probe the substance of that determination or assess whether it was supported by evidence. This framework reflects a judicial posture of restraint in matters touching on immigration policy and national security—a posture that significantly advantages the executive branch in litigation over entry restrictions.

It is worth noting that this limited judicial role applies specifically to the President's authority under § 1182(f) to suspend entry. The court reached a different conclusion regarding the APA challenges to the termination of resettlement services, finding that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of those claims. The distinction illustrates that deference to executive authority has limits; while the President's entry suspension authority under § 1182(f) is broad, statutory obligations regarding services for admitted refugees may be subject to more searching judicial scrutiny.
