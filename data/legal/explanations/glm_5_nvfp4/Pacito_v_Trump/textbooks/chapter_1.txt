Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of refugee admissions under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The executive order invoked two statutory provisions—8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)—as the legal foundation for its directives. Section 1182(f) provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." Relying on this authority, the President determined in Section 3(a) of the executive order that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings.

The breadth of Section 1182(f) became central to the litigation. As the Ninth Circuit would later observe, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the statute "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" The executive order's suspension of refugee admissions therefore presented a significant question about the scope of presidential authority over immigration—a question the court would ultimately resolve in favor of executive power.

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of foreign aid funding affecting domestic resettlement services

On January 24, 2025, four days after Executive Order 14163, the Department of State suspended all funding of the USRAP program. This action was taken pursuant to a separate executive order—Executive Order No. 14169, entitled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." The funding suspension extended to domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This aspect of the executive action would prove legally distinct from the admission suspension. Whereas Executive Order 14163 operated under the President's authority to suspend entry, the defunding of resettlement services implicated different statutory schemes—specifically, the Administrative Procedure Act and the Refugee Act of 1980's provisions regarding services for admitted refugees.

## The plaintiffs: admitted refugees, conditionally approved refugees, and resettlement organizations with cooperative agreements

The plaintiffs in this litigation fell into two categories: individual refugees and organizational entities. The individual plaintiffs included refugees who had previously been admitted to the United States as well as refugees who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. These individuals had varying relationships with the refugee admissions system—some had already entered and begun the resettlement process, while others had cleared the approval hurdles but had not yet physically arrived.

The organizational plaintiffs comprised three entities that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department. These agreements governed two distinct functions: providing overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and providing resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. The cooperative agreement structure is the mechanism through which the federal government has historically partnered with private nonprofit agencies to carry out refugee resettlement services authorized under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The termination of these agreements following the executive orders thus formed part of the plaintiffs' injury.

## Claims under the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, advancing two principal legal theories. First, they alleged that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102, which is codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The Supreme Court has described the Refugee Act as establishing "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The plaintiffs contended that the executive order's broad suspension conflicted with the statutory framework Congress established for refugee admissions.

Second, the plaintiffs challenged the defunding of USRAP under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. The APA provides a framework for judicial review of agency actions and requires, among other things, that agency actions not be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The plaintiffs' APA claims focused on the termination of funding for resettlement services—services they argued were mandated by statute for refugees already admitted to the United States. This distinction between the admission suspension and the funding termination would prove significant, as the court would ultimately reach different conclusions on these two claims.

## District court preliminary injunctions of February 25, February 28, and March 24, 2025

The district court acted quickly in response to the plaintiffs' motion for preliminary injunctive relief. On February 25, 2025, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of Sections 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States, as well as all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order defining the scope of the February 25 preliminary injunction, specifying that it also prohibits the suspension of USRAP funding. The government filed its notice of appeal that same day. The district court then issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government filed a second notice of appeal on March 25.

The district court's July 14, 2025 order established a framework for enforcing what the Ninth Circuit had called the "limited carveout" from the stay—referring to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This order appointed a magistrate judge to oversee the enforcement framework. The government's subsequent emergency motion on July 17, 2025, sought to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order.

## Prior Ninth Circuit orders: partial stay of March 25, clarifications of April 21 and May 9, and administrative stay of July 18, 2025

The appellate litigation began with the government seeking a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part on March 25, 2025. The panel observed that Section 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions. However, the panel denied the motion for a stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay."

The motions panel issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar. Following briefing but before the scheduled September 19 argument, the district court issued its July 14 order establishing an enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate judge. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion, and on July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's July 14 enforcement order and scheduled expedited oral argument.

The September 12, 2025 order vacated the administrative stay of July 18, vacated the prior stay of March 25 as amended, and stayed the district court's February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety—except for the order directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. The court thus maintained a partial injunction regarding services for admitted refugees while staying the injunction regarding refugee admissions.
