Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 6: # Procedural Significance of Stay Orders and Preliminary Injunctions

## The Distinction Between Administrative Stays and Stays Pending Appeal

The Pacito v. Trump litigation illustrates the important procedural distinction between two types of stay orders that appellate courts employ: administrative stays and stays pending appeal. Understanding this distinction is essential for following the trajectory of this case and similar high-stakes federal litigation.

An administrative stay is a temporary, interim measure designed to preserve the status quo for a brief period while the court considers whether to grant a more durable stay pending appeal. In this case, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay on July 18, 2025, to temporarily halt the district court's July 14 enforcement order while the court scheduled expedited oral argument. The administrative stay served as a "pause button"—a way to freeze the situation without prejudging the merits of the stay request.

By contrast, a stay pending appeal is a more considered ruling that typically follows briefing and sometimes oral argument. It remains in effect throughout the pendency of the appeal unless modified or vacated. The court's March 25 order, as amended in April and May, was a stay pending appeal—it granted partial relief from the district court's preliminary injunction while the appeal proceeded. Similarly, the September 12 order constitutes a stay pending appeal (and "further order of this court") that will govern until the court issues its merits opinion.

The court's September 12 order explicitly vacates both the administrative stay of July 18 and the prior stay pending appeal of March 25 (as amended), replacing them with a new stay pending appeal. This sequence demonstrates how these mechanisms relate to each other: the administrative buy time for deliberation, while stays pending appeal provide stability during the appellate process.

## How Motions Panels Operate and Their Authority to Grant Stays

Federal appellate courts typically handle stay requests through motions panels—small groups of judges (often three) authorized to rule on procedural matters that arise outside the normal course of merits briefing. The Pacito litigation demonstrates the significant authority motions panels wield.

The March 25 stay order was issued by a motions panel, which partially granted the government's request for a stay pending appeal. Notably, this panel did not merely grant or deny the stay wholesale; it crafted a nuanced "limited carveout from the stay," preserving the preliminary injunction's protection for refugees who had been conditionally approved for status before January 20, 2025. This example shows that motions panels can engage in sophisticated, partial relief tailored to the circumstances of the case.

The motions panel also "directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar." This illustrates another function of motions panels: case management. By ordering full briefing, the panel converted what began as an emergency motion into a regular appeal subject to complete argument on the merits.

Importantly, the authority of a motions panel is distinct from that of the merits panel that ultimately decides the appeal. The September 12 order—issued after oral argument before what appears to be the merits panel—vacates and supersedes the motions panel's prior stay orders. This hierarchy reflects the principle that interlocutory rulings (like stays) remain subject to revision as the case progresses.

## The Effect of Staying a Preliminary Injunction on the Underlying Litigation

When an appellate court stays a preliminary injunction, it does not reverse or overturn that injunction—it merely suspends its effectiveness while the appeal proceeds. This distinction is crucial for understanding what happens to the underlying litigation.

In Pacito, the district court issued two preliminary injunctions: one on February 28 (prohibiting enforcement of Executive Order 14163's provisions and the suspension of USRAP funding), and another on March 24 (ordering reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements). Both injunctions blocked the government's refugee policy from taking effect.

The Ninth Circuit's stay orders fundamentally changed the practical landscape. By staying the February 28 injunction as to most refugees, the court allowed the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions to proceed during the appeal. Similarly, the September 12 order stays both district court injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below"—meaning the preliminary injunctions cannot be enforced while the stay remains in effect.

However, the litigation itself continues. The appeal is "submitted for decision," and "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course." The stay merely determines what happens during the interim period before that merits decision. The district court's injunctions remain on the books—they are simply not operative while stayed. If the Ninth Circuit ultimately affirms the district court on the merits, the stay would dissolve and the injunctions would regain their force (unless the Supreme Court intervenes).

## The 'Limited Carveout' Mechanism and Its Enforcement Through Magistrate Judges

One of the most instructive procedural features of this case is the "limited carveout" the motions panel created in its March 25 order. The panel denied the government's stay request "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025."

This created a split situation: the executive order's admission suspension was stayed (and thus could proceed) for most refugees, but the preliminary injunction remained in effect for a specific subset—those with conditional approval before the change in administration. The carveout demonstrates how courts can balance equities by protecting those with the strongest reliance interests while allowing new policy to proceed more broadly.

The carveout's implementation generated its own litigation. On July 14, the district court issued an order "to establish a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee the framework." This illustrates how district courts retain authority to implement and enforce injunctions even during a stay of other aspects. Magistrate judges—judicial officers who assist district judges with various matters—are commonly used for ongoing supervision of complex injunctions.

The government's emergency motion challenging this enforcement order, and the Ninth Circuit's administrative stay of it on July 18, shows the friction that can arise between the district court's supervisory role and the appellate court's stay authority. The September 12 order addresses this by vacating the administrative stay and requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services to already-admitted refugees—effectively resolving the enforcement dispute through the stay order itself.

## Expedited Briefing and Oral Argument Procedures in Immigration Cases

This case demonstrates the accelerated timelines that courts employ when urgent government actions are at issue. The Ninth Circuit heard oral argument on September 3, 2025, on an "expedited basis," and issued its order just nine days later. This speed is typical of immigration cases involving executive branch policies, where both the government's interest in implementing its policy and the plaintiffs' interest in maintaining protections for affected individuals demand prompt resolution.

The procedural posture here was complex: the government had filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, following the district court's two preliminary injunctions. Between the March 25 stay order and the September 12 order, the motions panel issued "two additional orders clarifying this limited carveout from the stay" (April 21 and May 9). This sequence shows how stay orders may require subsequent clarification or modification as circumstances develop or disputes arise about their implementation.

The scheduling of oral argument for September 19, 2025, before the merits panel, and the subsequent July 14 district court enforcement order, created the need for expedited consideration of the emergency motion filed on July 17. The court's willingness to schedule "oral argument in this case on an expedited basis on September 3, 2025" reflects the ongoing nature of stay disputes—they can arise at multiple points during an appeal, not just at the outset.

## The Practical Effect of the Court's Order Pending Issuance of a Full Merits Opinion

The September 12 order represents an important procedural moment: the court has ruled on the stay but has not yet issued its merits opinion. Understanding what such an order accomplishes—and what it signals—is essential for litigants and observers.

First, the order determines what happens on the ground during the appeal. The stay pending appeal means the district court's injunctions (except as modified) do not govern while the appeal proceeds. The government may implement Executive Order 14163's admission suspension, subject to the specific requirements the appellate order imposes.

Second, the order signals the court's preliminary views on the merits. The court's analysis of the stay factors—particularly whether the applicant has made a "strong showing" of likely success on the merits—offers a preview of how the court may rule. Here, the court states that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions," citing the broad deference owed to presidential determinations under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Conversely, the court indicates that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding funding for reception and placement services.

Third, the order may itself create obligations. The court's directive that "the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services" shows that stay orders can do more than simply maintain the status quo—they can impose affirmative requirements. This is particularly significant where, as here, the government represented that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" for replacement services but could not confirm progress at oral argument.

Finally, the order's statement that "An opinion will issue in due course" signals that the court's full reasoning remains forthcoming. The stay order provides the "bottom line" with abbreviated analysis; the merits opinion will address the legal questions more comprehensively. Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent, which engages substantively with the majority's interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, previews the debate that the merits opinion will need to resolve.
