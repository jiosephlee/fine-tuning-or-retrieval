Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 25: # Presidential Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

This chapter examines the court's analysis of the President's statutory authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The central question before the court was whether the government had made a strong enough showing of likely success on the merits to warrant a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction. The court concluded that the government was likely to prevail on this claim, relying heavily on Supreme Court precedent interpreting the breadth of presidential authority under the immigration laws.

## 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): Text and Scope of Presidential Authority

The statutory foundation for the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions is found at 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This provision states that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

The Ninth Circuit panel observed that this statutory language "exudes deference" to the President. The text vests the President with what the court described as "ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act." Notably, the statute imposes only one condition before the President may act: he must "find" that entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The statute does not specify what evidence must support such a finding, what procedural steps must precede it, or how recent the finding must be. The temporal dimension—"for such period as he shall deem necessary"—also lies within the President's discretion.

In this case, Executive Order No. 14163 invoked this authority directly. The President determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The executive order thus tracked the statutory language closely, invoking the precise finding that § 1182(f) requires.

## Trump v. Hawaii Precedent and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The Ninth Circuit's analysis drew heavily from the Supreme Court's decision in Trump v. Hawaii, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). In that case, the Supreme Court considered a challenge to President Trump's "travel ban" executive order, which suspended entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. The Supreme Court upheld that order, interpreting § 1182(f) as conferring broad discretionary authority on the President.

The Ninth Circuit quoted the Hawaii decision's characterization that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. This phrase captures the Court's view that the statute's text—particularly phrases like "he may deem necessary" and "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate"—signaled Congress's intent to leave substantial policy judgments to the executive branch. The Supreme Court had emphasized that the statute "vests the President with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]."

This deference has important practical consequences for judicial review. Once the President makes the requisite "finding" under the statute, courts have limited tools to question either the substance of that finding or the scope of the resulting suspension. The statutory framework essentially asks courts to verify that the President has made the finding—that he has said the magic words—and then step back from closer scrutiny.

## The Sufficiency of the President's 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests' Finding

Applying Hawaii's framework, the Ninth Circuit concluded that President Trump had "undoubtedly fulfilled" the sole prerequisite that § 1182(f) imposes. The executive order contained an explicit finding that entry of refugees under the USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court required nothing more.

This conclusion illustrates the low threshold that § 1182(f) establishes. The statute does not require the President to explain his reasoning, provide evidence, or demonstrate that the finding is supported by the record. It requires only that he make the finding. The executive order's single sentence declaring that refugee entry "would be detrimental" satisfied the statutory requirement, at least as interpreted by the Hawaii Court.

The court's analysis here is notably brief. It did not examine whether the President had articulated particular harms, whether those harms were supported by any evidence, or whether the scope of the suspension matched the nature of the concerns. The brevity of the court's discussion itself demonstrates how deferential the standard is: once the President has "found" detriment, the statutory condition is met.

## Why Courts Cannot Conduct 'A Searching Inquiry' into Presidential Justifications

A critical limitation on judicial review emerges from the Supreme Court's admonition that courts may not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The Ninth Circuit quoted this language directly from Hawaii. This principle has significant implications for how courts must approach challenges to presidential action under § 1182(f).

The prohibition on "searching inquiry" means that courts cannot second-guess the President's policy judgments or demand that he justify his determination with evidence. The court cannot ask whether the President's assessment of the national interest is correct, whether the evidence supports his concerns, or whether less restrictive alternatives might address the same problems. The court's role is limited to verifying that the President has made the statutory finding and that the executive action falls within the textual bounds of the authority conferred.

This limitation reflects both separation of powers concerns and the particular nature of immigration and national security policy. The executive branch has superior institutional capacity to assess threats to national interests, and the political branches—not the judiciary—have primary responsibility for setting admission policy. The Hawaii framework thus constrains courts to a relatively deferential posture when reviewing exercises of § 1182(f) authority.

## The Majority's Conclusion on Likelihood of Success for the Admissions Suspension

The court concluded that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions." This conclusion was straightforward under the Hawaii framework. The executive order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority" because § 1182(f) "is clear" in authorizing the President to suspend entry of "any class of aliens."

The court's analysis merged the statutory text with the deference standard from Hawaii. The President had made the requisite finding; the suspension fell within the class-based language of the statute; and therefore the executive action appeared consistent with statutory authority. The Refugee Act of 1980, which plaintiffs invoked, did not alter this conclusion—at least as the court saw it. The court made no suggestion that the Refugee Act's provisions constrained the President's § 1182(f) authority in any way.

Notably, this portion of the court's order attracted no dissent. Judge Lee concurred in the majority's conclusion regarding the admission suspension, agreeing "that the President has the authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions." The unanimity on this issue underscores how firmly Hawaii established the scope of § 1182(f) authority: even a judge who would have ruled differently on related questions accepted that the President could suspend refugee admissions under this statute.

The court's treatment of the admissions suspension claim illustrates an important principle for understanding stays and preliminary injunctions. The first Nken factor—likelihood of success on the merits—was easily satisfied here. The combination of a clear statutory finding, text that "exudes deference," and a controlling Supreme Court precedent that limits judicial inquiry made it highly likely that the government would prevail on the merits of this claim when the court reached a final decision.
