Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 19: # The Facts and Procedural History

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked two statutory provisions: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a). Section 1182(f) provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." Relying on this authority, the President determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings.

The executive order thus effected a complete suspension of refugee admissions under USRAP. The scope of this suspension—and the statutory authority cited to support it—would become central to the litigation that followed. The government would argue that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions," while plaintiffs would contend that the suspension violated the Refugee Act of 1980.

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of Foreign Aid and Domestic Resettlement Funding

Four days after Executive Order 14163, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State took additional action pursuant to a separate executive order. Executive Order No. 14169, entitled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," provided the stated basis for the State Department's suspension of all funding for the USRAP program. Importantly, this suspension was not limited to overseas operations; it encompassed funding for domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This funding suspension would give rise to a distinct legal challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act, separate from the challenge to the admission suspension itself.

The dual-track approach—suspending both admissions and funding—created two interrelated but legally distinct issues. The admissions suspension touched on the President's broad authority over entry decisions under § 1182(f), while the funding suspension implicated statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services.

## The Plaintiffs: Individual Refugees and Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs in this action fell into two categories. The first group consisted of individual refugees: some had already been admitted to the United States, while others had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. These individual plaintiffs faced direct consequences from both the admission suspension and the funding termination.

The second group comprised three organizational plaintiffs. These organizations had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to perform two essential functions: (1) overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and (2) resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. These cooperative agreements represented the mechanism through which the federal government has historically discharged its obligations to refugees. The termination of these agreements thus threatened not only the organizational plaintiffs' operations but also the services that refugees would otherwise receive.

## District Court Proceedings: Preliminary Injunctions of February 25, February 28, and March 24, 2025

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025. Their complaint advanced two principal claims: (1) that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980, and (2) that the defunding of USRAP violated provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act. The Refugee Act, as the court noted, establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons."

The district court moved swiftly. On February 25, 2025, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. The very next day, February 26, the government responded by terminating every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements for USRAP processing support abroad.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order to define the scope of its February 25 preliminary injunction. Significantly, this written order specified that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. Thus, the district court's February 28 order extended the injunction's reach beyond the admission suspension to cover the funding termination as well.

The parties' conflict escalated further. On March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction, this time ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The district court thus fashioned injunctive relief that addressed both the admission suspension and the funding termination, including the specific mechanism—cooperative agreements—through which resettlement services were delivered.

The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, challenging both preliminary injunctions.

## Ninth Circuit Proceedings: Partial Stay, Clarifications, and Administrative Stay

Once the appeals were filed, the government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit acted on this request on March 25, 2025. Quoting the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel observed that the authority underlying Executive Order 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]."

The motions panel granted the stay in part but denied it "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—refugees who had received conditional approval before the executive order's issuance would remain protected by the injunction pending appeal.

The motions panel issued two additional orders, on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar.

A new dispute arose in July 2025. On July 14, the district court issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. The government responded on July 17 by filing an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order. The plaintiffs opposed this motion. On July 18, 2025, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's July 14 enforcement order and scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis.

Understanding the distinction between different types of stays is important here. An administrative stay is a temporary measure issued by a court to preserve the status quo while the court considers whether to grant a longer-term stay pending appeal. The March 25 order had been a stay pending appeal (albeit partial), while the July 18 order was an administrative stay of the district court's enforcement framework.

## The Government's Representations Regarding New Resettlement Contracts

A notable exchange occurred during the litigation regarding the government's plans to provide resettlement services through alternative mechanisms. On March 10, 2025, the government advised the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." This representation suggested that the government recognized some ongoing obligation to provide such services and was taking steps to fulfill that obligation through new contractual arrangements.

However, at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit panel, the government's position had shifted. When asked about progress toward securing a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services, the government advised the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty became significant to the court's analysis of whether the government could meet its statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 for refugees already admitted to the United States.

The panel's final order would cite this uncertainty as part of its reasoning for directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had been admitted. The gap between the government's March representation and its uncertainty at oral argument highlighted the practical consequences of the funding termination for refugees already in the United States.
