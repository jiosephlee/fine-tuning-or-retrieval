Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 29: # Factual and Procedural Background

## Executive Orders 14163 and 14169: Suspension of Refugee Admissions and Funding

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* arises from two executive actions taken in January 2025. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a) to suspend the entry of refugees into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). Section 1182(f) provides that whenever the President finds that the entry of any class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States," he may suspend their entry or impose restrictions. Executive Order 14163 made that finding and directed the suspension of refugee admissions pending further determinations by the administration.

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP. This action was taken pursuant to a separate executive order, Executive Order No. 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." The funding suspension extended not only to overseas refugee processing but also to domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. The combination of these two orders effectively halted both the admission of new refugees and the provision of support services to those already in the country or approved for resettlement.

## The Plaintiffs: Individual Refugees and Organizational Resettlement Agencies

The plaintiffs in this litigation fall into two categories: individual refugees and organizational providers. The individual plaintiffs include refugees who had already been admitted to the United States as well as refugees who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country. These individuals had successfully navigated the refugee approval process and were at various stages of the resettlement pipeline when the executive orders halted the program.

The organizational plaintiffs are three entities that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide services under USRAP. These organizations performed two distinct functions: overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and domestic resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. Under the cooperative agreement structure, the federal government partners with private nonprofit agencies to deliver the practical assistance refugees need upon arrival, such as housing placement, employment services, and language training.

## Claims Under the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act

The plaintiffs challenged the executive actions on two primary legal grounds. First, they alleged that Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980. Enacted as Pub. L. No. 96-212, the Refugee Act established a comprehensive framework for the treatment of refugees and displaced persons. The Supreme Court has described the Act as setting forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The plaintiffs contended that the President's order conflicted with the statutory scheme Congress created for refugee admissions.

Second, the plaintiffs challenged the termination of USRAP funding under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA provides the framework for judicial review of agency actions and requires that agencies follow proper procedures when taking actions that affect legal rights. The plaintiffs argued that the funding suspension violated various provisions of the APA, presumably including requirements for reasoned decision-making and compliance with statutory mandates.

## District Court Proceedings: Preliminary Injunctions of February and March 2025

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025. The district court acted quickly on their request for preliminary injunctive relief. On February 25, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of several sections of Executive Order 14163. The following day, the government terminated every cooperative agreement for domestic reception and placement services and nearly all cooperative agreements for overseas processing support.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order to define the scope of its February 25 injunction. Significantly, the written order specified that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. This order sought to preserve the status quo by maintaining funding for refugee services while the litigation proceeded.

The district court then issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24. This order directed the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated following the February 25 ruling. Together, the February and March orders represented a comprehensive rejection of the government's position and sought to restore the refugee program to its pre-executive order status.

The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, bringing the matter before the Ninth Circuit.

## Prior Ninth Circuit Orders: Partial Stay, Clarifications, and Administrative Stay

When the government appealed the district court's orders, it sought a stay pending appeal from the Ninth Circuit. A stay is an interim measure that suspends a lower court's order while the appeal is pending. The standard for granting a stay, as later explained in the court's September order, involves four factors drawn from *Nken v. Holder*: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable injury to the applicant absent a stay, (3) substantial injury to other parties if the stay is granted, and (4) the public interest.

A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted a partial stay on March 25, 2025. Quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel observed that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." However, the panel denied the stay motion "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—refugees who had been conditionally approved before the executive order was issued could continue in the program.

The motions panel issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying the scope of this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and placed the appeal on the next available calendar for argument.

Before the scheduled argument, however, the district court issued an order on July 14 establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. The government viewed this as overstepping the Ninth Circuit's stay order and filed an emergency motion on July 17 seeking to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay. The following day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is a temporary measure that preserves the status quo while the court considers the matter. The panel then scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis for September 3, 2025.
