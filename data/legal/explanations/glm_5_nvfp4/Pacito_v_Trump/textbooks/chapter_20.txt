Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 20: # The Presidential Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

## The Text and Scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The foundation of the court's analysis of the refugee admission suspension lies in the text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision that grants the President broad authority over the entry of non-citizens into the United States. The statute provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The breadth of this language is striking: the President may act "whenever" he makes the requisite finding, for "such period as he shall deem necessary," and may suspend entry of "any class of aliens" or impose "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The statute thus confers discretionary authority that appears expansive on its face.

In this case, President Trump invoked § 1182(f) in Executive Order No. 14163, determining that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directing that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The plaintiffs challenged this suspension as violating the Refugee Act of 1980, but the court's analysis of § 1182(f) quickly foreclosed that challenge at the stay stage.

## Trump v. Hawaii and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The court's interpretation of § 1182(f) is shaped fundamentally by the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). That case, which upheld a different Trump-era travel proclamation, established the governing framework for reviewing presidential action under § 1182(f). The Ninth Circuit quoted *Hawaii* for the proposition that the statute "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" This deferential posture is not merely a policy of judicial restraint; it flows directly from the statutory text itself. When Congress authorized the President to suspend entry whenever he "deems" it necessary and to impose whatever restrictions he "may deem to be appropriate," it necessarily committed the determination to presidential discretion.

The "exudes deference" formulation signals that courts reviewing presidential action under § 1182(f) will not subject the President's determination to searching scrutiny. The statute's broad delegations of authority, combined with the President's constitutionally recognized role in foreign affairs and immigration, create a zone of discretion that courts are reluctant to police.

## The President's 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests' Finding

Central to the operation of § 1182(f) is the finding requirement: the President must determine that the entry of the covered non-citizens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States." This finding is, as the Ninth Circuit emphasized, the "sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f)." The court quoted *Hawaii* directly on this point: "The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.' The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here."

The court's analysis underscores how minimal this prerequisite is in practice. The President need not provide extensive justification or submit evidence for judicial evaluation. The finding itself—articulated in the executive order—is sufficient. Executive Order No. 14163 contained the requisite finding: the President determined that refugee entry under USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Once that finding appears in the executive action, the statutory condition is satisfied.

## Why the Court Cannot Engage in 'A Searching Inquiry into the Persuasiveness' of Justifications

Perhaps the most significant aspect of the court's analysis for understanding judicial review under § 1182(f) is its explicit rejection of any power to evaluate the quality of the President's reasoning. The court stated flatly that it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This quotation from *Hawaii* establishes a critical boundary on judicial review: courts will not second-guess whether the President's stated reasons are convincing, well-supported, or adequate. The statute does not authorize courts to assess whether the President's determination of detriment is correct or reasonable—it requires only that the President has made such a finding.

This limitation follows logically from the statutory design. If Congress had wanted courts to evaluate the merits of the President's determination, it would have provided standards for such review. Instead, Congress left the judgment entirely to the President, using language that commits the decision to executive discretion. The court recognized that the language of § 1182(f) "is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." The textual limits are essentially procedural: the President must make the finding. Substantive evaluation of whether that finding is persuasive lies beyond judicial competence under the statute.

## The Majority's Conclusion: The Government Is Likely to Prevail on This Claim

Applying these principles, the Ninth Circuit concluded that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions." This conclusion was straightforward once the court accepted the governing framework from *Trump v. Hawaii*. The President had made the required finding of detriment, the executive order fell within the textual scope of § 1182(f) authority, and the court had no authority to probe the persuasiveness of the justification.

The court's stay analysis thus favored the government on this claim. The first stay factor—likelihood of success on the merits—weighed decisively in the government's favor. The remaining factors, the court noted, "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President," also favored the government. The result was a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction insofar as it restrained the government from implementing the refugee admission suspension.

Notably, Judge Lee concurred in this portion of the order, agreeing that "the President has the authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions." Despite the general rule that the President enjoys "vast discretion and deference in immigration matters," Judge Lee would have gone further—staying the district court's injunctions "in their entirety," including the portion directing reinstatement of resettlement services. But on the admission suspension itself, the panel was unanimous: the government was likely to prevail on the merits.
