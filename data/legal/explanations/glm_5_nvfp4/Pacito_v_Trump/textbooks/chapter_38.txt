Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 38: # The Court's Order and Judge Lee's Partial Dissent

## The Order's Practical Requirements: Reinstating Cooperative Agreements for Admitted Refugees

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* produces a split outcome that partially grants and partially denies the government's request for a stay. The court stays the district court's preliminary injunctions "in their entirety" with one critical exception: the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. This remedy is both narrow and specific. It does not require the government to resume admitting new refugees, nor does it restore funding for overseas processing of refugee applications. Instead, it targets a discrete category of services for a discrete category of individuals—those refugees already physically present in the United States who are entitled to certain resettlement services under federal law.

The court's directive focuses on cooperative agreements, which are the mechanism through which the federal government has historically provided these services. The State Department had terminated all such agreements in February 2025. The majority order requires the government to reinstate those agreements specifically for the purpose of providing "the usual and customary services" afforded under prior agreements. Importantly, the court links this remedy directly to its interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the majority reads as creating statutory obligations that the government must fulfill. The practical effect is that resettlement organizations that were parties to the terminated agreements must once again receive funding to provide services to already-admitted refugees.

## The Distinction Between Refugees Already Admitted Versus Those Awaiting Admission

The court's order draws a sharp line between two categories of refugees: those who have been admitted to the United States and those who remain outside the country awaiting admission. This distinction carries significant legal weight in the court's reasoning. For refugees outside the United States, the court accepts the government's position that the President has broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend their entry. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the majority concludes that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions." The statutory prerequisite for suspension—that the President find entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—has been satisfied, and the court declines to conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

For refugees already admitted, however, the analysis shifts. Once individuals have entered the United States, they fall within the ambit of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services. The majority reads this statute as imposing affirmative obligations on the government. Thus, while the President may suspend the entry of refugees who have not yet arrived, the government cannot terminate services for those already admitted. This distinction reflects a broader principle in immigration law: the government's authority over admission is distinct from its obligations toward those already lawfully present. The court's bifurcated order reflects this dual framework—staying the injunction as to admissions while partially denying the stay as to services for admitted refugees.

## Judge Lee's Agreement with the Majority on Presidential Admissions Authority

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent begins with an area of agreement: she joins the majority in concluding that the President has the authority to suspend refugee admissions. Like the majority, she reads 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as granting the President "vast discretion" in immigration matters. She agrees that the district court "incorrectly enjoined the executive branch from implementing the President's policy decision to limit admitting refugees and providing services for them"—at least with respect to admissions. On this front, she would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety," meaning she would permit the Executive Order's suspension of admissions to take full effect.

This agreement between the majority and Judge Lee reflects the Supreme Court's reasoning in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which held that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and provides "ample power to impose entry restrictions." Both the majority and Judge Lee accept that the statute requires only that the President make a finding that entry would be detrimental, and both conclude that such a finding has been made here. The divergence between Judge Lee and the majority arises not from disagreement about presidential admissions authority, but from a fundamental interpretive disagreement about what happens after refugees are admitted—specifically, whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates mandatory or discretionary obligations.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: § 1522 as Authorization, Not Mandate

Judge Lee's dissent centers on statutory interpretation. She argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is an authorization statute, not a mandatory spending statute. Her analysis begins with the text: the statute's title is "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, she notes that "authorization" is permissive—it means "permission to do something." In her view, Congress has permitted the executive branch to create refugee resettlement programs but has not required it to do so.

Judge Lee supports this interpretation by examining the statute's structure. The provisions that actually provide funding for refugee services—subsections (b) through (e)—state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. The repeated use of "authorized" signals congressional intent to grant discretion, not impose obligations. This stands in contrast to the majority's reliance on § 1522(a), which uses the word "shall" and references providing assistance "to the extent of available appropriations." Judge Lee reads this language as conditional: if the government chooses to fund programs under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b) through (e), then it must comply with certain conditions, such as providing employment training to promote self-sufficiency. The "shall" language does not, in her view, independently mandate that programs exist.

Judge Lee also addresses the specific subsections the majority cites as imposing obligations. She reads § 1522(d)(2)(B), which concerns unaccompanied refugee children, as "dripp[ing] with deference to the executive branch" because it requires action only "if necessary." Similarly, she interprets § 1522(e)(7)(A), requiring the Secretary to develop projects for cash and medical support "as needed," as leaving the "metes and bounds" of such programs to executive discretion. Neither provision, in her view, creates a firm congressional mandate. Her interpretive approach is straightforward: permissive statutory language cannot be transformed into mandatory requirements through selective reading of conditional provisions.

## The Separation of Powers Implications of Judicial Intervention in Executive Programs

The disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee carries significant separation of powers implications. At stake is whether the judiciary can compel the executive branch to operate federal programs that Congress has authorized but not mandated. Judge Lee's dissent articulates a sharp principle: "As the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services, our court is in no position to mandate them." This statement encapsulates a separation of powers concern that runs through her analysis. If Congress has given the executive branch discretion to create and fund programs, judicial orders requiring those programs to operate intrude on executive discretion.

The majority's approach represents a competing vision of the judicial role. By interpreting § 1522 as creating obligations, the majority positions the court as enforcing congressional mandates against executive non-action. The majority points to Congress's appropriation of funds for refugee assistance as evidence that Congress intended for these programs to operate. The separation of powers question thus turns on statutory interpretation: does the statute give the executive a choice, or does it create an enforceable duty? The majority and Judge Lee reach opposite conclusions, and their disagreement reflects fundamentally different views about when courts may order the executive branch to act.

## The Broader Significance of the Interpretive Disagreement Between Majority and Dissent

The interpretive dispute in *Pacito* exemplifies a recurring tension in administrative law: the distinction between statutory provisions that authorize agency action and those that require it. This distinction matters because it determines whether agency inaction is subject to judicial review. If § 1522 merely authorizes programs, the executive branch's decision to terminate those programs falls within its discretionary authority. But if § 1522 mandates services, then the executive's failure to provide them violates statutory law and can be remedied by the courts.

The majority and Judge Lee employ different interpretive methodologies. The majority reads the statute holistically, looking to Congress's appropriation of funds and the purpose of the Refugee Act to conclude that Congress intended for services to be provided. Judge Lee adheres more strictly to textual signals—the word "authorization" in the title, the phrase "is authorized" in funding provisions—which she reads as clear indicators of discretionary intent. This methodological divide reflects broader debates in statutory interpretation about how much weight to give statutory text versus purpose, and about what presumptions apply when Congress appropriates funds for authorized programs.

The practical stakes are significant for refugees who have already been admitted and now depend on resettlement services. Under the majority's reading, these individuals have enforceable statutory rights to reception and placement services. Under Judge Lee's reading, they have no such claim; their access to services depends entirely on executive discretion. The interpretive question thus translates directly into real-world consequences for vulnerable populations. The split outcome in *Pacito*—suspending admissions while preserving services for those already admitted—represents the majority's attempt to reconcile presidential authority over entry with what it perceives as statutory obligations to those already present.
