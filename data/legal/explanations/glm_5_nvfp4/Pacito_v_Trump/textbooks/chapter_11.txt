Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 11: # The Role of Appropriations in Determining Statutory Obligations

## The 'to the extent of available appropriations' language and its legal effect

A central question in *Pacito v. Trump* is whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates mandatory obligations for the executive branch or merely authorizes discretionary programs. The answer hinges in part on the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations," which appears in § 1522(a)(1)(A). This phrase represents a common feature in federal statutory schemes: a limitation on agency action tied to the availability of funds.

The majority opinion quotes § 1522(a)(1)(A) as requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide specific services: employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women to participate in training. The statute uses the word "shall"—traditionally signaling a mandatory duty—but immediately qualifies that duty with the appropriations limitation.

For law students, this presents a classic question of statutory interpretation: does "to the extent of available appropriations" mean that Congress created a mandatory obligation subject only to the availability of funds, or does it mean that Congress left the entire matter to executive discretion? The majority and dissent reach opposite conclusions on this question.

The majority treats the appropriations clause as a funding condition rather than a discretion grant. Under this reading, Congress imposed a duty to provide services, and the only question is whether Congress has appropriated sufficient funds. The majority's order directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements assumes that § 1522 creates enforceable obligations that the government must fulfill using available appropriations.

## Congress's FY 2025 appropriation for refugee assistance and the Rescissions Act of 2025

The opinion provides specific figures for refugee assistance funding in fiscal year 2025. Congress initially appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance through Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11), which adopted funding from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147. The Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6), subsequently reduced this amount by $800 million.

This factual detail matters for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Congress did appropriate funds for refugee assistance—funds that the majority believes must be used for the services described in § 1522. Second, the rescission shows that Congress actively adjusted funding levels, which the majority implies does not eliminate statutory obligations. The opinion does not specify the remaining amount after the rescission, but the figures suggest that substantial appropriations remained available for refugee services.

The majority's reference to these appropriations serves its analytical purpose: if § 1522 creates mandatory obligations "to the extent of available appropriations," and Congress did appropriate funds, then the executive branch has a duty to provide the specified services. The rescission reduced but did not eliminate available funding.

## Whether appropriations create a floor or a ceiling for executive obligations

The dispute between the majority and dissent reflects a deeper tension in federal fiscal law: whether an appropriation creates a floor (a minimum the agency must spend) or a ceiling (a maximum the agency may spend). This distinction has significant practical consequences for executive discretion.

Judge Lee's dissent articulates the "ceiling" view: § 1522 authorizes programs but does not mandate them, and the existence of appropriations merely permits rather than requires executive action. Under this interpretation, Congress provided funding that the executive branch may use if it chooses to operate refugee programs, but Congress did not compel the executive to spend those funds.

The majority implicitly adopts a "floor" reading, at least for certain categories of refugees. By ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services, the majority treats the statute as requiring the executive to spend available appropriations on mandated services. The majority does not say that every dollar appropriated must be spent, but it does hold that the government cannot simply refuse to provide services that Congress has directed and funded.

This debate implicates fundamental separation of powers questions. The executive branch typically argues that appropriations are permissive—Congress provides resources, but the President retains discretion over how to use them. Critics of this view counter that when Congress both directs an agency to act and appropriates funds for that purpose, the executive has a duty to implement Congress's will.

## The distinction between authorized programs and appropriated funds

Judge Lee's dissent draws a sharp distinction between authorization and appropriation, a foundational concept in federal budget law. An authorization statute establishes, continues, or modifies agencies and programs. An appropriation statute provides the actual funding that agencies may spend.

The dissent emphasizes that § 1522's title uses the word "Authorization": "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, Judge Lee defines "authorization" as "permission to do something"—permissive language rather than mandatory. This textual argument supports the dissent's conclusion that Congress allowed but did not require the executive branch to establish refugee programs.

Judge Lee further notes that the specific funding provisions in § 1522(b) through (e) use "is authorized" language: "the Director is authorized" to provide assistance. These provisions, according to the dissent, confirm that Congress granted discretion rather than imposed obligations.

The majority does not directly address this authorization argument. Instead, it focuses on § 1522(a), which uses "shall" language, and on specific subsections that impose obligations. The majority thus reads the statute as a mixed scheme: Congress authorized programs in general terms but mandated specific services for certain populations.

## How the majority and dissent differ on whether appropriated funds trigger mandatory services

The core disagreement between the majority and dissent concerns whether the existence of appropriations transforms statutory authorization into mandatory obligation. Both opinions analyze the same statutory text but reach opposite conclusions.

The majority reads § 1522(a) as creating mandatory duties. The statute requires the Director to make available resources for employment training, provide English language training, ensure cash assistance is available, and guarantee women equal opportunities. The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" operates as a funding condition: the duty exists, but its scope is limited by what Congress has appropriated. Because Congress did appropriate funds for refugee assistance, the duty is triggered.

Judge Lee offers a different reading. He argues that § 1522(a) applies only "[i]n providing assistance under this section"—meaning that the mandatory language kicks in only if the government chooses to provide assistance under the discretionary provisions that follow. The "conditions and considerations" in § 1522(a) thus apply only when the executive exercises its discretion to fund programs; they do not compel the executive to fund programs in the first instance.

The dissent also addresses specific subsections that the majority cites. For unaccompanied minors under § 1522(d)(2)(B), Judge Lee notes that the Director's responsibility arises only "if necessary"—language that retains executive discretion. For cash and medical assistance under § 1522(e)(7)(A), the dissent points out that programs must be provided "as needed," leaving the executive to determine what is needed.

These competing interpretations illustrate a fundamental divide over statutory construction. The majority reads the statute to create rights that refugees can enforce; the dissent reads it to grant the executive branch flexibility that courts cannot second-guess.

## The legal significance of the State Department's termination of cooperative agreements

The practical consequence of these interpretive disputes centers on the cooperative agreements that the State Department terminated. Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) provides that resettlement services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The organizational plaintiffs in this case held such cooperative agreements.

The majority opinion notes that the State Department terminated these agreements in February 2025, shortly after the district court's initial injunction. The government represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." Yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers.

This factual finding drives the majority's remedy. Because the government expressed "uncertainty regarding its ability to provide" statutorily mandated services, the court ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. The majority treats the termination of agreements as an executive action that cannot override statutory duties: if § 1522 requires services, the government cannot avoid that obligation by terminating the contracts through which services are delivered.

The dissent would likely respond that because § 1522 does not mandate services, the executive remains free to structure—or discontinue—programs as it sees fit. Judge Lee does not specifically address the cooperative agreements, but his reasoning implies that courts have no basis to order their reinstatement when Congress has not required the underlying services.

This practical dispute highlights what is at stake in the interpretive debate. The majority's reading gives effect to what it perceives as Congress's intent to provide services to vulnerable populations. The dissent's reading preserves what it sees as constitutional executive discretion over program implementation. Both invoke statutory text, but each emphasizes different provisions and different interpretive principles.
