Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 16: # The Cooperative Agreement Framework and Resettlement Services

## What Cooperative Agreements Are and How They Function in Federal Programs

To understand the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*, students must first understand the legal mechanism through which refugee resettlement services are delivered: the cooperative agreement. Federal agencies use several instruments to carry out statutory mandates, including contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements. Unlike contracts, which are used when the federal government is procuring goods or services for its own use, cooperative agreements are used when the federal government provides funding to another entity to carry out a public purpose authorized by statute. Unlike pure grants, cooperative agreements involve "substantial involvement" between the federal agency and the recipient in carrying out the funded activity.

The opinion makes clear that Congress explicitly authorized this funding mechanism for refugee resettlement. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), resettlement services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with 'public or private nonprofit agencies.'" The court emphasized that "the United States has historically entered into cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to provide such services." This historical practice matters because when the government suddenly terminated these agreements, it disrupted an established system that had been functioning pursuant to statutory authorization.

Cooperative agreements create legally binding obligations on both parties. Once executed, the federal government commits to provide specified funding, and the recipient organization commits to provide specified services. The agreements typically include detailed terms regarding the scope of services, reporting requirements, payment schedules, and termination procedures. Importantly for this case, cooperative agreements can be terminated—but as the dispute illustrates, termination has legal consequences that depend on whether the underlying statute requires the services to be provided.

## The State Department's Role in Administering Refugee Resettlement Agreements

The opinion identifies the Department of State as the federal agency responsible for administering the cooperative agreements at issue. The organizational plaintiffs—three nonprofit organizations—had "cooperative agreements with the Department of State to provide overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." This division of labor reflects the broader architecture of the refugee admissions program: the State Department handles pre-admission processing and initial domestic resettlement (reception and placement), while the Office of Refugee Resettlement within the Department of Health and Human Services administers longer-term support services.

The practical significance of this administrative structure became apparent when the executive orders were issued. On January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended all funding of the USRAP program pursuant to a separate executive order on foreign aid. Then, significantly, on February 26, 2025—the day after the district court's oral preliminary injunction—the government "terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad." The timing suggests the termination was a response to the district court's intervention, and the breadth of the termination affected nearly all organizations providing these services.

The March 24, 2025 district court order addressed this directly by ordering "the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling." The appellate court's treatment of this order—staying most provisions but reinstating the requirement for services to already-admitted refugees—hinged on whether the statute mandated the services themselves, regardless of the mechanism used to provide them.

## The Services Provided Under Reception and Placement Programs

The court's analysis depends on understanding what "reception and placement services" actually include. The opinion provides a detailed statutory breakdown. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement is required "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide four categories of services:

First, "make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible." Second, "provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training to enable them to become effectively resettled as quickly as possible." Third, "insure that cash assistance is made available to refugees in such a manner as not to discourage their economic self-sufficiency." Fourth, "insure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction."

The statute also addresses specific populations with particular needs. For unaccompanied refugee minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) provides that "during any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." For recently resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed."

These statutory provisions formed the core of the parties' dispute over whether the government had a non-discretionary duty to provide services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

## How Termination of Cooperative Agreements Affects Service Delivery

The opinion reveals a critical practical problem created by the termination of cooperative agreements. After terminating all the agreements in February 2025, the government represented to the district court on March 10, 2025 that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." This representation suggested that the government recognized some ongoing obligation and intended to establish an alternative mechanism to fulfill it.

However, at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, "the government advised the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services." This admission proved significant. The court noted "the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522." The gap between the government's March representation and its September admission—expressing uncertainty about any progress—suggested that termination of the cooperative agreements had created a situation where the government had no functioning delivery system for services it might be legally required to provide.

The termination thus raised a practical question: if the statute requires services to be provided, and the government has eliminated the only mechanism for providing them without securing a replacement, how can the statutory mandate be fulfilled? This practical reality informed the court's decision to order reinstatement of the cooperative agreements rather than simply directing the government to provide services through some unspecified means.

## The Practical Implications of Reinstating Terminated Agreements

The court's order directed the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This remedy has several practical implications worth understanding.

First, reinstatement means restoring the legal relationship between the government and the organizational plaintiffs. The organizations that had their agreements terminated would resume their role as service providers, with associated funding obligations flowing from the government. The court's order thus functions as an injunction requiring the executive branch to resume funding that it had decided to terminate.

Second, the scope of reinstatement is limited. The order applies only to refugees "who have been admitted to the United States"—not to those approved for resettlement but still abroad. This limitation reflects the court's reasoning that while the President has broad authority over admission decisions, once refugees have been lawfully admitted, different statutory obligations attach. Judge Lee's partial dissent disagreed with this distinction, arguing that § 1522 authorizes but does not mandate services even for admitted refugees.

Third, the reinstatement obligation applies only to "such cooperative agreements necessary to provide" the statutory services. The court recognized that not all previously terminated agreements might be required—only those needed to fulfill the statutory mandate. This gives the government some flexibility in determining which agreements to reinstate, though the practical reality is that the organizational plaintiffs whose agreements were terminated are the most obvious candidates for reinstatement.

## The "Usual and Customary Services" Standard

The court specified that reinstated services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This phrase is significant because it establishes a standard for what services must be provided, grounded in historical practice rather than abstract statutory interpretation.

The "usual and customary services" language appears designed to prevent the government from reinstating cooperative agreements in name only while providing a materially reduced scope of services. By referencing what had "historically" been provided, the court anchored the service obligation in the established practices that existed before the executive orders disrupted them. This approach avoids requiring the court to micromanage service delivery or specify every service in detail—instead, it points to the pre-existing framework as the benchmark.

The practical effect is that resettlement organizations would resume providing the same types and levels of services they provided before termination: initial reception, housing assistance, employment services, English language training, case management, and other support services that had become standard under the USRAP program. Judge Lee's dissent did not directly challenge this standard, focusing instead on whether any services were legally required in the first place.

For students reading this opinion, the "usual and customary services" phrase illustrates how courts sometimes resolve remedial questions by reference to established practice rather than formal legal standards. Where a statutory mandate is disputed but historical practice is clear, the practice itself can become the measure of compliance.
