Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 37: # The Stay Pending Appeal Framework and Application

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test

When a party seeks to pause a district court's order while an appeal proceeds, the mechanism is a "stay pending appeal." The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito v. Trump* applied the well-established four-factor test from *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), to determine whether to grant such relief. Understanding this framework is essential because stays are frequently sought in high-stakes litigation where immediate relief is critical.

The four factors are: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The court emphasized that these factors are not mere hurdles to be cleared equally—the first two factors are "the most critical."

This prioritization makes practical sense. If an applicant cannot show a decent chance of winning, pausing the lower court's order makes little sense. Similarly, if the applicant suffers no irreparable harm from complying with the order during appeal, there is no urgency warranting intervention. The third and fourth factors—harm to other parties and public interest—operate as balancing considerations, weighing the equities once the critical threshold showing is made.

In *Pacito*, the government sought a stay of two preliminary injunctions issued by the Western District of Washington. The district court had enjoined enforcement of Executive Order 14163, which suspended refugee admissions, and had also ordered the reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements that funded resettlement services. The government's request thus required the Ninth Circuit to evaluate each component of the district court's relief through the *Nken* lens.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: The 'Most Critical' Factor

The court's analysis of the first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—reveals how deference to executive authority in immigration matters can prove outcome-determinative. For the government's challenge to the injunction on refugee admissions, the court found the government likely to succeed. But for the challenge to the injunction requiring reinstatement of resettlement services, the court reached the opposite conclusion.

On the admissions suspension, the court relied heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which addressed the so-called "travel ban" executive order. The statutory authority at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." Quoting *Hawaii*, the Ninth Circuit observed that this provision "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions."

The court concluded that President Trump had satisfied the "sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f)—making the required finding that entry of refugees would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The court expressly declined to undertake "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The President's determination, within the broad discretion conferred by statute, sufficed. Accordingly, the government had made a strong showing that it would likely succeed in defending Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions.

The analysis shifted, however, when the court turned to the termination of funding for resettlement services. The plaintiffs had challenged this defunding under the Administrative Procedure Act, arguing it violated statutory mandates. The court examined 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement programs. Several provisions use mandatory language: § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Other subsections similarly use "shall" rather than "may."

Critically, the court noted that Congress had appropriated approximately $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for fiscal year 2025. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing" to secure a new resettlement agency but could not confirm at oral argument whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill what the court read as statutory obligations meant the government was not likely to succeed on the APA challenge to the services termination.

Judge Lee's partial dissent provides a counter-reading of the same statute. He emphasized that § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement"—signals permissive rather than mandatory language. In his view, the statute authorizes the executive branch to craft programs but does not compel their operation. This interpretive dispute highlights how statutory construction can determine the likelihood-of-success inquiry.

## Irreparable Injury Analysis for Both Government and Plaintiffs

The second *Nken* factor—irreparable injury—proved pivotal in the court's bifurcated analysis. A stay pending appeal is an equitable remedy, and equity acts only to prevent harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or other legal relief after the fact.

The court found that the government would suffer irreparable injury if forced to implement refugee admissions contrary to the President's suspension order. The Supreme Court has recognized that when an executive action grounded in statutory authority is enjoined, the government suffers a sovereign injury that is inherently irreparable—the executive branch is prevented from executing its lawful policy. Moreover, the court suggested that the "domestic and foreign interests of the United States," as determined by the President's broad discretion, would be impaired by permitting continued admissions during the appeal.

For the plaintiffs, irreparable injury cut the other way with respect to resettlement services. The court emphasized that refugees already admitted to the United States have a statutory entitlement to reception and placement services. If those services were terminated during the appeal, refugees would be left without the support Congress mandated—support designed to achieve economic self-sufficiency and effective resettlement. The harm to refugees denied such services during the appeal could not be undone by a later victory on the merits. Once a refugee goes without needed services, that gap in support cannot be remedied retroactively.

Judge Lee's dissent questioned whether the organizational plaintiffs—resettlement agencies whose cooperative agreements were terminated—could demonstrate irreparable harm. He noted that their injury appears to be loss of funding, which might be compensable through monetary relief. However, the majority's focus remained on the refugees themselves as the intended beneficiaries of the statutory mandate, not merely on the organizations that provide services.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest in Immigration Matters

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—harm to other parties and the public interest—are often analyzed together in immigration cases. The court must weigh the competing interests and determine where the balance lies during the pendency of appeal.

The court held that the balance favored the United States regarding refugee admissions. The "broad discretion" conferred on the President in immigration matters, as established in *Hawaii*, meant that the public interest in controlling admissions weighed in favor of staying the district court's injunction. The President's determination about what serves the national interest receives substantial deference, particularly at the intersection of immigration policy and foreign affairs.

However, the balance shifted regarding resettlement services. Congress, through § 1522, had already made a policy judgment that refugees admitted to the United States should receive reception and placement services. The public interest, as expressed by Congress through duly enacted statutes, supported continuing services to already-admitted refugees. When Congress appropriates funds and directs that certain services be provided, the executive branch's contrary policy preferences cannot automatically override that statutory mandate.

The court thus concluded that the public interest favored both: (1) permitting the President's suspension of refugee admissions to take effect, and (2) ensuring that refugees already present in the United States receive the services Congress has mandated. This nuanced result reflects the reality that "the public interest" is not monolithic—different statutory schemes and constitutional authorities may point in different directions within the same case.

## The Court's Application: Staying the Injunction on Admissions While Reinstating Services

Applying the *Nken* framework, the Ninth Circuit reached a bifurcated holding that illustrates how stay analysis can produce partial relief. The court vacated its prior stay orders and issued a new stay pending appeal that treated the two challenged actions differently.

First, the court stayed the district court's preliminary injunctions in their entirety regarding Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. The government had made a strong showing of likelihood of success based on the President's broad authority under § 1182(f), the irreparable injury to sovereign interests, and the public interest in deferring to executive judgment on admission policy. The President's determination that refugee entry would be detrimental was sufficient under *Hawaii*; no searching judicial inquiry into justifications was appropriate.

Second, the court declined to stay the district court's order requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for reception and placement services. The government had not shown it would likely succeed on the APA challenge given § 1522's mandatory language. The court specifically directed the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This order was limited to refugees already admitted, not those still awaiting admission abroad.

The court acknowledged that it would issue a full opinion on the merits "in due course." The stay order thus represents an interim disposition—a prediction about how the appeal will ultimately resolve, not a final merits determination. This is consistent with the nature of stay proceedings: the court asks what is likely to happen, not what the final outcome will be.

Judge Lee's partial dissent illustrates an alternative application of the same framework. He would have stayed the injunctions in their entirety, reading § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory. His reasoning underscores that the *Nken* factors, while providing a structured framework, depend heavily on antecedent interpretive questions—here, whether statutory language creates an enforceable duty or merely authorizes executive discretion.

The *Pacito* stay order demonstrates that intermediate appellate courts can disaggregate complex challenges and grant stays on some issues while denying them on others. The result preserves what the court views as valid exercises of executive authority while protecting what it reads as congressionally mandated rights—a balance the court struck through careful application of the *Nken* framework.
