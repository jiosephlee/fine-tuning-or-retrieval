Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the scope of presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) after Trump v. Hawaii?
Question:
The court cites Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that § 1182(f) 'exudes deference' to the President and that courts cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.' But this seems to give the President nearly unlimited power to suspend entry of any class of aliens. Are there any meaningful limits on this authority? The statute says the President must find that entry 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States' - is this a substantive requirement or purely procedural? Can courts ever invalidate a suspension under § 1182(f), or is the President's determination effectively unreviewable?
Answer:
## Answer

Based on *Trump v. Hawaii* and the Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump*, there are meaningful limits on presidential authority under § 1182(f), though they are narrow.

### The "Sole Prerequisite" Standard

The court in *Pacito* quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that **"the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."** This requirement is satisfied by the President's own determination—the statute does not require that a court independently agree with or validate that determination.

As the opinion states, courts **"cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."** The finding itself constitutes compliance; there is no substantive judicial review of whether entry would *actually* be detrimental.

### The Textual Limit

The primary constraint identified in both *Hawaii* and *Pacito* is **textual**. The court emphasized that § 1182(f) authority extends only to suspending the **"entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States."** The President may not act in a manner that **"exceed[s] any textual limit on the President's authority"** under the statute.

This textual limit becomes significant when other statutory provisions create obligations that conflict with or constrain executive action. In *Pacito*, while the court deferred to the President's suspension of refugee *entry* under § 1182(f), it simultaneously found that **8 U.S.C. § 1522 independently mandates certain reception and placement services for refugees who have already been admitted.** The court ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide those statutorily required services.

This illustrates a key boundary: **§ 1182(f) governs only entry.** Once aliens have been admitted, other statutory provisions may impose obligations that constrain executive discretion. The President cannot use § 1182(f) to circumvent mandates Congress has established elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

### Summary of Post-*Hawaii* Limits

Based on this opinion, presidential authority under § 1182(f) is constrained by:

1. **The finding requirement** — The President must actually make the determination that entry would be detrimental (this is easily satisfied and not subject to searching review);

2. **The textual scope** — The authority is limited to suspending "entry" of "aliens" or a "class of aliens"—actions beyond this scope may exceed statutory authority; and

3. **Other statutory mandates** — When other provisions of the INA impose obligations (such as § 1522's requirements for post-admission services), those may limit the practical effect of a § 1182(f) suspension.

The short answer to your question is yes, courts can invalidate a suspension under § 1182(f), but only if the President fails to make the required finding or exceeds the textual scope of the statute. The "detrimental to the interests" determination itself is effectively unreviewable—it is a procedural prerequisite, not a substantive standard for judicial evaluation.

### How do courts determine whether a statute creates a mandatory duty or merely authorizes discretionary action?
Question:
I'm struggling to understand the dispute between the majority and Judge Lee about 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority points to the word 'shall' in various subsections to argue that the statute mandates providing refugee services. But Judge Lee counters that the statute's title uses 'Authorization' and that the funding provisions say the Director 'is authorized' to provide assistance. How do courts typically resolve this kind of tension? When a statute's title says 'authorization' but the substantive provisions use 'shall,' which controls? Are there canons of statutory construction that apply here?
Answer:
This is a classic statutory interpretation dispute. Courts resolve this tension through several established principles, which both the majority and Judge Lee are implicitly applying.

## 1. "Shall" Creates a Presumption of Mandatory Duty

The starting point is that "shall" is generally mandatory, while "may" or "authorized" is generally permissive. The majority relies heavily on this principle, pointing to multiple instances where § 1522 uses "shall": § 1522(a)(1)(A) (the Director "shall... make available sufficient resources"), § 1522(d)(2)(B) (the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors), and § 1522(e)(7)(A) (the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects").

Judge Lee does not dispute this presumption. Instead, she argues the context transforms the meaning.

## 2. Context and Structure Can Qualify "Shall"

Judge Lee's strongest argument is structural. She notes that § 1522(a) is prefaced by "In providing assistance under this section"—meaning, in her reading, the mandatory "shall" provisions apply *only if* the Director has already chosen to provide assistance under the discretionary funding provisions in subsections (b)–(e). Those subsections, she emphasizes, state the Director "is authorized to provide assistance."

This reflects a recognized principle: courts must read statutory language in context. When mandatory language is embedded in a scheme that speaks primarily in permissive terms, the mandatory provisions may specify *how* a discretionary program must operate rather than requiring the program to exist.

## 3. Titles and Headings Carry Limited Weight

Judge Lee points to the section title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement"—as evidence that § 1522 is permissive.

Courts generally hold, however, that titles and headings cannot override clear operative text. Headings are properly used to *resolve* ambiguity, not to create it where the text is clear. The majority implicitly rejects Lee's title argument by not addressing it—suggesting the "shall" language in the operative provisions controls.

## 4. Appropriations Language Qualifies, Not Eliminates

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" in § 1522(a)(1)(A) is important. This type of language typically means a duty exists, but its *scope* is limited by congressional funding decisions. If Congress appropriates money, the duty is triggered; if not, the duty is suspended. The majority notes that Congress has in fact appropriated billions for refugee assistance, which would trigger the obligation.

Judge Lee reads this as reinforcing executive discretion. But the majority's approach—that appropriations qualify rather than eliminate duties—is consistent with how courts typically treat such language.

## 5. Qualifying Clauses Go to Scope, Not Existence

Judge Lee correctly notes that even the "shall" provisions contain qualifying language: § 1522(d)(2)(B) applies only "if necessary," and § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires action only "as needed."

The majority would likely respond that these qualifications go to the *manner and scope* of implementation, not whether a duty exists. The agency has discretion over *how* to fulfill its obligations, but not *whether* to fulfill them.

## Summary

When "shall" appears in a statute titled "Authorization," courts prioritize the operative text over the title, but examine whether context qualifies the mandatory language. Here, the majority reads the "shall" provisions as creating duties triggered by refugee admission and congressional appropriations, while Judge Lee reads them as conditions attached to discretionary funding authority. The dispute turns on which structural cues—the permissive funding provisions or the mandatory substantive language—should control.

### What does 'to the extent of available appropriations' mean in federal benefit statutes?
Question:
Section 1522(a)(1)(A) says the Director shall provide certain services 'to the extent of available appropriations.' The majority seems to read this as creating a mandatory obligation so long as Congress has appropriated funds. But could the executive branch argue that even with appropriations, the language gives them discretion over whether services are 'available'? And does the fact that Congress appropriated money (though reduced by the Rescissions Act) mean the 'available appropriations' condition is satisfied, or can the executive still decline to spend appropriated funds?
Answer:
### Answer

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A) is at the heart of the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*, and the panel's order reveals a fundamental split over how to interpret this kind of limitations language in federal benefit statutes.

**The Majority's Reading: A Funding Condition, Not a Discretion Grant**

The majority interprets "to the extent of available appropriations" as a *funding condition* on an otherwise mandatory obligation. Under this reading, the "shall" in § 1522(a)(1)(A) creates a statutory duty: the Director *must* provide the enumerated services (employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, etc.). The appropriations language operates only to limit the *scope* of that duty—i.e., the government need not spend more than Congress has provided—but it does not give the Executive discretion to decline spending appropriated funds altogether.

The majority supports this reading by pointing to the statute's mandatory text: the Director "shall... make available sufficient resources," "provide... opportunity," and "insure that cash assistance is made available." Having determined that Congress appropriated funds for FY 2025 (approximately $2.4 billion after the Rescissions Act), the majority concludes the condition is satisfied and the mandate applies. The government's uncertainty about alternative service provision further bolsters the court's order reinstating the cooperative agreements.

**Judge Lee's Dissent: The Appropriations Language as Part of a Permissive Scheme**

Judge Lee offers a competing framework. He argues that § 1522 as a whole is an *authorization* statute, not a mandate. His reasoning proceeds in three steps:

1. **The section title**: § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement," and "authorization" is permissive—it grants permission, not obligation.

2. **The funding provisions**: Subsections (b) through (e) consistently state the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance, signaling discretion.

3. **The limiting clause in (a)**: The "shall" language in § 1522(a) is prefaced by "In providing assistance under this section..."—meaning the mandatory conditions apply *only if* the government has chosen to exercise its discretionary authority to fund programs.

Under Judge Lee's reading, "to the extent of available appropriations" reinforces that this is a discretionary spending program: the executive may provide services so long as Congress appropriates funds, but Congress has not *required* it to do so.

**Can the Executive Decline to Spend Appropriated Funds?**

The opinion directly engages with your question about whether appropriated funds must be spent. The majority's answer is yes—at least where, as here, the statute uses mandatory "shall" language and Congress has satisfied the funding condition. Once appropriations are "available," the statutory duty activates.

Judge Lee's dissent would permit the Executive to decline, reading the entire scheme as discretionary. He explicitly states: "Section 1522 authorizes the executive branch to craft programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow. It does not require that those programs operate."

**The Broader Doctrine**

The dispute reflects an interpretive tension in federal benefits law. Phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations" or "subject to appropriations" can function either as:
- A **jurisdictional limit**: creating a mandate that binds the agency once funds are appropriated; or
- An **authorization qualifier**: confirming that Congress is permitting but not requiring expenditure.

Traditional canons favor finding mandatory obligations when Congress uses "shall" and appropriates funds, but agency discretion arguments gain traction when the overall statutory scheme is framed permissively.

**Bottom Line**

The majority holds that "to the extent of available appropriations" means the services are mandatory so long as Congress has appropriated funds—which it did here. The Executive cannot decline to spend those funds on the specified services. Judge Lee's dissent would permit that discretion, reading the phrase as part of an overall authorization scheme. The merits opinion to follow will provide more detailed reasoning, but the panel's interim order makes clear that at least two judges read the language as binding once appropriations exist.

### Can the President suspend refugee admissions but still be required to serve refugees already admitted?
Question:
The court reaches different conclusions on different parts of the case: the government is likely to prevail on the admissions suspension under § 1182(f), but unlikely to prevail on the services claim under § 1522. I'm confused about the relationship between these two issues. If the President can suspend refugee admissions entirely, why would Congress have required services for refugees who are already here? And does the court's ruling mean that the executive order is partially valid and partially invalid, or is the services requirement a separate statutory obligation that exists regardless of the admissions suspension?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important distinction that runs through refugee law: **the difference between "admission" and "treatment after admission."** These are governed by different statutory provisions, and they operate independently of each other.

**The Two Statutes Address Different Stages of the Refugee Process**

- **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** governs *entry*—who may come into the United States. Under this provision, the President may suspend the entry of any class of aliens if he finds their entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court concludes the President has broad discretion here, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and imposes only the minimal requirement of a detrimental-interest finding, which the President made here.

- **8 U.S.C. § 1522** governs *what happens after admission*—the services the government must provide to refugees already in the United States. This includes employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors. These services are often delivered through cooperative agreements with nonprofit resettlement agencies.

**Why This Distinction Matters**

The court's ruling reflects the logic that once a refugee has been lawfully admitted, they fall under a different statutory regime. Section 1522 creates obligations toward refugees "who have been admitted to the United States"—a status already achieved before the executive order took effect. The court explains that the government must provide services "to the extent of available appropriations," and Congress has appropriated funds for FY 2025. The President's authority to suspend future admissions under § 1182(f) does not negate Congress's separate directive, via § 1522, to care for refugees who are already here.

**The Practical Result**

The order stays the district court's injunction on the admissions suspension—the government is likely to prevail on that issue. But the court directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services to refugees already admitted. So yes, the ruling effectively treats the executive order as partially valid (the admission suspension) and partially problematic (the termination of services for admitted refugees), but more precisely, the services requirement exists as an independent statutory obligation that attaches once admission occurs, regardless of whether the President suspends future admissions.

**Judge Lee's Counterargument**

Judge Lee's partial dissent argues that § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory—pointing to the statute's title ("Authorization for programs") and the repeated use of "authorized" in the funding provisions. He reads the word "shall" in § 1522(a) as conditioning only *how* funds are spent if the government chooses to spend them, not as requiring spending in the first place. This interpretive dispute—whether § 1522 creates a mandate or merely authorizes discretionary programs—will likely be resolved in the forthcoming opinion.

### What standard do courts use when deciding whether to grant a stay pending appeal?
Question:
The court applies the four-factor test from Nken v. Holder but says the first two factors (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury) are 'the most critical.' What does this mean in practice? Does a strong showing on likelihood of success essentially decide the stay question, or can a party still lose a stay even if they're likely to succeed on the merits? Also, how does the stay standard differ from the standard for granting a preliminary injunction in the first place?
Answer:
### Answer

The statement that the first two Nken factors are "the most critical" means that likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury are *necessary predicates* for a stay—they are threshold requirements that the moving party must establish before the court weighs the equities. However, a strong showing on the merits does not automatically guarantee a stay, as the opinion in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates.

**Likelihood of Success Does Not Mechanically Decide the Outcome**

The court's analysis shows that the merits inquiry is dispositive *only when* the moving party is likely to succeed. Where the government was likely to prevail on its authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, the court granted the stay. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel concluded that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and that the Executive Order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority."

But the court's treatment of the APA claim shows the limits of a merits-only analysis. The court found that the government was **not** likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge regarding resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The statutory language used "shall" and imposed specific obligations, such as providing services "to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." Because the government could not demonstrate likely success on this claim, the court *denied* the stay in part, directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for resettlement services. This outcome illustrates that a party cannot obtain a stay where it fails the first critical factor, regardless of how the other factors might balance.

**The Interplay of the Four Factors**

The opinion suggests that when the first two factors strongly favor the moving party, the third and fourth factors (harm to other parties and the public interest) often align. The court noted that the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States" favored the government in the admissions context, where statutory deference is at its peak. However, the court did not suggest that the balance of equities could overcome a weak showing on the merits. The "most critical" language means these factors function as gatekeepers.

**Stay vs. Preliminary Injunction: The Key Distinctions**

While both preliminary injunctions and stays use the same four-factor framework, the practical posture differs in ways that matter:

1. **Burden and Posture**: A stay pending appeal operates against the backdrop of a district court ruling that has already found the moving party likely to lose on the merits. The moving party must convince the appellate court that the district court erred in that assessment. Here, the government had to show not just that the district court's injunction was questionable, but that its own position was likely to prevail on appeal.

2. **Deference to District Court**: An appellate court reviewing a stay request gives some weight to the district court's assessment of the equities, though legal conclusions are reviewed de novo. The panel in *Pacito* did not defer to the district court's conclusions about the scope of executive authority under § 1182(f), applying *Hawaii* directly.

3. **Practical Effect**: Granting a stay effectively reverses the status quo established by the preliminary injunction. The panel's partial stay meant the government could enforce its entry suspension but remained bound by statutory obligations to provide resettlement services to already-admitted refugees.

In sum, the "most critical" factors are dispositive in the negative: failure on either likelihood of success or irreparable injury dooms a stay motion. A strong showing on the merits creates a powerful presumption in favor of a stay, but courts still consider whether the equities tip sufficiently in the movant's favor.

### What is the legal status of 'conditionally approved' refugees in the admission process?
Question:
The earlier stay order created a carveout for individuals who were 'conditionally approved for refugee status' before January 20, 2025. What exactly does 'conditional approval' mean in the refugee context? Does this create some kind of procedural due process interest or expectation that the government must complete processing? And why would the motions panel treat conditionally approved refugees differently from those who had merely applied - is there a legal significance to the conditional approval status?
Answer:
### Answer

The opinion does not explicitly define "conditional approval," but we can draw several inferences about its legal significance from how the motions panel treated this category of refugees differently from those who had merely applied.

**What Conditional Approval Signifies**

From the opinion, we know that conditional approval is granted by "United States Citizenship and Immigration Services" and represents a stage in the USRAP process that occurs *before* final admission. The March 25 stay order carved out individuals who were "conditionally approved for refugee status... before January 20, 2025"—the date of Executive Order 14163. This timing suggests conditional approval represents an intermediate status: more advanced than a pending application, but short of actual admission to the United States.

**Why the Motions Panel Treated This Status Differently**

The motions panel's carveout implies that conditional approval creates legal distinctions worth preserving. The panel denied the stay only "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved." This selective treatment suggests the panel recognized that conditionally approved refugees may have stronger equitable and legal claims than those at earlier stages of the process.

However, the September 12 order from the merits panel takes a different approach. It now stays the preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below"—and the noted exception is limited to § 1522 reception and placement services for refugees *already admitted* to the United States, not those conditionally approved abroad. The conditional approval carveout from March 25 has been vacated.

**Due Process and Expectation Interests**

The opinion does not directly address whether conditional approval creates a procedural due process interest or an expectation that processing must be completed. What we can observe is:

1. The motions panel's initial carveout suggests preliminary concern that executive action may affect individuals differently depending on their stage in the process;
2. The merits panel's September 12 order, however, concludes that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the admission suspension itself, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions";
3. The current order focuses its protection on a different category entirely—refugees who have already been admitted and are entitled to § 1522 services.

**The Core Distinction**

The legal significance of conditional approval appears to be that it represents a substantive determination by USCIS that an individual qualifies as a refugee. This is more than a mere application, which is simply a request for adjudication. The motions panel may have been concerned that rescinding such approval after it was granted implicates reliance interests that mere applications do not.

That said, the September 12 order signals that, at least at the stay stage, the panel finds the government likely to succeed on its authority under § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions broadly—even for those conditionally approved. The opinion's analysis of § 1182(f) emphasizes that the President need only "find[] that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,'" which he has done.

The substantive opinion to follow may provide more analysis of what, if any, legal significance conditional approval carries. For now, the September 12 order treats all pre-admission refugees alike under the admission suspension, while distinguishing only those already admitted for purposes of § 1522 services.

### What APA violations are alleged when the government terminates cooperative agreements?
Question:
The court says the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' APA challenges, but doesn't explain the exact violations. When the State Department terminated all the cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations, what APA procedures would have been required? Is this about rulemaking vs. adjudication, or about arbitrary and capricious review under § 706? And what's the remedy if the termination was procedurally deficient - can the court order the government to reinstate the agreements, or just to follow proper procedures going forward?
Answer:
### Answer

The opinion doesn't explicitly enumerate each APA violation, but we can infer the likely claims from what the court discusses.

**The Core APA Claim: Action "Contrary to Law" Under § 706(2)(A)**

The primary APA challenge appears to be that the termination of cooperative agreements was "contrary to law"—specifically, that it violated the statutory mandates of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Under APA § 706(2)(A), a reviewing court must "hold unlawful and set aside" agency action that is "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law."

The majority reads § 1522 as *mandatory*: the statute uses "shall" and requires the government to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Because these services "may be provided through... cooperative agreements" (§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)), terminating *all* such agreements without securing alternative means to fulfill the statutory mandate would be contrary to law.

Judge Lee's dissent frames this as a question of statutory interpretation: he reads § 1522's title ("Authorization for programs") and repeated use of "is authorized" as creating discretionary authority, not a mandate. The dissent doesn't directly analyze APA standards—it argues there's no statutory duty that could ground an APA violation in the first place.

**Rulemaking vs. Adjudication**

The opinion doesn't address whether the terminations constituted rulemaking or adjudication, and for good reason: the plaintiffs' APA claim appears grounded in substantive illegality, not procedural deficiencies. The government terminated specific agreements with identified organizations—an individualized action resembling adjudication—but the court's analysis turns on whether the action violated statutory mandates, not on whether the APA's procedural requirements for rulemaking (§ 553) or adjudication (§ 554) were satisfied.

**The Remedy**

The court orders the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This is more than a prospective order to follow proper procedures—it restores the status quo ante for a specific category of refugees. However, this is an interim stay order; the full opinion "to follow" will presumably elaborate on the rationale and scope of relief.

**Summary**

The alleged APA violation is that the government terminated cooperative agreements in a manner "not in accordance with law" under § 706(2)(A), by failing to ensure compliance with the statutory mandate to provide services under § 1522. The remedy under consideration is reinstatement of the agreements, not merely prospective procedural compliance.

### Can a court order the executive branch to reinstate terminated contracts or cooperative agreements?
Question:
The court directs the government to 'reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services.' But these are contracts with private nonprofit organizations that the government terminated. Can a court really order the government to enter back into contracts with specific private parties? What if the organizations don't want to resume the agreements, or if the government has legitimate concerns about their performance? And what's the difference between a 'cooperative agreement' and a regular contract for purposes of judicial review of termination?
Answer:
### Answer

Yes, a court can order reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements under certain circumstances—specifically, where the court finds that a statute mandates the services those agreements provided, and termination of the agreements would violate that statutory mandate.

**The Legal Basis: Statutory Mandates Trump Executive Discretion**

The panel majority held that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a *mandatory* duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. The court pointed to several textual signals:

- § 1522(a)(1)(A) states the Director "shall" provide certain services "to the extent of available appropriations"
- § 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to "assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors in transit
- § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary "shall develop and implement" support projects

Because Congress appropriated funds for these services ($3.178 billion for FY 2025, later reduced by $800 million), the majority concluded the government cannot simply decline to provide them. The statutory authorization to use "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements" under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) describes the *mechanism* for fulfilling the mandatory duty—not a discretionary choice whether to provide services at all.

**Why Reinstatement Specifically?**

The court ordered reinstatement rather than simply requiring the government to provide services because:

1. The government admitted it was "actively preparing" a request for proposals for new providers but could not confirm any progress at oral argument;
2. No alternative mechanism existed to provide the mandated services; and
3. The agreements had been terminated *after* the litigation began and after the district court's initial injunction.

The remedy restores the status quo ante—the agreements that were in place before the government's abrupt termination without a replacement plan.

**Cooperative Agreements vs. Contracts**

The opinion does not distinguish cooperative agreements from contracts for purposes of judicial review. Both are authorized vehicles under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). The practical difference is largely procedural: cooperative agreements are used when "substantial involvement" between the agency and recipient is anticipated, while contracts are used for procurement of goods or services. For purposes of this dispute, the court treated the cooperative agreements as the *means* by which statutory duties were fulfilled—making their termination effectively a nullification of those duties.

**The Counterarguments (and Why They Didn't Prevail)**

Judge Lee's partial dissent argues that § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory. He emphasizes:
- The section title: "Authorization for programs" (not "Mandate");
- The repeated use of "is authorized" in subsections (b) through (e); and
- The qualifying language in (a) that applies only "In providing assistance under this section" (i.e., if the government chooses to fund programs).

Judge Lee reads the "shall" language as imposing conditions *if* the government exercises its discretion to fund programs—not as compelling funding in the first instance.

**Practical Concerns**

Your hypotheticals about unwilling organizations or performance concerns are not addressed in the opinion. The organizational plaintiffs here are actively seeking reinstatement. The court's remedy is limited to "refugees who have been admitted to the United States" and reinstating agreements "necessary" to serve that population. If an organization genuinely refused to resume, the government would presumably need to find alternative providers—but that burden falls on the government, not the court, to fulfill its statutory duties.

**The Bottom Line**

The court's order is an exercise in enforcing statutory mandates through injunctive relief, not commandeering executive contracting authority in the abstract. The legal dispute turns on whether § 1522 creates a duty or merely authorizes a program—and for now, the majority's reading controls.
