Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Exuding Deference: The Ninth Circuit Extends Trump v. Hawaii's Reach to Refugee Admissions

The Ninth Circuit's order yesterday in *Pacito v. Trump* marks a significant milestone in the post-*Trump v. Hawaii* landscape of executive immigration authority. In staying a district court injunction that had blocked the Trump administration's suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, the court has signaled that the Supreme Court's travel ban decision has effectively transformed 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) into a boundless grant of presidential power—one that requires little more than a presidential finding of "detriment" to justify wholesale suspension of entire immigration categories.

The stakes here are considerable. Executive Order 14163, issued on President Trump's first day back in office, effectively shuttered the nation's refugee resettlement program, suspending admissions and terminating funding for resettlement services. The order relies on the same statutory authority that sustained the travel ban: Section 1182(f), which permits the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whose admission "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The Ninth Circuit's analysis of that provision suggests the judiciary has largely abandoned any meaningful role in reviewing such determinations.

## The Admissions Suspension: A Blank Check

On the central question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions, the panel's reasoning tracks *Hawaii* with almost mechanical precision. Quoting the Supreme Court's observation that Section 1182(f) "exudes deference," the Ninth Circuit held that the President need only "find[]" that entry of covered aliens would be detrimental—nothing more. The court explicitly declined to engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This framing is striking. The Refugee Act of 1980 was enacted precisely to regularize and structure refugee admissions, establishing a comprehensive statutory framework that includes annual consultations with Congress, numerical ceilings, and detailed procedures for processing refugee claims. Yet under the panel's reading, the President may circumvent that entire edifice simply by invoking Section 1182(f)'s broad language. The "detrimental to the interests" standard, apparently, means whatever the President says it means—and courts will not second-guess that determination.

The doctrinal implications are sobering. *Trump v. Hawaii* involved a proclamation that suspended entry from several predominantly Muslim countries, justified on national security grounds. Whatever one's view of that decision, it at least involved a facial justification tied to specific national security concerns about particular countries. Here, by contrast, the President has suspended an entire global refugee program with no country-specific findings at all. If *Hawaii* extends this far, it is difficult to imagine any limit on Section 1182(f) short of an explicit statutory prohibition.

## The Services Split: A Statutory Duty?

The panel's treatment of funding for resettlement services reveals a more interesting fault line—one that produced a partial concurrence and dissent from Judge Lee. The majority concluded that the government is unlikely to succeed on plaintiffs' APA challenges to the termination of cooperative agreements providing "reception and placement" services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

The majority's reasoning turns on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement assistance. The court emphasized that § 1522(a) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," to make employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance available to refugees. Reading these provisions together with § 1522's authorization for cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations, the majority found a statutory duty that survives the admission suspension: once refugees are admitted, Congress has mandated certain services.

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a pointed counter-reading. Noting that § 1522's title uses the word "Authorization" rather than "Mandate," Judge Lee reads the statutory language as permissive—authorizing but not requiring the executive branch to fund and operate resettlement programs. This interpretive divide raises classic questions of statutory construction: when does "shall" impose an affirmative duty versus merely setting conditions for discretionary action?

The panel split here underscores an important doctrinal tension. The majority's approach treats § 1522 as establishing mandatory obligations that constrain executive discretion once refugees are admitted. Judge Lee's reading would give the executive broader authority to defund programs even for those already lawfully present. The practical consequence, for now, is that cooperative agreements must be reinstated to serve refugees already admitted—but the legal question remains unsettled pending the full opinion.

## Downstream Effects: The Hollowing of Judicial Review

The most significant long-term effect of this decision may be its reinforcement of *Hawaii*'s deferential framework. By applying that standard to a global program suspension with no individualized or country-specific findings, the Ninth Circuit has confirmed that Section 1182(f) operates as a nearly unlimited suspension power. The President's "finding" of detriment—unreviewable in any meaningful sense—becomes a self-executing justification for sweeping immigration restrictions.

This raises profound separation-of-powers questions. The Refugee Act represented a deliberate congressional effort to cabin executive discretion in refugee admissions, establishing a structured process that involves both branches. If Section 1182(f) can override that entire scheme on the strength of a presidential finding, the statutory framework becomes essentially advisory. Future presidents may invoke this precedent to suspend any immigration category they deem undesirable—from asylum seekers to diversity visa holders to students from disfavored nations.

The panel's willingness to order reinstatement of resettlement services for admitted refugees suggests one potential limit: Congress can mandate executive action through clear statutory duties tied to appropriations. But even this modest constraint rests on fragile footing given Judge Lee's dissenting interpretation. If the full opinion embraces her reading, even those who have already been admitted and resettled could find themselves without federal support—stranded by the very system that brought them here.

The Ninth Circuit's opinion to follow will warrant close attention. For now, the message is clear: when it comes to suspending immigration, *Trump v. Hawaii* has given presidents a power that courts are largely unwilling to check.
