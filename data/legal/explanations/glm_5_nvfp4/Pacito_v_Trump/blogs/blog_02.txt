Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Shall vs. Authorized: The Battle Over Statutory Mandatory Duties in Section 1522

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a rare glimpse into an emerging fault line in administrative law: how courts should interpret statutes that blend permissive and mandatory language. While the panel's split on the refugee program is notable on its own terms, the deeper significance lies in the methodological disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee's partial dissent over how to parse statutory text when Congress uses both "shall" and "authorized" in proximity.

## The Stakes: Two Statutory Regimes, Two Questions

The case involves President Trump's Executive Order 14163, which suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program under the broad authority granted to the President in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That provision allows the President to suspend entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The plaintiffs challenged both the suspension of admissions and the termination of funding for resettlement services.

The panel unanimously agreed that the President's suspension of refugee admissions is likely lawful, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*'s characterization of § 1182(f) as a provision that "exudes deference" to the President. The real dispute concerns the second question: whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a mandatory duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

The majority says yes; Judge Lee says no. Their disagreement reveals fundamentally different approaches to statutory construction.

## The Majority's Approach: "Shall" Means "Shall"

The majority anchors its analysis in § 1522(a)(1)(A), which provides that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall"—"to the extent of available appropriations"—make available certain services, including employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. The court emphasizes that Congress has appropriated over $2 billion for refugee assistance in FY 2025, meaning the "available appropriations" condition has been satisfied.

The panel also points to specific subsections that impose concrete obligations. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to "assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for unaccompanied refugee minors "if necessary." Section 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects" for interim support. The majority reads these provisions as imposing affirmative duties that persist even when the executive branch has suspended the broader admission program.

Critically, the majority notes that the government represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing" alternative arrangements for providing services but could not confirm at oral argument whether any progress had been made. This admission—that the government had no clear plan to satisfy the statutory requirements—appears to have influenced the panel's conclusion that the government is unlikely to succeed on the APA claim.

## Judge Lee's Counter: Title, Structure, and the Permission-to-Mandate Distinction

Judge Lee's dissent reads the same statute and reaches the opposite conclusion. His starting point is the provision's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." He invokes the dictionary definition of "authorization" as "permission to do something" rather than a command to act.

The structural argument is more substantial. Lee observes that the substantive funding provisions in § 1522(b) through (e) consistently use "authorized" language—the Director "is authorized" to provide cash assistance, medical assistance, and other services. In Lee's view, § 1522(a) merely sets forth the conditions that apply *if* the executive branch chooses to exercise that authorization. The "shall" language, he argues, is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section"—meaning the mandatory conditions attach only once a discretionary decision to provide assistance has been made.

Lee's interpretation has the virtue of taking seriously the repeated use of permissive language throughout the provision. If Congress meant to mandate funding, why would it have described these programs in terms of what the Director "is authorized" to do?

## Which Approach Better Reads the Statute?

Both readings have strengths. The majority's approach gives "shall" its ordinary meaning—courts typically presume that "shall" creates a mandatory duty, and the "to the extent of available appropriations" qualifier suggests Congress anticipated some limits but still imposed an obligation. The majority can also point to the purpose of the Refugee Act of 1980, which was meant to create a comprehensive and enduring framework for refugee resettlement—not merely an optional menu of services.

Lee's approach, however, highlights a genuine textual tension. It would be odd for Congress to frame a provision as an "authorization" and then use permissive language throughout, only to have courts read it as a mandate. And Lee is right to question whether subsections like (d)(2)(B)—which imposes responsibility for unaccompanied minors only "if necessary"—are really the stuff of judicially enforceable duties.

The dispute also raises practical concerns about judicial role. If § 1522 creates mandatory obligations, what remedy exists when the executive branch simply declines to act? The majority's order to reinstate cooperative agreements is a remarkably specific remedy—essentially compelling the government to contract with particular private organizations. Whether courts have authority to mandate such specific performance raises separation of powers questions that the panel opinion, forthcoming as it is, will need to address.

## Doctrinal Implications: The Authorization-Mandate Spectrum

The split in *Pacito* reflects a broader doctrinal uncertainty about statutes that blend authorization and mandate language. The Supreme Court has sometimes treated "authorized" language as merely clarifying that an agency has power to act, while at other times treating such language as indicative that Congress left the decision to agency discretion. See, for example, *Cunningham v. Fairhaven Nursing Home*, where the Court suggested that "shall" language in a statutory scheme that otherwise uses permissive terms may create only a "duty to act in a particular way *if* the agency chooses to act."

The Ninth Circuit's forthcoming opinion has an opportunity to provide clarity on an issue that extends well beyond immigration law. Many statutory regimes—environmental law, social welfare programs, education funding—use similar blends of "shall" and "authorized" language. How courts resolve this interpretive puzzle in *Pacito* could ripple through administrative law more broadly.

## What Comes Next

For now, the government may continue suspending refugee admissions but must reinstate cooperative agreements to provide services to refugees already admitted. The practical consequence is a bifurcated program: no new admissions, but continuing services for those already here—assuming the government can identify who falls into that category.

The panel has promised a full opinion "in due course." That opinion will need to grapple more thoroughly with Judge Lee's structural argument, explain how the mandatory language in § 1522(a) interacts with the permissive language in subsequent subsections, and clarify what remedy—if any—is appropriate for violations of whatever duty the statute imposes. Until then, the order stands as a reminder that even in areas of presumed executive dominance, statutory text still matters—and reasonable judges can read it quite differently.
