Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of refugee admissions under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The executive order invoked two statutory provisions—8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)—as the legal foundation for its directives. Section 1182(f) provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." Relying on this authority, the President determined in Section 3(a) of the executive order that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings.

The breadth of Section 1182(f) became central to the litigation. As the Ninth Circuit would later observe, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the statute "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" The executive order's suspension of refugee admissions therefore presented a significant question about the scope of presidential authority over immigration—a question the court would ultimately resolve in favor of executive power.

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of foreign aid funding affecting domestic resettlement services

On January 24, 2025, four days after Executive Order 14163, the Department of State suspended all funding of the USRAP program. This action was taken pursuant to a separate executive order—Executive Order No. 14169, entitled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." The funding suspension extended to domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This aspect of the executive action would prove legally distinct from the admission suspension. Whereas Executive Order 14163 operated under the President's authority to suspend entry, the defunding of resettlement services implicated different statutory schemes—specifically, the Administrative Procedure Act and the Refugee Act of 1980's provisions regarding services for admitted refugees.

## The plaintiffs: admitted refugees, conditionally approved refugees, and resettlement organizations with cooperative agreements

The plaintiffs in this litigation fell into two categories: individual refugees and organizational entities. The individual plaintiffs included refugees who had previously been admitted to the United States as well as refugees who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. These individuals had varying relationships with the refugee admissions system—some had already entered and begun the resettlement process, while others had cleared the approval hurdles but had not yet physically arrived.

The organizational plaintiffs comprised three entities that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department. These agreements governed two distinct functions: providing overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and providing resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. The cooperative agreement structure is the mechanism through which the federal government has historically partnered with private nonprofit agencies to carry out refugee resettlement services authorized under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The termination of these agreements following the executive orders thus formed part of the plaintiffs' injury.

## Claims under the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, advancing two principal legal theories. First, they alleged that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102, which is codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The Supreme Court has described the Refugee Act as establishing "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The plaintiffs contended that the executive order's broad suspension conflicted with the statutory framework Congress established for refugee admissions.

Second, the plaintiffs challenged the defunding of USRAP under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. The APA provides a framework for judicial review of agency actions and requires, among other things, that agency actions not be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The plaintiffs' APA claims focused on the termination of funding for resettlement services—services they argued were mandated by statute for refugees already admitted to the United States. This distinction between the admission suspension and the funding termination would prove significant, as the court would ultimately reach different conclusions on these two claims.

## District court preliminary injunctions of February 25, February 28, and March 24, 2025

The district court acted quickly in response to the plaintiffs' motion for preliminary injunctive relief. On February 25, 2025, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of Sections 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States, as well as all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order defining the scope of the February 25 preliminary injunction, specifying that it also prohibits the suspension of USRAP funding. The government filed its notice of appeal that same day. The district court then issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government filed a second notice of appeal on March 25.

The district court's July 14, 2025 order established a framework for enforcing what the Ninth Circuit had called the "limited carveout" from the stay—referring to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This order appointed a magistrate judge to oversee the enforcement framework. The government's subsequent emergency motion on July 17, 2025, sought to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order.

## Prior Ninth Circuit orders: partial stay of March 25, clarifications of April 21 and May 9, and administrative stay of July 18, 2025

The appellate litigation began with the government seeking a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part on March 25, 2025. The panel observed that Section 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions. However, the panel denied the motion for a stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay."

The motions panel issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar. Following briefing but before the scheduled September 19 argument, the district court issued its July 14 order establishing an enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate judge. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion, and on July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's July 14 enforcement order and scheduled expedited oral argument.

The September 12, 2025 order vacated the administrative stay of July 18, vacated the prior stay of March 25 as amended, and stayed the district court's February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety—except for the order directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. The court thus maintained a partial injunction regarding services for admitted refugees while staying the injunction regarding refugee admissions.

# Presidential Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

## Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): The President's Power to Suspend Entry of Aliens Deemed Detrimental to U.S. Interests

The foundation of the government's position in *Pacito v. Trump* rests on a single statutory provision that grants the President sweeping authority over the admission of foreign nationals. Section 1182(f) of Title 8 of the United States Code provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

The court's opinion quotes this statutory language in full, underscoring its significance to the case. Several features of this text merit attention. First, the statute is triggered by a presidential *finding*—a determination that entry of a particular class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States." Second, upon making such a finding, the President "may" suspend entry "for such period as he shall deem necessary." Third, the statute permits the President to act "by proclamation"—a formal executive instrument. Fourth, the scope of authority extends not only to suspension but also to the imposition of "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

In *Pacito*, President Trump invoked this authority in Executive Order 14163, determining "that entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The President then directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings. The plaintiffs challenged this suspension as violating the Refugee Act of 1980, setting up a statutory conflict that the court had to resolve in assessing the likelihood of success on the merits.

## Trump v. Hawaii (2018) and the "Exudes Deference" Standard of Review

The court's analysis of the § 1182(f) issue is shaped almost entirely by the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*. That case arose from a challenge to a different executive order—one commonly known as the "travel ban"—that restricted entry from several predominantly Muslim countries. The Supreme Court's holding in that case established an interpretive framework that the Ninth Circuit found directly controlling.

The *Pacito* court quotes *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" This quoted language does significant work in the court's reasoning. The phrase "exudes deference" signals that judicial scrutiny of presidential action under § 1182(f) is highly constrained. The reference to "ample power" confirms that the statute grants the President broad discretionary authority. The acknowledgment that such power extends to restrictions "in addition to those elsewhere enumerated" suggests that § 1182(f) operates as an independent grant of authority, not merely a gap-filler for situations unaddressed by other provisions.

For the law student, it is essential to understand that *Trump v. Hawaii* did not merely apply deference—it articulated a standard of extreme deference to presidential determinations under § 1182(f). The Supreme Court emphasized that the statute's text leaves the determination of what is "detrimental to the interests of the United States" to the President's judgment. The Ninth Circuit's reliance on this precedent in *Pacito* demonstrates the binding effect of Supreme Court interpretations of statutory grants of executive power.

## The President's "Detrimental to the Interests of the United States" Finding and Its Sufficiency Under § 1182(f)

The court's discussion of the merits reveals just how minimal the judicial role is in assessing the validity of presidential action under § 1182(f). The opinion states plainly: "The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" According to the court, "The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here."

Note what the court does *not* do. It does not evaluate whether the President's determination was correct or reasonable. It does not examine the evidentiary basis for the finding. It does not consider whether the factual premises underlying the determination were sound. Instead, the court treats the presidential finding itself as discharging the statutory prerequisite. So long as the President makes the requisite finding, the statutory condition is satisfied.

The court then extends this reasoning to conclude that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority" to suspend the "entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States." This holding confirms that refugees—as a "class of aliens"—fall within the scope of the President's suspension authority. The Refugee Act of 1980, which established the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program and set forth procedures for refugee admissions, does not strip the President of the power that § 1182(f) confers. To the extent the plaintiffs argued that the Refugee Act limited the President's § 1182(f) authority, the court's analysis suggests such arguments would not likely succeed.

## Why Courts Cannot Engage in "A Searching Inquiry into the Persuasiveness of the President's Justifications"

Perhaps the most significant passage in the court's analysis is its direct quotation from *Trump v. Hawaii*: "we cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This statement defines the outer boundary of judicial review under § 1182(f).

To understand what this means, consider what a "searching inquiry" would entail. A court conducting such an inquiry might demand evidence supporting the President's detrimental-interest finding, evaluate whether the evidence supports the conclusion reached, assess whether less restrictive alternatives could address the identified concerns, or examine whether the stated justifications are pretextual. The Supreme Court explicitly foreclosed such inquiries in *Trump v. Hawaii*, and the Ninth Circuit applied that prohibition in *Pacito*.

The court's statement that it "cannot engage" in such inquiry suggests that this is not merely a matter of judicial restraint or prudential deference—it is a matter of institutional competence and constitutional allocation of power. The judiciary lacks both the expertise and the constitutional authority to second-guess presidential national security and foreign policy determinations made pursuant to a statutory grant of authority.

The practical consequence is that challenges to executive orders issued under § 1182(f) face a nearly insurmountable bar. So long as the President makes the required finding, courts will not probe whether that finding is supported by evidence, whether it is persuasive, or whether it reflects good policy. The finding itself satisfies the statute's textual requirement.

## The Limited Role of Judicial Review in National Security and Foreign Affairs Contexts

The court's analysis of the stay factors reinforces the themes developed in its discussion of § 1182(f). Addressing the remaining factors—"whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding" and "where the public interest lies"—the court states that these factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President," favor the United States as well.

This passage illustrates how deference to the President under § 1182(f) extends beyond the threshold question of statutory validity to the balancing of equities that accompanies any request for interim relief. The court treats the "domestic and foreign interests of the United States" as matters committed to presidential determination. The President's assessment of these interests, like his assessment of what is "detrimental," receives deference.

The interplay between statutory interpretation and constitutional structure deserves attention. Section 1182(f) is a statute—Congress could repeal it or narrow its scope. But while it remains in force, the statute operates to delegate to the President a determination that the Constitution might otherwise commit to the executive branch anyway. Immigration policy and foreign affairs are core executive functions. When Congress enacts a statute like § 1182(f), it is not merely delegating authority—it is recognizing and reinforcing the President's constitutional role in these domains.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent does not challenge this understanding of § 1182(f). Judge Lee agrees "that the President has the authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions and would thus stay the district court's injunction on that issue." The disagreement among the judges concerns a different statutory provision—8 U.S.C. § 1522—and whether it mandates funding for resettlement services. On the § 1182(f) question, the panel was unanimous: the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions is broad, the statutory prerequisites are minimal, and judicial review is limited to confirming that those prerequisites have been facially satisfied.

# Statutory Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligations or Discretionary Authorization?

The central legal dispute in *Pacito v. Trump* concerns whether the federal government is legally required to provide resettlement services to refugees already admitted to the United States, or whether such services are discretionary. This question turns on the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a provision of the Refugee Act of 1980. The panel majority and Judge Lee reach opposite conclusions about what this statute commands, illustrating how statutory interpretation can turn on close readings of textual cues—including section titles, verb forms, and the relationship between different subsections.

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and the statutory framework for refugee resettlement services

The Refugee Act of 1980 establishes the legal framework for how the United States handles refugee admissions and resettlement. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act sets forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The statute is codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act, including 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which addresses domestic resettlement services.

Section 1522 provides the statutory basis for what are commonly called "reception and placement" services—assistance provided to refugees after they arrive in the United States. These services include employment training and placement, English language instruction, cash assistance, and medical services. Under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), these services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." Historically, the State Department has entered into cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations to deliver these services. The question before the court was whether, having admitted refugees, the government is obligated to continue providing these services, or whether it may terminate them at its discretion.

## Majority's reading of § 1522: mandatory duties triggered by refugee admission

The majority concludes that the government is "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding the termination of resettlement services. In reaching this conclusion, the court interprets § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations on the government. The majority reads the statute to require that certain services be provided to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The majority's interpretation rests on several textual foundations. First, the court notes that § 1522 establishes requirements for services that must be provided "to refugees after their admission into the United States." This framing suggests that admission triggers statutory obligations. The court quotes extensively from the statute's operative language, emphasizing provisions that use the word "shall"—a term courts often interpret as mandatory rather than permissive.

## Key statutory language: 'shall,' 'to the extent of available appropriations,' and specific service mandates

The majority's analysis centers on the language of § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement shall, "to the extent of available appropriations," provide several categories of assistance. These include: making available resources for employment training and placement to achieve economic self-sufficiency; providing English language training; ensuring cash assistance is available; and ensuring women have equal opportunities to participate in training. The use of "shall" signals to the majority that these are mandatory duties.

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" functions as a limit on the government's obligation, not as a grant of discretion. To the majority, this language means Congress obligated the government to provide these services when funds are available—it does not give the executive branch discretion to withhold services that Congress has funded.

The majority also highlights § 1522(d)(2)(B), which addresses unaccompanied refugee minors. This provision states that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary" during interim periods before placement. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, providing interim support, medical services, and case management "as needed."

The majority further notes that Congress has appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The existence of these appropriations, combined with the mandatory language of the statute, suggests to the majority that Congress intended for these services to continue. The government's admission at oral argument that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies" to provide reception and placement services reinforces the majority's concern that the government may be failing to fulfill statutory obligations.

## Judge Lee's dissent: the significance of 'Authorization' in the section title

Judge Lee offers a fundamentally different reading of § 1522, one that begins with the section's title. Section 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee emphasizes the word "Authorization," citing the Black's Law Dictionary definition: "permission to do something" or an "official document granting such permission." To Judge Lee, this title signals that the section is permissive rather than mandatory—it grants the executive branch permission to act but does not compel action.

This approach reflects a standard principle of statutory interpretation: that section titles can inform the meaning of statutory text. While titles are not controlling, courts may consult them to resolve ambiguities or confirm textual readings. Judge Lee argues that the word "Authorization" in the title establishes the overall character of § 1522 as granting discretionary authority rather than imposing mandatory duties.

## Use of 'is authorized' language in subsections (b) through (e) as indicators of discretion

Building on the significance of the section title, Judge Lee points to the language used in the specific funding provisions—subsections (b) through (e). Each of these subsections states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance. For example, the subsections on initial resettlement and on cash and medical assistance use this permissive phrasing. To Judge Lee, the repeated use of "is authorized" throughout the operative provisions confirms that Congress granted discretion to the executive branch over whether and how to fund these programs.

This reasoning draws on a basic canon of statutory interpretation: courts generally presume that "may" or "is authorized" creates discretionary authority, while "shall" creates mandatory obligations. Judge Lee argues that the consistent use of authorization language across the specific funding provisions demonstrates Congress's intent to give the executive branch flexibility in administering refugee services.

## The interplay between § 1522(a)'s conditions and the discretionary funding provisions

The majority and Judge Lee fundamentally disagree about how to read the relationship between § 1522(a) and the funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e). Section 1522(a) is titled "Conditions and consideration" and uses the word "shall." The majority relies on this language to find mandatory obligations.

Judge Lee offers a competing interpretation. He argues that § 1522(a)'s mandatory language is qualified by the introductory phrase: "In providing assistance under this section." This phrase, to Judge Lee, means that the "conditions and considerations" in § 1522(a) apply only if the government chooses to provide assistance under the discretionary funding provisions. In other words, if the government exercises its discretion to fund refugee programs, it must meet certain conditions—such as providing employment training aimed at achieving economic self-sufficiency. But the government may also choose not to fund such programs at all.

This interpretive dispute illustrates a common challenge in statutory interpretation: determining how different provisions within a section relate to each other. The majority reads § 1522(a) as independently mandating services. Judge Lee reads § 1522(a) as subordinate to the discretionary funding provisions—setting conditions that apply only if the government chooses to act.

## The interpretive significance of subsection (d)(2)(B) for unaccompanied minors and (e)(7)(A) for interim support

The majority and dissent also differ in their readings of specific provisions addressing unaccompanied minors and interim support—provisions that both sides acknowledge use the word "shall."

Judge Lee addresses § 1522(d)(2)(B), which concerns unaccompanied refugee children. While acknowledging that this provision uses "shall," Judge Lee notes that it requires the Director to assume responsibility only "if necessary" and authorizes the Director "to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." Judge Lee argues that these qualifications—"if necessary" and the authorization language—inject executive discretion into what might otherwise appear mandatory. Moreover, Judge Lee notes that the record does not establish whether any unaccompanied refugee children are actually at issue in this case.

Similarly, Judge Lee reads § 1522(e)(7)(A), which requires the Secretary to develop projects for cash and medical support "as needed," as leaving the scope and nature of such programs to executive discretion. The phrase "as needed" suggests that the executive branch determines when such projects are necessary and what form they should take.

The majority, by contrast, reads these provisions as evidence of mandatory duties. The fact that Congress used "shall" in these specific contexts—even if qualified by phrases like "if necessary" or "as needed"—demonstrates that Congress knew how to impose obligations when it wished to do so. To the majority, these provisions reinforce the broader reading of § 1522 as creating duties, not merely granting permission.

The practical outcome of this interpretive divide is significant. The majority's reading leads to an order requiring the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Judge Lee's reading would have resulted in a complete stay of the district court's injunctions, leaving the government free to terminate services entirely. The split illustrates how different approaches to statutory interpretation—emphasizing different textual cues and reading provisions in different relationships to each other—can produce dramatically different practical consequences.

# Standards for Stay Pending Appeal and the Court's Disposition

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stay Pending Appeal

When a district court issues an order granting preliminary injunctive relief, the losing party may seek a "stay pending appeal"—an order from the appellate court that temporarily suspends the district court's injunction while the appeal proceeds. The Supreme Court established the governing standard in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), articulating four factors that courts must weigh: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likely success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other interested parties; and (4) where the public interest lies.

These factors are not weighted equally. The first two—likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 434. This hierarchical structure makes sense: if an applicant cannot show a reasonable chance of prevailing on appeal, the balance of hardships becomes largely irrelevant. Conversely, without a showing of irreparable harm, the applicant could simply wait for the appeal to run its course. The standard is demanding because a stay pending appeal represents an extraordinary intervention by an appellate court into the ongoing proceedings of a coordinate branch.

In *Pacito*, the government sought a stay of the district court's preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of Executive Order 14163 and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugee services. The Ninth Circuit applied the *Nken* framework to determine whether to grant relief pending resolution of the merits appeal.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits as the 'Most Critical' Factor

The court's analysis of the first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—revealed a divided outcome. On the question of the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions under Executive Order 14163, the court found the government likely to prevail. The panel relied heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which upheld a presidential proclamation restricting entry from several countries under the same statutory provision at issue here: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f).

Section 1182(f) provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." Quoting *Hawaii*, the court emphasized that the statute "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." The panel concluded that "the sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f) is a presidential finding of detriment, and President Trump had satisfied that requirement.

Critically, the court rejected any "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This deferential standard reflects separation-of-powers concerns: when Congress delegates broad authority to the executive branch in matters of immigration and national security, courts generally do not second-guess the President's policy judgments. The court thus determined the government was likely to succeed in defending the validity of Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions.

However, the court reached the opposite conclusion regarding the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges to the termination of funding for refugee resettlement services. The court indicated that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." This split determination on the first *Nken* factor set the stage for the court's bifurcated disposition.

## Irreparable Injury Analysis for Both the Government and the Plaintiffs

The irreparable injury inquiry examines whether the party seeking a stay faces harm that cannot be adequately remedied by monetary damages or other compensatory relief after the appeal is resolved. In *Nken*, the Supreme Court stressed that this factor and likelihood of success are "the most critical" because without irreparable injury, there is no urgency warranting appellate intervention.

For the government, the court implicitly accepted that the executive branch would suffer institutional injury from having to implement a refugee admissions program that the President had determined to be detrimental to national interests. This type of injury—being compelled to act contrary to lawful policy judgments—qualifies as irreparable because it cannot be undone after the fact. Once refugees are admitted, they cannot be "un-admitted," and the executive branch's authority over entry decisions would be undermined.

For the plaintiffs, the organizational appellants and individual refugees, the irreparable injury calculus was different. The organizations faced termination of cooperative agreements that provided essential funding for their operations. Refugees already admitted to the United States risked losing access to statutorily mandated reception and placement services—services designed to help them achieve economic self-sufficiency and navigate their first months in a new country. The court recognized that denial of these services could cause hardship that compensation after the fact could not adequately remedy.

Judge Lee's partial dissent took issue with the majority's analysis of the statutory obligations. He argued that Section 1522 is permissive, not mandatory, and therefore the government faced no legal duty that could justify injunctive relief. This disagreement about the nature of the statutory obligation informed the divergent views on whether irreparable injury existed on the services question.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest Considerations in Immigration and Foreign Affairs

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—substantial injury to other parties and the public interest—converge in cases involving immigration and foreign affairs. Courts recognize that executive decisions in these domains implicate "both domestic and foreign interests of the United States" that are entrusted to the President's "broad discretion." *Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 683–84.

The court in *Pacito* found that these factors favored the government with respect to the admissions suspension. The President's determination that refugee admissions would be detrimental to national interests carried substantial weight. Courts are generally reluctant to override executive judgments about national security and foreign policy, particularly when Congress has delegated broad authority to the executive branch. The panel accordingly concluded that the balance of equities and public interest supported staying the injunction as to admissions.

The calculus shifted, however, regarding resettlement services for refugees already admitted. Congress had enacted specific statutory requirements in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 directing the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide certain services "to the extent of available appropriations." These provisions reflected a congressional judgment that the United States owes certain obligations to individuals it has already admitted as refugees. The public interest in fulfilling statutory commitments to vulnerable populations who have already been granted entry provided a counterweight to executive discretion.

This distinction—between those seeking admission and those already admitted—proved crucial to the court's ultimate disposition. The balance of equities analysis recognized that different considerations apply to different categories of affected individuals.

## The Court's Bifurcated Disposition: Staying the Injunction on Admissions While Reinstating Cooperative Agreements for Resettlement Services

Having applied the *Nken* factors, the court issued a carefully calibrated order. The district court's preliminary injunctions were stayed "in their entirety, except as noted below." The effect was to allow the government to implement Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions while simultaneously requiring compliance with statutory obligations to refugees already in the United States.

This bifurcated approach reflected the court's split determination on likelihood of success on the merits. Because the government was likely to prevail on the validity of the admissions suspension under § 1182(f), the court stayed the injunction blocking that aspect of Executive Order 14163. The President's statutory authority to suspend entry of a class of aliens was clear and entitled to deference.

Conversely, because the government was not likely to prevail on the APA challenges to defunding resettlement services, the court declined to stay that portion of the district court's orders. The statutory framework in § 1522 imposed obligations that the executive branch could not unilaterally set aside through funding termination. The court thus maintained injunctive relief as to the services component, ensuring that statutory mandates would continue to be fulfilled while the appeal proceeded.

Judge Lee dissented from this aspect of the order. He would have stayed the district court's injunctions in their entirety, reading § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandate. This interpretive disagreement—whether statutory language creates a duty or merely permits action—represents a fundamental question of statutory construction that would presumably be addressed in the forthcoming merits opinion.

## The Order to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements for Refugees Already Admitted to the United States

The court's order included specific directives regarding implementation. The government was "directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This requirement extended to "the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

The court grounded this directive in the statutory text of § 1522. Several subsections imposed specific obligations: § 1522(a)(1)(A) required the Director to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance designed to promote economic self-sufficiency. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) required the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children during interim periods. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) required the Secretary of Health and Human Services to develop alternative projects providing interim support, medical services, and case management for recently resettled refugees.

The court noted that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for fiscal year 2025, though this amount was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The existence of appropriated funds triggered the "to the extent of available appropriations" language in § 1522(a), suggesting that spending authority existed.

Significantly, the court highlighted the government's own uncertainty about its ability to provide mandated services. At oral argument, the government "advised the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services"—despite having represented to the district court that it was actively preparing a request for proposals. This gap between representation and reality strengthened the court's conclusion that reinstatement of existing cooperative agreements was necessary to fulfill statutory obligations.

## The Limited Carveout and Its Practical Implications for Resettlement Agencies

The procedural history revealed an earlier attempt to craft a limited exception to the stay. The March 25, 2025 order had created a "limited carveout from the stay" for individuals who were "conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." The motions panel issued subsequent orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025 clarifying this carveout.

This earlier approach attempted to distinguish between refugees at different stages of the admissions process—those who had received conditional approval before the executive order versus those whose applications remained pending. The practical effect was to allow entry for certain refugees whose cases had advanced substantially through the vetting process.

However, the panel's current order vacated the March 25 stay "as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025" and replaced it with a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal. This procedural reset meant that the earlier carveout framework was superseded. The new order instead drew the line between admissions (which could be suspended) and services for those already admitted (which must continue).

For the organizational plaintiffs—the three resettlement agencies that had cooperative agreements with the State Department—the order had immediate practical implications. The agencies were entitled to reinstatement of their agreements for purposes of serving refugees already present in the United States. This restored their ability to provide the reception and placement services that constitute their core mission, at least for that population.

The practical effect of the order was to maintain a functional refugee resettlement infrastructure for individuals already admitted while allowing the executive branch to implement its policy judgment regarding new admissions. The court thus navigated between competing considerations—preserving statutory obligations to refugees who had already entered the country while deferring to the President's authority over entry decisions.

# The Separation of Powers Dimension

## Congressional Power versus Executive Discretion in Immigration Policy

The Pacito v. Trump order presents a fundamental separation of powers question: when Congress legislates in the immigration field, to what extent can the President override or ignore statutory commands by invoking his constitutional and statutory authority over entry and foreign affairs? The court's resolution of this question reveals a split between the President's authority to exclude noncitizens from the country and Congress's authority to mandate how the executive branch treats those already admitted.

The government's primary argument rests on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the statutory provision authorizing the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Citing Trump v. Hawaii, the panel majority acknowledged that this provision "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act." The court emphasized that under § 1182(f), the "sole prerequisite" is that the President "find" that entry of the covered aliens would be detrimental—a finding the court would not second-guess. This reasoning reflects the traditional judicial reluctance to intrude upon the political branches' authority over admission of noncitizens, a domain where executive discretion has long been considered at its zenith.

However, the court drew a critical distinction between the President's authority to suspend entry and his authority to terminate services for refugees already admitted. This distinction tracks the fundamental difference between exclusion—denying entry at the border—and treatment—determining what obligations the government owes to those within its jurisdiction. The majority found that while the President could suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f), Congress had created mandatory obligations for the treatment of admitted refugees through the Refugee Act of 1980, specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The separation of powers analysis thus turned on whether Congress, through legislation, had constrained executive discretion in a manner that the President could not override through executive order.

## The Role of Appropriations in Determining Executive Obligations

A secondary but significant separation of powers question concerns the relationship between congressional appropriations and executive obligations. The majority opinion noted that § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to perform certain functions "to the extent of available appropriations." The court also observed that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, though this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025.

The presence of appropriations language in the statute introduces complexity into the separation of powers analysis. On one hand, appropriations represent Congress's power of the purse—its constitutional authority to control federal spending. On the other hand, the "to the extent of available appropriations" language might suggest that Congress made the executive's obligations contingent on funding, thereby giving the executive discretion to decline services by manipulating funding mechanisms. The majority's order implicitly rejected this interpretation, treating the appropriations language as addressing the scope of services rather than their existence. The court ordered the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide services, suggesting that Congress's appropriation of funds created a corresponding duty to actually provide the services the statute describes.

Judge Lee's dissent took a different view of the appropriations question. In his reading, § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signaled that Congress was merely permitting rather than mandating executive action. The dissent emphasized that authorization is "permissive, not mandatory," defining it as "permission to do something." Under this interpretation, the existence of appropriations does not create an obligation to spend; rather, it creates permission to spend if the executive chooses to operate the programs. This reading would preserve maximum executive discretion, treating congressional appropriations as enabling rather than constraining executive action.

## Judicial Competence to Review National Security and Foreign Policy Determinations

The court's approach to the President's national security justifications illuminates another dimension of separation of powers: the institutional competence of courts to review executive determinations in sensitive domains. The majority quoted Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" when the President acts under § 1182(f). This deference reflects judicial recognition that national security and foreign policy determinations involve considerations—diplomatic relations, intelligence assessments, geopolitical strategy—for which courts lack both expertise and institutional legitimacy.

The court's refusal to question the President's finding that refugee entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States" exemplifies the political question doctrine's practical operation, even when not formally invoked. The court treated the President's determination under § 1182(f) as effectively unreviewable, requiring only that the statutory prerequisite—a presidential finding—be satisfied. The majority thus deferred to the executive branch on the admission suspension while simultaneously asserting judicial authority to enforce statutory mandates for treatment of admitted refugees.

This bifurcated approach suggests a judicial theory of separated spheres: the political branches have plenary authority over admission decisions, but once Congress legislates how the government must treat persons within U.S. territory, the judiciary has an obligation to enforce those statutory commands. The court thus navigated between two separation of powers extremes—total judicial abstention and aggressive judicial oversight—by deferring where executive authority is constitutionally and statutorily at its peak, while asserting judicial authority where Congress has created justiciable legal standards.

## The Debate over Whether Courts Can Mandate Executive Branch Program Operations

The heart of the separation of powers dispute in Pacito v. Trump centers on whether a court can order the executive branch to operate a statutory program that the executive wishes to terminate. The majority's order directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements represents a significant assertion of judicial authority. The court did not merely prohibit executive action; it affirmatively required the executive to enter into or reinstate contracts with private organizations to provide federally funded services.

The government's uncertainty about its ability to provide services appears to have influenced the majority's decision. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing new agencies to provide reception and placement services, despite having represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals." This practical gap between the government's stated intentions and its actual capacity to fulfill statutory obligations created a factual basis for judicial intervention. The court framed its order as ensuring that the government could meet its statutory duties, not as usurping policy-making authority.

Judge Lee's dissent articulated a competing institutional perspective. He argued that Section 1522 "authorizes the executive branch to craft programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow" but "does not require that those programs operate." In his view, "the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services," and therefore the court was "in no position to mandate them." This position reflects a broader theory of executive discretion: when Congress authorizes but does not mandate executive action, courts lack any basis to compel the executive to act. The dissent thus raised a foundational question about the nature of statutory obligations and the judicial role in enforcing them.

## The Practical Implications of Ordering Reinstatement of Terminated Cooperative Agreements

The court's remedy—ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements—raises practical separation of powers concerns about judicial management of executive operations. Cooperative agreements are a specific type of legal instrument used by federal agencies to accomplish public purposes in collaboration with non-governmental partners. Unlike contracts, which are used when the agency's principal purpose is to acquire goods or services, cooperative agreements involve substantial agency involvement in the funded activity. By ordering reinstatement, the court effectively directed the State Department to restore particular contractual relationships with particular organizations.

This remedial order intersects with separation of powers concerns in several ways. First, it intrudes on executive discretion over procurement and contracting decisions—decisions traditionally committed to agency expertise. Second, it requires the executive to spend appropriated funds in a specific manner, potentially conflicting with the executive's interpretation of its statutory discretion. Third, it creates ongoing judicial supervision of executive branch performance, as the court's reference to "usual and customary services" suggests that future disputes about service adequacy may return to the court.

The government's response to this order—whether it can effectively reinstate agreements, whether the organizations can resume operations, whether funding mechanisms can be restored—will test the practical limits of judicial authority. If the executive refuses or claims inability to comply, the court may face the question of whether it can enforce its order through contempt sanctions or other coercive measures—a scenario that would intensify the separation of powers conflict.

## The Broader Significance of the Majority-Dissent Split on Statutory Interpretation Methodology

The disagreement between the majority and dissent reflects a deeper methodological divide in statutory interpretation that carries separation of powers implications. The majority focused on operative statutory language: the use of "shall" in § 1522(a) and specific subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A). Under traditional canons of interpretation, "shall" creates mandatory obligations, while "may" creates discretionary authority. The majority's reading treated these textual commands as binding on the executive, regardless of the broader statutory context.

The dissent employed a competing interpretive methodology, beginning with the statute's title—"Authorization for programs"—and reading the operative language in light of this framing device. Judge Lee argued that the title signals permissive rather than mandatory intent and that the "authorized" language in subsections (b) through (e) confirms that Congress granted discretion rather than imposed duties. The dissent also employed structural reasoning, arguing that § 1522(a)'s "shall" language is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section"—meaning that the mandatory conditions apply only if the government chooses to provide assistance in the first instance.

This methodological split has separation of powers dimensions because different interpretive approaches allocate power differently between the branches. A text-focused approach that emphasizes "shall" as mandatory tends to enforce congressional constraints on executive discretion. An approach that emphasizes statutory titles, structure, and authorization language tends to preserve executive flexibility. The majority's methodology expanded judicial authority to enforce statutory obligations against the executive, while the dissent's methodology would have preserved executive discretion and limited judicial intervention.

The practical stakes of this methodological disagreement extend beyond this case. Future disputes over the executive's obligations under authorization statutes—across immigration, environmental law, social welfare programs, and other domains—will depend on which interpretive approach courts adopt. The Pacito v. Trump order, though interim, signals that at least some courts will read seemingly discretionary authorization statutes as creating enforceable obligations when combined with mandatory language and congressional appropriations, a position with significant implications for the balance of power among the three branches.

# Procedural Significance of Stay Orders and Preliminary Injunctions

## The Distinction Between Administrative Stays and Stays Pending Appeal

The Pacito v. Trump litigation illustrates the important procedural distinction between two types of stay orders that appellate courts employ: administrative stays and stays pending appeal. Understanding this distinction is essential for following the trajectory of this case and similar high-stakes federal litigation.

An administrative stay is a temporary, interim measure designed to preserve the status quo for a brief period while the court considers whether to grant a more durable stay pending appeal. In this case, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay on July 18, 2025, to temporarily halt the district court's July 14 enforcement order while the court scheduled expedited oral argument. The administrative stay served as a "pause button"—a way to freeze the situation without prejudging the merits of the stay request.

By contrast, a stay pending appeal is a more considered ruling that typically follows briefing and sometimes oral argument. It remains in effect throughout the pendency of the appeal unless modified or vacated. The court's March 25 order, as amended in April and May, was a stay pending appeal—it granted partial relief from the district court's preliminary injunction while the appeal proceeded. Similarly, the September 12 order constitutes a stay pending appeal (and "further order of this court") that will govern until the court issues its merits opinion.

The court's September 12 order explicitly vacates both the administrative stay of July 18 and the prior stay pending appeal of March 25 (as amended), replacing them with a new stay pending appeal. This sequence demonstrates how these mechanisms relate to each other: the administrative buy time for deliberation, while stays pending appeal provide stability during the appellate process.

## How Motions Panels Operate and Their Authority to Grant Stays

Federal appellate courts typically handle stay requests through motions panels—small groups of judges (often three) authorized to rule on procedural matters that arise outside the normal course of merits briefing. The Pacito litigation demonstrates the significant authority motions panels wield.

The March 25 stay order was issued by a motions panel, which partially granted the government's request for a stay pending appeal. Notably, this panel did not merely grant or deny the stay wholesale; it crafted a nuanced "limited carveout from the stay," preserving the preliminary injunction's protection for refugees who had been conditionally approved for status before January 20, 2025. This example shows that motions panels can engage in sophisticated, partial relief tailored to the circumstances of the case.

The motions panel also "directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar." This illustrates another function of motions panels: case management. By ordering full briefing, the panel converted what began as an emergency motion into a regular appeal subject to complete argument on the merits.

Importantly, the authority of a motions panel is distinct from that of the merits panel that ultimately decides the appeal. The September 12 order—issued after oral argument before what appears to be the merits panel—vacates and supersedes the motions panel's prior stay orders. This hierarchy reflects the principle that interlocutory rulings (like stays) remain subject to revision as the case progresses.

## The Effect of Staying a Preliminary Injunction on the Underlying Litigation

When an appellate court stays a preliminary injunction, it does not reverse or overturn that injunction—it merely suspends its effectiveness while the appeal proceeds. This distinction is crucial for understanding what happens to the underlying litigation.

In Pacito, the district court issued two preliminary injunctions: one on February 28 (prohibiting enforcement of Executive Order 14163's provisions and the suspension of USRAP funding), and another on March 24 (ordering reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements). Both injunctions blocked the government's refugee policy from taking effect.

The Ninth Circuit's stay orders fundamentally changed the practical landscape. By staying the February 28 injunction as to most refugees, the court allowed the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions to proceed during the appeal. Similarly, the September 12 order stays both district court injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below"—meaning the preliminary injunctions cannot be enforced while the stay remains in effect.

However, the litigation itself continues. The appeal is "submitted for decision," and "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course." The stay merely determines what happens during the interim period before that merits decision. The district court's injunctions remain on the books—they are simply not operative while stayed. If the Ninth Circuit ultimately affirms the district court on the merits, the stay would dissolve and the injunctions would regain their force (unless the Supreme Court intervenes).

## The 'Limited Carveout' Mechanism and Its Enforcement Through Magistrate Judges

One of the most instructive procedural features of this case is the "limited carveout" the motions panel created in its March 25 order. The panel denied the government's stay request "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025."

This created a split situation: the executive order's admission suspension was stayed (and thus could proceed) for most refugees, but the preliminary injunction remained in effect for a specific subset—those with conditional approval before the change in administration. The carveout demonstrates how courts can balance equities by protecting those with the strongest reliance interests while allowing new policy to proceed more broadly.

The carveout's implementation generated its own litigation. On July 14, the district court issued an order "to establish a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee the framework." This illustrates how district courts retain authority to implement and enforce injunctions even during a stay of other aspects. Magistrate judges—judicial officers who assist district judges with various matters—are commonly used for ongoing supervision of complex injunctions.

The government's emergency motion challenging this enforcement order, and the Ninth Circuit's administrative stay of it on July 18, shows the friction that can arise between the district court's supervisory role and the appellate court's stay authority. The September 12 order addresses this by vacating the administrative stay and requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services to already-admitted refugees—effectively resolving the enforcement dispute through the stay order itself.

## Expedited Briefing and Oral Argument Procedures in Immigration Cases

This case demonstrates the accelerated timelines that courts employ when urgent government actions are at issue. The Ninth Circuit heard oral argument on September 3, 2025, on an "expedited basis," and issued its order just nine days later. This speed is typical of immigration cases involving executive branch policies, where both the government's interest in implementing its policy and the plaintiffs' interest in maintaining protections for affected individuals demand prompt resolution.

The procedural posture here was complex: the government had filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, following the district court's two preliminary injunctions. Between the March 25 stay order and the September 12 order, the motions panel issued "two additional orders clarifying this limited carveout from the stay" (April 21 and May 9). This sequence shows how stay orders may require subsequent clarification or modification as circumstances develop or disputes arise about their implementation.

The scheduling of oral argument for September 19, 2025, before the merits panel, and the subsequent July 14 district court enforcement order, created the need for expedited consideration of the emergency motion filed on July 17. The court's willingness to schedule "oral argument in this case on an expedited basis on September 3, 2025" reflects the ongoing nature of stay disputes—they can arise at multiple points during an appeal, not just at the outset.

## The Practical Effect of the Court's Order Pending Issuance of a Full Merits Opinion

The September 12 order represents an important procedural moment: the court has ruled on the stay but has not yet issued its merits opinion. Understanding what such an order accomplishes—and what it signals—is essential for litigants and observers.

First, the order determines what happens on the ground during the appeal. The stay pending appeal means the district court's injunctions (except as modified) do not govern while the appeal proceeds. The government may implement Executive Order 14163's admission suspension, subject to the specific requirements the appellate order imposes.

Second, the order signals the court's preliminary views on the merits. The court's analysis of the stay factors—particularly whether the applicant has made a "strong showing" of likely success on the merits—offers a preview of how the court may rule. Here, the court states that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions," citing the broad deference owed to presidential determinations under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Conversely, the court indicates that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding funding for reception and placement services.

Third, the order may itself create obligations. The court's directive that "the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services" shows that stay orders can do more than simply maintain the status quo—they can impose affirmative requirements. This is particularly significant where, as here, the government represented that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" for replacement services but could not confirm progress at oral argument.

Finally, the order's statement that "An opinion will issue in due course" signals that the court's full reasoning remains forthcoming. The stay order provides the "bottom line" with abbreviated analysis; the merits opinion will address the legal questions more comprehensively. Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent, which engages substantively with the majority's interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, previews the debate that the merits opinion will need to resolve.

# The Legislative Framework: The Refugee Act of 1980 and Its Interaction with INA Provisions

## Historical Background and Purpose of the Refugee Act of 1980

The Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102, represents a watershed moment in U.S. immigration law. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act established "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009). Before 1980, refugee admissions were governed by a patchwork of ad hoc provisions that often responded to specific geopolitical crises. The Refugee Act regularized this process, creating a systematic framework for both the admission of refugees and their subsequent resettlement within the United States.

The Act amended the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) by adding new provisions codified throughout Title 8 of the U.S. Code. These amendments created two interconnected but distinct systems: one governing the admission of refugees into the country, and another governing the services provided to refugees after they have been admitted. This structural duality lies at the heart of the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*, as the parties and judges disagree about whether Congress intended these two systems to operate independently or in tandem.

## The Placement of Refugee Provisions Within the Immigration and Nationality Act Structure

Understanding *Pacito* requires locating the relevant statutory provisions within the broader INA framework. The INA is codified in Title 8 of the U.S. Code, beginning at section 1101. The provisions at issue in this case span different sections of Title 8, reflecting the two-track system Congress created.

The admission-related provisions appear in Chapter 12 of Title 8, which governs "Immigration and Nationality." Specifically, 8 U.S.C. § 1182 enumerates classes of aliens ineligible for visas or admission, while § 1185 governs entry and departure controls. Section 1182(f), invoked by President Trump in Executive Order No. 14163, authorizes the President to suspend the entry of aliens when the President "finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States."

The resettlement provisions appear in a different subchapter altogether—Subchapter II of Chapter 12, which addresses "Immigration"—with 8 U.S.C. § 1522 specifically addressing "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." This structural separation between admission provisions (in § 1182) and resettlement provisions (in § 1522) informs the interpretive dispute between the majority and the dissent.

## The Relationship Between Admission Provisions and Resettlement Provisions

The central statutory tension in *Pacito* concerns the relationship between § 1182(f), which governs entry suspensions, and § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services. The executive order at issue suspended "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP" pursuant to § 1182(f). The government argued that this suspension authority necessarily extended to terminating the funding for resettlement services, because refugees who cannot enter cannot receive domestic resettlement services.

The panel majority implicitly rejected this connection. While agreeing that § 1182(f) grants the President broad authority to suspend refugee admissions—a point both the majority and Judge Lee's partial dissent accepted—the majority drew a line at services for refugees already admitted to the United States. In the court's view, the statutory duty to provide resettlement services under § 1522 continues to apply to refugees who have already been admitted, regardless of whether the President has suspended future admissions under § 1182(f).

Judge Lee's partial dissent would have treated the two provisions more holistically, staying the injunctions in their entirety. In his view, because § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs" rather than "Requirement," the provision is permissive rather than mandatory. This interpretive dispute illustrates how the structural relationship between admission and resettlement provisions remains contested even within the same panel.

## Congressional Intent Regarding the Balance Between Executive Discretion and Mandatory Protections

The Refugee Act reflects Congress's attempt to balance executive flexibility in admission decisions with substantive protections for admitted refugees. This balance becomes apparent when examining the contrasting language across the statutory provisions.

Section 1182(f) uses open-ended language: the President "may" suspend entry when he "finds" that such entry "would be detrimental." As the Supreme Court observed in *Trump v. Hawaii*, this language "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions." 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018). The sole prerequisite is that the President make the requisite finding—a low threshold the Court described as "undoubtedly fulfilled" by a presidential determination.

Section 1522, by contrast, uses mandatory language alongside permissive language in ways that generated interpretive disagreement on the panel. The majority focused on the word "shall" in subsections (a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A), reading these provisions as imposing duties on the government to provide specified services. The majority noted that § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance.

Judge Lee's partial dissent emphasized the statute's title—"Authorization for programs"—and the repeated use of the word "authorized" throughout subsections (b) through (e). In his view, § 1522(a) merely sets forth "conditions and considerations" that apply *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretionary authority under the subsequent subsections. This interpretive divide demonstrates how the same statutory text can support competing readings of congressional intent regarding the balance between discretion and obligation.

## The Role of the Office of Refugee Resettlement and the State Department

The Refugee Act divides implementation responsibilities between two executive agencies, a structure the opinion discusses in analyzing the government's obligations. The Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), located within the Department of Health and Human Services, bears primary responsibility for domestic resettlement services. Section 1522 specifically directs the ORR Director to carry out the functions described in that provision.

The State Department, meanwhile, has historically administered the overseas processing of refugee applications. The organizational plaintiffs in *Pacito* held cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide "overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." This division of labor between ORR (domestic services) and State Department (overseas processing and initial reception) reflects the two-track system Congress created.

Critically, both agencies operate through cooperative agreements with private nonprofit organizations rather than providing services directly. Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) authorizes services to be provided through "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The State Department's termination of these cooperative agreements—which the government represented would be replaced by a "new resettlement agency"—triggered much of the present litigation. At oral argument, however, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers.

## How Appropriations Legislation Interacts with Statutory Mandates

The opinion highlights an important feature of refugee resettlement law: the interaction between statutory mandates and the appropriations process. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) qualifies its mandatory language with the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations." This phrase, central to the interpretive dispute, could be read either as a limit on the government's obligation (services are only required to the extent Congress has funded them) or as a trigger (once appropriations are available, the duties take effect).

The opinion notes that for fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, though this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025. The existence of some appropriation suggests that funds remain "available" within the meaning of § 1522(a)(1)(A), even if reduced from initial levels. Judge Lee's dissent, however, suggests that the "to the extent of available appropriations" language merely reinforces that § 1522 operates at executive discretion—if Congress appropriates funds, the executive may use them, but is not required to do so.

This appropriations dimension connects to a practical concern the majority raised: the government's uncertainty about its ability to provide statutorily mandated services. The court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted. This remedy suggests the majority read the appropriations clause not as defeating the statutory duty, but as ensuring the duty could be fulfilled once Congress has provided funding.

The interpretive questions the panel divides on—whether § 1522 creates mandatory obligations or permissive authorizations, whether the "shall" language overrides the "authorization" framing, and how appropriations interact with duties—demonstrate that the Refugee Act's framework continues to generate disputes about the proper balance between executive discretion and congressional mandates. Understanding this legislative framework is essential to following the merits opinion that will follow this interim order.

# The Standard of Judicial Review in Immigration and National Security Cases

## The Doctrine of Deference to the Political Branches in Immigration Matters

The court's analysis in *Pacito v. Trump* begins from a foundational premise that runs throughout immigration law: courts accord extraordinary deference to the political branches—particularly the executive branch—when they act in matters of immigration and national security. This doctrine of deference reflects the Constitution's allocation of foreign affairs and immigration powers primarily to the political branches, not the judiciary. The Supreme Court has long recognized that "the authority to control immigration—how and when noncitizens may enter and remain in the United States—is vested primarily in the political branches of government."

In *Pacito*, this deference shapes the entire analysis. The court does not approach the President's suspension of refugee admissions as it would a typical agency action under the Administrative Procedure Act. Instead, it recognizes that when the President acts pursuant to statutory authority affecting immigration and foreign policy, courts must step back. Judge Lee's concurrence acknowledges this starting point, noting that "the President enjoys vast discretion and deference in immigration matters." This consensus among the panel members—even as they disagree on other issues—signals how deeply entrenched this doctrine is in federal immigration jurisprudence.

The practical consequence is that the government, as the stay applicant, faces a lower burden in showing likelihood of success on the merits for the admission suspension claim. The deference embedded in the governing statute effectively does much of the analytical work for the government.

## Trump v. Hawaii and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The central precedent guiding the court's analysis is *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), the Supreme Court decision upholding President Trump's "travel ban" Proclamation. The Ninth Circuit quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." This phrase—"exudes deference"—is not merely rhetorical flourish; it establishes the governing standard of review.

*Hawaii* addressed a challenge to Proclamation 9645, which restricted entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. The Supreme Court held that § 1182(f) grants the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens he determines would be detrimental to the national interest. Critically, the Court rejected the plaintiffs' request that it conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito* directly imports this language, stating it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This is a limitation on the court's role: it is not the judiciary's function to second-guess the wisdom or accuracy of the President's national security and foreign policy determinations.

The "exudes deference" standard means that the statutory text is interpreted broadly in favor of executive authority. When the court quotes *Hawaii*'s statement that § 1182(f) is "clear" and does not impose "any textual limit on the President's authority," it is signaling that the statute's grant of power is essentially unlimited within its four corners. The President need only make the required finding—that entry of a class of aliens would be detrimental—and the statutory prerequisite is satisfied.

## The Limited Scope of Judicial Review of Executive Determinations Under § 1182(f)

Section 1182(f) provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The Ninth Circuit's analysis focuses on what this text requires—and significantly, what it does not.

The court emphasizes that "The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" Once that finding is made, the statute grants the President authority to suspend entry "for such period as he shall deem necessary" and to impose "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The repeated use of "deem" reinforces congressional delegation of discretionary judgment to the executive.

In *Pacito*, President Trump's Executive Order 14163 includes the requisite finding: it states that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [USRAP] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court concludes that "The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here." This statement is remarkable for its brevity and finality. The court does not examine whether the factual basis for this finding is sound, whether the President considered the right factors, or whether the finding is reasonable. The inquiry ends at the existence of the finding itself.

This approach leaves almost no room for meaningful judicial scrutiny. So long as the President articulates the statutory finding—even if only in conclusory terms—the court's role under § 1182(f) is essentially satisfied. The court confirms this when it states that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority."

## The Distinction Between Reviewing the Facial Legality of Executive Action Versus Its Substantive Justifications

A critical distinction emerges from the court's analysis: the difference between reviewing whether executive action is facially lawful and examining the substantive justifications for that action. The former is a minimal threshold inquiry; the latter is precisely what *Hawaii* forbids.

Facial legality review asks whether the President acted within the statutory grant of authority. Did the President invoke the correct statute? Did the executive order contain the required finding? Did the action fall within the category of actions the statute permits? In *Pacito*, the court conducts this analysis and concludes that Executive Order 14163 "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority" to suspend entry of "any class of aliens."

What the court does not do—and explicitly says it cannot do—is probe the substance of the President's determination. The court quotes *Hawaii*'s prohibition on "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This means the court will not ask whether refugees actually pose a detriment to national interests, whether the President's assessment of that detriment is factually supported, or whether less restrictive alternatives might achieve the same goals. The President's judgment on these matters is effectively unreviewable.

This distinction has profound implications for litigants challenging executive action under § 1182(f). Arguments about policy errors, factual mistakes, or pretextual motivations face an almost insurmountable barrier. The court's role is confined to checking whether the statutory boxes are ticked, not whether the executive's policy judgments are sound.

## How the Court's Approach in This Case Fits Within Broader Administrative Law Principles

The deference applied in *Pacito* might seem jarring to students familiar with administrative law doctrines like *Chevron* deference or "hard look" review under the APA. Those frameworks generally require courts to examine whether agency action is "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law." Yet the court's review of the refugee suspension claim operates under a different and far more deferential framework.

This divergence reflects a fundamental distinction in administrative law: not all executive action is subject to the same standard of review. When an agency acts pursuant to its delegated authority in areas of domestic economic regulation, courts may engage in searching review of the agency's reasoning and factual support. But when the President acts directly—rather than through an agency—in areas touching on foreign affairs, immigration, and national security, the standard shifts dramatically.

The APA does apply to some aspects of immigration policy, as the court's analysis of the resettlement services issue demonstrates. The court finds that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding the termination of cooperative agreements for refugee services. This shows that APA review remains available for certain executive actions, particularly those involving domestic implementation of statutory programs. The distinction turns on both the nature of the action (immigration admission decisions versus domestic service provision) and the statutory framework governing it (§ 1182(f)'s open-ended delegation versus § 1522's more specific mandates).

Thus, *Pacito* illustrates how administrative law principles apply unevenly depending on context. The same executive branch can face highly deferential review for one action and more rigorous scrutiny for another, based on the statutory scheme and the subject matter involved.

## The Tension Between Meaningful Judicial Review and Deference to Executive Expertise

The court's approach in *Pacito* raises a fundamental tension in constitutional and administrative law: the role of the judiciary in reviewing executive action. On one hand, the framers of the Constitution designed courts to provide a check on executive power and to protect individual rights. The very concept of judicial review presupposes that courts will engage in meaningful scrutiny of government action. On the other hand, the political branches possess expertise, accountability, and constitutional authority that courts lack, particularly in domains like foreign affairs and national security.

The deference standard applied in *Pacito* resolves this tension almost entirely in favor of executive authority. The court's statement that it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications'" signals judicial restraint that borders on judicial abstention. Judge Lee's partial dissent illustrates how far this deference extends: while the majority preserves some obligations for already-admitted refugees, Judge Lee would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety," arguing that even domestic resettlement services are discretionary.

The tension is particularly acute where, as here, the executive order affects vulnerable populations who have already been vetted and approved for refugee status. The plaintiffs include refugees "who were previously admitted to the United States or who have been approved for resettlement." Yet even for these individuals, the court applies the same deferential framework, concluding that the President's determination of detrimental impact precludes meaningful judicial examination of the policy's substance.

Students should consider whether this level of deference is compatible with the judiciary's role as a check on executive power. The court's analysis suggests that in immigration and national security matters, the check is minimal: courts ensure only that the executive has identified the proper statutory hook, not that it has exercised its power wisely or fairly. Whether this allocation of institutional roles is appropriate remains one of the central debates in administrative and constitutional law.

# The Majority Opinion's Bifurcated Analysis

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* presents a striking example of how a court can grant a stay in part while denying it in part, based on fundamentally different legal analyses for different aspects of the same executive action. The court reached opposite conclusions about two provisions of the same executive order and related agency actions: it found the government likely to succeed on the merits of suspending refugee admissions, but unlikely to succeed on the termination of funding for resettlement services. Understanding this bifurcated analysis requires examining how the court applied different statutory frameworks to distinguish between the President's authority over entry and the executive branch's obligations to those already admitted.

## Why the Admissions Suspension Falls Squarely Within Presidential Authority

The court's analysis of the refugee admissions suspension begins and ends with 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision the Supreme Court construed in *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018). Section 1182(f) provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." The Ninth Circuit emphasized that this language "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions."

The court's analysis here is notable for its brevity and its reliance on the Supreme Court's prior interpretation. The sole prerequisite under § 1182(f) is that the President "find" that entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Executive Order No. 14163 contained precisely such a finding, determining that entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Once that finding is made, the statute imposes no additional textual limits on the President's authority to suspend entry. The court quoted *Hawaii* for the proposition that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This portion of the analysis demonstrates a critical feature of immigration law: the broad deference accorded to the political branches, particularly the President, on matters of admission and exclusion. The court did not evaluate whether the President's determination was correct or wise. Rather, it asked only whether the statutory prerequisites were satisfied—and found them plainly satisfied on the face of the executive order.

## The Government's Admission of Uncertainty Regarding Ability to Provide Mandatory Services

The court's analysis shifted dramatically when it turned to the funding suspension for refugee resettlement services. Unlike the admissions question, which turned on the President's broad discretionary authority, the services question required the court to interpret specific statutory mandates in 8 U.S.C. § 1522. This provision creates obligations for the Office of Refugee Resettlement within the Department of Health and Human Services.

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director, "to the extent of available appropriations," to provide employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and ensure equal opportunities for women. The court also highlighted provisions concerning unaccompanied minor refugees, which require the Director to "assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" during interim periods, and provisions requiring the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects" providing interim support, medical services, and case management for recently resettled refugees.

A pivotal moment in the court's reasoning came from the government's own representations. At oral argument, the government "advised the panel" that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services." This admission proved crucial. The court had previously noted that the government represented to the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." The gap between that representation and the government's inability at oral argument to confirm any progress signaled to the court that the statutory mandate might go unfulfilled.

The court interpreted this uncertainty as evidence that the government could not currently satisfy its statutory obligations under § 1522. Where the statute imposes duties—such as assuming responsibility for unaccompanied minors or providing interim support—the government's lack of a functioning mechanism to deliver these services suggested a likely violation of law.

## The Significance of the Government's Representation About Preparing New Resettlement Contracts

The government's March 10, 2025 representation to the district court—that it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency—played an important role in the court's analysis. This representation acknowledged, implicitly or explicitly, that some mechanism for providing reception and placement services was necessary. The government did not argue that it could cease providing all services; rather, it suggested that services would continue through a different contractual arrangement.

Yet by the time of oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made on this new contracting process. This discrepancy between representation and reality undermined the government's position that the termination of existing cooperative agreements was lawful. The court inferred that the government had terminated the existing agreements without having replacement services in place, creating a gap in statutorily mandated services.

This aspect of the opinion illustrates a practical principle of administrative law: agencies may not represent to courts that they are taking steps to comply with statutory mandates while failing to follow through. The government's inability to report progress on securing alternative service providers contradicted its earlier assurances and suggested that the funding termination was not simply a policy choice but a potential statutory violation.

## How the Court Distinguished Between Entry Decisions and Post-Admission Obligations

The fundamental distinction underlying the court's bifurcated analysis is the difference between admission and post-admission treatment. The authority to deny entry to a class of aliens under § 1182(f) operates prospectively—it governs who may cross the border. Once an individual has been admitted, however, different statutory provisions apply, and those provisions may create affirmative obligations.

The court treated refugee admissions as within the President's broad discretionary authority over entry. But refugees who had already been admitted to the United States stood in a different position. For them, § 1522's provisions for "reception and placement" services applied. These services are designed to help refugees achieve "economic self-sufficiency" and become "effectively resettled." The statute does not condition these services on ongoing presidential approval of refugee admissions.

This distinction reflects a basic structure of immigration law: the line between exclusion and treatment. The political branches have nearly plenary authority to determine who may enter, but once the government admits individuals and Congress has enacted statutory protections for them, those protections bind the executive branch. The court thus rejected the government's apparent argument that the President's suspension authority under § 1182(f) also authorized cutting off statutorily mandated services to those already admitted.

## The Narrow Tailoring of the Order: Reinstating Only Necessary Cooperative Agreements

The court's remedy was carefully circumscribed. Rather than invalidating the funding suspension altogether, the court directed the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This limitation serves multiple functions.

First, the order applies only to refugees who have already been admitted—not to those whose admissions were suspended under Executive Order No. 14163. The court thus preserved the President's suspension of new admissions while ensuring that those already present receive services Congress has mandated. Second, the order requires reinstatement only of "such cooperative agreements necessary" to provide the required services. The court did not mandate the reinstatement of every terminated agreement, only those essential to fulfilling statutory obligations.

Third, the order addressed only reception and placement services—the specific services described in § 1522. Other aspects of the funding suspension, such as overseas processing support, were not addressed in this portion of the order. The court's narrow tailoring reflects the preliminary nature of its ruling: it preserved the status quo only to the extent necessary to prevent likely statutory violations pending full merits review.

## The 'Usual and Customary Services' Standard and Its Practical Application

The court's order specified that reinstated services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This standard provides practical guidance for implementing the order while avoiding micromanagement of agency operations.

By referencing "usual and customary services," the court looked to the historical practice of the refugee resettlement program. The organizational plaintiffs had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide reception and placement services; those agreements presumably specified the scope and nature of services. The court's order effectively requires the government to maintain continuity with established practice rather than inventing new service configurations.

This standard also creates a manageable benchmark for compliance. The government need not guess what services the court expects; it need only provide what it has historically provided under similar agreements. At the same time, the standard provides flexibility—the court did not specify every service in detail, leaving room for reasonable adjustments as circumstances warrant.

The "usual and customary" formulation also signals the preliminary nature of the court's ruling. On fuller merits review, the court may address whether specific services are statutorily required or whether different service configurations might satisfy statutory mandates. For now, the court preserved existing arrangements to prevent irreparable harm to refugees while the appeal proceeds.

# Judge Lee's Dissent: A Competing Interpretation of Statutory Structure

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a sharply different approach to interpreting 8 U.S.C. § 1522, reaching the opposite conclusion from the majority on the critical question of whether the government is legally obligated to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. Understanding this disagreement requires examining how Judge Lee deploys traditional tools of statutory construction—particularly her focus on statutory titles, organizational structure, and the distinction between permissive and mandatory language.

## The Significance of Section Titles in Statutory Interpretation

Judge Lee begins her analysis with a tool that many law students overlook: the title of a statutory section. The section at issue is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." For Judge Lee, this single word—"authorization"—is decisive. She invokes a standard definition from Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "authorization" as "permission to do something" or the "official document granting such permission." This definition, in her view, signals congressional intent to permit rather than compel executive action.

This approach reflects a broader principle of statutory interpretation: courts may consider a section's title as evidence of legislative meaning. While titles are not always controlling—Congress sometimes uses imprecise headings—the title here, according to Judge Lee, provides the "first signal" of how the statute should be read. By contrast, the majority does not directly address the significance of the title, instead focusing on the operative language within the statute's subsections. This divergence illustrates how different judges can weight the same interpretive tools differently.

## The Authorization Versus Mandate Distinction

The heart of Judge Lee's dissent turns on a fundamental distinction in legislative drafting: the difference between provisions that authorize action and those that mandate it. When Congress intends to compel executive action, Judge Lee argues, it typically uses unambiguous mandatory language such as "shall" or "must" in an unqualified manner. When Congress instead uses "authorized" or similar permissive terms, it grants discretion to the executive branch to act—or not act—as it sees fit.

Judge Lee points out that the specific funding provisions within § 1522—including subsections (b) through (e)—repeatedly state that the "Director is authorized" to provide various forms of assistance. She reads this language as a consistent signal that Congress has given the executive branch discretion over whether to establish and maintain these programs. In her view, the majority's contrary interpretation ignores this repeated use of permissive terminology throughout the statute's operative provisions.

## Structural Reading of § 1522: Subsections (b) Through (e)

Judge Lee's approach exemplifies what is sometimes called "structural" or "holistic" statutory interpretation—reading the provision as a coherent whole rather than isolating individual phrases. She argues that the majority makes a critical error by focusing on subsection (a) without adequately accounting for how it relates to the provisions that follow.

According to Judge Lee, subsections (b) through (e) represent the statute's core operative provisions—these are the sections that actually describe what services might be provided: initial resettlement programs, cash assistance, medical assistance, and related support. Each of these provisions states that the Director "is authorized" to provide the described assistance. For Judge Lee, this structure reveals that Congress created a discretionary grant of authority, not a mandatory obligation.

## Interpreting "Shall" Language as Conditioned on Discretionary Funding

Perhaps the most technically sophisticated aspect of Judge Lee's dissent is her response to the majority's reliance on the word "shall" in subsection (a). The majority points to language requiring the Director to "make available sufficient resources for employment training" and to ensure that cash assistance is made available—language that seems to create mandatory duties. Judge Lee counters by reading this language in its full textual context.

She notes that subsection (a) is titled "Conditions and considerations" and begins with a crucial qualifying phrase: "In providing assistance under this section..." This phrase, in Judge Lee's reading, makes clear that the mandatory obligations in subsection (a) apply only if the government has first chosen to provide assistance under the discretionary programs authorized in subsections (b) through (e). In other words, the "shall" language creates requirements that condition how the government must operate any program it chooses to establish—but it does not compel the government to establish such a program in the first place.

This interpretive move demonstrates an important principle: the word "shall," while typically mandatory, derives its meaning from context. A requirement that the government "do X when providing Y" does not necessarily mean the government must provide Y. Judge Lee argues that this is precisely the structure Congress created in § 1522.

## Qualified Obligations in Subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A)

Judge Lee also addresses the specific statutory provisions that the majority cites as evidence of mandatory obligations: subsection (d)(2)(B), concerning unaccompanied refugee children, and subsection (e)(7)(A), concerning cash and medical assistance projects. Even when these provisions use "shall," Judge Lee argues, they contain qualifiers that preserve executive discretion.

Subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children only "if necessary"—language that, in Judge Lee's view, "drips with deference to the executive branch." Similarly, subsection (e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to develop projects for refugee support "as needed"—a phrase that leaves the scope and existence of such projects to executive judgment. Judge Lee also notes that the record does not clearly indicate whether any unaccompanied refugee children are at issue in this case.

## The Institutional Argument Against Judicial Mandates

Beyond textual interpretation, Judge Lee invokes institutional considerations that counsel against the majority's approach. She argues that courts are "in no position to mandate" programs that Congress has not clearly required the executive branch to provide. This reflects a separation-of-powers concern: when statutory language is genuinely ambiguous, courts should not resolve that ambiguity by compelling executive action that Congress did not unequivocally mandate.

This institutional argument connects to Judge Lee's broader view of the role of judicial review in immigration matters. She acknowledges that the President "enjoys vast discretion and deference in immigration matters," and she would stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety—including the portion requiring continued funding for resettlement services. Her dissent thus reflects both a particular reading of the statute and a reluctance to impose judicial oversight on executive discretion in areas where Congress has not spoken clearly.

## Policy Implications of Requiring Executive Programs to Operate

The practical consequence of the majority's interpretation, according to Judge Lee, is to transform a discretionary authorization into a judicially enforceable mandate. If courts can order the executive branch to continue operating programs that Congress merely authorized, the distinction between authorization and mandate loses meaningful effect. Future executives would find their discretion constrained not by congressional command but by judicial decree based on ambiguous statutory language.

Judge Lee's dissent does not dispute that refugees benefit from reception and placement services. Rather, she argues that the question of whether such services must be provided is one for Congress to decide through clear statutory language. Absent such clarity, the political branches—not the courts—should determine whether these programs continue to operate. This disagreement between the majority and the dissent illustrates how statutory interpretation can have profound practical consequences, determining not just the meaning of words but the allocation of power among the branches of government.

# The Role of Appropriations in Determining Statutory Obligations

## The 'to the extent of available appropriations' language and its legal effect

A central question in *Pacito v. Trump* is whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates mandatory obligations for the executive branch or merely authorizes discretionary programs. The answer hinges in part on the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations," which appears in § 1522(a)(1)(A). This phrase represents a common feature in federal statutory schemes: a limitation on agency action tied to the availability of funds.

The majority opinion quotes § 1522(a)(1)(A) as requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide specific services: employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women to participate in training. The statute uses the word "shall"—traditionally signaling a mandatory duty—but immediately qualifies that duty with the appropriations limitation.

For law students, this presents a classic question of statutory interpretation: does "to the extent of available appropriations" mean that Congress created a mandatory obligation subject only to the availability of funds, or does it mean that Congress left the entire matter to executive discretion? The majority and dissent reach opposite conclusions on this question.

The majority treats the appropriations clause as a funding condition rather than a discretion grant. Under this reading, Congress imposed a duty to provide services, and the only question is whether Congress has appropriated sufficient funds. The majority's order directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements assumes that § 1522 creates enforceable obligations that the government must fulfill using available appropriations.

## Congress's FY 2025 appropriation for refugee assistance and the Rescissions Act of 2025

The opinion provides specific figures for refugee assistance funding in fiscal year 2025. Congress initially appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance through Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11), which adopted funding from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147. The Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6), subsequently reduced this amount by $800 million.

This factual detail matters for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Congress did appropriate funds for refugee assistance—funds that the majority believes must be used for the services described in § 1522. Second, the rescission shows that Congress actively adjusted funding levels, which the majority implies does not eliminate statutory obligations. The opinion does not specify the remaining amount after the rescission, but the figures suggest that substantial appropriations remained available for refugee services.

The majority's reference to these appropriations serves its analytical purpose: if § 1522 creates mandatory obligations "to the extent of available appropriations," and Congress did appropriate funds, then the executive branch has a duty to provide the specified services. The rescission reduced but did not eliminate available funding.

## Whether appropriations create a floor or a ceiling for executive obligations

The dispute between the majority and dissent reflects a deeper tension in federal fiscal law: whether an appropriation creates a floor (a minimum the agency must spend) or a ceiling (a maximum the agency may spend). This distinction has significant practical consequences for executive discretion.

Judge Lee's dissent articulates the "ceiling" view: § 1522 authorizes programs but does not mandate them, and the existence of appropriations merely permits rather than requires executive action. Under this interpretation, Congress provided funding that the executive branch may use if it chooses to operate refugee programs, but Congress did not compel the executive to spend those funds.

The majority implicitly adopts a "floor" reading, at least for certain categories of refugees. By ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services, the majority treats the statute as requiring the executive to spend available appropriations on mandated services. The majority does not say that every dollar appropriated must be spent, but it does hold that the government cannot simply refuse to provide services that Congress has directed and funded.

This debate implicates fundamental separation of powers questions. The executive branch typically argues that appropriations are permissive—Congress provides resources, but the President retains discretion over how to use them. Critics of this view counter that when Congress both directs an agency to act and appropriates funds for that purpose, the executive has a duty to implement Congress's will.

## The distinction between authorized programs and appropriated funds

Judge Lee's dissent draws a sharp distinction between authorization and appropriation, a foundational concept in federal budget law. An authorization statute establishes, continues, or modifies agencies and programs. An appropriation statute provides the actual funding that agencies may spend.

The dissent emphasizes that § 1522's title uses the word "Authorization": "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, Judge Lee defines "authorization" as "permission to do something"—permissive language rather than mandatory. This textual argument supports the dissent's conclusion that Congress allowed but did not require the executive branch to establish refugee programs.

Judge Lee further notes that the specific funding provisions in § 1522(b) through (e) use "is authorized" language: "the Director is authorized" to provide assistance. These provisions, according to the dissent, confirm that Congress granted discretion rather than imposed obligations.

The majority does not directly address this authorization argument. Instead, it focuses on § 1522(a), which uses "shall" language, and on specific subsections that impose obligations. The majority thus reads the statute as a mixed scheme: Congress authorized programs in general terms but mandated specific services for certain populations.

## How the majority and dissent differ on whether appropriated funds trigger mandatory services

The core disagreement between the majority and dissent concerns whether the existence of appropriations transforms statutory authorization into mandatory obligation. Both opinions analyze the same statutory text but reach opposite conclusions.

The majority reads § 1522(a) as creating mandatory duties. The statute requires the Director to make available resources for employment training, provide English language training, ensure cash assistance is available, and guarantee women equal opportunities. The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" operates as a funding condition: the duty exists, but its scope is limited by what Congress has appropriated. Because Congress did appropriate funds for refugee assistance, the duty is triggered.

Judge Lee offers a different reading. He argues that § 1522(a) applies only "[i]n providing assistance under this section"—meaning that the mandatory language kicks in only if the government chooses to provide assistance under the discretionary provisions that follow. The "conditions and considerations" in § 1522(a) thus apply only when the executive exercises its discretion to fund programs; they do not compel the executive to fund programs in the first instance.

The dissent also addresses specific subsections that the majority cites. For unaccompanied minors under § 1522(d)(2)(B), Judge Lee notes that the Director's responsibility arises only "if necessary"—language that retains executive discretion. For cash and medical assistance under § 1522(e)(7)(A), the dissent points out that programs must be provided "as needed," leaving the executive to determine what is needed.

These competing interpretations illustrate a fundamental divide over statutory construction. The majority reads the statute to create rights that refugees can enforce; the dissent reads it to grant the executive branch flexibility that courts cannot second-guess.

## The legal significance of the State Department's termination of cooperative agreements

The practical consequence of these interpretive disputes centers on the cooperative agreements that the State Department terminated. Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) provides that resettlement services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The organizational plaintiffs in this case held such cooperative agreements.

The majority opinion notes that the State Department terminated these agreements in February 2025, shortly after the district court's initial injunction. The government represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." Yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers.

This factual finding drives the majority's remedy. Because the government expressed "uncertainty regarding its ability to provide" statutorily mandated services, the court ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. The majority treats the termination of agreements as an executive action that cannot override statutory duties: if § 1522 requires services, the government cannot avoid that obligation by terminating the contracts through which services are delivered.

The dissent would likely respond that because § 1522 does not mandate services, the executive remains free to structure—or discontinue—programs as it sees fit. Judge Lee does not specifically address the cooperative agreements, but his reasoning implies that courts have no basis to order their reinstatement when Congress has not required the underlying services.

This practical dispute highlights what is at stake in the interpretive debate. The majority's reading gives effect to what it perceives as Congress's intent to provide services to vulnerable populations. The dissent's reading preserves what it sees as constitutional executive discretion over program implementation. Both invoke statutory text, but each emphasizes different provisions and different interpretive principles.

# Key Takeaways and the Pending Merits Opinion

## The Immediate Practical Effect of the Stay Order

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* produces a bifurcated outcome with significant real-world consequences. As a practical matter, the stay order allows the executive branch to implement the refugee admission suspension while simultaneously compelling the government to maintain certain services for refugees already admitted to the United States. This means the government may block new refugees from entering the country, but must continue providing reception and placement services to those refugees who have already been admitted.

Understanding what a "stay pending appeal" actually does is essential. A stay does not reverse the district court's injunction; rather, it presses pause on the injunction's operation while the appellate court considers the appeal. Here, the Ninth Circuit lifted its previous stays and issued a new stay that freezes the district court's orders—except for the portion requiring continuation of reception and placement services. The order explicitly directs the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

The practical effect is that refugee resettlement organizations—like the three organizational plaintiffs—must have their cooperative agreements reinstated for the limited purpose of serving already-admitted refugees. The court's frustration with the government's uncertainty about providing these services is palpable: despite representing on March 10 that it was "actively preparing" to secure a new agency, the government could not confirm any progress at the September oral argument. This gap between representation and reality appears to have influenced the court's decision to maintain the service requirement.

## What the Court Left Undecided Pending the Merits Opinion

The panel was explicit that this order is interim in nature. The opening sentence announces that "[a]n opinion will issue in due course," and the discussion section reiterates that "[a]n opinion on the merits will issue in due course." This procedural posture is important for students to understand: the court has made preliminary determinations about likelihood of success on the merits—the standard for issuing a stay—but has not issued final rulings on the legal questions.

On the admission suspension, the panel expressed confidence in the government's likely success, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. The court signaled that it views the President's authority as broad and that judicial inquiry into the President's justifications is limited. However, even here, the court has not definitively ruled; it has only determined that the government has made a "strong showing" of likely success under the first *Nken* factor.

On the APA challenge to the funding termination, the court reached the opposite preliminary conclusion but provided limited analysis. The order states that "[f]or reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow," the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim. The court's discussion of § 1522's statutory language and the government's uncertainty about service provision offers hints about the merits reasoning, but the full analysis awaits the opinion to come.

The dissent from Judge Lee further highlights what remains contested. Judge Lee's argument that § 1522 is permissive rather than mandatory—a question of statutory interpretation—will presumably be addressed in the merits opinion. The panel majority and the dissent have staked out competing readings of the same statutory text, and the forthcoming opinion will need to explain which interpretation prevails and why.

## The Significance of This Order as Precedent for Future Refugee Policy Disputes

While an interim stay order carries less precedential weight than a full merits opinion, *Pacito* nonetheless signals important principles for future litigation over executive refugee policy. The order reinforces *Trump v. Hawaii*'s teaching that § 1182(f) grants the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens. Future litigants challenging presidential suspension of refugee admissions under this statute face a steep climb; the Ninth Circuit's citation to *Hawaii* suggests that courts will not probe the President's stated justifications with any degree of rigor.

At the same time, the order draws a line: executive authority over admission does not necessarily translate into authority to terminate all associated services. The court's analysis suggests that once refugees are admitted, different statutory provisions govern their treatment. This distinction—between admission and post-admission treatment—may prove significant in future disputes. The Refugee Act of 1980, as described in *Negusie v. Holder*, establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees," and the court appears to read that ongoing treatment obligation as surviving a suspension of new admissions.

The order also demonstrates that procedural history matters. The government's representation that it was preparing to secure alternative service providers, followed by its inability to confirm progress, created a factual record that undercut its position. Future government litigants would be wise to ensure that representations to courts about plans and preparations can be substantiated.

## How the Bifurcated Analysis Reflects Broader Tensions in Immigration Law

*Pacito* exemplifies a fundamental tension in immigration law: the divide between the political branches' plenary power over admission and the competing imperative to protect the rights of noncitizens once present within the United States. The panel's willingness to stay the admission suspension while maintaining the service requirement mirrors this doctrinal boundary.

The admission decision receives deference so profound that it borders on non-justiciability. The court quotes *Hawaii* for the principle that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This deference reflects the longstanding doctrine that the political branches—and particularly the executive—possess near-exclusive authority over who may enter the nation. The "detrimental to the interests of the United States" finding in § 1182(f) requires only that the President make such a finding; it does not require that courts evaluate whether the finding is correct.

Yet once individuals have been admitted, the legal landscape shifts. The court's analysis of § 1522 proceeds without reference to the deference that governed the admission question. Instead, the court engages in traditional statutory interpretation, parsing the text to determine congressional intent. This shift reflects the principle that noncitizens physically present in the United States enjoy greater legal protections than those seeking entry. The organizational plaintiffs' cooperative agreements, and the services those agreements fund for admitted refugees, occupy this more protected legal space.

Judge Lee's dissent identifies this tension and resolves it differently. By reading § 1522 as permissive, Judge Lee would extend executive discretion beyond admission to encompass service provision. The majority's reading preserves a sphere of statutory protection for admitted refugees. This disagreement reflects competing visions of where the line between admission and treatment should be drawn, and how much deference the executive deserves in each domain.

## The Institutional Relationship Between District Courts and Courts of Appeals in Immigration Cases

The procedural history of *Pacito* illustrates the complex interplay between district courts and courts of appeals in fast-moving immigration litigation. The district court here acted quickly: issuing an oral preliminary injunction on February 25, a written order on February 28, and a second preliminary injunction on March 24. Each order sought to preserve the status quo and prevent irreparable harm to refugees and resettlement organizations.

The Ninth Circuit, in turn, moved rapidly to stay portions of those injunctions. The March 25 order, issued by a motions panel, partially stayed the district court's injunction. Subsequent orders on April 21 and May 9 clarified the "limited carveout" for refugees conditionally approved before January 20. The July 18 administrative stay briefly froze the district court's enforcement framework. This back-and-forth—district court injunction, partial stay, clarification, administrative stay—demonstrates how emergency litigation proceeds in the immigration context.

The standard for appellate review of preliminary injunctions differs from the standard district courts apply in issuing them. The district court applied the traditional preliminary injunction factors, concluding that plaintiffs were likely to succeed and would suffer irreparable harm absent relief. The Ninth Circuit, reviewing the government's stay motion, applied the *Nken* factors, which mirror the preliminary injunction standard but from the movant's perspective. This procedural posture means that the appellate court was not reviewing the district court's decision for abuse of discretion, but rather making an independent assessment of whether a stay was warranted.

The district court's July 14 order, establishing an enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate judge, represents an effort to monitor compliance with its injunction. The Ninth Circuit's initial administrative stay of that order, followed by the decision to lift the stay on the service provision component, shows how appellate courts can cabin district court oversight in complex institutional litigation.

## The Potential Implications for Future Challenges to Executive Immigration Actions

*Pacito* offers a roadmap for both litigants and courts in future challenges to executive immigration actions. For challengers, the opinion suggests that claims based on statutory interpretation of post-admission obligations may succeed even when admission-related claims fail. Plaintiffs who can frame their claims around statutory mandates—like the § 1522 requirements—may find more judicial receptiveness than those challenging executive admission decisions.

For the executive branch, the opinion signals that representations to courts about plans and preparations will be scrutinized. The government's inability to confirm progress on securing alternative service providers weakened its position and may have influenced the court's decision to maintain the service requirement. Future administrations would be well advised either to have concrete plans in place before representing them to courts or to acknowledge uncertainty honestly.

The disagreement between the panel majority and Judge Lee over § 1522's interpretation highlights a broader question: how should courts read statutes that confer both authority and funding? The majority reads "shall" provisions as imposing obligations; Judge Lee reads the overall statutory structure as permissive. This question of statutory interpretation—when "shall" means "must" versus when it operates within a broader permissive scheme—will likely recur in immigration litigation and beyond.

Finally, the opinion reinforces that executive power, while vast in immigration matters, is not unlimited. The court's willingness to compel reinstatement of cooperative agreements demonstrates that even in a highly deferential doctrinal environment, courts will enforce statutory limits. The scope of those limits, and the precise boundary between permissible executive discretion and mandatory statutory duty, await clarification in the forthcoming merits opinion.

# Practical Skills: Analyzing Stay Pending Appeal Motions

## How to Structure a Stay Pending Appeal Motion Under Nken Factors

When a district court issues a preliminary injunction, the losing party often seeks a stay pending appeal—a temporary order that freezes the injunction while the appellate court considers the case. The Supreme Court established the governing framework in *Nken v. Holder*, requiring courts to weigh four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The first two factors are "the most critical."

In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit applied this framework to the government's request to stay two preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of Executive Order 14163. The court's analysis demonstrates how practitioners should organize stay motions: lead with likelihood of success, because a strong merits showing can anchor the entire analysis. Here, the court concluded the government was "likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions" under § 1182(f), quoting *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that the statute "exudes deference" to presidential entry restrictions.

Critically, the court then turned to the APA claims—a separate merits question where the government's likelihood of success was less certain. The court found that "for reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow," the government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" regarding statutory mandates under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. This split merits ruling—strong on the executive order, weaker on the funding termination—produced a partial stay, illustrating how stay motions can succeed on some issues while failing on others.

When structuring a stay motion, practitioners should address each *Nken* factor sequentially but recognize their interdependence. A powerful showing on the merits may compensate for weaker irreparable harm arguments, while significant harm to the opposing party might require an even stronger merits showing. The *Pacito* court's approach shows that courts will make nuanced, issue-by-issue assessments rather than all-or-nothing rulings.

## Strategic Considerations for Both Government and Plaintiffs in Stay Litigation

Stay litigation moves quickly and often occurs before full briefing on the merits. In *Pacito*, the timeline was compressed: the Executive Order issued January 20, 2025; the district court entered preliminary injunctions in February and March; and the Ninth Circuit had already issued multiple stay-related orders by the time of oral argument on September 3. This expedited schedule shapes strategic choices for both sides.

For the government, one strategic priority is establishing deference. The *Pacito* court emphasized that it could not engage in "'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications'" and that "'[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President "find[]" that the entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."'" By framing the appeal around broad statutory authority and executive discretion, the government positioned itself for success on the entry suspension question.

However, the government's strategy revealed a vulnerability: representations made to courts can constrain later positions. The court noted that "the government advised the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was 'actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.'" At oral argument, however, "the government advised the panel . . . that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency." This gap between representation and reality undermined the government's position on the APA claims, suggesting it could not fulfill statutory mandates even if it wanted to.

For plaintiffs, the strategic focus is preserving the status quo. Plaintiffs won preliminary injunctions below, so a stay reverses their victory. Their best arguments often focus on irreparable harm and the third *Nken* factor—injury to the parties. Here, plaintiffs included refugees who had been admitted to the United States and organizations that provided resettlement services under cooperative agreements. The court's order partially accommodated these interests by directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

A key strategic lesson: parties should anticipate that stay orders may create hybrid outcomes. The *Pacito* court stayed the injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below," while simultaneously directing specific performance on the cooperative agreements. Neither side got everything, but both preserved core interests for the merits appeal.

## The Importance of the Record in Demonstrating Irreparable Harm

The second *Nken* factor—irreparable injury—requires more than speculation. Courts look to the record for concrete evidence of harm that cannot be undone through monetary damages or later relief. *Pacito* illustrates how factual uncertainty in the record can drive outcomes.

On the government's side, the court accepted that enforcing the preliminary injunctions pending appeal would cause irreparable harm by requiring continued operation of a refugee admissions program the President had determined was "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court's reference to "broad discretion" conferred on the President and "domestic and foreign interests" suggests that executive branch judgments about national security and foreign policy are entitled to particular weight in the irreparable harm analysis for immigration cases.

For plaintiffs, the record revealed a critical gap: the government had terminated all cooperative agreements but had no clear plan to replace them. When asked at oral argument, the government could not confirm progress on securing new agencies to provide services. This record failure directly supported the court's order requiring reinstatement of the cooperative agreements. The court noted it was acting "in light of the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522."

Practitioners should ensure the record documents specific harms with supporting evidence. Affidavits, declarations, and documents showing the practical consequences of a stay (or its denial) give courts concrete footing. Vague assertions of harm are easily rejected. Conversely, admissions or uncertainty—like the government's inability to report progress on new contracts—can become the factual basis for court-ordered relief.

The *Pacito* order demonstrates that the record on irreparable harm is not static. Events occurring after the district court's injunction—such as representations to the court about future plans, or the failure to fulfill those plans—can become part of the appellate court's stay analysis. Practitioners should monitor developments and be prepared to update the court on material changes.

## How Courts Weigh Public Interest Arguments in Immigration Cases

The fourth *Nken* factor—public interest—takes on distinctive dimensions in immigration cases. Courts recognize that immigration policy implicates foreign relations, national security, and humanitarian concerns, often pulling in different directions.

In *Pacito*, the court's public interest analysis was succinct but telling: "The remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President, favor the United States as well." This formulation subordinates the court's independent judgment to the executive's assessment of national interest. The court did not attempt to weigh for itself whether suspending the refugee program served the public interest; instead, it deferred to the President's determination.

This deference has strategic implications. When the government argues public interest in immigration cases, it should anchor those arguments in executive branch determinations, citing the President's statutory authority and foreign policy expertise. Courts are generally reluctant to second-guess such judgments. As the court observed, citing *Hawaii*, the statutory language "exudes deference."

However, *Pacito* also shows limits to this deference. Even while deferring to the President on entry restrictions, the court still ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted. The public interest analysis thus balanced presidential discretion against competing interests: refugees legally present in the United States who needed services Congress had authorized by statute. The public interest, in other words, includes faithful execution of statutory mandates as much as deference to presidential policy judgments.

For plaintiffs, public interest arguments should focus on the harms from non-enforcement of statutory protections, the rule of law, and humanitarian interests Congress has codified. While courts may defer to the President on who may enter, they may be less deferential regarding obligations to those already admitted.

## The Relationship Between the Stay Standard and the Preliminary Injunction Standard

Stay pending appeal and preliminary injunction analyses share common elements but serve different functions. A preliminary injunction freezes proceedings pending trial; a stay pending appeal freezes a lower court order pending appellate review. This difference affects how courts apply the relevant standards.

Both analyses ask about likelihood of success on the merits. But for a stay, the question is whether the appellant is likely to succeed in overturning the order below. The *Pacito* court's merits analysis demonstrates this: it assessed whether the government would prevail on appeal of the district court's injunction. The court concluded the government was likely to succeed on the Executive Order's validity but not on the APA challenge—leading to a partial stay.

Both analyses also consider irreparable harm, but the focus shifts. For a preliminary injunction, courts ask whether the moving party will suffer irreparable harm without injunctive relief. For a stay, courts ask whether the appellant will suffer irreparable harm from enforcement of the order being appealed. In *Pacito*, the government argued it would be irreparably harmed by being forced to continue a refugee program it had determined was detrimental. The court accepted this, staying the injunctions barring Executive Order enforcement.

The relationship between the standards also affects the standard of review. The Ninth Circuit reviews preliminary injunctions for abuse of discretion but may review underlying legal conclusions de novo. This means a stay motion gives the appellate court an early opportunity to signal how it will treat legal questions on appeal. The *Pacito* court's conclusion that the government was "likely to prevail" on § 1182(f) strongly suggests the merits opinion will reach the same result on that issue.

Practitioners should recognize that a stay motion is essentially a preview of the merits appeal. Arguments that persuade the motions panel on likelihood of success often persuade the merits panel as well. Conversely, a loss on the stay motion does not end the case, but it signals an uphill battle on the merits.

## Drafting Effective Stay Orders: Lessons from the Court's Approach

The *Pacito* order provides a model for drafting stay orders that are clear, practical, and enforceable. Several drafting lessons emerge.

First, the order clearly states what is stayed and what is not. The court specified that "the district court's orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety, except as noted below." This prevents confusion about what relief remains in effect.

Second, the order provides specific direction on the unstayed portion rather than leaving implementation to speculation. The court directed the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." The order further specified that "such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This level of detail gives parties clear marching orders.

Third, the order explains the court's reasoning. The court briefly addressed each *Nken* factor, citing precedent and explaining why the factors favored a stay on the Executive Order but not on the APA claims. This reasoned approach facilitates compliance and provides a roadmap for future proceedings. Judge Lee's partial dissent illustrates how even disagreement with the outcome benefits from clear reasoning—his argument that § 1522 is an authorization, not a mandate, sets up the issue for the merits opinion.

Fourth, the order acknowledges procedural context. The court noted that briefing was complete and oral argument heard, the case was "submitted for decision," and "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course." This signals to the parties that the stay order is an interim measure, not the final word.

Finally, the order vacates prior stay-related orders, ensuring there is no conflicting authority. The court vacated the administrative stay of July 18 and the stay of March 25 "as amended on April 21 and May 9." This housekeeping prevents confusion about which order governs.

# Comparative Analysis: This Case and Trump v. Hawaii

This chapter examines how the Ninth Circuit relied upon and distinguished the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). Understanding this relationship is essential to reading the opinion, because the court explicitly anchored its reasoning in *Hawaii*'s interpretation of the presidential authority at issue. At the same time, the court recognized limits to what *Hawaii* resolved, leading to a partial stay rather than a complete victory for the government.

## The Factual Similarities and Differences: The Travel Ban versus Refugee Admissions Suspension

Both *Hawaii* and this case involved executive orders issued by President Trump that invoked statutory authority to restrict the entry of non-citizens into the United States. In *Hawaii*, the President issued Executive Order No. 13,780, which suspended entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. In this case, Executive Order No. 14163 suspended the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). Both executive orders rested primarily on the President's authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which permits the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."

The court noted this common statutory foundation explicitly, quoting *Hawaii*'s observation that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." This deferential standard from *Hawaii* carried significant weight in the court's analysis of the suspension of refugee admissions.

A critical difference between the cases, however, lay in what the government did beyond suspending entry. In *Hawaii*, the challenged action was the entry suspension itself. Here, the government not only suspended refugee admissions but also terminated all funding for domestic resettlement services—cutting off support for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This additional action created a separate legal question that *Hawaii* did not address. The court thus had to determine whether *Hawaii*'s reasoning about entry restrictions extended to the termination of statutorily mandated services for those already admitted.

## How the Ninth Circuit Applied the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The Ninth Circuit applied *Hawaii*'s deferential standard to uphold the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions. Quoting *Hawaii* directly, the court stated that it could not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The court emphasized that "the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" Finding that the President had "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement," the court concluded the government was likely to succeed on the merits of this portion of the challenge.

The court also adopted *Hawaii*'s characterization of the statutory text, noting that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear" and that the executive order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." This language mirrors *Hawaii*'s holding that § 1182(f) grants the President broad discretion to impose entry restrictions. The court thus treated *Hawaii* as controlling precedent on the question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f).

However, the court did not extend this deference to the separate question of domestic resettlement services. The court distinguished between the President's authority to control entry—which *Hawaii* addressed—and statutory obligations that attach after admission. This distinction reveals an important boundary to *Hawaii*'s reach: the "exudes deference" standard governs entry decisions, but it does not automatically sweep in all subsequent governmental actions related to the affected class of aliens.

## The Absence of Constitutional Claims in This Case versus the Establishment Clause Claim in Hawaii

A significant difference between the two cases is the nature of the legal challenges. In *Hawaii*, the plaintiffs raised a constitutional claim under the Establishment Clause, arguing that the travel ban was motivated by anti-Muslim animus. The Supreme Court's majority opinion rejected this claim, holding that the President had provided sufficient justification for the proclamation and that the court should not inquire into the President's motives.

This case did not involve an Establishment Clause challenge or any other constitutional claim. The plaintiffs' challenge was purely statutory: they argued that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of USRAP violated the Administrative Procedure Act. The absence of constitutional claims simplified the analysis in one sense—there was no need to apply the heightened scrutiny that might accompany a claim of religious discrimination. But the statutory framework presented its own interpretive difficulties.

Without a constitutional claim, the court's analysis turned entirely on statutory construction: what did § 1182(f) permit, and what did 8 U.S.C. § 1522 require? The court could not rely on the political question or justiciability doctrines that sometimes limit judicial review of executive actions in the immigration context. Instead, the court had to interpret specific statutory provisions—a task that led it to different conclusions on different parts of the challenge.

## The Statutory Basis: § 1182(f) in Both Cases

Section 1182(f) was the pivotal statutory provision in both *Hawaii* and this case. The statute provides:

> "Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

In *Hawaii*, the Supreme Court held that this language "exudes deference" and grants the President "ample power" to suspend entry. The Court emphasized the breadth of the phrase "any class of aliens" and the lack of any limiting principle beyond the President's finding of detrimental impact.

The Ninth Circuit applied this interpretation directly. The court quoted *Hawaii*'s statement that § 1182(f) vests the President with authority "to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." The court concluded that the suspension of refugee admissions fell squarely within this authority. The President had made the required finding—that entry of refugees under USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—and the statute imposed no other substantive limitation on that exercise of discretion.

This portion of the opinion demonstrates how a precedent interpreting statutory authority can constrain later courts. The Ninth Circuit treated *Hawaii*'s interpretation of § 1182(f) as binding, even if it might have reached a different conclusion as an original matter. The court's duty was to apply the Supreme Court's construction, not to reexamine whether that construction was correct.

## Whether Hawaii Resolved the Question of Mandatory Services under § 1522

A central question in reading this opinion is whether *Hawaii* resolved the issue of domestic resettlement services. The Ninth Circuit concluded that it did not. *Hawaii* addressed the President's authority to suspend entry; it did not address any statutory obligations to provide services to refugees who had already been admitted.

The court analyzed 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which provides for domestic resettlement services, independently of *Hawaii*. The court cited several provisions within § 1522 that use mandatory language ("shall") and impose specific obligations on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement. For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary of Health and Human Services to develop support projects for recently resettled refugees.

Judge Lee's partial dissent challenged this interpretation. Judge Lee emphasized that the title of § 1522 uses the word "Authorization"—which she defined as permissive rather than mandatory. She argued that the mandatory language in subsection (a) is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section," meaning that the obligations attach only if the government chooses to provide assistance. In her view, the statute authorizes but does not mandate funding.

This interpretive disagreement illustrates an important point about precedent: *Hawaii* did not answer every question about the scope of executive power over refugee policy. The Supreme Court in *Hawaii* resolved the validity of entry suspensions under § 1182(f), but it did not interpret the separate provisions governing domestic resettlement services. The Ninth Circuit thus had to engage in independent statutory construction on this issue, leading to a different outcome than the government sought.

## What Hawaii Did and Did Not Decide about the Scope of Presidential Authority over Refugees

Understanding the scope of *Hawaii*'s holding is essential to reading this opinion. *Hawaii* decided that the President has broad authority under § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of any class of aliens, including the nationals of designated countries. The Court held that courts should not second-guess the President's finding that entry would be detrimental to the national interest.

*Hawaii* did not decide several questions that arose in this case. First, it did not address whether the President may suspend entry of refugees specifically, though the Ninth Circuit had little difficulty extending *Hawaii*'s reasoning to that context. Second, and more importantly, *Hawaii* did not address whether statutory obligations to provide services persist even after the President suspends entry of additional members of the class.

The court thus bifurcated its analysis. It applied *Hawaii* to uphold the admission suspension, but it looked elsewhere—to the text and structure of § 1522—to assess the funding termination. This approach reflects a basic principle of precedent: courts apply controlling authority to the questions it resolved and engage in independent analysis on questions left open.

The outcome was a partial stay of the district court's injunction. The court stayed the portions of the injunction that blocked the admission suspension, giving the government a victory on that issue. But the court declined to stay the portions of the injunction that required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for resettlement services—services that the court found were statutorily mandated for refugees already admitted. The court even ordered the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services, a remedy that went beyond simply denying the stay.

This split result illustrates how a single case can both follow and distinguish a precedent. The Ninth Circuit followed *Hawaii* where it found *Hawaii* controlling but recognized that *Hawaii*'s reasoning about entry restrictions did not extend to post-admission obligations. The opinion thus teaches students to read precedents carefully, identifying not only what they hold but also what they leave unresolved.

# The Administrative Procedure Act Claims

## The Plaintiffs' APA Claims in This Case

The plaintiffs in *Pacito v. Trump* brought claims under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenging the government's termination of funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). Specifically, the plaintiffs alleged that the defunding of domestic resettlement services violated provisions of the APA. This claim was distinct from their challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, which the court analyzed under the Immigration and Nationality Act and the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f).

The APA challenge focused on the government's obligation to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. When the State Department terminated all cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations in February 2025, the plaintiffs argued that this action violated statutory mandates governing refugee resettlement. The organizational plaintiffs—three entities that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide these services—had standing to challenge the terminations, as they were directly affected by the funding cutoff.

## The Elements of an APA Claim and How They Apply Here

The APA provides a framework for judicial review of agency actions. Under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2), a reviewing court shall hold unlawful and set aside agency actions that are "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law." This latter phrase—"not in accordance with law"—is central to the plaintiffs' APA claim in this case.

To succeed on an APA claim alleging that agency action is "not in accordance with law," a plaintiff must demonstrate that the agency violated a statutory mandate. The court must determine what the relevant statute requires and then assess whether the agency's action comports with that requirement. If the statute imposes a mandatory duty on the agency, the agency lacks discretion to disregard that duty, and a court can order compliance.

In this case, the plaintiffs' APA claim required the court to interpret 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic refugee resettlement services. The question was whether § 1522 creates mandatory obligations that the government must fulfill, or whether it merely authorizes the government to provide services at its discretion. This statutory interpretation question was essential to determining whether the government's termination of funding violated the APA.

## Why the Court Found the Government Unlikely to Prevail on the APA Claim Regarding § 1522

Applying the stay factors, the court concluded that the government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." The court's analysis rested on its interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the court read as imposing mandatory obligations on the government to provide certain services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The court identified several provisions of § 1522 that it found to be mandatory in nature. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and ensure equitable participation by women in training programs. The court noted that § 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors "if necessary" during interim periods before placement. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary of Health and Human Services to "develop and implement alternative projects" providing interim support, medical services, and case management for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months.

Crucially, the court observed that Congress had appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025. The "to the extent of available appropriations" language in § 1522(a)(1)(A) thus did not excuse non-performance, because appropriations were in fact available.

The court also found significant the government's admission that it had terminated all cooperative agreements providing resettlement services while expressing uncertainty about whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency," yet at oral argument could not confirm whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill its statutory obligations weighed against the government's request for a complete stay.

Based on this analysis, the court directed the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

## The Distinction Between APA Review of Statutory Interpretation Versus Agency Discretion

This case illustrates an important distinction in APA litigation: the difference between challenges to agency actions as "contrary to law" versus challenges to agency actions as "arbitrary and capricious" or an abuse of discretion. When a statute mandates specific action, an agency has no discretion to act contrary to that mandate, and a court can order the agency to comply. However, when a statute grants an agency discretion to act, courts have limited ability to intervene.

The majority and dissent articulated fundamentally different views of whether § 1522 creates mandatory duties or discretionary authority. The majority read provisions using "shall" as imposing mandatory obligations, particularly where appropriations were available and services were statutorily required. Judge Lee's partial dissent, by contrast, emphasized that the section's title uses "Authorization"—which he defined as permissive—and that the specific funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) state that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance.

Judge Lee argued that the "shall" language in § 1522(a) applies only "[i]n providing assistance under this section"—meaning it imposes conditions on discretionary funding decisions, not independent mandates. Under this reading, if the government chooses to fund refugee programs, it must do so in accordance with certain conditions; but the government retains discretion whether to fund those programs at all.

This interpretive disagreement reflects a broader tension in administrative law: determining when statutory language creates enforceable duties versus when it grants agencies flexibility. The majority's approach focused on the mandatory phrasing of specific provisions, while the dissent looked to structural signals like the section title and permissive language in the funding provisions.

## How the APA Interacts with the Immigration Context

The immigration context adds complexity to APA analysis because of the significant deference courts traditionally accord to the political branches in immigration matters. The court acknowledged that the President has "broad discretion" in immigration matters, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*. However, the court distinguished between the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f)—where the court found sweeping presidential discretion—and the government's obligations to provide services to refugees who have already been admitted.

This distinction is crucial. The court deferred to the President's determination that refugee admissions would be detrimental to the United States, holding that the government was likely to prevail on the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of admissions. But the court applied a different analysis to post-admission resettlement services, finding that once refugees are admitted, different statutory provisions govern the government's obligations.

The court's approach recognizes that executive authority over admissions does not automatically extend to all aspects of immigration policy. Different statutory schemes may impose different constraints, and Congress can create mandatory obligations that limit executive discretion even in immigration matters. The key question becomes one of statutory interpretation: what did Congress require, and what did it leave to agency discretion?

## The Significance of the Court's Finding on the APA Claim for Future Litigation

The court's treatment of the APA claim has several implications for future litigation involving refugee policy and immigration administration. First, it suggests that courts may scrutinize executive actions affecting post-admission services more closely than actions affecting admissions themselves. While the President's § 1182(f) authority to suspend entry received extreme deference, statutory obligations to serve already-admitted refugees were treated as judicially enforceable mandates.

Second, the case demonstrates that appropriations matter. The court specifically noted that Congress had appropriated funds for refugee assistance, making the "to the extent of available appropriations" limitation inapplicable. Future litigants challenging executive defunding of statutory programs may similarly point to available appropriations as evidence that agencies cannot claim funding limitations to excuse non-compliance.

Third, the court's remedy—ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements—shows that courts may order specific performance when statutory mandates are clear. Rather than simply prohibiting the government from taking certain actions, the court affirmatively required the government to reinstate agreements necessary to provide required services.

Finally, the split between the majority and Judge Lee's dissent highlights ongoing uncertainty about how to interpret statutory schemes that combine mandatory language with authorization language. Courts will continue to grapple with whether provisions like § 1522 create enforceable rights or discretionary programs, and the outcome of this appeal may provide additional guidance on this question when the court issues its merits opinion.

# The Cooperative Agreement Framework and Resettlement Services

## What Cooperative Agreements Are and How They Function in Federal Programs

To understand the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*, students must first understand the legal mechanism through which refugee resettlement services are delivered: the cooperative agreement. Federal agencies use several instruments to carry out statutory mandates, including contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements. Unlike contracts, which are used when the federal government is procuring goods or services for its own use, cooperative agreements are used when the federal government provides funding to another entity to carry out a public purpose authorized by statute. Unlike pure grants, cooperative agreements involve "substantial involvement" between the federal agency and the recipient in carrying out the funded activity.

The opinion makes clear that Congress explicitly authorized this funding mechanism for refugee resettlement. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), resettlement services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with 'public or private nonprofit agencies.'" The court emphasized that "the United States has historically entered into cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to provide such services." This historical practice matters because when the government suddenly terminated these agreements, it disrupted an established system that had been functioning pursuant to statutory authorization.

Cooperative agreements create legally binding obligations on both parties. Once executed, the federal government commits to provide specified funding, and the recipient organization commits to provide specified services. The agreements typically include detailed terms regarding the scope of services, reporting requirements, payment schedules, and termination procedures. Importantly for this case, cooperative agreements can be terminated—but as the dispute illustrates, termination has legal consequences that depend on whether the underlying statute requires the services to be provided.

## The State Department's Role in Administering Refugee Resettlement Agreements

The opinion identifies the Department of State as the federal agency responsible for administering the cooperative agreements at issue. The organizational plaintiffs—three nonprofit organizations—had "cooperative agreements with the Department of State to provide overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." This division of labor reflects the broader architecture of the refugee admissions program: the State Department handles pre-admission processing and initial domestic resettlement (reception and placement), while the Office of Refugee Resettlement within the Department of Health and Human Services administers longer-term support services.

The practical significance of this administrative structure became apparent when the executive orders were issued. On January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended all funding of the USRAP program pursuant to a separate executive order on foreign aid. Then, significantly, on February 26, 2025—the day after the district court's oral preliminary injunction—the government "terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad." The timing suggests the termination was a response to the district court's intervention, and the breadth of the termination affected nearly all organizations providing these services.

The March 24, 2025 district court order addressed this directly by ordering "the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling." The appellate court's treatment of this order—staying most provisions but reinstating the requirement for services to already-admitted refugees—hinged on whether the statute mandated the services themselves, regardless of the mechanism used to provide them.

## The Services Provided Under Reception and Placement Programs

The court's analysis depends on understanding what "reception and placement services" actually include. The opinion provides a detailed statutory breakdown. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement is required "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide four categories of services:

First, "make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible." Second, "provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training to enable them to become effectively resettled as quickly as possible." Third, "insure that cash assistance is made available to refugees in such a manner as not to discourage their economic self-sufficiency." Fourth, "insure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction."

The statute also addresses specific populations with particular needs. For unaccompanied refugee minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) provides that "during any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." For recently resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed."

These statutory provisions formed the core of the parties' dispute over whether the government had a non-discretionary duty to provide services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

## How Termination of Cooperative Agreements Affects Service Delivery

The opinion reveals a critical practical problem created by the termination of cooperative agreements. After terminating all the agreements in February 2025, the government represented to the district court on March 10, 2025 that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." This representation suggested that the government recognized some ongoing obligation and intended to establish an alternative mechanism to fulfill it.

However, at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, "the government advised the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services." This admission proved significant. The court noted "the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522." The gap between the government's March representation and its September admission—expressing uncertainty about any progress—suggested that termination of the cooperative agreements had created a situation where the government had no functioning delivery system for services it might be legally required to provide.

The termination thus raised a practical question: if the statute requires services to be provided, and the government has eliminated the only mechanism for providing them without securing a replacement, how can the statutory mandate be fulfilled? This practical reality informed the court's decision to order reinstatement of the cooperative agreements rather than simply directing the government to provide services through some unspecified means.

## The Practical Implications of Reinstating Terminated Agreements

The court's order directed the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This remedy has several practical implications worth understanding.

First, reinstatement means restoring the legal relationship between the government and the organizational plaintiffs. The organizations that had their agreements terminated would resume their role as service providers, with associated funding obligations flowing from the government. The court's order thus functions as an injunction requiring the executive branch to resume funding that it had decided to terminate.

Second, the scope of reinstatement is limited. The order applies only to refugees "who have been admitted to the United States"—not to those approved for resettlement but still abroad. This limitation reflects the court's reasoning that while the President has broad authority over admission decisions, once refugees have been lawfully admitted, different statutory obligations attach. Judge Lee's partial dissent disagreed with this distinction, arguing that § 1522 authorizes but does not mandate services even for admitted refugees.

Third, the reinstatement obligation applies only to "such cooperative agreements necessary to provide" the statutory services. The court recognized that not all previously terminated agreements might be required—only those needed to fulfill the statutory mandate. This gives the government some flexibility in determining which agreements to reinstate, though the practical reality is that the organizational plaintiffs whose agreements were terminated are the most obvious candidates for reinstatement.

## The "Usual and Customary Services" Standard

The court specified that reinstated services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This phrase is significant because it establishes a standard for what services must be provided, grounded in historical practice rather than abstract statutory interpretation.

The "usual and customary services" language appears designed to prevent the government from reinstating cooperative agreements in name only while providing a materially reduced scope of services. By referencing what had "historically" been provided, the court anchored the service obligation in the established practices that existed before the executive orders disrupted them. This approach avoids requiring the court to micromanage service delivery or specify every service in detail—instead, it points to the pre-existing framework as the benchmark.

The practical effect is that resettlement organizations would resume providing the same types and levels of services they provided before termination: initial reception, housing assistance, employment services, English language training, case management, and other support services that had become standard under the USRAP program. Judge Lee's dissent did not directly challenge this standard, focusing instead on whether any services were legally required in the first place.

For students reading this opinion, the "usual and customary services" phrase illustrates how courts sometimes resolve remedial questions by reference to established practice rather than formal legal standards. Where a statutory mandate is disputed but historical practice is clear, the practice itself can become the measure of compliance.

# Conclusion and Study Questions

## Summary of the Court's Key Holdings

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* represents a mixed outcome for the parties, with the court granting the government's request for a stay in substantial part but preserving certain protections for refugees already admitted to the United States. The holdings emerge from the court's application of the four-factor stay standard established in *Nken v. Holder*.

On the central question of Executive Order 14163's validity, the court found the government likely to succeed on the merits. The court applied *Trump v. Hawaii*'s interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), emphasizing that the statute "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power" to suspend the entry of any class of aliens he determines would be detrimental to the national interest. The court concluded that the President's finding of detrimentality satisfied the statute's "sole prerequisite" and that the executive order did not exceed any textual limit on presidential authority. Accordingly, the district court's preliminary injunction prohibiting enforcement of the admission suspension was stayed in its entirety.

However, the court reached a different conclusion regarding the government's obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority determined that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges concerning reception and placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. The court read § 1522 as creating statutory mandates for the provision of certain services, including employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors. The court found significant the government's representation that it was "actively preparing" to secure new resettlement agencies yet its inability at oral argument to confirm any progress. The court therefore directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide these mandated services to admitted refugees.

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part. He agreed that the President has authority to suspend refugee admissions but disagreed with the majority's interpretation of § 1522. In his view, the statute's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement"—signals permissive rather than mandatory language. Judge Lee argued that the "shall" language in § 1522(a) is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section," meaning the mandatory conditions apply only if the government chooses to fund programs in the first instance. He would have stayed the district court's injunctions in their entirety.

## The Broader Significance for Immigration Law and Separation of Powers

This order illustrates the continuing tension between broad executive authority over immigration and statutory constraints on executive action. The court's contrasting treatment of the admission suspension and the funding termination reveals an important doctrinal boundary: while the political branches possess sweeping power over the admission of foreign nationals, once Congress has created statutory entitlements and appropriated funds, the executive may not simply ignore those mandates.

The decision also demonstrates the limits of deference. *Trump v. Hawaii* established that courts should not conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" under § 1182(f). Yet this deference has boundaries. The majority did not extend similar deference to the government's interpretation of its obligations under § 1522. The court engaged in careful statutory interpretation of the refugee resettlement provisions, weighing the text, structure, and practical implications of the government's position. This suggests that while courts may show substantial deference to the political branches on questions of admission, they maintain their role as interpreters of statutory rights and obligations.

The procedural posture matters. This was a stay decision, applying a likelihood-of-success standard rather than issuing a final merits ruling. The court was not definitively resolving the underlying claims but rather assessing the parties' relative positions for purposes of interim relief. The court's reasoning signals where it is likely headed on the merits, but a formal opinion will follow.

## Unresolved Questions Awaiting the Merits Opinion

Several significant questions remain open. The court stated it would issue "an opinion on the merits... in due course." This merits opinion will need to address several issues left unresolved by the stay order.

First, the scope of § 1522's mandates remains uncertain. The majority and Judge Lee disagree on whether the statute authorizes or requires services. The merits opinion will need to conduct a fuller statutory analysis, potentially addressing the significance of the "to the extent of available appropriations" language and the interaction between the statute's title and its operative provisions.

Second, the relationship between the admission suspension and funding obligations requires clarification. The government's position appears to be that suspending admissions under § 1182(f) effectively nullifies any ongoing obligations under § 1522. The court's order suggests a different view: that obligations to already-admitted refugees persist regardless of changes to admission policy.

Third, the APA claims require fuller development. The stay order addressed only whether the government was likely to prevail; it did not analyze the elements of the APA claims or the appropriate remedies if violations are found.

Fourth, the practical implementation of the court's directive to reinstate cooperative agreements raises questions. The order requires reinstatement of agreements "necessary" to provide services, but does not specify which agreements satisfy this standard or how the government should proceed if the original cooperative partners are unwilling or unable to resume operations.

## Study Questions for Further Analysis

1. **Statutory Interpretation**: Judge Lee relies heavily on the title of § 1522—"Authorization for programs"—as evidence that the provision is permissive rather than mandatory. How much weight should courts give to statutory titles when interpreting operative text? Does the presence of both "authorized" and "shall" language in the same statute create an internal tension, and how should courts resolve such tensions?

2. **Deference Doctrines**: The court applies highly deferential review to the President's determination under § 1182(f), citing *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that courts cannot conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness" of the President's justifications. Yet the court does not extend similar deference to the government's interpretation of § 1522. Is this distinction consistent? What distinguishes questions that warrant deference from those that do not?

3. **The Stay Standard**: Under *Nken*, the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." In this case, the court found the government likely to succeed on one issue and unlikely to succeed on another. How does this mixed assessment affect the stay analysis? If the movant is likely to succeed on some issues but not others, should the court grant a partial stay, or must it weigh the relative importance of the different issues?

4. **The Temporal Dimension**: The court distinguishes between refugees seeking admission (whose entry the government may suspend) and refugees already admitted (to whom the government may owe ongoing obligations). What is the legal significance of this temporal boundary? Does admission create rights that the executive cannot subsequently withdraw?

5. **Separation of Powers**: The government argues that the executive has broad discretion over immigration and foreign affairs. The court accepts this as to admission but not as to funding. What does this reveal about the constitutional allocation of power between Congress and the President? Can Congress use spending statutes to constrain executive discretion in areas of presidential authority?

6. **Remedies**: The court directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements. What authority does a court have to order specific performance of government contracts? How should courts structure remedies when statutory violations continue but the government expresses uncertainty about its ability to comply?

# Introduction and Case Overview

## The Significance of Pacito v. Trump in the Landscape of Executive Immigration Authority

*Pacito v. Trump* arrives at the intersection of some of the most contested questions in modern constitutional law: the scope of presidential power over immigration, the limits of judicial review over national security determinations, and the extent to which Congress can constrain executive action through statutory mandates. The case arises from Executive Order No. 14163, issued on January 20, 2025, in which President Trump invoked his authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of all refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). The President determined that such entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—the statutory trigger for suspension authority under § 1182(f).

This opinion is significant because it represents an early appellate application of the Supreme Court's landmark decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which upheld the travel ban executive order under the same statutory provision. The Ninth Circuit explicitly quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." In doing so, the panel signals that courts must not conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" when the President acts under this statute. This deferential standard effectively insulates the executive's determination from meaningful judicial scrutiny, provided the President makes the requisite finding of detriment.

At the same time, *Pacito* introduces a wrinkle: the court refuses to defer entirely to the executive branch when it comes to statutory obligations regarding refugees *already admitted* to the United States. The opinion thus draws a line between the President's broad authority to exclude aliens at the border and Congress's power to mandate how the executive treats noncitizens once they have been lawfully admitted. This distinction—between exclusion and treatment—may prove consequential for future litigation involving executive immigration policy.

## The Intersection of Refugee Law, Administrative Law, and Separation of Powers

The opinion sits at the crossroads of three distinct bodies of law, each carrying its own doctrines and standards of review. First, refugee law is governed by the Refugee Act of 1980, which Congress enacted to bring U.S. law into compliance with international refugee conventions. The Refugee Act created a statutory framework for refugee admissions and resettlement, codified across multiple provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The plaintiffs in *Pacito* argue that Executive Order 14163 violates the Refugee Act by suspending admissions in a manner inconsistent with Congress's statutory scheme.

Second, administrative law principles come into play through the plaintiffs' claims under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq., provides the default framework for judicial review of agency action. Under the APA, a reviewing court must set aside agency action that is "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law." 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The plaintiffs contend that the executive branch's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations violated APA procedures or exceeded statutory authority.

Third, separation of powers questions pervade the opinion. The court must determine whether the President has exceeded his statutory authority under § 1182(f), whether Congress has imposed mandatory obligations through § 1522 that constrain executive discretion, and how courts should navigate the "political question" of foreign policy and national security when reviewing executive immigration action. The opinion's split disposition—staying the injunction as to admissions but reinstating funding as to resettlement services—reflects the court's attempt to honor both executive authority and congressional mandates.

## The Procedural Context: An Interim Order Pending Appeal

Understanding the procedural posture of *Pacito* is essential to reading the opinion correctly. The Ninth Circuit has not yet issued a merits decision. Instead, this document is an order granting a stay pending appeal—a provisional remedy that maintains the status quo while the appellate court considers the underlying appeal. A stay "pending appeal" freezes the district court's order (here, the preliminary injunctions) until the appeal is resolved.

The standards for granting a stay come from *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), which requires courts to consider four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likely success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The first two factors, the court notes, are "the most critical." This procedural context matters because the court's analysis is necessarily predictive: the court is assessing *likelihood* of success, not finally resolving the merits. The court can (and does) indicate how it leans, but a merits opinion "will issue in due course."

The opinion also vacates prior stay orders that had created a "limited carveout" for refugees who were conditionally approved for admission before January 20, 2025. By vacating these prior stays and issuing a new stay "in its entirety," the panel simplifies the procedural landscape—but also reveals the evolving nature of interim orders in complex litigation.

## Overview of the Key Legal Issues and the Court's Approach

The opinion addresses two central legal issues, each governed by different statutes and standards.

**Issue One: The Validity of the Admission Suspension Under § 1182(f).** The court concludes that the government is likely to succeed on its argument that Executive Order 14163 falls within the President's § 1182(f) authority. The statute provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation... suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." The court applies *Trump v. Hawaii* deferentially, holding that the President's determination of detriment satisfies the statutory prerequisite and that the order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." On this issue, the court stays the district court's injunction—meaning the suspension of refugee admissions may proceed.

**Issue Two: The Legality of Terminating Resettlement Services Under § 1522.** Here the court reaches the opposite conclusion. Section 1522 of Title 8 addresses "programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." The court reads certain provisions of § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations on the executive branch to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees who have already been admitted. Notably, the court focuses on language directing that the Director "shall" provide specific services "to the extent of available appropriations." Because the government could not confirm whether it had secured alternative providers for these mandated services, the court directs the government to reinstate the terminated cooperative agreements necessary to fulfill its statutory obligations.

The result is a split ruling: the President's admission suspension stands, but the funding termination for resettlement services is partially enjoined.

## Roadmap of the Opinion Structure: From Background to Stay Analysis to the Split Decision

The opinion follows a logical structure that students should track carefully:

**Background Section.** The court first establishes the factual and procedural foundation: the executive orders, the statutory framework, the plaintiffs' identity and claims, and the district court proceedings. This section is largely undisputed and provides the necessary context for the legal analysis.

**Discussion Section.** The court then applies the *Nken* stay factors. The analysis bifurcates based on the two legal issues. For the admission suspension, the court finds the government likely to succeed based on deference under *Hawaii*. For the APA challenge to the funding termination, the court finds the government not likely to succeed because § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations that the executive cannot simply ignore. The court's disposition follows from this split: stay the injunction as to admissions, but require reinstatement of cooperative agreements for resettlement services.

**Judge Lee's Partial Concurrence and Partial Dissent.** Judge Lee agrees with the majority on the admission issue but dissents from the conclusion that § 1522 mandates funding. His dissent rests on a textual analysis of § 1522's title ("Authorization for programs...") and the permissive language ("authorized") found in the funding subsections. This disagreement over statutory interpretation—whether "shall" creates a mandate or merely sets conditions on discretionary authority—represents a classic divide in statutory construction.

Students should pay attention to how each section builds toward the court's ultimate holding. The background provides facts; the discussion applies standards; the split disposition reflects the court's attempt to balance competing legal obligations. Judge Lee's separate opinion highlights the reasonable disagreements that can arise even when judges apply the same legal framework.

# The Facts and Procedural History

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked two statutory provisions: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a). Section 1182(f) provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." Relying on this authority, the President determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings.

The executive order thus effected a complete suspension of refugee admissions under USRAP. The scope of this suspension—and the statutory authority cited to support it—would become central to the litigation that followed. The government would argue that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions," while plaintiffs would contend that the suspension violated the Refugee Act of 1980.

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of Foreign Aid and Domestic Resettlement Funding

Four days after Executive Order 14163, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State took additional action pursuant to a separate executive order. Executive Order No. 14169, entitled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," provided the stated basis for the State Department's suspension of all funding for the USRAP program. Importantly, this suspension was not limited to overseas operations; it encompassed funding for domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This funding suspension would give rise to a distinct legal challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act, separate from the challenge to the admission suspension itself.

The dual-track approach—suspending both admissions and funding—created two interrelated but legally distinct issues. The admissions suspension touched on the President's broad authority over entry decisions under § 1182(f), while the funding suspension implicated statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services.

## The Plaintiffs: Individual Refugees and Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs in this action fell into two categories. The first group consisted of individual refugees: some had already been admitted to the United States, while others had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. These individual plaintiffs faced direct consequences from both the admission suspension and the funding termination.

The second group comprised three organizational plaintiffs. These organizations had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to perform two essential functions: (1) overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and (2) resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. These cooperative agreements represented the mechanism through which the federal government has historically discharged its obligations to refugees. The termination of these agreements thus threatened not only the organizational plaintiffs' operations but also the services that refugees would otherwise receive.

## District Court Proceedings: Preliminary Injunctions of February 25, February 28, and March 24, 2025

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025. Their complaint advanced two principal claims: (1) that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980, and (2) that the defunding of USRAP violated provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act. The Refugee Act, as the court noted, establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons."

The district court moved swiftly. On February 25, 2025, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. The very next day, February 26, the government responded by terminating every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements for USRAP processing support abroad.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order to define the scope of its February 25 preliminary injunction. Significantly, this written order specified that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. Thus, the district court's February 28 order extended the injunction's reach beyond the admission suspension to cover the funding termination as well.

The parties' conflict escalated further. On March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction, this time ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The district court thus fashioned injunctive relief that addressed both the admission suspension and the funding termination, including the specific mechanism—cooperative agreements—through which resettlement services were delivered.

The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, challenging both preliminary injunctions.

## Ninth Circuit Proceedings: Partial Stay, Clarifications, and Administrative Stay

Once the appeals were filed, the government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit acted on this request on March 25, 2025. Quoting the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel observed that the authority underlying Executive Order 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]."

The motions panel granted the stay in part but denied it "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—refugees who had received conditional approval before the executive order's issuance would remain protected by the injunction pending appeal.

The motions panel issued two additional orders, on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar.

A new dispute arose in July 2025. On July 14, the district court issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. The government responded on July 17 by filing an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order. The plaintiffs opposed this motion. On July 18, 2025, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's July 14 enforcement order and scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis.

Understanding the distinction between different types of stays is important here. An administrative stay is a temporary measure issued by a court to preserve the status quo while the court considers whether to grant a longer-term stay pending appeal. The March 25 order had been a stay pending appeal (albeit partial), while the July 18 order was an administrative stay of the district court's enforcement framework.

## The Government's Representations Regarding New Resettlement Contracts

A notable exchange occurred during the litigation regarding the government's plans to provide resettlement services through alternative mechanisms. On March 10, 2025, the government advised the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." This representation suggested that the government recognized some ongoing obligation to provide such services and was taking steps to fulfill that obligation through new contractual arrangements.

However, at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit panel, the government's position had shifted. When asked about progress toward securing a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services, the government advised the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty became significant to the court's analysis of whether the government could meet its statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 for refugees already admitted to the United States.

The panel's final order would cite this uncertainty as part of its reasoning for directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had been admitted. The gap between the government's March representation and its uncertainty at oral argument highlighted the practical consequences of the funding termination for refugees already in the United States.

# The Presidential Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

## The Text and Scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The foundation of the court's analysis of the refugee admission suspension lies in the text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision that grants the President broad authority over the entry of non-citizens into the United States. The statute provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The breadth of this language is striking: the President may act "whenever" he makes the requisite finding, for "such period as he shall deem necessary," and may suspend entry of "any class of aliens" or impose "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The statute thus confers discretionary authority that appears expansive on its face.

In this case, President Trump invoked § 1182(f) in Executive Order No. 14163, determining that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directing that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The plaintiffs challenged this suspension as violating the Refugee Act of 1980, but the court's analysis of § 1182(f) quickly foreclosed that challenge at the stay stage.

## Trump v. Hawaii and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The court's interpretation of § 1182(f) is shaped fundamentally by the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). That case, which upheld a different Trump-era travel proclamation, established the governing framework for reviewing presidential action under § 1182(f). The Ninth Circuit quoted *Hawaii* for the proposition that the statute "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" This deferential posture is not merely a policy of judicial restraint; it flows directly from the statutory text itself. When Congress authorized the President to suspend entry whenever he "deems" it necessary and to impose whatever restrictions he "may deem to be appropriate," it necessarily committed the determination to presidential discretion.

The "exudes deference" formulation signals that courts reviewing presidential action under § 1182(f) will not subject the President's determination to searching scrutiny. The statute's broad delegations of authority, combined with the President's constitutionally recognized role in foreign affairs and immigration, create a zone of discretion that courts are reluctant to police.

## The President's 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests' Finding

Central to the operation of § 1182(f) is the finding requirement: the President must determine that the entry of the covered non-citizens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States." This finding is, as the Ninth Circuit emphasized, the "sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f)." The court quoted *Hawaii* directly on this point: "The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.' The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here."

The court's analysis underscores how minimal this prerequisite is in practice. The President need not provide extensive justification or submit evidence for judicial evaluation. The finding itself—articulated in the executive order—is sufficient. Executive Order No. 14163 contained the requisite finding: the President determined that refugee entry under USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Once that finding appears in the executive action, the statutory condition is satisfied.

## Why the Court Cannot Engage in 'A Searching Inquiry into the Persuasiveness' of Justifications

Perhaps the most significant aspect of the court's analysis for understanding judicial review under § 1182(f) is its explicit rejection of any power to evaluate the quality of the President's reasoning. The court stated flatly that it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This quotation from *Hawaii* establishes a critical boundary on judicial review: courts will not second-guess whether the President's stated reasons are convincing, well-supported, or adequate. The statute does not authorize courts to assess whether the President's determination of detriment is correct or reasonable—it requires only that the President has made such a finding.

This limitation follows logically from the statutory design. If Congress had wanted courts to evaluate the merits of the President's determination, it would have provided standards for such review. Instead, Congress left the judgment entirely to the President, using language that commits the decision to executive discretion. The court recognized that the language of § 1182(f) "is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." The textual limits are essentially procedural: the President must make the finding. Substantive evaluation of whether that finding is persuasive lies beyond judicial competence under the statute.

## The Majority's Conclusion: The Government Is Likely to Prevail on This Claim

Applying these principles, the Ninth Circuit concluded that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions." This conclusion was straightforward once the court accepted the governing framework from *Trump v. Hawaii*. The President had made the required finding of detriment, the executive order fell within the textual scope of § 1182(f) authority, and the court had no authority to probe the persuasiveness of the justification.

The court's stay analysis thus favored the government on this claim. The first stay factor—likelihood of success on the merits—weighed decisively in the government's favor. The remaining factors, the court noted, "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President," also favored the government. The result was a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction insofar as it restrained the government from implementing the refugee admission suspension.

Notably, Judge Lee concurred in this portion of the order, agreeing that "the President has the authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions." Despite the general rule that the President enjoys "vast discretion and deference in immigration matters," Judge Lee would have gone further—staying the district court's injunctions "in their entirety," including the portion directing reinstatement of resettlement services. But on the admission suspension itself, the panel was unanimous: the government was likely to prevail on the merits.

# The Dispute Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligation or Discretionary Authorization?

## Overview of the Refugee Act of 1980 and § 1522

The Refugee Act of 1980 represents Congress's comprehensive statutory framework for the treatment of refugees and displaced persons. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." At the heart of this case lies a fundamental question of statutory interpretation: does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 require the executive branch to provide resettlement services to refugees, or does it merely permit such services at the government's discretion?

Section 1522 governs domestic resettlement assistance for refugees who have been admitted to the United States. The statute addresses how the federal government provides what are commonly called "reception and placement" services—support designed to help refugees achieve economic self-sufficiency, acquire English language skills, and access cash and medical assistance during their initial resettlement period. These services may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies, a structure the United States has historically employed by partnering with resettlement organizations.

The interpretive dispute arises from competing readings of the statute's text and structure. The majority and dissent approach the same statutory provisions with different methodological emphases—one focusing on mandatory language within specific subsections, the other on the permissive framing of the provision as a whole. This chapter examines how each side constructs its argument and what tools of statutory interpretation each employs.

## The Majority's Interpretation: 'Shall' Language Creates Mandatory Obligations

The majority concludes that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges because § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations. In reaching this conclusion, the panel emphasizes the statute's use of the word "shall"—the traditional language of statutory command.

The court's analysis begins with the principle that when Congress uses "shall," it ordinarily imposes a duty rather than granting discretion. The majority reads § 1522 as establishing binding obligations on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, an office within the Department of Health and Human Services. Under this reading, the government cannot simply decline to provide the services Congress has specified; the statute makes those services a legal entitlement for refugees who meet the eligibility criteria.

This interpretation matters because it determines whether the executive branch's termination of cooperative agreements violated governing law. If § 1522 creates mandatory obligations, then the government's failure to provide reception and placement services through any mechanism—whether existing cooperative agreements or new contracts—would constitute a statutory violation. The majority's reasoning thus sets the stage for its remedial order directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide the mandated services.

## The Majority's Reliance on Specific Subsections (a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The majority builds its interpretation on several specific textual provisions within § 1522. First, the court cites § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director "shall"—"to the extent of available appropriations"—make available resources for employment training, provide English language training, ensure cash assistance is available, and guarantee women equal participation opportunities. The majority appears to read this language as creating affirmative duties, with the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" establishing the funding condition rather than converting the obligations into discretionary options.

The court further relies on § 1522(d)(2)(B), addressing unaccompanied refugee minors. This provision states that during interim periods before placement, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." The mandatory "shall" coupled with financial responsibility suggests to the majority that Congress created a non-discretionary duty in at least some circumstances.

Finally, the majority points to § 1522(e)(7)(A), which provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months." This language directs the Secretary to act, suggesting another mandatory obligation. The majority also notes that Congress has appropriated substantial funds—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million—for migration and refugee assistance, indicating that appropriations exist to support these statutory obligations.

The majority observes that the government represented it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" for a new resettlement agency but then could not confirm at oral argument whether any progress had been made. This practical uncertainty about the government's ability to provide statutorily mandated services reinforces the court's conclusion that the government is unlikely to prevail on the APA challenge concerning funding termination.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: The Significance of 'Authorization' in the Section Title

Judge Lee opens his partial dissent with a different starting point: the statute's title. Section 1522 is entitled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." For Judge Lee, the word "authorization" carries decisive interpretive weight. Citing Black's Law Dictionary, he notes that authorization means "permission to do something"—permissive language, not mandatory command.

This approach reflects a standard principle of statutory interpretation: courts should consider the statute's title as part of the interpretive inquiry. While section titles cannot override clear statutory text, they can inform how ambiguous provisions should be read. Judge Lee argues that Congress labeled this provision an authorization, signaling its intent to permit rather than require the described programs.

Judge Lee further notes that the specific funding provisions in § 1522(b) through (e)—including the "Program of initial resettlement" and "Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees"—all state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. The repeated use of "authorized" rather than "required" or "shall" in these operative funding subsections reinforces his view that Congress granted discretion rather than imposed duties. This pattern of permissive language throughout the section's substantive provisions forms the foundation of the dissent's structural argument.

## The Dissent's Structural Argument: § 1522(a) Imposes Conditions If Funding Is Provided

The dissent's central structural claim is that § 1522(a) does not independently mandate services but instead establishes "conditions and considerations" that apply if the government exercises its discretion to fund refugee programs. Judge Lee reads the phrase "In providing assistance under this section" as qualifying everything that follows in § 1522(a). Under this reading, the "shall" language operates only once the government chooses to act under the discretionary funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e).

To illustrate this interpretation, Judge Lee offers an example: if the government decides to fund employment training under § 1522(c), then § 1522(a) requires that such training be designed "to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible." The mandatory language thus governs how programs operate, not whether they must operate. This reading preserves the "authorization" framework of the statute's title while giving effect to the "shall" language in § 1522(a).

Judge Lee also addresses the specific subsections the majority relies upon. He argues that § 1522(d)(2)(B), dealing with unaccompanied minors, imposes responsibility on the Director only when "necessary"—a condition that invokes executive judgment. Similarly, he reads § 1522(e)(7)(A)'s requirement that the Secretary "develop and implement" projects as leaving the "metes and bounds" of such programs to executive discretion. Moreover, he questions whether any unaccompanied refugee children are even at issue in this case, suggesting the provision may be irrelevant to the present dispute.

## The Practical Implications of Each Interpretation for Executive Discretion

The interpretive dispute carries significant consequences for the separation of powers and executive discretion in immigration policy. Under the majority's reading, Congress has created enforceable statutory entitlements for refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Once refugees lawfully enter the country, the executive branch must provide certain reception and placement services, subject only to available appropriations. The government cannot terminate funding mechanisms without ensuring alternative means of delivering these mandatory services.

Under Judge Lee's reading, by contrast, Congress has granted the executive branch broad discretion over whether to provide domestic resettlement services at all. The President, having determined that continued refugee admissions would be detrimental to national interests, may suspend not only entry but also the support services that accompany resettlement. The statute's authorization framework, in this view, permits but does not compel executive action.

The practical stakes are reflected in the court's order. The majority directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. Judge Lee would stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, leaving the executive branch free to terminate both admissions and services pending appeal. The difference between these outcomes turns entirely on whether § 1522 speaks in the language of obligation or permission—and whether courts or the political branches decide what services, if any, admitted refugees must receive.

# The Stay Pending Appeal Framework and Application

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test

When a party seeks to pause a lower court's order while an appeal proceeds, it requests a "stay pending appeal." The Ninth Circuit applied the well-established four-factor framework from *Nken v. Holder* to evaluate the government's request. Understanding this framework is essential because stays represent a form of interim relief that can dramatically affect the real-world operation of judicial decisions long before an appeal is resolved.

The four factors are: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." This makes intuitive sense: a stay is an extraordinary remedy that freezes the status quo, and courts are reluctant to grant such relief unless the applicant can show both that they have a strong case and that they face serious harm without immediate intervention.

A stay pending appeal differs from an injunction. An injunction resolves the merits of a claim (at least preliminarily), while a stay merely suspends the effect of a lower court ruling during appellate review. The stay preserves the status quo and prevents irreparable harm while the appellate court deliberates. Because the district court had already issued preliminary injunctions against the government, the government bore the burden of demonstrating that these injunctions should be frozen pending resolution of the appeal.

The *Nken* framework operates as a sliding scale—the factors do not require equal weight, and courts may calibrate their analysis accordingly. A particularly strong showing on one factor may compensate for a weaker showing on another, though the "most critical" factors of merits and irreparable injury must still be adequately established.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Different Conclusions for Different Claims

The court's analysis of the first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—reveals how the same case can present multiple distinct legal claims requiring separate analysis. The court reached opposite conclusions on the two main categories of claims, and this divergence shaped the ultimate structure of the stay.

On the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, the court found the government likely to prevail. The analysis centered on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." Quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court emphasized that this statutory language "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions."

The court's analysis illustrates an important principle of statutory interpretation in the immigration context: when Congress grants the President broad authority over admission of aliens, courts apply significant deference and do not conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The President's finding that refugee entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States" satisfied the sole prerequisite in § 1182(f). The court thus concluded the executive order did not exceed any textual limit on presidential authority.

However, on the plaintiffs' challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act regarding refugee resettlement services, the court reached the opposite conclusion. The government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." The court's analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1522—which governs domestic refugee resettlement—reveals how different statutory schemes invite different interpretive approaches. Unlike § 1182(f)'s broad grant of presidential discretion, § 1522 contains specific mandates using mandatory language like "shall." For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) mandates that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied refugee minors. Section 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects" providing interim support and medical services.

The court noted that Congress had appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—approximately $3.178 billion for FY 2025, reduced by $800 million via rescission. The statutory structure therefore created obligations on the executive branch that could not simply be disregarded through executive action.

This split outcome on the first factor illustrates a crucial point for understanding stays: the "likelihood of success" analysis can yield different results for different claims within the same case. The court's approach demonstrated that a stay can be granted in part and denied in part, matching the distinct legal issues presented.

## Irreparable Injury: Considerations for the Government and Plaintiffs

The second *Nken* factor—irreparable injury—requires the stay applicant to demonstrate harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or other legal relief after the fact. The court's analysis of this factor operated differently for the admissions suspension versus the services termination.

For the admissions suspension, the court's treatment of irreparable injury was relatively brief, folded into its discussion of the "remaining factors" that "touch upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States." This brevity reflects a practical reality: when a court has already found a strong likelihood of success on the merits, the irreparable injury analysis may require less extensive discussion, particularly where the government invokes its authority over foreign affairs and national security.

The government's interest in controlling the admission of aliens into the United States has long been recognized as falling within the executive's constitutional and statutory domain. When the executive branch determines that entry of a class of aliens would be detrimental to national interests, courts have been reluctant to second-guess that determination. The irreparable injury here consisted of the harm to the government's ability to implement its chosen immigration policy during the potentially lengthy pendency of appeal—a policy determination the court had already deemed likely lawful.

For the services termination, the irreparable injury analysis shifted. The plaintiffs—refugees already admitted to the United States and the organizations that serve them—faced the immediate loss of statutorily-mandated services. The court was particularly troubled by the government's uncertainty about its ability to provide required services. At oral argument, the government admitted it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency to provide reception and placement services, despite having told the district court it was "actively preparing" such a request. This uncertainty created a risk that refugees lawfully present in the United States would be deprived of services Congress had mandated, creating irreparable harm that could not be undone by later appellate rulings.

The court's concern about the government's admitted uncertainty demonstrates an important aspect of irreparable injury analysis: courts consider not just the theoretical availability of relief, but the practical reality of whether plaintiffs can actually receive what statute entitles them to receive.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest in the Immigration Context

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—balance of equities and public interest—often merge in cases involving the federal government, because the government can invoke the public interest as a component of its own interests. The court's analysis in *Pacito* illustrates how immigration cases present unique considerations at this stage of the inquiry.

The court noted that "the remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President," favored the government with respect to the admissions suspension. This formulation reflects the principle that the political branches—Congress and the President—possess primary authority over immigration policy. When the executive branch makes determinations about the admission of aliens, courts give substantial weight to the executive's assessment of the national interest.

However, this deference has limits. The public interest also includes fidelity to statutory mandates enacted by Congress. When Congress has created specific obligations for the treatment of refugees—such as the requirements in § 1522 for reception and placement services—the public interest includes enforcement of those statutory duties. The court's recognition that the government was unlikely to succeed on its APA claims meant that the public interest favored maintaining required services for refugees already admitted.

The organizational plaintiffs—resettlement agencies with cooperative agreements—also had distinct equities. The government had terminated their cooperative agreements, which the district court ordered reinstated. These organizations had relied on continued performance of these agreements and faced disruption from sudden termination. The balance of equities thus required consideration of the reliance interests of organizations that had built operations around providing federally-mandated services.

The immigration context also implicates foreign relations considerations. The executive branch has unique competence in assessing how refugee policy affects relations with other nations and international perceptions of the United States. This factor reinforced the court's deference to executive determinations about admission policy. But foreign policy concerns did not entirely displace domestic statutory obligations—Congress had made the policy judgment that admitted refugees should receive certain services, and the executive could not simply disregard that judgment.

## The Court's Application: Stay on Admissions, Reinstatement on Services

The court's ultimate disposition exemplifies how appellate courts tailor interim relief to match their analysis of each claim. Rather than granting or denying the stay in toto, the court issued a split ruling that stayed the district court's injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below."

On the admissions suspension, the court stayed the preliminary injunctions that had prohibited enforcement of Executive Order 14163. This meant the government could resume implementing its suspension of refugee admissions under USRAP while the appeal proceeded. The court concluded that the government was likely to succeed on its argument that § 1182(f) authorized the President's determination, and that the balance of equities and public interest favored allowing the executive to implement its chosen admission policy.

On the resettlement services issue, the court took the opposite approach. Because the government was unlikely to prevail on the APA claims regarding services, and because of the government's admitted uncertainty about providing required services, the court "directed [the government] to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This reinstatement was limited to "refugees who have been admitted"—that is, those already lawfully present in the country—and included "the usual and customary services" provided under prior cooperative agreements.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent highlights the interpretive disagreement at the heart of the services issue. Judge Lee read § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs"—as indicating that the statute was permissive rather than mandatory. Under this view, "authorization" grants permission but does not compel action. Judge Lee also emphasized that the specific funding provisions in § 1522(b)-(e) use the phrase "Director is authorized," which he interpreted as discretionary language.

The majority's contrary reading focused on the mandatory "shall" language in § 1522(a) and specific subsections like (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A). This interpretive dispute—between reading the statute as authorization versus mandate—represents a fundamental disagreement about statutory construction that would presumably be addressed more fully in the merits opinion.

For students of appellate procedure, the decision demonstrates several practical points. First, stays pending appeal are neither all-or-nothing propositions nor mere procedural formalities—they can be carefully calibrated to preserve the status quo in light of the court's preliminary assessment of the merits. Second, the stay determination itself can reveal the court's tentative views on substantive legal questions, providing a preview of the eventual merits opinion. Third, even in areas of substantial executive discretion, such as immigration, statutory limits remain enforceable through judicial review. The President's broad authority over admission of aliens does not extinguish Congress's separate authority to mandate how those already admitted must be treated.

# The Court's Order and Judge Lee's Partial Dissent

## The Order's Practical Requirements: Reinstating Cooperative Agreements

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* produces an unusual outcome: the government largely prevails on the question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions, yet it must still take affirmative action to reinstate certain funding mechanisms. Understanding this result requires careful attention to the procedural posture and the court's application of the stay factors.

The court begins by vacating its prior stays—the administrative stay issued July 18, 2025, and the earlier stay issued March 25, 2025 (as amended in April and May). These prior orders had temporarily frozen the district court's preliminary injunctions while the appeal was being briefed. By vacating these stays, the court might seem to be restoring the status quo from the district court. But that is not what happens. Instead, the court issues a new stay pending appeal—one that has a different scope than the prior stays.

The court stays the district court's February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below." The "except as noted below" carveout is critical. The court directs the government "to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This is not merely a denial of the government's request for a complete stay; it is an affirmative requirement that the government act. The order specifies that the services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

This aspect of the order illustrates an important feature of stay litigation. A stay is typically understood as a suspension of a lower court's order—maintaining the status quo while the appeal proceeds. Here, however, the court uses its stay authority not simply to freeze proceedings, but to require the government to take steps it had previously refused to take. The court justifies this approach through its analysis of the Administrative Procedure Act claims, finding that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of those claims. The practical effect is that while the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions remains in effect, the funding for services to refugees already in the country must continue.

## The Distinction Between Refugees Already Admitted Versus Those Awaiting Admission

Central to the court's reasoning is a distinction between two categories of individuals: refugees who have already been admitted to the United States, and refugees who have been approved for resettlement but remain outside the country. This distinction drives the different treatment of the two main issues in the case.

For refugees awaiting admission, the court gives the President's suspension order full effect. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court concludes that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and that the President has satisfied the statute's sole prerequisite—finding that entry of the covered class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court emphasizes that it cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Under this highly deferential standard, the executive order's suspension of admissions is likely to be upheld.

For refugees already admitted, however, the court reaches a different conclusion. The court reads 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing statutory obligations to provide "reception and placement services" to refugees "after their admission into the United States." The statute requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide services including employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. The statute authorizes these services to be provided through cooperative agreements with nonprofit organizations—the very agreements the State Department terminated in February 2025.

The court expresses concern about the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide these services. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency," but at oral argument, the government was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies." This uncertainty, combined with the court's reading of the statutory mandates in § 1522, leads the court to require reinstatement of the cooperative agreements—at least for refugees already in the country.

This bifurcated approach reflects the court's attempt to navigate between two competing legal frameworks. The President's authority over admission decisions under § 1182(f) is nearly plenary, governed by a standard of extreme deference. But once individuals have been admitted, different statutory provisions apply—provisions that, in the court's view, create concrete obligations. The distinction between "entry" and "post-entry" rights is well established in immigration law, and the court's order draws on that tradition.

## Judge Lee's Agreement on Presidential Authority to Suspend Admissions

Judge Lee concurs with the majority's conclusion that the President has authority to suspend refugee admissions. This agreement reflects the Supreme Court's holding in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which established that § 1182(f) grants the President broad power to suspend the entry of any class of aliens when the President finds that their entry would be detrimental to national interests. Judge Lee accepts that this authority extends to the refugee context, notwithstanding the specific statutory framework created by the Refugee Act of 1980.

Judge Lee's agreement on this point underscores that the dissent is narrow in scope. Judge Lee would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety"—meaning Judge Lee would not require the government to provide services even to refugees already admitted. The disagreement, therefore, is not about the scope of presidential power over admissions, but rather about whether statutory obligations persist after admission has occurred.

This alignment on the admissions question also reveals something about the procedural posture. The majority and dissent both apply the stay factors from *Nken v. Holder*, and both conclude that the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. The split emerges only when the court turns to the APA claims concerning funding for resettlement services—specifically, the interpretation of § 1522.

## Judge Lee's Dissenting Interpretation of § 1522

Judge Lee's dissent centers on a fundamental question of statutory interpretation: Does § 1522 create mandatory obligations, or does it merely authorize discretionary programs? The answer to this question determines whether the court can properly order the executive branch to continue funding resettlement services.

Judge Lee begins with the statute's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Drawing on Black's Law Dictionary, Judge Lee argues that "authorization" is permissive—it means "permission to do something," not a command to act. This is a textualist argument, using the statutory title as evidence of congressional intent.

The dissent then examines the substantive provisions of § 1522, particularly subsections (b) through (e), which describe specific refugee service programs. Each of these provisions states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance. For Judge Lee, this language signals congressional choice to permit but not require executive action. If Congress had wanted to mandate these services, Judge Lee argues, it would have used the word "shall."

The majority, by contrast, focuses on § 1522(a), which uses mandatory language. The Director "shall"—"to the extent of available appropriations"—provide employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and ensure equal opportunity for women. Judge Lee offers a different reading of this language. The phrase "In providing assistance under this section" qualifies the obligations in § 1522(a), Judge Lee argues. In other words, the mandatory conditions apply only if the Director has already chosen to provide assistance under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b) through (e). This creates a conditional structure: if you fund programs, you must do so in accordance with certain conditions. But you are not required to fund programs in the first place.

Judge Lee also addresses two provisions that the majority cites as evidence of mandatory obligations: § 1522(d)(2)(B), concerning unaccompanied refugee children, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), concerning interim support projects. Judge Lee reads these provisions as retaining executive discretion. Subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children only "if necessary," and the determination of necessity lies with the executive. Subsection (e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to develop projects "as needed"—again, in Judge Lee's view, a standard that leaves room for executive judgment.

This disagreement illustrates a recurring tension in statutory interpretation: how to read statutes that mix permissive and mandatory language. When Congress uses both "authorized" and "shall" in the same statutory scheme, courts must determine how these terms relate to each other. Judge Lee reads the authorization language as the governing structural principle, with the mandatory language serving as conditional requirements. The majority, presumably, reads the mandatory language as independently operative—creating obligations that exist so long as appropriations are available.

## The Broader Separation of Powers Implications of the Majority-Dissent Split

The disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee reflects deeper questions about the separation of powers and the role of courts in reviewing executive action. When Congress enacts a statute that creates a federal program, but the executive branch decides not to implement that program, can the courts order implementation? The answer depends on whether the statute creates judicially enforceable rights—a question that sits at the intersection of statutory interpretation, administrative law, and constitutional structure.

Judge Lee's dissent articulates a vision of executive discretion that echoes themes from the "political question" doctrine and unitary executive theory. If Congress has only "authorized" the executive to act, then the decision whether to act belongs to the political branches, not the judiciary. Judge Lee frames this as a matter of institutional competence: "the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services, our court is in no position to mandate them." This suggests that even if the court believes providing services would be good policy, the court lacks authority to order it.

The majority's approach, by contrast, reflects the traditional role of courts in enforcing statutory obligations. If Congress has mandated certain services through validly enacted legislation, and those services are not being provided, then a court may order the executive branch to comply. This reflects a view of checks and balances in which the judiciary ensures that the executive does not disregard statutory commands.

The separation of powers question becomes more complex given the context: immigration and refugee policy, areas where executive power is traditionally at its apex. The majority acknowledges that the President has broad authority over admission decisions, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*. But the majority distinguishes between admission (where executive power is vast) and post-admission services (where statutory obligations may apply). This line-drawing exercise—determining where plenary executive power ends and statutory obligations begin—implicates fundamental questions about the scope of judicial review in immigration cases.

Both sides invoke text, but they read the same text differently. Both sides claim fidelity to congressional intent, but they disagree about what Congress intended. This pattern is familiar in statutory interpretation cases, and it underscores that textualism alone rarely resolves hard cases. Judges must make choices about how to read statutory language, and those choices often reflect deeper commitments about institutional roles and the proper balance of power among the branches of government.

# Background and Context

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." This executive order invoked two statutory provisions—8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)—as the legal foundation for its directives. Section 1182(f) is particularly significant: it provides that whenever the President finds that the entry of any class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

Relying on this authority, Executive Order 14163 made a presidential determination "that entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The order then directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings. The scope of this suspension is comprehensive: it applies to refugees seeking admission under the entire USRAP framework. The government's legal position, which the Ninth Circuit references, is that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to presidential judgment and vests the President with "ample power to impose entry restrictions" beyond those specifically enumerated elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of Foreign Aid Funding

A separate executive order, issued on the same day as Executive Order 14163, also affected the refugee program. Executive Order No. 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," directed the Department of State to suspend foreign aid funding pending review. On January 24, 2025, four days after the order's issuance, the State Department acted pursuant to this directive to suspend all funding for the USRAP program.

Critically, this funding suspension extended beyond overseas operations. It encompassed funding for "domestic resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." This aspect of the funding freeze would become central to the litigation, as plaintiffs argued that defunding services for refugees already in the United States violated statutory mandates. The dual executive orders thus operated on two distinct tracks: EO 14163 addressed the admission of new refugees, while EO 14169's implementation cut off funding for services supporting refugees who had already been admitted.

## The Plaintiffs: Admitted Refugees, Conditionally Approved Refugees, and Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs in this case fall into three categories, each representing different interests affected by the executive orders. The first group consists of "refugees who were previously admitted to the United States." These individuals had already lawfully entered the country under the refugee program but faced the termination of resettlement services that Congress had mandated. Their interest is in continuing services to support their integration and self-sufficiency.

The second group comprises refugees who "have been approved for resettlement to the United States but remain outside the country." These individuals had completed the approval process and were awaiting admission when the executive order suspended the program. Their legal interest centers on whether the government could halt their entry after they had already been conditionally approved—a question the prior motions panel addressed by carving out this group from the initial stay.

The third group of plaintiffs consists of "three organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States." These organizational plaintiffs had direct contractual relationships with the government; the State Department terminated those cooperative agreements on February 26, 2025. Their standing derives from the direct economic and operational harm caused by the funding termination, in addition to their interest in carrying out their organizational missions.

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions and Their Scope

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, alleging violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The Refugee Act, passed by Congress in 1980, established "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The plaintiffs argued that Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated this statutory framework and that the defunding of USRAP violated various APA provisions.

The district court responded with two preliminary injunctions. On February 25, the court orally issued its first preliminary injunction, prohibiting enforcement of Executive Order 14163's provisions suspending refugee admissions. The following day, February 26, the government terminated every cooperative agreement providing reception and placement services domestically and nearly all agreements for overseas processing. The district court then issued a written order on February 28 to "define the scope" of its preliminary injunction, explicitly specifying that the injunction also "prohibits the suspension of USRAP funding." This order (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) thus extended judicial protection to the funding streams that enabled resettlement services.

On March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), this time ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. This second injunction was practical in nature: it sought to restore the operational capacity of the resettlement organizations that the government had defunded. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, bringing both injunctions before the Ninth Circuit.

## Prior Ninth Circuit Orders and the Procedural Posture Leading to This Decision

The procedural history of this appeal involves multiple orders from the Ninth Circuit, reflecting the expedited and evolving nature of the dispute. The government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part on March 25. In that order, the panel quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to presidential authority in immigration matters. However, the panel denied the stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—refugees who had already received conditional approval before the executive order would continue to receive protection from the injunction.

The motions panel issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying the scope of this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk to place the appeal on the next available calendar for oral argument. Before the scheduled argument on September 19, however, the district court issued an order on July 14 establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. This prompted the government to file an "emergency motion to enforce, clarify or amend the stay order" on July 17.

The Ninth Circuit responded on July 18 by issuing an "administrative stay" of the district court's July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is a temporary measure that preserves the status quo while the court considers the merits of a motion. The court then scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis for September 3, 2025. The September 12, 2025 order—the subject of this chapter—represents the court's ruling following that argument. The court vacated both its administrative stay of July 18 and its prior stay of March 25 (as amended), replacing them with a new stay pending appeal. The court also partially granted and partially denied the government's requests, staying the district court's injunctions in their entirety except as specified in the order itself—most significantly, requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

# Presidential Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

This chapter examines the court's analysis of the President's statutory authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The central question before the court was whether the government had made a strong enough showing of likely success on the merits to warrant a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction. The court concluded that the government was likely to prevail on this claim, relying heavily on Supreme Court precedent interpreting the breadth of presidential authority under the immigration laws.

## 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): Text and Scope of Presidential Authority

The statutory foundation for the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions is found at 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This provision states that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

The Ninth Circuit panel observed that this statutory language "exudes deference" to the President. The text vests the President with what the court described as "ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act." Notably, the statute imposes only one condition before the President may act: he must "find" that entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The statute does not specify what evidence must support such a finding, what procedural steps must precede it, or how recent the finding must be. The temporal dimension—"for such period as he shall deem necessary"—also lies within the President's discretion.

In this case, Executive Order No. 14163 invoked this authority directly. The President determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The executive order thus tracked the statutory language closely, invoking the precise finding that § 1182(f) requires.

## Trump v. Hawaii Precedent and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard

The Ninth Circuit's analysis drew heavily from the Supreme Court's decision in Trump v. Hawaii, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). In that case, the Supreme Court considered a challenge to President Trump's "travel ban" executive order, which suspended entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. The Supreme Court upheld that order, interpreting § 1182(f) as conferring broad discretionary authority on the President.

The Ninth Circuit quoted the Hawaii decision's characterization that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. This phrase captures the Court's view that the statute's text—particularly phrases like "he may deem necessary" and "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate"—signaled Congress's intent to leave substantial policy judgments to the executive branch. The Supreme Court had emphasized that the statute "vests the President with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]."

This deference has important practical consequences for judicial review. Once the President makes the requisite "finding" under the statute, courts have limited tools to question either the substance of that finding or the scope of the resulting suspension. The statutory framework essentially asks courts to verify that the President has made the finding—that he has said the magic words—and then step back from closer scrutiny.

## The Sufficiency of the President's 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests' Finding

Applying Hawaii's framework, the Ninth Circuit concluded that President Trump had "undoubtedly fulfilled" the sole prerequisite that § 1182(f) imposes. The executive order contained an explicit finding that entry of refugees under the USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court required nothing more.

This conclusion illustrates the low threshold that § 1182(f) establishes. The statute does not require the President to explain his reasoning, provide evidence, or demonstrate that the finding is supported by the record. It requires only that he make the finding. The executive order's single sentence declaring that refugee entry "would be detrimental" satisfied the statutory requirement, at least as interpreted by the Hawaii Court.

The court's analysis here is notably brief. It did not examine whether the President had articulated particular harms, whether those harms were supported by any evidence, or whether the scope of the suspension matched the nature of the concerns. The brevity of the court's discussion itself demonstrates how deferential the standard is: once the President has "found" detriment, the statutory condition is met.

## Why Courts Cannot Conduct 'A Searching Inquiry' into Presidential Justifications

A critical limitation on judicial review emerges from the Supreme Court's admonition that courts may not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The Ninth Circuit quoted this language directly from Hawaii. This principle has significant implications for how courts must approach challenges to presidential action under § 1182(f).

The prohibition on "searching inquiry" means that courts cannot second-guess the President's policy judgments or demand that he justify his determination with evidence. The court cannot ask whether the President's assessment of the national interest is correct, whether the evidence supports his concerns, or whether less restrictive alternatives might address the same problems. The court's role is limited to verifying that the President has made the statutory finding and that the executive action falls within the textual bounds of the authority conferred.

This limitation reflects both separation of powers concerns and the particular nature of immigration and national security policy. The executive branch has superior institutional capacity to assess threats to national interests, and the political branches—not the judiciary—have primary responsibility for setting admission policy. The Hawaii framework thus constrains courts to a relatively deferential posture when reviewing exercises of § 1182(f) authority.

## The Majority's Conclusion on Likelihood of Success for the Admissions Suspension

The court concluded that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions." This conclusion was straightforward under the Hawaii framework. The executive order "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority" because § 1182(f) "is clear" in authorizing the President to suspend entry of "any class of aliens."

The court's analysis merged the statutory text with the deference standard from Hawaii. The President had made the requisite finding; the suspension fell within the class-based language of the statute; and therefore the executive action appeared consistent with statutory authority. The Refugee Act of 1980, which plaintiffs invoked, did not alter this conclusion—at least as the court saw it. The court made no suggestion that the Refugee Act's provisions constrained the President's § 1182(f) authority in any way.

Notably, this portion of the court's order attracted no dissent. Judge Lee concurred in the majority's conclusion regarding the admission suspension, agreeing "that the President has the authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions." The unanimity on this issue underscores how firmly Hawaii established the scope of § 1182(f) authority: even a judge who would have ruled differently on related questions accepted that the President could suspend refugee admissions under this statute.

The court's treatment of the admissions suspension claim illustrates an important principle for understanding stays and preliminary injunctions. The first Nken factor—likelihood of success on the merits—was easily satisfied here. The combination of a clear statutory finding, text that "exudes deference," and a controlling Supreme Court precedent that limits judicial inquiry made it highly likely that the government would prevail on the merits of this claim when the court reached a final decision.

# Statutory Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Services vs. Discretionary Authorization

## Overview of § 1522 and the Refugee Act of 1980 Framework

The Refugee Act of 1980, passed as an amendment to the Immigration and Nationality Act, established the framework for the admission and resettlement of refugees in the United States. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act sets forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." Within this framework, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs the domestic resettlement services that the federal government provides to refugees after their admission into the United States.

Section 1522 sits at the heart of the dispute in *Pacito v. Trump*. The organizational plaintiffs—nonprofit agencies that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide resettlement services—challenged the government's termination of those agreements under the Administrative Procedure Act. The threshold question before the Ninth Circuit was whether § 1522 creates mandatory obligations that the government must fulfill, or whether it merely authorizes discretionary programs that the executive branch may choose to fund or not. This question of statutory interpretation determines whether the government's actions violated the APA, which prohibits agency action that is "not in accordance with law."

The majority and dissent reached opposite conclusions on this question, employing different tools of statutory interpretation. Their disagreement illustrates how courts wrestle with ambiguous statutory language and the significance of textual clues like section titles, mandatory versus permissive verbs, and statutory structure.

## The Majority's Reading: 'Shall' Language Creates Mandatory Duties

The majority concluded that the government was "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" because § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations to provide certain resettlement services. The opinion begins its analysis with § 1522(a)(1)(A), which states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall," "to the extent of available appropriations," provide several categories of assistance.

The use of "shall" is significant in statutory interpretation. The word "shall" is generally presumed to impose a mandatory duty rather than granting discretionary authority. Here, the majority read § 1522(a)(1)(A) as requiring the Director to make available employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunities for women refugees—to the extent Congress has appropriated funds for these purposes. The majority also noted that these services must be provided "as quickly as possible" to achieve economic self-sufficiency, suggesting congressional intent that refugees receive prompt assistance.

Crucially, the majority observed that Congress appropriated approximately $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 (though reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025). The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" thus operates as a limiting principle: the government's obligation extends only as far as Congress has provided funding. But once appropriations exist, the majority concluded, the statute requires the government to fulfill its statutory duties.

The majority further noted that § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) permits these services to be provided through "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The organizational plaintiffs held such cooperative agreements, which the government terminated in February 2025. Given the government's uncertainty at oral argument about whether it could secure alternative providers, the majority ordered the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

## Key Provisions: § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The majority relied on three specific provisions to support its reading of § 1522 as creating mandatory obligations.

First, § 1522(a)(1)(A) establishes the baseline obligations for refugee services. This provision requires the Director to provide four categories of assistance: "(i) make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement," "(ii) provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training," "(iii) insure that cash assistance is made available to refugees," and "(iv) insure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction." Each of these mandatory commands is modified by the prefatory language limiting the obligation "to the extent of available appropriations." The majority understood this structure as creating a binding duty tied to congressional funding decisions.

Second, the majority pointed to § 1522(d)(2)(B), which addresses unaccompanied refugee minors. This provision states that "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." The majority found this language significant because it imposes a direct obligation on the Director to take responsibility for unaccompanied minors during the interim period before placement. The "if necessary" qualifier, in the majority's view, does not eliminate the mandatory nature of the duty but rather specifies when that duty is triggered.

Third, § 1522(e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary of Health and Human Services to "develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." Again, the majority read "shall develop and implement" as creating a mandatory obligation, with "as needed" merely defining the scope of services required.

Together, these provisions persuaded the majority that Congress intended to require the executive branch to provide resettlement services when refugees have been admitted and appropriations are available. The government's termination of cooperative agreements, coupled with its uncertainty about replacement providers, suggested that it was failing to meet these statutory obligations.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: Significance of 'Authorization' in Section Title

Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, offered a fundamentally different interpretation of § 1522. In his view, "the provision is most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate." Judge Lee began with a textualist starting point: the section's title.

Section 1522's title reads: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee emphasized that "authorization" is permissive, not mandatory. He cited Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "authorization" as "permission to do something" or "the official document granting such permission." This definitional approach reflects the principle that statutory titles and headings can inform interpretation, particularly when they are part of the enacted statute rather than merely editorial additions.

Judge Lee observed that the specific funding provisions throughout § 1522 consistently use permissive language. Sections 1522(b) through (e)—which provide funding for various refugee services including "Program of initial resettlement" and "Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees"—all state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. In Judge Lee's reading, this repeated use of "authorized" rather than "required" or "shall" signals Congress's intent to grant discretion to the executive branch.

This interpretive dispute illustrates how courts must weigh competing textual signals. The majority focused on the "shall" language in § 1522(a), while Judge Lee focused on the section title and the "authorized" language in the substantive funding provisions. Both sides claimed that their reading reflected the statute's "natural" meaning, demonstrating how reasonable judges can disagree about which textual indicators should control.

## Structural Interpretation: § 1522(a) as Conditions on Discretionary Funding

Judge Lee's dissent offered a structural interpretation that sought to reconcile the "shall" language in § 1522(a) with the permissive language elsewhere in the section. He argued that § 1522(a) does not independently mandate funding but rather imposes mandatory conditions that apply *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretion under the funding provisions.

The key textual clue, according to Judge Lee, is that § 1522(a) is titled "Conditions and consideration." This title suggests that subsection (a) establishes requirements that govern how programs must operate, not whether they must operate. Judge Lee pointed to the qualifying phrase in § 1522(a): "In providing assistance under this section." This phrase, he argued, signals that the mandatory "shall" language applies only when the government is already "providing assistance"—that is, when it has chosen to exercise the discretion granted in the subsequent subsections.

Under this reading, the structure of § 1522 works as follows: subsections (b) through (e) authorize the executive branch to establish and fund refugee programs. If the government elects to provide such assistance, then subsection (a) imposes mandatory "conditions and considerations" that must be followed. For example, if the Director exercises discretion to fund employment training under § 1522(c), then § 1522(a)(1)(A)(i) requires that such training aim to "achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible."

Judge Lee applied similar reasoning to the provisions the majority cited. Subsection (d)(2)(B)'s requirement that the Director assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors only applies "if necessary," and it "drips with deference to the executive branch." Subsection (e)(7)(A)'s requirement that the Secretary "develop and implement" projects applies only "as needed," leaving the "metes and bounds of the programs" to executive discretion. In Judge Lee's view, these qualifiers undermine any claim of a firm congressional mandate.

## The Role of Appropriations in Determining the Scope of Obligations

Both the majority and the dissent grappled with the significance of the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" in § 1522(a)(1)(A). This phrase is central to the dispute because it links statutory obligations to congressional funding decisions, but the two opinions understood that link differently.

For the majority, appropriations operate as an enabling condition: once Congress appropriates funds, the government has a duty to provide the services described in § 1522. The majority noted that Congress appropriated billions of dollars for refugee assistance for FY 2025, which meant that appropriations were "available" and the government's obligations were triggered. The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" merely limits the scope of services to what Congress has funded; it does not eliminate the underlying duty.

Judge Lee offered a different perspective. In his view, the appropriations language is consistent with § 1522 being an authorization statute—a type of legislation that permits spending but does not require it. He argued that "Section 1522 authorizes the executive branch to craft programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow. It does not require that those programs operate." Under this reading, appropriations are a necessary but not sufficient condition for services to be provided; the executive retains discretion over whether to actually establish programs even when funding exists.

This disagreement has practical implications that go beyond this case. If § 1522 creates mandatory obligations, then refugees admitted to the United States have enforceable legal rights to receive certain services, and agencies can be sued under the APA for failing to provide them. If § 1522 merely authorizes discretionary programs, then the executive branch has greater flexibility to reduce or eliminate services, subject only to political accountability rather than judicial review.

The majority's order addressed these practical concerns by directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to refugees "who have been admitted to the United States." This limitation reflects the majority's recognition that the government's obligations under § 1522 apply specifically to those refugees who have already been admitted—an important distinction given that the court simultaneously stayed the injunction blocking the suspension of new refugee admissions.

# The Stay Pending Appeal Framework

When a district court issues a preliminary injunction, the losing party often seeks immediate relief from the appellate court rather than waiting for the full appeal to be resolved. The procedural mechanism for such relief is a "stay pending appeal"—a temporary freeze of the lower court's order while the appeal proceeds. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit confronted a request to stay two preliminary injunctions that had blocked key aspects of the Trump administration's refugee policy. The court's analysis provides a clear illustration of how the stay pending appeal framework operates and how courts can reach different conclusions about different aspects of a single case.

## Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stay Pending Appeal

The Supreme Court established the governing standard for stays pending appeal in *Nken v. Holder*. The test requires courts to weigh four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Importantly, the Court emphasized that the first two factors—the likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are "the most critical."

This framework is discretionary and fact-intensive. Courts do not mechanically tally the factors; rather, they engage in a balancing exercise. The "strong showing" language for the first factor signals that a stay applicant faces a higher burden than a party seeking a preliminary injunction. The applicant must demonstrate more than a mere possibility of success—it must show a substantial case on the merits. The reasoning behind this elevated burden is that a stay pending appeal upsets the status quo established by the district court's careful consideration of the case. The appellate court must therefore have solid justification for intervening before the appeal is fully briefed and argued.

The *Nken* test reflects competing institutional concerns. On one hand, appellate courts must respect the district court's reasoned judgment and the presumption against disturbing its orders. On the other hand, if the district court has likely erred and the appellant faces serious irreparable harm, the appellate court has an obligation to provide interim relief. The balancing of these concerns is inherently case-specific, which is why courts often reach nuanced, fact-dependent conclusions.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Divergence Between Admissions and Services Claims

The most significant aspect of *Pacito* is the court's divergent analysis of two distinct legal claims: the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions and the challenge to the termination of resettlement services funding. The court reached opposite conclusions on the likelihood of success for these claims, illustrating how the same statutory scheme can support different outcomes for different issues.

For the admissions suspension claim, the court found the government likely to succeed. The executive order rested on authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." The court quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that this provision "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." The "sole prerequisite" under the statute is that the President find that entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The court concluded that President Trump had "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement" through Executive Order 14163. Because the statutory language was clear and the executive order did not exceed textual limits, the government had a strong likelihood of success on this claim.

The services claim required a different analysis. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the federal government has obligations regarding refugee resettlement services. The court examined several subsections of this provision. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to make available employment training, provide English language instruction, ensure cash assistance is available, and guarantee equal opportunity for women. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) provides that for unaccompanied refugee minors, the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary." Section 1522(e)(7)(A) states that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for refugees in their first thirty-six months in the United States.

Congress had appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance—$3.178 billion for fiscal year 2025, reduced by $800 million through later rescission. These appropriations meant the "to the extent of available appropriations" limitation in § 1522(a)(1)(A) was satisfied. Yet at oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency to provide reception and placement services, despite having represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals." This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill statutory obligations led the court to conclude that the government was not likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act challenges to the funding termination.

Judge Lee's partial dissent provides an important contrast. He argued that § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signals that the provision is permissive, not mandatory. He cited the dictionary definition of "authorization" as "permission to do something." He further noted that the specific funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance, using permissive rather than mandatory language. In Judge Lee's reading, § 1522(a)'s use of "shall" merely imposes conditions that apply *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretion to fund refugee programs. This interpretive dispute highlights how statutory construction can determine the outcome of the stay analysis.

## Irreparable Injury Considerations

The irreparable injury factor asks whether the party seeking the stay faces harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or corrected after the appeal. Traditional examples include loss of constitutional rights, permanent loss of business opportunities, or environmental damage. Courts consider whether the harm is imminent, whether it is certain to occur, and whether the injury is truly irreparable.

For the admissions suspension, the court's analysis of irreparable injury was implicit in its conclusion that the government was likely to succeed. The court emphasized that the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States" favored the government, given the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in immigration matters under *Hawaii*. The government's interest in controlling admissions and conducting foreign policy was sufficient to satisfy the irreparable injury requirement for this portion of the stay.

For the services claim, the court focused on the consequences for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. These individuals had no control over the government's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations. Without the reception and placement services historically provided through these agreements, admitted refugees would face immediate hardship in securing basic necessities—employment training, language instruction, and cash assistance. The court's eventual order to reinstate cooperative agreements reflects its concern that failure to provide these services would cause irreparable harm to a vulnerable population that Congress had specifically intended to protect.

The organizational plaintiffs—the three resettlement organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department—also faced irreparable injury. Their contracts were terminated, their ability to fulfill their mission was eliminated, and their relationship with the refugee population they served was disrupted. This harm extended beyond mere financial loss to encompass the destruction of ongoing relationships and programs.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—the balance of equities and the public interest—often merge in practice. Courts consider who wins and who loses from the stay and what outcome best serves broader societal interests. These factors can sometimes cut in different directions, requiring careful calibration.

In *Pacito*, the court found that the balance of equities and public interest favored different outcomes for different claims. For the admissions suspension, the court deferred to the President's assessment of what serves the national interest. *Trump v. Hawaii* established that courts should not engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications" for entry restrictions under § 1182(f). The public interest in controlling borders and determining who may enter the country weighed heavily in favor of staying the injunction against the admissions suspension.

For the services claim, however, the public interest cut the other way. Congress had spoken through the Refugee Act of 1980, creating statutory obligations for the treatment of refugees who have been admitted. The public interest in the executive branch complying with statutory mandates, and in ensuring that vulnerable individuals receive the assistance Congress has provided, supported maintaining some level of services. The court's concern was not that the administration had made a policy choice about funding levels, but that it had terminated cooperative agreements without any clear plan for providing the services that the statute may require.

The court's practical solution—staying the injunction against the admissions suspension while reinstating cooperative agreements for services to already-admitted refugees—represents an attempt to respect both the President's broad authority over admissions and Congress's specific directives regarding treatment of admitted refugees. This bifurcated approach demonstrates how courts can craft stays that are responsive to the specific legal issues and equities at stake in each portion of a complex case.

## The Court's Practical Disposition: Reinstating Cooperative Agreements for Admitted Refugees

The court's order reflects a pragmatic approach to the stay pending appeal. Rather than granting or denying the stay in its entirety, the court crafted a tailored remedy that addressed its concerns about compliance with statutory obligations while respecting executive authority. The court vacated its prior stays and the district court's preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below." The exception required the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

This order is significant for several reasons. First, it limited the services mandate to refugees who have already been admitted—not those approved for resettlement but still abroad. This distinction tracks the statutory language: § 1522 governs "domestic resettlement," implying it applies to those who have already entered. Second, the order required the government to provide "the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements," providing concrete guidance about what services must be provided. Third, the court explicitly stated that it was not resolving the merits of the appeal, reserving its full reasoning "for reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow."

The court's willingness to order reinstatement of cooperative agreements at the stay stage is notable. Typically, stays preserve the status quo rather than mandate affirmative action. But the court concluded that the status quo could not simply be frozen—refugees who had been admitted were already in the United States and required immediate assistance. The cooperative agreements had been terminated after the litigation began, and reinstatement was necessary to restore a functioning system for providing the services that Congress had mandated.

Judge Lee's partial dissent underscores the contentious nature of this remedy. In his view, § 1522 creates no legal duty to provide services, meaning the court had no authority to order the government to reinstate cooperative agreements. He would have stayed the district court's injunctions "in their entirety," giving the executive branch complete discretion over both admissions and services. This disagreement reflects a deeper divide over statutory interpretation and the respective roles of Congress and the President in refugee policy.

The *Pacito* order illustrates that stay decisions can have immediate, practical consequences even before an appellate court decides the merits. The court's determination that the government was unlikely to succeed on the services claim led to immediate reinstatement of cooperative agreements, ensuring continuity of services for admitted refugees while the appeal proceeds. Conversely, the court's conclusion that the government was likely to succeed on the admissions claim meant that the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions could continue during the appeal. This combination demonstrates the *Nken* framework in action: a four-factor balancing test that allows courts to provide tailored interim relief in complex cases involving multiple legal issues and competing interests.

# Judge Lee's Partial Dissent and Separation of Powers Implications

## Judge Lee's Agreement on Presidential Authority Over Admissions

Judge Lee's partial dissent begins with an important point of consensus: he agrees with the majority that the President possesses broad authority to suspend refugee admissions. This agreement reflects the Supreme Court's holding in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which established that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and grants him "ample power to impose entry restrictions" beyond those specifically enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act. Judge Lee would therefore stay the district court's injunction in its entirety as it relates to the admission of refugees.

This portion of the dissent demonstrates a fundamental principle of immigration law: the political branches—and particularly the President—wield near-plenary power over admissions decisions. Judge Lee recognizes that courts "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications'" for entry suspensions. Once the President makes the requisite finding under § 1182(f) that entry of a class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States," judicial review is highly constrained. The majority and dissent thus align on the scope of presidential power over the border.

The split emerges solely on the question of domestic resettlement services—what happens to refugees already admitted or in transit when the suspension takes effect. This division transforms the case from a challenge to presidential admissions authority into a dispute over statutory interpretation and the judicial role in enforcing statutory mandates against the executive branch.

## The Dissent's Statutory Interpretation Methodology

Judge Lee's dissent employs a systematic approach to statutory interpretation that begins with the text's structure and proceeds through its provisions in order. His methodology offers a clear framework for understanding how courts parse ambiguous statutory language.

The dissent opens with a textualist observation: the title of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee emphasizes the word "authorization," defining it through Black's Law Dictionary as "permission to do something." Under this reading, the statute permits the executive branch to act but does not compel action. This starting point reflects the canon that statutory titles can inform interpretation, though they are not controlling.

Judge Lee then surveys the operative provisions of § 1522, noting that subsections (b) through (e)—which actually provide funding for different refugee services—all use permissive language. These provisions state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance for initial resettlement, cash assistance, medical assistance, and other services. The repeated use of "authorized" rather than "shall" or "must" signals congressional intent to grant discretion rather than impose obligations.

The majority had relied on § 1522(a), which employs mandatory language: the Director "shall" make available resources for employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Judge Lee counters by examining the provision's qualifying phrase: "In providing assistance under this section." He argues this language conditions the mandatory obligations on the government's decision to provide assistance in the first instance. In other words, § 1522(a) specifies what the government must do *if* it chooses to fund refugee programs—but it does not require that funding to occur.

This interpretive dispute illustrates a common scenario in administrative law: determining whether "shall" language creates an individual entitlement or simply structures executive discretion. Judge Lee reads the statutory scheme as creating a conditional mandate—the government has discretion whether to operate resettlement programs, but if it does so, it must meet certain conditions. The majority reads the same provisions as creating affirmative obligations to provide services to refugees who have been admitted, given that Congress has appropriated funds for that purpose.

Judge Lee also addresses the majority's invocation of specific subsections dealing with unaccompanied minors and interim support. He notes that § 1522(d)(2)(B), which requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children "if necessary," still invokes executive discretion through the necessity qualifier. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A)'s command that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" is tempered by the phrase "as needed"—leaving the determination of need to executive judgment.

## The Separation of Powers Implications of Judicial Mandates on Executive Programs

The heart of Judge Lee's dissent concerns the proper role of the judiciary in ordering the executive branch to administer statutory programs. This question implicates core separation of powers principles that animate much of administrative law.

When a court orders an executive agency to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements and provide specific services, it intrudes upon the executive's constitutional role to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." U.S. Const. art. II, § 3. Judge Lee's dissent can be read as a warning against judicial overreach: if Congress did not clearly mandate the provision of services, the court has no authority to impose such a requirement through an injunction.

This concern reflects the "clear statement" principle that runs through separation of powers jurisprudence. When a statute is interpreted to constrain executive discretion in significant ways, courts often demand textual clarity. Judge Lee's interpretation—reading § 1522 as permissive—preserves maximum executive flexibility. The majority's reading—finding mandatory obligations—transforms judicial power into a tool for compelling executive action.

The appropriations context heightens these separation of powers concerns. Congress appropriated funds for refugee assistance, but appropriations alone do not mandate expenditure. Under the Constitution's Appropriations Clause, no money can be drawn from the Treasury "but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 7. However, appropriations typically authorize rather than require spending—they permit the executive to spend up to a certain amount, leaving discretion about whether and how to use those funds. The majority implicitly reads the Refugee Act as creating a statutory entitlement to services, converting appropriated funds into mandatory spending. Judge Lee resists this conversion.

The dissent also raises practical concerns about judicial competence. Courts lack expertise in managing refugee resettlement programs, negotiating cooperative agreements with service providers, or balancing the many factors that inform executive decisions about resource allocation. By ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements, the majority effectively assumes management authority over an executive program—a role for which courts are ill-suited.

## The Practical and Institutional Consequences of the Majority-Dissent Split

The division between the majority and Judge Lee produces two sharply different outcomes with real-world consequences for refugees, resettlement organizations, and the executive branch.

Under the majority's approach, the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. This provides continuity of services for vulnerable populations who have already been through the admissions process and are legally present in the country. Resettlement organizations can continue their work, at least pending the full merits decision. Refugees receive the support services—employment training, English instruction, case management—that facilitate integration and self-sufficiency.

Under Judge Lee's approach, the district court's injunctions would be stayed entirely, meaning the executive order suspending admissions and the funding termination would both go into effect. The President's policy judgment about refugee admissions and services would govern without judicial interference. This preserves executive flexibility and avoids what Judge Lee sees as judicial commandeering of executive functions.

The split also signals deeper institutional tensions that the full opinion must address. The motions panel that first heard this case created a "limited carveout" protecting refugees who had been conditionally approved for admission before January 20, 2025. The majority's order extends further, mandating services for those already admitted. This progression suggests the court is struggling to identify where presidential authority ends and statutory obligations begin.

For the law student, this case illustrates how preliminary injunctions and stays operate as battlegrounds for substantive disputes. The court's stay order—which is supposed to be a temporary measure preserving the status quo pending appeal—effectively resolves the merits of the APA claim regarding resettlement services. Judge Lee's dissent highlights this anomaly: by determining that the government is "not likely to prevail" on the APA claims, the majority has prejudged the merits before full briefing and argument on those issues.

The practical outcome reflects this preliminary posture. The court orders reinstatement of cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This narrow tailoring attempts to minimize judicial interference while still enforcing what the majority sees as statutory obligations. The order defers to the executive branch to determine which cooperative agreements are necessary and what services fall within "usual and customary" practice—preserving some executive discretion within the judicial mandate.

Judge Lee's dissent serves as a reminder that even interim orders can have lasting consequences. The cooperative agreements must be reinstated. Services must be provided. Organizational plaintiffs must resume operations. These practical effects may be difficult to reverse if the full opinion ultimately sides with Judge Lee's interpretation. The case thus demonstrates how the timing and sequencing of judicial intervention can shape outcomes as much as the legal reasoning itself.

# Factual and Procedural Background

## Executive Orders 14163 and 14169: Suspension of Refugee Admissions and Funding

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* arises from two executive actions taken in January 2025. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a) to suspend the entry of refugees into the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). Section 1182(f) provides that whenever the President finds that the entry of any class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States," he may suspend their entry or impose restrictions. Executive Order 14163 made that finding and directed the suspension of refugee admissions pending further determinations by the administration.

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP. This action was taken pursuant to a separate executive order, Executive Order No. 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." The funding suspension extended not only to overseas refugee processing but also to domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. The combination of these two orders effectively halted both the admission of new refugees and the provision of support services to those already in the country or approved for resettlement.

## The Plaintiffs: Individual Refugees and Organizational Resettlement Agencies

The plaintiffs in this litigation fall into two categories: individual refugees and organizational providers. The individual plaintiffs include refugees who had already been admitted to the United States as well as refugees who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country. These individuals had successfully navigated the refugee approval process and were at various stages of the resettlement pipeline when the executive orders halted the program.

The organizational plaintiffs are three entities that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide services under USRAP. These organizations performed two distinct functions: overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and domestic resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. Under the cooperative agreement structure, the federal government partners with private nonprofit agencies to deliver the practical assistance refugees need upon arrival, such as housing placement, employment services, and language training.

## Claims Under the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act

The plaintiffs challenged the executive actions on two primary legal grounds. First, they alleged that Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980. Enacted as Pub. L. No. 96-212, the Refugee Act established a comprehensive framework for the treatment of refugees and displaced persons. The Supreme Court has described the Act as setting forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." The plaintiffs contended that the President's order conflicted with the statutory scheme Congress created for refugee admissions.

Second, the plaintiffs challenged the termination of USRAP funding under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA provides the framework for judicial review of agency actions and requires that agencies follow proper procedures when taking actions that affect legal rights. The plaintiffs argued that the funding suspension violated various provisions of the APA, presumably including requirements for reasoned decision-making and compliance with statutory mandates.

## District Court Proceedings: Preliminary Injunctions of February and March 2025

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025. The district court acted quickly on their request for preliminary injunctive relief. On February 25, the court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of several sections of Executive Order 14163. The following day, the government terminated every cooperative agreement for domestic reception and placement services and nearly all cooperative agreements for overseas processing support.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order to define the scope of its February 25 injunction. Significantly, the written order specified that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. This order sought to preserve the status quo by maintaining funding for refugee services while the litigation proceeded.

The district court then issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24. This order directed the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated following the February 25 ruling. Together, the February and March orders represented a comprehensive rejection of the government's position and sought to restore the refugee program to its pre-executive order status.

The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, bringing the matter before the Ninth Circuit.

## Prior Ninth Circuit Orders: Partial Stay, Clarifications, and Administrative Stay

When the government appealed the district court's orders, it sought a stay pending appeal from the Ninth Circuit. A stay is an interim measure that suspends a lower court's order while the appeal is pending. The standard for granting a stay, as later explained in the court's September order, involves four factors drawn from *Nken v. Holder*: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable injury to the applicant absent a stay, (3) substantial injury to other parties if the stay is granted, and (4) the public interest.

A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted a partial stay on March 25, 2025. Quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel observed that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." However, the panel denied the stay motion "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—refugees who had been conditionally approved before the executive order was issued could continue in the program.

The motions panel issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying the scope of this limited carveout. The panel also directed full briefing and placed the appeal on the next available calendar for argument.

Before the scheduled argument, however, the district court issued an order on July 14 establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee that framework. The government viewed this as overstepping the Ninth Circuit's stay order and filed an emergency motion on July 17 seeking to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay. The following day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is a temporary measure that preserves the status quo while the court considers the matter. The panel then scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis for September 3, 2025.

# Presidential Authority Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

## Text and Scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The principal statutory authority invoked by Executive Order No. 14163 is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision that has become central to debates over the scope of presidential power over immigration. The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* provides an opportunity to examine how this statute operates and how courts interpret its broad language.

Section 1182(f) provides in relevant part that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The Ninth Circuit's recitation of this language highlights several structural features that shape its interpretation.

First, the statute is triggered by a presidential "find[ing]"—a determination that entry of a particular class of aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests. Second, the statute grants the President authority to act "by proclamation," suggesting a formal executive action. Third, the President may act "for such period as he shall deem necessary," indicating temporal discretion. Fourth, the President may "suspend the entry" of aliens or "impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

The breadth of this language is striking. The statute refers to "any aliens or any class of aliens," permitting the President to act categorically rather than on an individualized basis. The phrase "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate" confers substantial discretion over the substance of those restrictions. Importantly, the statute operates as an additional grant of authority—the Ninth Circuit noted that it "vests [the President] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]."

In *Pacito*, President Trump invoked this authority to suspend the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). The Executive Order determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The legal question before the Ninth Circuit was whether this invocation of § 1182(f) was valid and, more immediately, whether the government was likely to succeed on the merits of that claim for purposes of a stay pending appeal.

## Trump v. Hawaii and the 'Exudes Deference' Standard of Review

The governing precedent for evaluating presidential action under § 1182(f) is the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). The Ninth Circuit's analysis is deeply informed by this decision, and understanding *Hawaii* is essential to understanding the court's reasoning in *Pacito*.

*Trump v. Hawaii* arose from Presidential Proclamation No. 9645, which restricted entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. The proclamation was issued under the authority of § 1182(f), among other statutory provisions. Challengers argued that the proclamation exceeded the President's statutory authority and violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.

The Supreme Court upheld the proclamation. Writing for the majority, Chief Justice Roberts articulated a highly deferential standard of review for presidential actions under § 1182(f). The Court held that the statute "exudes deference" to the President and emphasized that the text vests the President with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions. This language—"exudes deference"—became a touchstone for the Ninth Circuit's analysis in *Pacito*.

The *Hawaii* Court explained that § 1182(f) grants the President authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens he finds detrimental to U.S. interests. The Court rejected the argument that the statute should be read more narrowly, holding that "the language of § 1182(f) is clear" and that the proclamation did "not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." This clear-statement approach means that courts will not read implicit limitations into the statute's broad grant of power.

Critically, the *Hawaii* Court addressed the scope of judicial review over presidential findings under § 1182(f). The Court held that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This formulation establishes a narrow role for judicial scrutiny of the President's "detrimental" finding. Courts will examine whether the President has made the required finding, but they will not second-guess the substantive basis for that finding or probe the adequacy of the President's reasoning.

The Ninth Circuit's stay order in *Pacito* applies this framework directly. Quoting *Hawaii*, the court stated that it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" The court treated *Hawaii* as establishing both the governing standard—extreme deference—and the proper methodology—look to the text of the statute rather than the quality of the executive's reasoning.

## The President's 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests' Finding and Its Sufficiency

The Ninth Circuit's analysis of the President's "detrimental" finding illustrates how *Hawaii* operates in practice. The court focused on whether the President satisfied the statutory prerequisite, not whether the finding was well-supported or persuasive.

Under § 1182(f), the "sole prerequisite" is "that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The Ninth Circuit quoted this formulation from *Hawaii* and applied it to Executive Order No. 14163. The Executive Order stated that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." This statement, the court concluded, satisfied the statutory requirement: "The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here."

Note what the court did not do. The court did not examine the factual basis for the President's determination. The court did not ask whether refugees actually pose a detriment to U.S. interests. The court did not evaluate whether the President's stated reasons were genuine or pretextual. The court simply confirmed that the President made the requisite finding, and that finding was facially sufficient under the statute.

This approach follows directly from *Hawaii*. In that case, the Supreme Court rejected the argument that courts should look beyond the proclamation's text to assess whether the President's stated national security justifications were credible. The Court held that the proclamation "is expressly premised on legitimate purposes" and that "the text says nothing about religion." The Court declined to investigate whether these stated purposes were pretextual, emphasizing that the statutory framework grants the President broad discretion.

The Ninth Circuit's reasoning in *Pacito* reflects this same approach. Once the President has made the statutory finding, the court's inquiry is essentially complete. The President has "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement" by stating that refugee entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The court's use of the word "undoubtedly" signals the deference that *Hawaii* requires.

This aspect of the decision has significant practical implications. A President seeking to suspend the entry of any class of aliens need only state that such entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The President does not need to provide detailed factual support, and challengers cannot prevail by showing that the finding is factually weak or poorly reasoned. As long as the President makes the finding, the statutory prerequisite is satisfied.

## The Limited Role of Judicial Review in Immigration and National Security

The Ninth Circuit's analysis reflects broader principles governing judicial review of executive action in the immigration and national security contexts. These principles limit the role of courts even when individual rights are at stake.

The court's reference to the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in immigration matters echoes a long-standing theme in Supreme Court jurisprudence. Immigration policy is understood to involve foreign policy and national security considerations that are committed to the political branches. Courts have traditionally been reluctant to second-guess executive judgments in these areas, recognizing that the President and Congress have institutional advantages in assessing threats and formulating responses.

The Ninth Circuit applied this principle in its analysis of the stay factors. After concluding that the government was likely to succeed on the merits of the § 1182(f) issue, the court addressed the remaining stay factors—"touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States"—by noting that these interests are determined by the "broad discretion" conferred on the President. This formulation treats the President's assessment of the public interest as controlling for purposes of the stay analysis.

The limited role of judicial review is particularly pronounced when the executive invokes national security justifications. In *Hawaii*, the Supreme Court noted that the proclamation was "expressly premised on legitimate purposes" including "the preclusion of 'detrimental' aliens" and "the 'legitimate purpose' of 'obtain[ing] additional information' about nationals of particular countries." The Court stated that it took "no issue with" the plaintiffs' claim that the President has "broad discretion" over immigration and national security matters.

The practical effect of these principles in *Pacito* is that the plaintiffs' challenge to the refugee admission suspension faced an uphill battle. The President's determination that refugee entry would be detrimental was effectively unreviewable under *Hawaii*'s standard. The court could not probe the substance of that determination or assess whether it was supported by evidence. This framework reflects a judicial posture of restraint in matters touching on immigration policy and national security—a posture that significantly advantages the executive branch in litigation over entry restrictions.

It is worth noting that this limited judicial role applies specifically to the President's authority under § 1182(f) to suspend entry. The court reached a different conclusion regarding the APA challenges to the termination of resettlement services, finding that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of those claims. The distinction illustrates that deference to executive authority has limits; while the President's entry suspension authority under § 1182(f) is broad, statutory obligations regarding services for admitted refugees may be subject to more searching judicial scrutiny.

# The Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligation or Discretionary Authorization?

## Overview of § 1522 and its placement within the Refugee Act of 1980

The Refugee Act of 1980, passed as an amendment to the Immigration and Nationality Act, establishes the legal framework for the admission and resettlement of refugees in the United States. Section 1522 of Title 8 sits at the heart of the domestic resettlement scheme. Titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," this provision governs the services the federal government provides to refugees after they have been admitted to the United States. The statute addresses employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, medical services, and special protections for unaccompanied refugee minors.

The plaintiffs in *Pacito* challenged the government's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), arguing that § 1522 creates statutory duties that the executive branch cannot simply abandon. The government countered that § 1522 is permissive—it authorizes but does not mandate any particular level of service. This dispute presented the Ninth Circuit with a fundamental question of statutory interpretation: does § 1522 create legally enforceable obligations, or does it merely grant discretionary authority to the executive branch?

The panel's split on this question illustrates how courts approach statutory text, structure, and context when determining congressional intent. The majority and the dissent both claimed fidelity to the text but reached opposite conclusions about what that text requires.

## The majority's interpretation: 'shall' language and mandatory duties

The majority focused its analysis on the mandatory language scattered throughout § 1522. The court began with § 1522(a)(1)(A), which directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to accomplish four specified objectives: providing employment training and placement, offering English language training, ensuring cash assistance is available, and guaranteeing women equal participation opportunities. The majority read these directives as imposing binding obligations, tempered only by the availability of appropriations.

The court reinforced its interpretation by pointing to other subsections that use similarly mandatory language. Under § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for unaccompanied refugee minors during interim periods before placement. Under § 1522(e)(7)(A), the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects" providing interim support, medical services, and case management for recently resettled refugees. The majority treated these "shall" provisions as creating statutory duties that the government cannot simply disregard.

The court buttressed its reading with practical evidence from the record. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" for a new resettlement agency, yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill statutory obligations reinforced the majority's conclusion that the APA challenge had merit. Accordingly, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements sufficient to provide the services § 1522 describes.

## Judge Lee's dissent: significance of 'Authorization' in the section title

Judge Lee opened his dissent with a lesson in statutory interpretation: the title of a statutory provision can illuminate congressional intent. Section 1522's heading reads "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee fastened onto the word "authorization," defining it in accordance with Black's Law Dictionary as "permission to do something." From this starting point, he argued that the section permits but does not compel government action. Congress "allows action in § 1522," he wrote; "it does not require it."

This approach reflects a broader principle of statutory construction: courts should read statutes as a coherent whole, and section titles provide context for interpreting specific provisions within that section. Judge Lee contended that a section labeled "authorization" should presumptively be understood as granting discretionary power rather than imposing mandatory duties. The majority never directly addressed this textual clue from the section heading.

Judge Lee's interpretive move demonstrates how judges can reach different conclusions even when examining the same statutory text. Where the majority saw "shall" language as the key to unlocking congressional intent, Judge Lee saw the section title as the controlling signal that those mandatory phrases should be read in a discretionary context.

## The role of 'is authorized' language in subsections (b) through (e)

Judge Lee deepened his analysis by examining the operative language in the funding provisions themselves. Subsections (b) through (e) of § 1522—which provide for the initial resettlement program, project grants and contracts, cash assistance, and medical assistance—all state that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance. Judge Lee emphasized that this language appears in the provisions that actually appropriate funds and establish programs. By contrast, the "shall" language in § 1522(a) appears in what he called a "conditions and consideration" provision that only applies if the government chooses to exercise its authorized discretion.

This structural argument carries weight in statutory interpretation. If Congress intended to mandate services, Judge Lee reasoned, why would the operative funding provisions use permissive language? The pattern suggests that Congress gave the executive branch discretion over whether to establish programs, while specifying certain conditions that apply if the government chooses to do so. The majority did not directly grapple with this structural point, instead treating the "shall" language in § 1522(a) as independent mandates.

The disagreement illustrates how courts must weigh competing textual signals. Both sides cited the statute's actual language; neither invented words that do not appear. The conflict arose from which textual cues the judges treated as more significant—the "shall" language cited by the majority or the "is authorized" language emphasized in the dissent.

## The interplay between § 1522(a) conditions and discretionary funding provisions

The heart of the interpretive dispute concerned how § 1522(a) relates to the funding provisions that follow. The majority read § 1522(a) as creating standalone obligations: the Director "shall" provide employment training, English instruction, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women, subject only to the "extent of available appropriations." The appropriations qualifier, in the majority's view, merely limited the government's obligations to what Congress had funded—not eliminated them entirely.

Judge Lee offered a competing framework. He read § 1522(a) as a conditional provision that applies only "[i]n providing assistance under this section"—that is, under the discretionary funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e). In his reading, Congress authorized the executive to establish programs, and if the executive chose to do so, it had to comply with certain conditions. The "shall" language thus created mandatory standards for any programs the government decided to run, but did not compel the government to run those programs in the first place.

This dispute mirrors a classic tension in statutory interpretation: whether to read provisions as independent commands or as interlocking parts of a unified scheme. The majority's approach isolated the "shall" language and treated it as self-executing. Judge Lee's approach read the statute structurally, treating the enabling language in subsections (b) through (e) as the operative grant of power, with § 1522(a) supplying conditions that attach only when that power is exercised.

## The practical consequences of each interpretation

The stakes of this interpretive disagreement extended beyond abstract legal theory to the lived experiences of refugees already admitted to the United States. Under the majority's reading, the government remained obligated to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had already arrived, even after the executive order suspended new admissions. The court's order directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements meant that resettlement organizations could continue providing the "usual and customary services" refugees had historically received.

Under Judge Lee's reading, by contrast, the government bore no such ongoing obligation. If § 1522 is purely discretionary, the executive branch could terminate funding and rely on whatever alternative mechanisms it deemed appropriate—even if, as the record suggested, no clear alternative was yet in place. The practical effect of Judge Lee's approach would be to stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, leaving recently admitted refugees without guaranteed federal support.

The split also signals what may happen when the court issues its merits opinion. The majority's clear indication that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" strongly suggests the final opinion will hold that § 1522 creates enforceable statutory duties. Judge Lee's dissent provides a roadmap for potential Supreme Court review, should the government seek to challenge the Ninth Circuit's interpretation. For law students, the case offers a vivid illustration of how courts resolve statutory ambiguities—and how the choice between competing interpretive frameworks can produce dramatically different outcomes for the people affected by those decisions.

# The Stay Pending Appeal Analysis and Final Order

## The Nken Four-Factor Test for Stay Pending Appeal

When a party seeks to pause a district court's order while an appeal proceeds, the party requests a "stay pending appeal." The governing standard comes from *Nken v. Holder*, in which the Supreme Court articulated a four-factor test that courts must apply. The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito* began its analysis by reciting these four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies.

A stay pending appeal is distinct from an injunction. While an injunction grants or denies substantive relief, a stay merely suspends a lower court's order during appellate review. The stay preserves the status quo—or in some cases, restores a prior status quo—so that the appellate court can deliberate without the underlying controversy becoming moot or causing irreversible harm. The *Nken* Court emphasized that the first two factors—the likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are "the most critical." This hierarchy makes sense: if an applicant cannot show both a real chance of winning and a real risk of serious harm, a court should not intervene before deciding the appeal.

In this case, the government sought a stay of the district court's preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of Executive Order 14163 and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugee services. The Ninth Circuit's task was to determine whether the government met the *Nken* standard. The court's analysis would reveal that the factors do not always point uniformly in one direction—different aspects of a single case may yield different conclusions under the same test.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Different Conclusions for Different Claims

The first *Nken* factor required the court to assess the government's likelihood of success on appeal. The panel's analysis revealed a crucial insight: when a single case involves multiple legal claims, the likelihood-of-success inquiry must be conducted claim by claim. The court reached opposite conclusions on two different aspects of the plaintiffs' challenge.

On the challenge to Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, the court found the government likely to succeed. The court's reasoning centered on statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which authorizes the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens when he "finds that their entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court emphasized that this statutory language "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power" over entry restrictions. The "sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f) is that the President make the required finding of detriment—which President Trump had done. The court concluded it could not "engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This portion of the opinion illustrates the concept of judicial deference to executive branch determinations in certain statutory contexts. When Congress uses language granting broad discretion to the President, courts will not second-guess the policy merits of the executive's decision. The panel treated the President's finding of detriment as conclusive for purposes of the stay analysis.

On the Administrative Procedure Act challenge to the defunding of refugee resettlement services, however, the court reached the opposite conclusion. The government was not likely to succeed on this claim. The court identified statutory mandates in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that require the government to provide certain "reception and placement" services to refugees after their admission. The court quoted several subsections detailing specific obligations: employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and special provisions for unaccompanied minors. These statutory provisions, the court suggested, create duties that the executive branch cannot simply ignore through executive order or funding suspension.

The court's bifurcated analysis demonstrates that the first *Nken* factor is not a binary assessment of who will win the entire appeal. Rather, a court must examine each distinct legal claim. This granular approach allows a court to grant a stay in part while denying it in part—a possibility that *Nken* itself contemplates but that the *Pacito* opinion illustrates concretely.

## Irreparable Injury, Balance of Equities, and Public Interest

After finding the government likely to succeed on the admissions question but unlikely to succeed on the services question, the court addressed the remaining *Nken* factors. The opinion's treatment of these factors is notably compressed. The court stated that the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President, favor the United States as well."

This terse analysis reflects several principles. First, when the first two factors strongly favor one party, the balance of equities and public interest often align in the same direction. Second, in cases involving immigration and national security, courts traditionally give substantial weight to the executive branch's assessment of the public interest. The court quoted *Trump v. Hawaii*'s reference to "broad discretion" conferred on the President in this domain.

The opinion does not separately analyze harm to the plaintiffs—the refugees and resettlement organizations who would be affected by the executive order. This omission likely reflects the court's conclusion that the government was unlikely to succeed on the services claim. Because the court ultimately ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees, the balance-of-equities analysis for that portion of the case necessarily favored the plaintiffs. The court's reasoning implies that the government's interest in controlling admissions outweighed plaintiffs' interests, but plaintiffs' interest in receiving statutorily mandated services outweighed the government's interest in terminating those services.

The panel's approach illustrates how the *Nken* factors interact. The four-factor test is not a simple checklist. The factors may pull in different directions, and the weight given to each depends heavily on the nature of the underlying claims.

## The Order to Reinstatute Cooperative Agreements for Admitted Refugees

Having concluded that the government was unlikely to succeed on the APA claim regarding resettlement services, the court issued a specific remedial order. The government was "directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

This order raises an important question about the scope of interim relief on appeal. A stay pending appeal typically maintains the status quo; it does not grant affirmative relief. Yet the court's order reinstated terminated cooperative agreements—an affirmative remedy. The justification for this order lies in the court's assessment that § 1522 creates mandatory obligations. If the government has a statutory duty to provide services, and if those services have historically been provided through cooperative agreements, then reinstating those agreements is necessary to preserve the status quo of statutory compliance.

The court grounded its order in factual findings about the government's uncertainty. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency," but at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government admitted it was "unaware whether any progress had been made." This uncertainty about the executive branch's ability to fulfill its statutory obligations supported the court's decision to restore the pre-existing cooperative agreements rather than simply stay the district court's order.

The court's reference to appropriations is also noteworthy. The opinion recites that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, reduced by $800 million through rescission. This funding context supported the conclusion that the statutory obligations under § 1522 were not merely aspirational—Congress had provided resources for their implementation.

## The Distinction Between Staying the Injunction on Admissions Versus Services

The panel's final order reflects a calibrated approach that distinguishes between the executive order's admissions suspension and its services termination. The district court's preliminary injunctions were "stayed in their entirety, except as noted below"—with the "except" referring to the order to reinstate cooperative agreements for admitted refugees.

This bifurcated disposition makes conceptual sense given the court's legal analysis. The admissions suspension, the court held, was likely within the President's statutory authority under § 1182(f). The district court had enjoined that suspension, so staying the injunction allowed the suspension to take effect. The services termination, by contrast, was likely ultra vires—beyond the executive branch's authority—because § 1522 mandates certain assistance. The district court had ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements to fulfill those mandates, and the Ninth Circuit required the government to continue providing services.

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent illuminates the dividing line. Judge Lee agreed with the majority that the President had authority to impose the admissions moratorium; he would have stayed the injunction in full. But Judge Lee disagreed with the majority's interpretation of § 1522. In his view, the statute's title—"Authorization for programs"—indicated that the provisions were permissive rather than mandatory. The word "authorization," he argued, signals congressional permission for the executive to act, not a congressional command that it must act. The statutory text repeatedly states that the Director "is authorized" to provide assistance, which Judge Lee read as confirming discretion.

The majority's contrary reading treated § 1522(a) as imposing conditions on how the government must act if it provides assistance, while the specific authorization language in subsections (b) through (e) still leaves room for executive discretion. Judge Lee's dissent highlights an interpretive debate that the forthcoming merits opinion will presumably address: when does "shall" impose an obligation, and when does "authorized" language negate that obligation?

The distinction drawn in the panel order has practical significance. The admissions suspension applies broadly—refugees outside the United States may not enter under USRAP. But refugees already admitted remain entitled to reception and placement services. The court's order thus creates two categories of affected persons: those seeking entry (subject to the suspension) and those already present (entitled to services). This distinction reflects the court's understanding of the statutory architecture: Congress gave the President broad power over entry, but Congress also imposed specific obligations regarding the treatment of refugees after admission.

# Judge Lee's Partial Dissent and Institutional Implications

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent provides an important counterpoint to the majority's analysis, illustrating how reasonable jurists can reach divergent conclusions when interpreting the same statutory text. Her opinion highlights fundamental questions about the nature of statutory obligations and the proper role of the judiciary in overseeing executive branch programs.

## Judge Lee's Agreement on Presidential Admissions Authority

Judge Lee begins by establishing common ground with the majority. She agrees that the President possesses "vast discretion and deference in immigration matters" and that the executive order suspending refugee admissions falls within that authority. On this issue, she would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety," meaning she would allow the President's suspension of refugee admissions to proceed unhindered by the preliminary injunction.

This agreement reflects the Supreme Court's holdings in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which the majority cited for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. Judge Lee accepts that framework without reservation. Her partial concurrence thus joins the majority in recognizing broad executive power over the admission of aliens at the border—a power the court treats as largely immune from searching judicial review.

Where Judge Lee departs from the majority is on the question of services for refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. This distinction—between those seeking admission and those already present—forms the fault line between concurrence and dissent.

## The Dissent's Interpretation of § 1522: Authorization Rather Than Mandate

Judge Lee's central argument is that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates discretionary authority, not a mandatory obligation. She reaches this conclusion through careful attention to the statutory text and structure.

First, Judge Lee points to the section's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, she explains that "authorization" means "permission to do something"—a permissive grant of power, not a command. Under traditional canons of statutory construction, titles and section headings can inform interpretation of ambiguous provisions. Judge Lee treats the title as a strong signal that Congress intended to permit, not compel, executive action.

Second, she examines the operative language in the funding provisions. The specific subsections providing for different refugee services—such as programs for initial resettlement and cash assistance—state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. Judge Lee reads this language as confirming the permissive nature of the statute. The repeated use of "authorized" throughout subsections (b) through (e) reinforces her view that Congress gave the executive branch discretion over whether to fund these programs at all.

The majority, by contrast, had pointed to § 1522(a), which uses the word "shall" and references services the Director must provide "to the extent of available appropriations." Judge Lee offers a competing reading: the "shall" language in subsection (a) is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section." In her view, this creates a conditional structure—if the government chooses to provide assistance under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b) through (e), then certain conditions and considerations apply. The "shall" does not independently require the government to act; it merely specifies what must happen if the government does act.

## Methodological Disagreement over Statutory Construction

The clash between the majority and Judge Lee illustrates a classic problem in statutory interpretation: determining when "shall" creates a mandatory duty versus when it operates within a broader permissive framework.

The majority emphasizes the word "shall" in § 1522(a) and the mandatory language in subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which require the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors and the Secretary to develop support projects. Under this reading, Congress created specific entitlements that the executive must fulfill.

Judge Lee counters that these provisions cannot be read in isolation. She argues that the overall structure of § 1522—including its title, the "authorized" language in the funding provisions, and the conditional framing of subsection (a)—demonstrates that Congress left the ultimate decision to the executive branch. She also notes that subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume responsibility only "if necessary" and authorizes rather than commands decisions about immediate care. Similarly, subsection (e)(7)(A) leaves the "metes and bounds" of support programs to executive discretion.

Judge Lee also raises a factual point: the record is unclear whether any unaccompanied refugee children are actually at issue in this case. This observation underscores her broader concern that the majority may be extending judicial mandates beyond what the statute requires and what the facts support.

## Separation of Powers Concerns Regarding Judicial Mandates on Executive Programs

Judge Lee's dissent carries significant separation of powers implications. Her core institutional argument is that courts exceed their proper role when they mandate executive action that Congress has merely authorized.

If § 1522 is permissive, then the executive branch has discretion to determine whether and how to provide resettlement services. A court that orders the reinstatement of cooperative agreements has effectively converted a grant of discretion into a judicially enforced obligation. Judge Lee warns that "our court is in no position to mandate them."

This concern connects to broader principles of executive discretion in foreign affairs and immigration. Judge Lee's approach would leave the political branches—the President and Congress—to negotiate the scope and funding of refugee programs. Her dissent reflects a view that judicial intervention in such matters risks disrupting the constitutional allocation of powers.

The majority, implicitly, takes a different view: when Congress enacts specific statutory provisions protecting refugees who have already been admitted, the judiciary has a role in ensuring those provisions are enforced. The majority's order reinstating cooperative agreements reflects this enforcement orientation.

## The Broader Significance of the Majority-Dissent Split

The split in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates that reasonable judges can disagree on fundamental questions of statutory interpretation, even when applying the same textual tools to the same provision. The majority and Judge Lee both rely on the text of § 1522, its structure, and relevant canons of construction—yet reach opposite conclusions about whether the statute requires or merely permits the provision of reception and placement services.

This disagreement has immediate practical consequences. The majority's order requires the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted to the United States, ensuring they receive services Congress has funded. Judge Lee would have stayed the injunctions in their entirety, leaving the executive free to terminate all such agreements.

The practical outcome thus represents a compromise: the President's suspension of new refugee admissions may proceed (as both the majority and Judge Lee agree), but services for already-admitted refugees must continue (as only the majority holds). The panel has split the difference between two visions of executive power and statutory obligation.

The panel's statement that "[a]n opinion on the merits will issue in due course" signals that this order is an interim measure. The reasoning underlying the split will be elaborated in a subsequent written opinion, potentially providing fuller analysis of the interpretive questions Judge Lee's dissent raises. For now, the order illustrates the tensions inherent in judicial review of executive immigration policy—and the difficult line courts must walk between enforcing statutory mandates and respecting executive discretion.

# Case Background and Procedural History

Understanding any appellate opinion requires a firm grasp of how the case arrived before the court. This chapter walks through the factual foundation and procedural timeline of *Pacito v. Trump*, introducing the executive actions that precipitated the lawsuit, the parties who brought the challenge, the legal claims they asserted, and the series of judicial rulings that preceded this Ninth Circuit order.

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Admissions Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked two statutory provisions—8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)—as the legal basis for suspending refugee admissions. Section 1182(f) is a broad delegation of authority to the President regarding immigration: "Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

Executive Order 14163 made the requisite "finding" under § 1182(f): the President determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Based on this finding, Section 3(a) of the order directed that refugee entry under USRAP "be suspended" pending further findings. The statutory framework matters because, as the Ninth Circuit would later observe, § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests the executive branch with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions.

## Executive Order 14169 and the Suspension of USRAP Funding

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the USRAP program. This action was purportedly taken pursuant to a different executive order—Executive Order No. 14169, entitled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." Although the funding suspension arose under a separate order, its practical effect was to halt not only the overseas processing of refugees but also the domestic resettlement services provided to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. The funding suspension thus expanded the scope of the conflict beyond the admission of new refugees to include the treatment of those already present in the country.

## The Plaintiffs: Refugees and Resettlement Organizations with Cooperative Agreements

The plaintiffs in this case fall into two categories. The first group consists of individual refugees: some were previously admitted to the United States, while others had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. The second group comprises three organizations that had entered into "cooperative agreements" with the State Department. These agreements authorized the organizations to provide two types of services: overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status, and resettlement services for refugees who had been admitted to the United States. The organizational plaintiffs thus stood in a direct contractual relationship with the federal government, which the government would later terminate.

## Claims Under the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, advancing two principal legal theories. First, they alleged that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980. The Refugee Act, passed as an amendment to the Immigration and Nationality Act, establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons," as the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder* (2009). The plaintiffs argued that the executive order conflicted with the statutory scheme Congress created for refugee admissions.

Second, the plaintiffs contended that the defunding of USRAP violated provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA governs the procedures by which federal agencies act and provides a mechanism for judicial review of agency actions. While the Refugee Act challenge targeted the President's authority to suspend admissions, the APA claim focused on the procedural and substantive legality of the funding termination—a distinction that would prove significant on appeal.

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions and Their Scope

The litigation moved quickly in the district court. On February 25, 2025, following an oral ruling, the district court issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of Sections 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. A preliminary injunction is an interim remedy that preserves the status quo while litigation proceeds; it requires the moving party to show a likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, and that the balance of equities and public interest favor injunctive relief.

The following day, February 26, the government responded by terminating every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States, as well as all but one of the agreements for USRAP processing support abroad. This termination came after the district court had already ruled orally against the executive order, suggesting a deliberate effort to advance the policy even while judicial review was underway.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order defining the scope of its preliminary injunction, specifying that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. Then, on March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, bringing both injunctions before the Ninth Circuit.

## Ninth Circuit Procedural History: Partial Stay, Clarifications, and Administrative Stay

Once the government appealed, it sought a "stay pending appeal"—a judicial order that suspends the effect of the lower court's injunction while the appellate court reviews the case. On March 25, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018), the panel observed that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and stayed the district court's injunction regarding refugee admissions. However, the panel denied the stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status" before January 20, 2025. This created what the court called a "limited carveout from the stay"—certain refugees with conditional approval would still benefit from the injunction during the appeal.

The Ninth Circuit issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, clarifying the scope of this carveout. The motions panel also directed full briefing and calendared the case for oral argument.

While the appeal was pending, the district court on July 14 issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee compliance. The government filed an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order on July 17. The following day, July 18, the Ninth Circuit issued an "administrative stay"—a temporary measure preserving the status quo while the court considered the emergency motion. The court then scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis for September 3, 2025. The order we are now examining was issued on September 12, 2025, following that oral argument.

# Presidential Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

## Text and Scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): Presidential Power to Suspend Entry

The central statutory provision at issue in this case is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which grants the President broad authority to suspend the entry of foreign nationals into the United States. The statute provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate."

Several features of this text are significant. First, the statute vests the President with discretion: he "may" suspend entry, not "shall." Second, the scope is remarkably broad—the President may suspend "any aliens or any class of aliens," whether they are "immigrants or nonimmigrants." Third, the President determines the duration of any suspension—"for such period as he shall deem necessary." Fourth, the statute does not enumerate specific criteria beyond the single prerequisite that the President must "find" that entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States."

In this case, President Trump invoked § 1182(f) in Executive Order No. 14163, determining that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directing that such entry "be suspended" pending further findings. The plaintiffs challenged this suspension as violating the Refugee Act of 1980, but the court concluded that § 1182(f) provides independent statutory authority for the President's action.

## Trump v. Hawaii: The 'Exudes Deference' Standard and Limited Judicial Review

The Ninth Circuit's analysis in this case is governed by the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which established the framework for evaluating presidential action under § 1182(f). In *Hawaii*, the Supreme Court considered a challenge to Presidential Proclamation 9645, which restricted entry of nationals from several predominantly Muslim countries. The Court upheld the proclamation, articulating a highly deferential standard of review.

As the Ninth Circuit observed, the Supreme Court in *Hawaii* held that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." This deferential posture flows from both the text of the statute and the constitutional separation of powers. Immigration policy, particularly decisions about which foreign nationals may enter the United States, has long been recognized as falling within the President's authority over foreign affairs and national security.

Critically, *Hawaii* established that courts must accept the President's statutory finding that entry would be detrimental. The Court held that "the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" Once the President makes such a finding, "the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement." The Court rejected arguments that judges should scrutinize the President's rationale or second-guess the policy merits of the entry restrictions.

## The President's Finding That Refugee Entry Would Be 'Detrimental to U.S. Interests'

In Executive Order No. 14163, President Trump made the requisite finding under § 1182(f): he determined that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." This finding satisfied the statutory requirement identified in *Hawaii* as "the sole prerequisite" for invoking § 1182(f).

The Ninth Circuit treated this finding as dispositive. Quoting *Hawaii*, the court stated: "The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here." The court did not require the President to elaborate on the specific reasons why refugee entry would be detrimental, nor did it demand that the government produce evidence substantiating the finding. The mere existence of the presidential finding was sufficient.

This aspect of the decision illustrates the practical operation of the *Hawaii* standard: once the President articulates the statutory finding, courts have limited room to question whether the finding is factually supported or well-reasoned. The statute commits that determination to the President's judgment, not to judicial review.

## Why Courts Cannot Engage in 'A Searching Inquiry' Into Presidential Justifications

The Ninth Circuit explicitly invoked *Hawaii*'s prohibition on "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." This principle marks a significant limitation on judicial power in this domain. Courts may not probe whether the President's determination that entry would be detrimental is persuasive, well-supported, or明智. The statute does not require the President to demonstrate detrimentality to a court's satisfaction; it requires only that the President "find" that the statutory condition is met.

This limitation serves several functions. First, it respects the separation of powers: the political branches, not the judiciary, are responsible for assessing threats to the national interest and setting immigration policy accordingly. Second, it recognizes that the President possesses institutional advantages—access to classified intelligence, diplomatic communications, and national security expertise—that courts lack. Third, it prevents federal courts from becoming entangled in policy debates that the Constitution assigns to the President and Congress.

Judge Lee's concurrence reinforces this point: "the President enjoys vast discretion and deference in immigration matters." Both the majority and the concurring judge agreed that the President has authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions, reflecting a shared understanding of the limited judicial role in reviewing § 1182(f) determinations.

## The Majority's Conclusion: Government Likely to Prevail on the Admissions Claim

Applying the four-factor test for a stay established in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the Ninth Circuit concluded that the government had made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits with respect to the plaintiffs' challenge to Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions.

The court's reasoning was straightforward: under *Hawaii*, "the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority" to suspend the "entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States." Because the President made the requisite finding and suspended entry of a defined class of aliens (refugees under USRAP), the statutory requirements were satisfied. The court therefore held that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions."

Importantly, this conclusion addressed only the President's authority under § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions. The court separately analyzed the government's termination of funding for refugee resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, reaching a different conclusion on that issue. Thus, the stay pending appeal vacated the district court's preliminary injunctions "in their entirety" as to the admissions suspension, while preserving certain obligations regarding resettlement services.

This bifurcated outcome illustrates an important lesson in statutory interpretation and administrative law: even when the President acts pursuant to clear statutory authority in one domain, executive action may be vulnerable to challenge under other statutes. The court's deference to presidential authority under § 1182(f) did not extend to the separate question whether the government had satisfied its obligations under § 1522.

# The Dispute Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Mandatory Obligations or Discretionary Authority?

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and the Framework for Domestic Resettlement Services

The Refugee Act of 1980 established the legal framework governing how the United States receives and resettles refugees. As the Supreme Court explained in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act sets forth "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." Within this statutory scheme, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs what the majority opinion terms "reception and placement" services—domestic resettlement assistance provided to refugees after their admission into the United States. The section addresses services such as employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and medical support. A central question in *Pacito v. Trump* is whether § 1522 obligates the executive branch to provide these services to refugees who have already been admitted, or whether the statute merely authorizes—but does not require—the government to do so.

The organizational plaintiffs in this case had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide these resettlement services. The government terminated these agreements following Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee admissions program. The plaintiffs argued, and the district court agreed, that these terminations violated the Administrative Procedure Act because the statute requires the government to provide such services. The government contended that it has discretion over whether to continue funding these programs.

## The Majority's Interpretation: "Shall" Language Creates Mandatory Duties for Admitted Refugees

The majority approaches the statutory interpretation question by examining the text of § 1522. The panel concludes that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA" because the statute imposes mandatory obligations. Central to this reasoning is the presence of the word "shall" in key provisions—a term courts traditionally interpret as creating mandatory duties rather than granting discretionary authority.

The majority's analysis emphasizes that once refugees have been admitted to the United States, statutory obligations attach. The court notes that for fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated over $3 billion for migration and refugee assistance, though this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million. The existence of appropriations, combined with the statutory language, suggests to the majority that Congress intended for the executive branch to actually carry out these programs, not merely have the option to do so.

The practical context reinforces this interpretation. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing new agencies to provide reception and placement services. This uncertainty about the government's ability to provide statutorily described services bolsters the majority's conclusion that the statute requires their provision—otherwise, admitted refugees would be left without the support Congress mandated.

## Key Provisions Analyzed: § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)

The majority opinion examines three specific provisions to support its reading of mandatory obligations. First, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women to participate in programs. The majority reads this provision as creating duties that attach once refugees are admitted, subject only to the limitation of available appropriations.

Second, § 1522(d)(2)(B) addresses unaccompanied refugee minors. It provides that during any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." The majority reads this as imposing an obligation on the Director to provide care and support for unaccompanied minors during transitional periods.

Third, § 1522(e)(7)(A) provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." Again, the mandatory "shall" language signals to the majority that Congress required the development and implementation of these support projects for recently resettled refugees.

The majority notes that these services may be provided through cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies, a mechanism the government has historically employed. The court therefore directs the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: The Significance of "Authorization" in the Section Title

Judge Lee offers a fundamentally different reading of the same statutory text. His analysis begins with the section's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, Judge Lee notes that "authorization" is defined as "permission to do something"—a permissive, not mandatory, term. This framing suggests Congress granted the executive branch discretion over whether to implement these programs.

For Judge Lee, the title provides important interpretive guidance. When Congress intends to create mandatory obligations, it typically uses language that clearly communicates that intent. The word "authorization" in the section heading signals that Congress was permitting the executive branch to act, not commanding it to do so. This approach reflects a broader principle of statutory interpretation: courts should read statutory language in context, and section titles can inform the meaning of substantive provisions.

This interpretive dispute illustrates how the same statutory text can yield different conclusions depending on which textual signals judges find most significant. The majority emphasizes the word "shall" in subsections (a), (d), and (e). Judge Lee emphasizes the word "authorization" in the section title. Both readings have textual support, but they lead to opposite conclusions about whether the government must provide resettlement services.

## The Structural Argument: Subsections (b) Through (e) Use "Is Authorized" Language

Judge Lee's dissent also relies on the structural organization of § 1522. He observes that the specific funding provisions—subsections (b) through (e)—use the phrase "is authorized" rather than "shall." These subsections address the program of initial resettlement, project grants and contracts, cash and medical assistance, and other specific services. The consistent use of "authorized" language throughout these operative provisions suggests to Judge Lee that Congress granted the executive branch discretion over whether to fund these programs.

This structural argument highlights an important principle of statutory interpretation: courts should read individual provisions in light of the broader statutory scheme. If Congress intended to mandate refugee services, Judge Lee reasons, the funding provisions would use mandatory language like "shall provide" or "must fund." Instead, they consistently use the permissive "is authorized," indicating that the executive branch has discretion over these programs.

Judge Lee contrasts this with statutes that genuinely create spending mandates—those typically use "shall" in the operative funding provisions. The structure of § 1522, with discretionary language in the substantive funding provisions, reinforces his reading that Congress authorized but did not require the government to provide these services.

## How § 1522(a) Functions as Conditions on Discretionary Funding Decisions

The crux of the interpretive dispute centers on how to read § 1522(a), which does use "shall" language, in relation to the rest of the section. The majority reads § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing mandatory duties. Judge Lee reads it differently: as establishing conditions that apply only if the government chooses to exercise its discretionary authority under subsections (b) through (e).

Judge Lee points to the opening phrase of § 1522(a): "In providing assistance under this section..." This qualifier, he argues, signals that the "shall" requirements in § 1522(a) apply only when the government is already providing assistance. In other words, § 1522(a) sets forth mandatory conditions and considerations that govern how discretionary programs must operate if the government chooses to implement them. For example, if the Director exercises discretion to fund employment training under § 1522(c), § 1522(a) requires that such training be designed to achieve economic self-sufficiency as quickly as possible.

Under this reading, the "shall" language in § 1522(a)(1)(A), § 1522(d)(2)(B), and § 1522(e)(7)(A) does not independently create obligations. Rather, the requirements in these provisions assume that the government is already operating programs under the discretionary authority granted in subsections (b) through (e). The "to the extent of available appropriations" phrase in § 1522(a)(1)(A) similarly suggests to Judge Lee that the provision governs how funds must be used if appropriated, not that Congress created an entitlement to funding.

Judge Lee also addresses the specific provisions the majority cites. As for § 1522(d)(2)(B), he notes that the provision requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors only when "necessary," and authorizes the Director "to make necessary decisions"—language that preserves executive discretion. Moreover, he questions whether the record even establishes that unaccompanied refugee minors are at issue in this case. As for § 1522(e)(7)(A), he reads the phrase "as needed" as similarly deferring to executive judgment about what services are required.

This interpretive dispute over § 1522 remains unresolved pending the full opinion on the merits. The panel's order granting a partial stay while directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees reflects the majority's preliminary conclusion that at least some statutory obligations exist. Judge Lee's dissent signals that the full merits opinion will need to address these statutory interpretation questions more thoroughly. The case thus presents a classic example of how courts grapple with ambiguous statutory text and competing canons of construction in the immigration context, where executive authority and statutory obligations often intersect.

# The Stay Pending Appeal Framework and Application

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test

When a party seeks to pause a district court's order while an appeal proceeds, the mechanism is a "stay pending appeal." The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito v. Trump* applied the well-established four-factor test from *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), to determine whether to grant such relief. Understanding this framework is essential because stays are frequently sought in high-stakes litigation where immediate relief is critical.

The four factors are: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The court emphasized that these factors are not mere hurdles to be cleared equally—the first two factors are "the most critical."

This prioritization makes practical sense. If an applicant cannot show a decent chance of winning, pausing the lower court's order makes little sense. Similarly, if the applicant suffers no irreparable harm from complying with the order during appeal, there is no urgency warranting intervention. The third and fourth factors—harm to other parties and public interest—operate as balancing considerations, weighing the equities once the critical threshold showing is made.

In *Pacito*, the government sought a stay of two preliminary injunctions issued by the Western District of Washington. The district court had enjoined enforcement of Executive Order 14163, which suspended refugee admissions, and had also ordered the reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements that funded resettlement services. The government's request thus required the Ninth Circuit to evaluate each component of the district court's relief through the *Nken* lens.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: The 'Most Critical' Factor

The court's analysis of the first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—reveals how deference to executive authority in immigration matters can prove outcome-determinative. For the government's challenge to the injunction on refugee admissions, the court found the government likely to succeed. But for the challenge to the injunction requiring reinstatement of resettlement services, the court reached the opposite conclusion.

On the admissions suspension, the court relied heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which addressed the so-called "travel ban" executive order. The statutory authority at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), provides that "whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens." Quoting *Hawaii*, the Ninth Circuit observed that this provision "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions."

The court concluded that President Trump had satisfied the "sole prerequisite" under § 1182(f)—making the required finding that entry of refugees would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The court expressly declined to undertake "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The President's determination, within the broad discretion conferred by statute, sufficed. Accordingly, the government had made a strong showing that it would likely succeed in defending Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions.

The analysis shifted, however, when the court turned to the termination of funding for resettlement services. The plaintiffs had challenged this defunding under the Administrative Procedure Act, arguing it violated statutory mandates. The court examined 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement programs. Several provisions use mandatory language: § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. Other subsections similarly use "shall" rather than "may."

Critically, the court noted that Congress had appropriated approximately $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for fiscal year 2025. The government had represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing" to secure a new resettlement agency but could not confirm at oral argument whether any progress had been made. This uncertainty about the government's ability to fulfill what the court read as statutory obligations meant the government was not likely to succeed on the APA challenge to the services termination.

Judge Lee's partial dissent provides a counter-reading of the same statute. He emphasized that § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement"—signals permissive rather than mandatory language. In his view, the statute authorizes the executive branch to craft programs but does not compel their operation. This interpretive dispute highlights how statutory construction can determine the likelihood-of-success inquiry.

## Irreparable Injury Analysis for Both Government and Plaintiffs

The second *Nken* factor—irreparable injury—proved pivotal in the court's bifurcated analysis. A stay pending appeal is an equitable remedy, and equity acts only to prevent harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or other legal relief after the fact.

The court found that the government would suffer irreparable injury if forced to implement refugee admissions contrary to the President's suspension order. The Supreme Court has recognized that when an executive action grounded in statutory authority is enjoined, the government suffers a sovereign injury that is inherently irreparable—the executive branch is prevented from executing its lawful policy. Moreover, the court suggested that the "domestic and foreign interests of the United States," as determined by the President's broad discretion, would be impaired by permitting continued admissions during the appeal.

For the plaintiffs, irreparable injury cut the other way with respect to resettlement services. The court emphasized that refugees already admitted to the United States have a statutory entitlement to reception and placement services. If those services were terminated during the appeal, refugees would be left without the support Congress mandated—support designed to achieve economic self-sufficiency and effective resettlement. The harm to refugees denied such services during the appeal could not be undone by a later victory on the merits. Once a refugee goes without needed services, that gap in support cannot be remedied retroactively.

Judge Lee's dissent questioned whether the organizational plaintiffs—resettlement agencies whose cooperative agreements were terminated—could demonstrate irreparable harm. He noted that their injury appears to be loss of funding, which might be compensable through monetary relief. However, the majority's focus remained on the refugees themselves as the intended beneficiaries of the statutory mandate, not merely on the organizations that provide services.

## Balance of Equities and Public Interest in Immigration Matters

The third and fourth *Nken* factors—harm to other parties and the public interest—are often analyzed together in immigration cases. The court must weigh the competing interests and determine where the balance lies during the pendency of appeal.

The court held that the balance favored the United States regarding refugee admissions. The "broad discretion" conferred on the President in immigration matters, as established in *Hawaii*, meant that the public interest in controlling admissions weighed in favor of staying the district court's injunction. The President's determination about what serves the national interest receives substantial deference, particularly at the intersection of immigration policy and foreign affairs.

However, the balance shifted regarding resettlement services. Congress, through § 1522, had already made a policy judgment that refugees admitted to the United States should receive reception and placement services. The public interest, as expressed by Congress through duly enacted statutes, supported continuing services to already-admitted refugees. When Congress appropriates funds and directs that certain services be provided, the executive branch's contrary policy preferences cannot automatically override that statutory mandate.

The court thus concluded that the public interest favored both: (1) permitting the President's suspension of refugee admissions to take effect, and (2) ensuring that refugees already present in the United States receive the services Congress has mandated. This nuanced result reflects the reality that "the public interest" is not monolithic—different statutory schemes and constitutional authorities may point in different directions within the same case.

## The Court's Application: Staying the Injunction on Admissions While Reinstating Services

Applying the *Nken* framework, the Ninth Circuit reached a bifurcated holding that illustrates how stay analysis can produce partial relief. The court vacated its prior stay orders and issued a new stay pending appeal that treated the two challenged actions differently.

First, the court stayed the district court's preliminary injunctions in their entirety regarding Executive Order 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. The government had made a strong showing of likelihood of success based on the President's broad authority under § 1182(f), the irreparable injury to sovereign interests, and the public interest in deferring to executive judgment on admission policy. The President's determination that refugee entry would be detrimental was sufficient under *Hawaii*; no searching judicial inquiry into justifications was appropriate.

Second, the court declined to stay the district court's order requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for reception and placement services. The government had not shown it would likely succeed on the APA challenge given § 1522's mandatory language. The court specifically directed the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." This order was limited to refugees already admitted, not those still awaiting admission abroad.

The court acknowledged that it would issue a full opinion on the merits "in due course." The stay order thus represents an interim disposition—a prediction about how the appeal will ultimately resolve, not a final merits determination. This is consistent with the nature of stay proceedings: the court asks what is likely to happen, not what the final outcome will be.

Judge Lee's partial dissent illustrates an alternative application of the same framework. He would have stayed the injunctions in their entirety, reading § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory. His reasoning underscores that the *Nken* factors, while providing a structured framework, depend heavily on antecedent interpretive questions—here, whether statutory language creates an enforceable duty or merely authorizes executive discretion.

The *Pacito* stay order demonstrates that intermediate appellate courts can disaggregate complex challenges and grant stays on some issues while denying them on others. The result preserves what the court views as valid exercises of executive authority while protecting what it reads as congressionally mandated rights—a balance the court struck through careful application of the *Nken* framework.

# The Court's Order and Judge Lee's Partial Dissent

## The Order's Practical Requirements: Reinstating Cooperative Agreements for Admitted Refugees

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* produces a split outcome that partially grants and partially denies the government's request for a stay. The court stays the district court's preliminary injunctions "in their entirety" with one critical exception: the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. This remedy is both narrow and specific. It does not require the government to resume admitting new refugees, nor does it restore funding for overseas processing of refugee applications. Instead, it targets a discrete category of services for a discrete category of individuals—those refugees already physically present in the United States who are entitled to certain resettlement services under federal law.

The court's directive focuses on cooperative agreements, which are the mechanism through which the federal government has historically provided these services. The State Department had terminated all such agreements in February 2025. The majority order requires the government to reinstate those agreements specifically for the purpose of providing "the usual and customary services" afforded under prior agreements. Importantly, the court links this remedy directly to its interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the majority reads as creating statutory obligations that the government must fulfill. The practical effect is that resettlement organizations that were parties to the terminated agreements must once again receive funding to provide services to already-admitted refugees.

## The Distinction Between Refugees Already Admitted Versus Those Awaiting Admission

The court's order draws a sharp line between two categories of refugees: those who have been admitted to the United States and those who remain outside the country awaiting admission. This distinction carries significant legal weight in the court's reasoning. For refugees outside the United States, the court accepts the government's position that the President has broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend their entry. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the majority concludes that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions." The statutory prerequisite for suspension—that the President find entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—has been satisfied, and the court declines to conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

For refugees already admitted, however, the analysis shifts. Once individuals have entered the United States, they fall within the ambit of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services. The majority reads this statute as imposing affirmative obligations on the government. Thus, while the President may suspend the entry of refugees who have not yet arrived, the government cannot terminate services for those already admitted. This distinction reflects a broader principle in immigration law: the government's authority over admission is distinct from its obligations toward those already lawfully present. The court's bifurcated order reflects this dual framework—staying the injunction as to admissions while partially denying the stay as to services for admitted refugees.

## Judge Lee's Agreement with the Majority on Presidential Admissions Authority

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent begins with an area of agreement: she joins the majority in concluding that the President has the authority to suspend refugee admissions. Like the majority, she reads 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as granting the President "vast discretion" in immigration matters. She agrees that the district court "incorrectly enjoined the executive branch from implementing the President's policy decision to limit admitting refugees and providing services for them"—at least with respect to admissions. On this front, she would "stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety," meaning she would permit the Executive Order's suspension of admissions to take full effect.

This agreement between the majority and Judge Lee reflects the Supreme Court's reasoning in *Trump v. Hawaii*, which held that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and provides "ample power to impose entry restrictions." Both the majority and Judge Lee accept that the statute requires only that the President make a finding that entry would be detrimental, and both conclude that such a finding has been made here. The divergence between Judge Lee and the majority arises not from disagreement about presidential admissions authority, but from a fundamental interpretive disagreement about what happens after refugees are admitted—specifically, whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates mandatory or discretionary obligations.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: § 1522 as Authorization, Not Mandate

Judge Lee's dissent centers on statutory interpretation. She argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is an authorization statute, not a mandatory spending statute. Her analysis begins with the text: the statute's title is "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Citing Black's Law Dictionary, she notes that "authorization" is permissive—it means "permission to do something." In her view, Congress has permitted the executive branch to create refugee resettlement programs but has not required it to do so.

Judge Lee supports this interpretation by examining the statute's structure. The provisions that actually provide funding for refugee services—subsections (b) through (e)—state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. The repeated use of "authorized" signals congressional intent to grant discretion, not impose obligations. This stands in contrast to the majority's reliance on § 1522(a), which uses the word "shall" and references providing assistance "to the extent of available appropriations." Judge Lee reads this language as conditional: if the government chooses to fund programs under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b) through (e), then it must comply with certain conditions, such as providing employment training to promote self-sufficiency. The "shall" language does not, in her view, independently mandate that programs exist.

Judge Lee also addresses the specific subsections the majority cites as imposing obligations. She reads § 1522(d)(2)(B), which concerns unaccompanied refugee children, as "dripp[ing] with deference to the executive branch" because it requires action only "if necessary." Similarly, she interprets § 1522(e)(7)(A), requiring the Secretary to develop projects for cash and medical support "as needed," as leaving the "metes and bounds" of such programs to executive discretion. Neither provision, in her view, creates a firm congressional mandate. Her interpretive approach is straightforward: permissive statutory language cannot be transformed into mandatory requirements through selective reading of conditional provisions.

## The Separation of Powers Implications of Judicial Intervention in Executive Programs

The disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee carries significant separation of powers implications. At stake is whether the judiciary can compel the executive branch to operate federal programs that Congress has authorized but not mandated. Judge Lee's dissent articulates a sharp principle: "As the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services, our court is in no position to mandate them." This statement encapsulates a separation of powers concern that runs through her analysis. If Congress has given the executive branch discretion to create and fund programs, judicial orders requiring those programs to operate intrude on executive discretion.

The majority's approach represents a competing vision of the judicial role. By interpreting § 1522 as creating obligations, the majority positions the court as enforcing congressional mandates against executive non-action. The majority points to Congress's appropriation of funds for refugee assistance as evidence that Congress intended for these programs to operate. The separation of powers question thus turns on statutory interpretation: does the statute give the executive a choice, or does it create an enforceable duty? The majority and Judge Lee reach opposite conclusions, and their disagreement reflects fundamentally different views about when courts may order the executive branch to act.

## The Broader Significance of the Interpretive Disagreement Between Majority and Dissent

The interpretive dispute in *Pacito* exemplifies a recurring tension in administrative law: the distinction between statutory provisions that authorize agency action and those that require it. This distinction matters because it determines whether agency inaction is subject to judicial review. If § 1522 merely authorizes programs, the executive branch's decision to terminate those programs falls within its discretionary authority. But if § 1522 mandates services, then the executive's failure to provide them violates statutory law and can be remedied by the courts.

The majority and Judge Lee employ different interpretive methodologies. The majority reads the statute holistically, looking to Congress's appropriation of funds and the purpose of the Refugee Act to conclude that Congress intended for services to be provided. Judge Lee adheres more strictly to textual signals—the word "authorization" in the title, the phrase "is authorized" in funding provisions—which she reads as clear indicators of discretionary intent. This methodological divide reflects broader debates in statutory interpretation about how much weight to give statutory text versus purpose, and about what presumptions apply when Congress appropriates funds for authorized programs.

The practical stakes are significant for refugees who have already been admitted and now depend on resettlement services. Under the majority's reading, these individuals have enforceable statutory rights to reception and placement services. Under Judge Lee's reading, they have no such claim; their access to services depends entirely on executive discretion. The interpretive question thus translates directly into real-world consequences for vulnerable populations. The split outcome in *Pacito*—suspending admissions while preserving services for those already admitted—represents the majority's attempt to reconcile presidential authority over entry with what it perceives as statutory obligations to those already present.
