Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Exuding Deference: The Ninth Circuit Extends Trump v. Hawaii's Reach to Refugee Admissions

The Ninth Circuit's order yesterday in *Pacito v. Trump* marks a significant milestone in the post-*Trump v. Hawaii* landscape of executive immigration authority. In staying a district court injunction that had blocked the Trump administration's suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, the court has signaled that the Supreme Court's travel ban decision has effectively transformed 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) into a boundless grant of presidential power—one that requires little more than a presidential finding of "detriment" to justify wholesale suspension of entire immigration categories.

The stakes here are considerable. Executive Order 14163, issued on President Trump's first day back in office, effectively shuttered the nation's refugee resettlement program, suspending admissions and terminating funding for resettlement services. The order relies on the same statutory authority that sustained the travel ban: Section 1182(f), which permits the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whose admission "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The Ninth Circuit's analysis of that provision suggests the judiciary has largely abandoned any meaningful role in reviewing such determinations.

## The Admissions Suspension: A Blank Check

On the central question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions, the panel's reasoning tracks *Hawaii* with almost mechanical precision. Quoting the Supreme Court's observation that Section 1182(f) "exudes deference," the Ninth Circuit held that the President need only "find[]" that entry of covered aliens would be detrimental—nothing more. The court explicitly declined to engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This framing is striking. The Refugee Act of 1980 was enacted precisely to regularize and structure refugee admissions, establishing a comprehensive statutory framework that includes annual consultations with Congress, numerical ceilings, and detailed procedures for processing refugee claims. Yet under the panel's reading, the President may circumvent that entire edifice simply by invoking Section 1182(f)'s broad language. The "detrimental to the interests" standard, apparently, means whatever the President says it means—and courts will not second-guess that determination.

The doctrinal implications are sobering. *Trump v. Hawaii* involved a proclamation that suspended entry from several predominantly Muslim countries, justified on national security grounds. Whatever one's view of that decision, it at least involved a facial justification tied to specific national security concerns about particular countries. Here, by contrast, the President has suspended an entire global refugee program with no country-specific findings at all. If *Hawaii* extends this far, it is difficult to imagine any limit on Section 1182(f) short of an explicit statutory prohibition.

## The Services Split: A Statutory Duty?

The panel's treatment of funding for resettlement services reveals a more interesting fault line—one that produced a partial concurrence and dissent from Judge Lee. The majority concluded that the government is unlikely to succeed on plaintiffs' APA challenges to the termination of cooperative agreements providing "reception and placement" services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

The majority's reasoning turns on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement assistance. The court emphasized that § 1522(a) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," to make employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance available to refugees. Reading these provisions together with § 1522's authorization for cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations, the majority found a statutory duty that survives the admission suspension: once refugees are admitted, Congress has mandated certain services.

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a pointed counter-reading. Noting that § 1522's title uses the word "Authorization" rather than "Mandate," Judge Lee reads the statutory language as permissive—authorizing but not requiring the executive branch to fund and operate resettlement programs. This interpretive divide raises classic questions of statutory construction: when does "shall" impose an affirmative duty versus merely setting conditions for discretionary action?

The panel split here underscores an important doctrinal tension. The majority's approach treats § 1522 as establishing mandatory obligations that constrain executive discretion once refugees are admitted. Judge Lee's reading would give the executive broader authority to defund programs even for those already lawfully present. The practical consequence, for now, is that cooperative agreements must be reinstated to serve refugees already admitted—but the legal question remains unsettled pending the full opinion.

## Downstream Effects: The Hollowing of Judicial Review

The most significant long-term effect of this decision may be its reinforcement of *Hawaii*'s deferential framework. By applying that standard to a global program suspension with no individualized or country-specific findings, the Ninth Circuit has confirmed that Section 1182(f) operates as a nearly unlimited suspension power. The President's "finding" of detriment—unreviewable in any meaningful sense—becomes a self-executing justification for sweeping immigration restrictions.

This raises profound separation-of-powers questions. The Refugee Act represented a deliberate congressional effort to cabin executive discretion in refugee admissions, establishing a structured process that involves both branches. If Section 1182(f) can override that entire scheme on the strength of a presidential finding, the statutory framework becomes essentially advisory. Future presidents may invoke this precedent to suspend any immigration category they deem undesirable—from asylum seekers to diversity visa holders to students from disfavored nations.

The panel's willingness to order reinstatement of resettlement services for admitted refugees suggests one potential limit: Congress can mandate executive action through clear statutory duties tied to appropriations. But even this modest constraint rests on fragile footing given Judge Lee's dissenting interpretation. If the full opinion embraces her reading, even those who have already been admitted and resettled could find themselves without federal support—stranded by the very system that brought them here.

The Ninth Circuit's opinion to follow will warrant close attention. For now, the message is clear: when it comes to suspending immigration, *Trump v. Hawaii* has given presidents a power that courts are largely unwilling to check.



# Shall vs. Authorized: The Battle Over Statutory Mandatory Duties in Section 1522

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a rare glimpse into an emerging fault line in administrative law: how courts should interpret statutes that blend permissive and mandatory language. While the panel's split on the refugee program is notable on its own terms, the deeper significance lies in the methodological disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee's partial dissent over how to parse statutory text when Congress uses both "shall" and "authorized" in proximity.

## The Stakes: Two Statutory Regimes, Two Questions

The case involves President Trump's Executive Order 14163, which suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program under the broad authority granted to the President in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). That provision allows the President to suspend entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The plaintiffs challenged both the suspension of admissions and the termination of funding for resettlement services.

The panel unanimously agreed that the President's suspension of refugee admissions is likely lawful, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*'s characterization of § 1182(f) as a provision that "exudes deference" to the President. The real dispute concerns the second question: whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a mandatory duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

The majority says yes; Judge Lee says no. Their disagreement reveals fundamentally different approaches to statutory construction.

## The Majority's Approach: "Shall" Means "Shall"

The majority anchors its analysis in § 1522(a)(1)(A), which provides that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall"—"to the extent of available appropriations"—make available certain services, including employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. The court emphasizes that Congress has appropriated over $2 billion for refugee assistance in FY 2025, meaning the "available appropriations" condition has been satisfied.

The panel also points to specific subsections that impose concrete obligations. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to "assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for unaccompanied refugee minors "if necessary." Section 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects" for interim support. The majority reads these provisions as imposing affirmative duties that persist even when the executive branch has suspended the broader admission program.

Critically, the majority notes that the government represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing" alternative arrangements for providing services but could not confirm at oral argument whether any progress had been made. This admission—that the government had no clear plan to satisfy the statutory requirements—appears to have influenced the panel's conclusion that the government is unlikely to succeed on the APA claim.

## Judge Lee's Counter: Title, Structure, and the Permission-to-Mandate Distinction

Judge Lee's dissent reads the same statute and reaches the opposite conclusion. His starting point is the provision's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." He invokes the dictionary definition of "authorization" as "permission to do something" rather than a command to act.

The structural argument is more substantial. Lee observes that the substantive funding provisions in § 1522(b) through (e) consistently use "authorized" language—the Director "is authorized" to provide cash assistance, medical assistance, and other services. In Lee's view, § 1522(a) merely sets forth the conditions that apply *if* the executive branch chooses to exercise that authorization. The "shall" language, he argues, is qualified by the phrase "In providing assistance under this section"—meaning the mandatory conditions attach only once a discretionary decision to provide assistance has been made.

Lee's interpretation has the virtue of taking seriously the repeated use of permissive language throughout the provision. If Congress meant to mandate funding, why would it have described these programs in terms of what the Director "is authorized" to do?

## Which Approach Better Reads the Statute?

Both readings have strengths. The majority's approach gives "shall" its ordinary meaning—courts typically presume that "shall" creates a mandatory duty, and the "to the extent of available appropriations" qualifier suggests Congress anticipated some limits but still imposed an obligation. The majority can also point to the purpose of the Refugee Act of 1980, which was meant to create a comprehensive and enduring framework for refugee resettlement—not merely an optional menu of services.

Lee's approach, however, highlights a genuine textual tension. It would be odd for Congress to frame a provision as an "authorization" and then use permissive language throughout, only to have courts read it as a mandate. And Lee is right to question whether subsections like (d)(2)(B)—which imposes responsibility for unaccompanied minors only "if necessary"—are really the stuff of judicially enforceable duties.

The dispute also raises practical concerns about judicial role. If § 1522 creates mandatory obligations, what remedy exists when the executive branch simply declines to act? The majority's order to reinstate cooperative agreements is a remarkably specific remedy—essentially compelling the government to contract with particular private organizations. Whether courts have authority to mandate such specific performance raises separation of powers questions that the panel opinion, forthcoming as it is, will need to address.

## Doctrinal Implications: The Authorization-Mandate Spectrum

The split in *Pacito* reflects a broader doctrinal uncertainty about statutes that blend authorization and mandate language. The Supreme Court has sometimes treated "authorized" language as merely clarifying that an agency has power to act, while at other times treating such language as indicative that Congress left the decision to agency discretion. See, for example, *Cunningham v. Fairhaven Nursing Home*, where the Court suggested that "shall" language in a statutory scheme that otherwise uses permissive terms may create only a "duty to act in a particular way *if* the agency chooses to act."

The Ninth Circuit's forthcoming opinion has an opportunity to provide clarity on an issue that extends well beyond immigration law. Many statutory regimes—environmental law, social welfare programs, education funding—use similar blends of "shall" and "authorized" language. How courts resolve this interpretive puzzle in *Pacito* could ripple through administrative law more broadly.

## What Comes Next

For now, the government may continue suspending refugee admissions but must reinstate cooperative agreements to provide services to refugees already admitted. The practical consequence is a bifurcated program: no new admissions, but continuing services for those already here—assuming the government can identify who falls into that category.

The panel has promised a full opinion "in due course." That opinion will need to grapple more thoroughly with Judge Lee's structural argument, explain how the mandatory language in § 1522(a) interacts with the permissive language in subsequent subsections, and clarify what remedy—if any—is appropriate for violations of whatever duty the statute imposes. Until then, the order stands as a reminder that even in areas of presumed executive dominance, statutory text still matters—and reasonable judges can read it quite differently.



# Beyond the Threshold: Distinguishing Admission from Entry in Immigration Law

The Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump* renders a split decision that hinges on a distinction too often glossed over in immigration litigation: the difference between the government's power to exclude and its obligations to those already within the national community. The panel's September 12 order stays the district court's injunction blocking the President's suspension of refugee admissions—but simultaneously requires the government to reinstate cooperative agreements providing resettlement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The split outcome illuminates an important structural principle: the President's broad exclusionary authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) has limits, and those limits are reached when statutory duties attach to individuals who have crossed the threshold of admission.

## The Exclusionary Power Reaffirmed

On the question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions, the panel's reasoning tracks closely with *Trump v. Hawaii*. Citing § 1182(f)—the provision authorizing the President to suspend entry of any class of aliens whose entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—the court emphasized that the statute "exudes deference" to the President. The panel concluded that it cannot conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications," borrowing language directly from *Hawaii*. The Executive Order's suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program thus falls comfortably within the President's statutory authority.

This portion of the order is unsurprising. The Supreme Court's decision in *Hawaii* all but foreclosed judicial scrutiny of presidential determinations under § 1182(f). The Ninth Circuit's application here demonstrates that *Hawaii*'s deferential standard is not limited to national security justifications—any finding of "detriment" suffices. The panel did not attempt to parse whether the President's rationale was persuasive or even plausible. Under *Hawaii*, the "sole prerequisite" is that the President make the requisite finding, and here, that finding was made.

## The Statutory Duty to Provide Services

But the panel drew a sharp line at the threshold of admission. For refugees already admitted to the United States, the court found that the government has statutory obligations it cannot simply abandon. The majority pointed to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement services, and concluded that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' APA challenges.

The key statutory provisions at issue include § 1522(a)(1)(A), which requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide certain services "to the extent of available appropriations"—including employment training, English language instruction, and cash assistance. The court also highlighted § 1522(d)(2)(B), addressing unaccompanied refugee minors, which states that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for such children, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), requiring the Secretary of HHS to develop support projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months.

Crucially, the panel noted that Congress has appropriated funds for these purposes—approximately $2.378 billion after rescissions for FY 2025. The "to the extent of available appropriations" language thus has real bite: Congress has spoken, and the executive cannot ignore that appropriation by simply terminating the cooperative agreements through which services are delivered.

The panel's practical concern was palpable. When pressed at oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement agencies to provide these services. The court was unwilling to leave refugees who had already been admitted in a legal limbo—admitted to the United States but cut off from the statutory support framework designed to facilitate their resettlement.

## Judge Lee's Dissent: Authorization or Mandate?

Judge Lee's partial dissent offers a competing interpretation of § 1522 that merits attention. Lee argues that the provision's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signals congressional intent to authorize, not mandate. Under this reading, the "shall" language in § 1522(a) merely imposes conditions *if* the government chooses to fund programs under subsections (b) through (e), not an independent obligation to do so.

Lee's textual argument has some force. The word "authorization" in a statute's title often signals permissive rather than mandatory intent. Moreover, the subsections Lee cites consistently state that the Director "is authorized" to provide various forms of assistance—a formulation that typically confers discretion rather than imposes duty.

The majority, however, implicitly rejected this framing. By ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements, the panel concluded that at least some of § 1522's provisions create judicially enforceable obligations. The distinction the majority draws—between the President's authority over entry and statutory duties that attach post-admission—suggests that the court views congressional mandates affecting already-admitted refugees as operating in a different constitutional and statutory register than exclusion determinations.

## Doctrinal Implications and the Road Ahead

The panel's split decision clarifies an important boundary in immigration law. The President's authority under § 1182(f) is at its zenith when regulating entry at the border. But that authority does not automatically extinguish statutory obligations that Congress has imposed with respect to individuals already admitted. The court's implicit theory appears to be that once an individual has been admitted to the United States, their relationship to the government shifts—from being subject to exclusionary power to being entitled to whatever statutory framework Congress has enacted.

This principle could have significant downstream effects. If § 1522's mandates are judicially enforceable, then other statutory obligations governing the treatment of admitted refugees and asylees may similarly bind the executive. The decision also raises questions about the administration's broader efforts to dismantle refugee resettlement infrastructure: can the executive effectively nullify statutory mandates by defunding or terminating the mechanisms through which they are carried out?

The court's concern about the government's failure to secure replacement service providers suggests that agencies cannot simply cut funding mechanisms and walk away. At a minimum, the executive must have a good-faith plan to meet its statutory obligations. The panel's order essentially holds the government to its March 2025 representation that it was "actively preparing" a request for proposals for new resettlement agencies—but demanded actual progress, not mere promises.

## Conclusion

*Pacito v. Trump* underscores that executive power in immigration has outer boundaries. The President may have sweeping authority to suspend the entry of entire classes of aliens. But once individuals have been admitted, statutory obligations take hold—and those obligations are not mere suggestions. The Ninth Circuit's order reinstating cooperative agreements for resettlement services respects the distinction between exclusion and inclusion that runs throughout immigration law. Whether the Supreme Court will ultimately endorse this distinction remains to be seen, but for now, the panel has drawn a line that the executive cannot cross without consequences.

The merits opinion, when it issues, will provide fuller reasoning. But the panel's order signals that at least some judges are willing to enforce Congress's statutory framework even in an area where executive power is otherwise at its apex. That alone makes *Pacito* worth watching.
