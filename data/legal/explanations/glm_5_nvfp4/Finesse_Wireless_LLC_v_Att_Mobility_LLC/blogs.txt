Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Expert Testimony Crumbles: The Federal Circuit Reinforces Limits on Contradictory Infringement Opinions

Patent litigation lives and dies on expert testimony. Juries rely on technical experts to translate complex engineering concepts into intelligible opinions on infringement and validity. But the Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that there are limits to what contradictory expert testimony can support—and that when an expert's opinion collapses under cross-examination without coherent explanation, no reasonable jury can sustain a verdict based on it.

## The Stakes

The case involved two patents relating to methods for mitigating signal interference in cellular radios—a technical problem that plagues wireless networks when transmit signals combine upon encountering obstacles like metal fences or corroded connectors, creating unwanted "intermodulation products" that interfere with received signals. Finesse Wireless sued AT&T and Nokia, and a jury in the Eastern District of Texas returned a $166 million verdict finding both patents infringed.

The Federal Circuit reversed, finding that Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, offered testimony so internally inconsistent that no reasonable jury could have found infringement on either patent. In doing so, the court reaffirmed a critical but sometimes overlooked principle: when the party bearing the burden of proof rests its case on self-contradictory expert testimony, judgment as a matter of law is appropriate.

## The '134 Patent: An Impossible Mapping

The first patent claimed a method for reducing interference by sampling received signals that include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," then using computational techniques to cancel out the interference. The critical limitation required the radio to sample a passband containing both types of signals simultaneously.

Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical diagram to opine on infringement. He consistently testified that the diagram's "downlink TX reference" was the "signal of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" was the "interference generating signal." He repeated this mapping in his expert report and throughout direct examination.

But on cross-examination, defense counsel exposed a fundamental problem: the modeled PIM path is generated *downstream* of the radio's analog-to-digital converter, after sampling occurs. It is physically impossible for a receiver to sample a signal that doesn't yet exist. When confronted with this reality, Dr. Wells pivoted to vague testimony about "two signals on that red path" referenced as "x1 and x2" in the diagram, without ever clearly mapping x1 or x2 to the claimed "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals."

The district court attempted to salvage the testimony, finding that "Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2." Chief Judge Moore, writing for the Federal Circuit, would have none of it: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony." The expert never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claim elements, and his continued insistence that the modeled PIM path was the interference generating signal contradicted any such reading. The court cited *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, where it similarly held that contradictory expert testimony cannot sustain a jury verdict when the expert offers no coherent explanation for the inconsistency.

## The '775 Patent: Missing Multiplications

The second patent required generating interference cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific combinations—a precise mathematical formula set forth in the claims. The question was whether the accused Nokia radios performed all seven claimed multiplications.

Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia documentation. When pressed on how three multiplications could satisfy a claim requiring seven, he offered no coherent mapping. Finesse's counsel attempted to supply the missing explanation on appeal, arguing that Nokia's documents actually showed ten multiplications and that the seven claimed multiplications were somehow a subset. But the court properly rejected this post-hoc rationalization: "Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications."

This is an important point of appellate practice. Parties cannot rescue inadequate expert testimony through appellate briefing. The jury's verdict must stand or fall on the evidence presented at trial.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision reinforces an important boundary in patent litigation. Experts are permitted to change their opinions under cross-examination; indeed, clarifying or refining testimony in response to challenging questions is perfectly appropriate. But when an expert pivots mid-testimony without acknowledging the change or explaining the apparent contradiction, courts will not allow juries to rely on that testimony.

The *Johns Hopkins* precedent the court cited has been on the books since 2008, but *Finesse Wireless* demonstrates its continuing vitality. That case involved an expert who testified that an accused device contacted a blood vessel in three dimensions, then contradicted himself by acknowledging it only made two points of contact. The Federal Circuit held that such self-contradiction cannot support a jury verdict. *Finesse Wireless* extends this principle to the patent context where an expert's technical mapping becomes incoherent under scrutiny.

## Practical Takeaways for Litigators

For trial lawyers, the decision offers several lessons. First, when preparing expert witnesses, ensure they can withstand the inevitable confrontation with contrary evidence without resorting to confusing or contradictory pivots. If the facts don't support the opinion, the opinion won't survive cross-examination—or appellate review.

Second, when opposing experts offer self-contradictory testimony, preserve the issue properly. AT&T and Nokia moved for JMOL at the close of evidence and renewed the motion after verdict, giving the Federal Circuit a clean record to review. The standard is demanding: the evidence must "point[] so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that... reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." But as *Finesse Wireless* shows, contradictory expert testimony can meet that threshold.

Third, don't rely on appellate counsel to explain what trial testimony meant. The Federal Circuit rejected Finesse's attempt to piece together a coherent infringement theory from scattered testimony separated by "ninety pages" in the record. If the expert cannot articulate a clear, consistent opinion at trial, no amount of appellate advocacy can fix it.

The $166 million verdict is vacated. The patents remain valid but unenforced against AT&T and Nokia. And the lesson endures: expert testimony that crumbles under scrutiny cannot sustain a jury verdict, no matter how favorable that verdict might have been.



## Prosecution History Estoppel or Mere Amendment? The Court Draws a Line on 'Clear and Unambiguous' Disavowal

The Federal Circuit's decision last week in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* offers a useful reminder about the high bar for establishing prosecution history disclaimer—and a case study in how expert testimony can sink an infringement case even when the claim construction battle goes the patent owner's way.

The case involved patents on methods for mitigating interference in radio systems, specifically addressing "passive intermodulation products" (PIM)—those pesky distortion signals created when transmitted signals encounter rusty connectors, loose cable connections, or other passive obstacles. Finesse Wireless sued AT&T and Nokia over the PIM cancellation feature in Nokia radios, winning a $166 million jury verdict. The Federal Circuit reversed on noninfringement across the board.

But tucked into the analysis of the '775 patent lies a noteworthy holding on prosecution history estoppel that deserves attention.

## The Amendment in Question

During prosecution of the '775 patent, Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3" that must be digitally multiplied in seven specified combinations to generate interference cancellation signals. The original claims had referenced "two or three signals."

AT&T and Nokia argued that this amendment represented a surrender of two-signal implementations. Their theory: the examiner had rejected claims over the Filipovic reference, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs, and Finesse narrowed to "three signals" to distinguish that prior art. Under prosecution history estoppel principles, they contended, Finesse should be estopped from now asserting that products using only two unique input signals fall within the claim scope.

The Federal Circuit rejected this argument.

## The Continental Circuits Standard

The court applied the standard from *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019), holding that a claim amendment rises to the level of prosecution history disclaimer only when there is a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope. Mere amendment to overcome prior art is not enough—the patent owner must clearly disavow the subject matter at issue.

Here, the court found that Finesse overcame the prior art rejection primarily by explaining that Filipovic was "limited to mitigating active IMPs" whereas the claimed method addressed passive IMPs—a distinction having nothing to do with the number of input signals. While Finesse did amend to require "three signals," the court concluded this fell short of a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of two-signal implementations. At most, the amendment required three "separately identifiable" signals, which the district court had construed not to require three *unique* signals.

This holding is significant because it rejects a more aggressive reading of prosecution history estoppel that would treat any narrowing amendment targeting prior art as automatically surrendering the amended-over subject matter. The court's approach preserves the distinction between a claim amendment and a true disclaimer—a distinction that matters enormously for the range of equivalents available to patent owners.

## The Real Problem: Seven Multiplications, Not Three

The prosecution history estoppel argument ultimately mattered little because Finesse lost on the underlying infringement analysis. The claim required seven specific third-order multiplications of three signals. Dr. Wells, Finesse's expert, identified only three distinct multiplications in the accused devices—and couldn't explain how those three mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications.

The court was characteristically blunt: "No reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications."

This is the more fundamental lesson of *Finesse Wireless*: even favorable claim constructions and successful defenses against estoppel arguments cannot rescue an infringement case when the expert testimony fails to connect the dots. The court repeatedly noted Dr. Wells' confusing and contradictory testimony, and Finesse's attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal—without having presented that evidence to the jury—went nowhere.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision reinforces that prosecution history disclaimer remains an exacting standard. Courts will not infer surrender of claim scope from claim amendments alone; they require clear evidence that the patent owner disavowed the subject matter at issue. This protects patent owners from having every narrowing amendment treated as a potential estoppel trap.

But the decision also illustrates how independent the infringement analysis remains. Win the claim construction, survive the estoppel challenge, and you still need an expert who can persuasively map the accused product onto the claim limitations. Dr. Wells' testimony was sufficiently problematic that the court found "no reasonable jury" could have found infringement—a conclusion that left the $166 million verdict without a leg to stand on.

For practitioners, the case is a reminder that prosecution history estoppel arguments require showing more than "they amended the claims to overcome prior art." And for litigators, it's yet another example of how expert testimony that can't withstand cross-examination—or that shifts ground mid-trial—can transform a winning claim construction into a complete loss.



# Claim Limitations as Technical Impossibilities: When the Accused Product Literally Cannot Infringe

Patent litigation often turns on nuanced claim construction debates or factual disputes about how an accused product operates. But sometimes a claim limitation creates a more fundamental problem: the accused product cannot possibly satisfy the claim because of how its components actually work. The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* provides a striking example of this principle in action—one that should give patent holders pause when drafting method claims involving sequential processing steps.

## The Timing Problem

The '134 patent claims methods for mitigating intermodulation product interference in radios. The claimed method begins with a sampling step: "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." The claimed receiver must sample *both* the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals" together at the RF ADC stage.

Here's where things went sideways for Finesse. Their expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, consistently testified that the "interference generating signals" corresponded to the "modeled PIM path" shown in Nokia's technical diagrams. That mapping was central to Finesse's infringement theory. But there was a fatal problem: the modeled PIM path is generated *downstream* of the RF ADC, inside the GROOT FPGA. The interference generating signals, as Dr. Wells defined them, literally did not exist at the sampling stage.

You cannot sample a signal before it is generated. This isn't a close call about claim construction or a factual dispute for a jury. It's a logical impossibility.

When confronted with this problem on cross-examination, Dr. Wells pivoted, suggesting that "two signals on that red path" corresponded to x1 and x2, signals that do exist at the sampling stage. But the Federal Circuit found his testimony fundamentally contradictory. Dr. Wells never clearly mapped x1 and x2 to the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" as required by the claims. Instead, he continued to refer to the modeled PIM path as the interference generating signal—creating a timing problem his attempted pivot never resolved.

## When Expert Testimony Collapses Under Its Own Weight

The Federal Circuit's analysis is instructive for patent litigators. The court declined to allow Finesse to salvage its infringement case by "piecing together parts of Dr. Wells' testimony over ninety pages apart in the record." The opinion cites *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, noting that when a party's expert offers self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be legally insufficient to support a jury verdict.

This principle is critical. Patent holders cannot rest their infringement case on expert testimony that collapses under scrutiny. When an expert's own testimony reveals a fundamental problem with the infringement theory—in this case, that the accused product's signal flow makes the claimed method impossible—no amount of post-hoc argument can save the verdict.

## Drafting Implications

The case illustrates why claim drafters must carefully consider the technical architecture their claims describe. The '134 patent claims a specific signal processing sequence: first sample both signal types together, then isolate them, then compute cancellation estimates. A claim requiring all signal types to be present at the sampling stage creates a structural requirement that may be impossible to satisfy depending on how the accused product actually operates.

A more flexible claim architecture—perhaps describing a system that generates cancellation signals from transmitter signals without specifying exactly where and when all signals are sampled—might have avoided this trap. But once the claim requires sampling "signals of interest and interference generating signals" together, products that generate those signals at different stages become categorically non-infringing.

## The Takeaway

*Finesse Wireless* reinforces that claim limitations are not merely semantic obstacles to be overcome through creative expert testimony. They describe technical requirements that must be actually satisfied. When a claim requires sampling signals that don't yet exist, no jury can find infringement regardless of how the expert tries to map the claim language onto the accused product.

The $166 million damages award evaporated because of a timing problem the expert's own testimony created. That should serve as a cautionary tale for both claim drafters and litigators: understand the signal flow, or watch your case flow away.
