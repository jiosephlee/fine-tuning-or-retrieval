Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the Federal Circuit's substantial evidence standard interact with the regional circuit's JMOL standard?
Question:
I'm reading Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility, and the court says it reviews JMOL denials under regional circuit law (Fifth Circuit here) but also reviews jury findings for 'substantial evidence.' The Fifth Circuit standard asks whether 'reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion,' while substantial evidence asks whether 'a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party.' These sound like opposite directions of the same question. Are they actually different standards, or are they just two ways of asking the same thing? How do courts apply both in practice?
Answer:
They are functionally the same standard, just framed from opposite perspectives.

**The Two Formulations**

The Fifth Circuit's JMOL standard asks whether "reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion" — that is, whether the evidence so overwhelmingly favors the movant that no reasonable jury could find for the opponent.

The substantial evidence standard asks whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party" — that is, whether evidence exists such that a reasonable jury *could* reach that conclusion.

These are mirror images of each other. If substantial evidence supports the verdict, then reasonable jurors *could* arrive at that conclusion, and JMOL should be denied. If substantial evidence does *not* support the verdict, then reasonable jurors *could not* arrive at that conclusion, and JMOL should be granted.

**How the Court Applies Them Together**

The Federal Circuit must honor both formulations because JMOL review combines procedural and substantive components:

1. **Procedural standard** (regional circuit): Sets the threshold for when JMOL is appropriate — the evidence must "point[] so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party" that reasonable jurors could not disagree.

2. **Evidentiary standard** (substantial evidence): Evaluates whether the jury's factual findings have adequate support — whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party."

In practice, the court applies them as a single inquiry. Here, the court found Dr. Wells's testimony so "confusing" and self-contradictory that "no reasonable jury could have found" infringement. That single conclusion satisfied *both* standards simultaneously — the evidence was insufficient (no substantial evidence), meaning reasonable jurors could not have found for Finesse (JMOL should have been granted).

**The Key Point**

The court reverses JMOL denials when "the jury's infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence" — exactly what happened here for both patents. The standards converge: if there's no substantial evidence for the verdict, the evidence points so strongly the other way that JMOL must be granted.

### When does contradictory expert testimony become insufficient to support a jury verdict?
Question:
In Finesse Wireless, the expert Dr. Wells kept testifying that the 'modeled PIM path' was the 'interference generating signals,' but on cross-examination he seemed to pivot and say something about x1 and x2 signals instead. The Federal Circuit cited Johns Hopkins v. Datascope Corp. and held this 'confusing change of course' wasn't sufficient evidence. But isn't weighing conflicting testimony exactly what juries are supposed to do? At what point does contradictory expert testimony cross the line from 'credibility issue for the jury' to 'legally insufficient evidence'?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important doctrinal line. The short answer: expert testimony becomes legally insufficient when it is **internally contradictory on a dispositive issue** and the expert never resolves the contradiction, leaving no coherent opinion for a jury to credit.

## The Distinction Between Credibility and Sufficiency

Your instinct is correct that juries ordinarily weigh conflicting evidence. But there's a critical difference between:

1. **Conflicting testimony between witnesses** (classic jury question), and
2. **A single witness's self-contradictory testimony on the point they must prove** (potential legal insufficiency)

The first scenario asks jurors to decide *whom* to believe. The second presents jurors with nothing coherent to believe at all.

## What *Johns Hopkins* Actually Held

The Federal Circuit's citation to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), is the key. There, the patentee's expert testified the accused device contacted a vessel "along the length of the vessel"—suggesting three-dimensional contact. But on cross-examination, the same expert admitted the device only contacted the vessel "at two places." The court held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the device literally met this limitation based on [expert's] opinion, *given his contradictory testimony*." 543 F.3d at 1349.

The court didn't weigh credibility. It asked: what opinion did this witness actually offer? The answer was irreconcilable statements. A jury cannot "credit" a witness by picking whichever half of a contradiction helps their case when the witness never explains which half is correct.

## How Finesse Wireless Applied This Principle

Dr. Wells faced a similar problem. The claim required the radio to sample "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." His testimony went like this:

- **Direct examination**: He mapped "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path" (repeatedly and consistently)
- **Cross-examination**: Confronted with the fact that the modeled PIM path is generated *after* sampling (making it physically impossible to be the sampled signal), he mentioned "x1 and x2" but *never mapped them to the claim elements*
- **Subsequent testimony**: He continued stating the "red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path"

As the court emphasized, Dr. Wells "never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals'" and "offered no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

The court wasn't disbelieving Dr. Wells; it was observing that his testimony never coalesced into a complete opinion on infringement. Finesse attempted to construct an infringement theory by "piecing together parts of Dr. Wells' testimony over ninety pages apart in the record"—but no reasonable jury could connect those dots without speculation.

## The Practical Test

Contradictory expert testimony crosses from "jury question" to "legally insufficient" when:

1. The contradiction concerns an element the expert must establish (here, mapping the claim terms to the accused product)
2. The expert never resolves the contradiction by clearly explaining which statement is correct and why
3. The party with the burden cannot point to coherent testimony that, if credited, would support the verdict

If Dr. Wells had said, "I was wrong earlier—let me clarify: x1 is the signal of interest and x2 is the interference generating signal, and here's why," the jury could evaluate that clarified opinion. That would be a credibility question. But he never did.

### What is the difference between 'separately identifiable' and 'unique' signals in patent claim construction?
Question:
The district court in Finesse Wireless construed 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to mean 'separately identifiable' but not 'unique' signals. The court said using two signals plus a copy of one could meet this construction. But doesn't 'separately identifiable' mean they have to be different from each other? If I have signal S1 and then make a copy of it, aren't those two things just the same signal twice? What exactly makes signals 'separately identifiable' versus 'unique'?
Answer:
The distinction matters for claim scope. Here's what the Federal Circuit explained:

## "Separately Identifiable" ≠ "Unique"

The district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean "separately identifiable" but **not** "unique." J.A. 115. Under this construction:

- **"Separately identifiable"** means you can distinguish and track each signal as a separate entity in the system
- **"Unique"** would require all three signals to be different from each other—no copies allowed

The court explicitly held that "using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals." J.A. 38.

## Why a Copy Counts as Separately Identifiable

A copy of S1 can be "separately identifiable" from S1 even though it carries the same information. Think of it this way: if you have a file on your computer and make a copy, you now have two files. They contain identical content, but you can identify each one separately—they have different locations, can be modified independently, and are processed separately.

The court credited inventor testimony that "S1, S2 and S3 represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals." J.A. 109–14. The signals need to be identifiable as distinct inputs to the multiplication process, not different in content.

## The Practical Consequence

Under this construction, if you have signals x1 and x2, you could designate:
- S1 = x1
- S2 = x2  
- S3 = x1 (a copy)

This satisfies "three separately identifiable signals" but would fail a "three unique signals" requirement.

---

**Note:** The Federal Circuit reversed the infringement verdict on other grounds—because Finesse's expert failed to show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–70. The claim construction itself wasn't overturned; the evidence simply didn't support infringement under any construction.

### What constitutes a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope during prosecution?
Question:
Finesse Wireless originally claimed 'digitally multiplying two or three signals' but amended to 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to overcome prior art. AT&T argued this surrendered claim scope over two-signal products, but the Federal Circuit held there was no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal under Continental Circuits v. Intel. I'm confused about where the line is drawn. If changing 'two or three' to just 'three' isn't a clear disavowal of two-signal products, what would be? How explicit does the prosecution history need to be?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit's holding in *Finesse Wireless* illustrates an important principle: **claim amendment alone does not automatically constitute a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal.** The prosecution history must contain explicit statements that would lead a reasonable competitor to understand that the patentee has surrendered particular subject matter.

## What Happened in *Finesse Wireless*

The original claim language recited "digitally multiplying **two or three** signals." During prosecution, Finesse amended this to "**three signals S1, S2 and S3**" in order to overcome a prior art rejection based on *Filipovic*, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs.

AT&T argued that by deleting "two" from the claim language, Finesse had clearly disavowed products using only two signals. The Federal Circuit disagreed, holding that this "does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products... with two unique input signals."

## Why the Amendment Alone Was Insufficient

The critical fact is that Finesse overcame the prior art rejection **primarily on other grounds.** As the court explained:

> "Finesse overcame that rejection in part by explaining 'Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs' and not passive IMPs that the claimed method also addresses."

The amendment to "three signals" was secondary. The patentee never argued that the invention required three *unique* input signals, nor did they explicitly disclaim two-signal embodiments. The court observed:

> "At most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

Because "three signals S1, S2 and S3" can be satisfied by two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals (making them "separately identifiable" but not "unique"), there was no clear surrender of two-signal products.

## What Would Constitute a Clear Disavowal

Under *Continental Circuits* and *Finesse Wireless*, a clear and unambiguous disavowal typically requires one of the following:

1. **Explicit disclaimer language.** Statements such as "the present invention does not include..." or "we are disclaiming embodiments that..."

2. **Arguments distinguishing the invention from prior art on a specific basis that requires surrendering claim scope.** For example, if Finesse had argued: "The prior art teaches two signals, but our invention *requires* three distinct input signals to achieve the claimed result," that would likely constitute disavowal.

3. **Surrender made necessary to overcome the rejection.** If the only way to distinguish the prior art was to require three *unique* signals, that would signal a clear disavowal.

In *Finesse Wireless*, none of these occurred. The primary distinction was about active versus passive IMPs—not the number of signals—and the claim language itself ("three signals") was not inherently limited to three unique inputs.

## The Practical Line

The takeaway is that **prosecution history estoppel for disavowal purposes focuses on what the patentee *said*, not merely what they *did*.** Amending claim language from "two or three" to "three" narrows the literal scope, but without accompanying statements clearly disavowing two-signal products, competitors cannot rely on that amendment alone to establish surrender.

A reasonable competitor reading Finesse's prosecution history would see that Finesse distinguished *Filipovic* primarily on the active/passive IMP distinction, not on the number of signals. That is not enough to raise a clear and unambiguous disavowal under *Continental Circuits*.

### Why didn't the waiver of the 'unique signals' argument preclude appellate review?
Question:
The district court found that AT&T waived its argument that 'three signals' must be unique by not raising it at the Rule 50(a) stage. But the Federal Circuit still analyzed the merits of whether the accused radios use three signals and perform seven multiplications. Did the appellate court implicitly reject the waiver finding, or is there some exception that allows arguments to be raised for the first time on appeal? I thought Rule 50(b) motions were strictly limited to arguments made in Rule 50(a) motions.
Answer:
### Why the Federal Circuit Could Review the 'Three Signals' Argument Despite Waiver

The Federal Circuit's ability to review this argument turns on the distinction between **factual sufficiency arguments** (subject to Rule 50's waiver rules) and **legal questions of claim scope** (reviewed de novo).

**The waiver finding was limited to a specific argument.**

The district court found AT&T waived the argument that "S1, S2 and S3 must be unique signals" by not raising it at the Rule 50(a) stage. This is a factual sufficiency argument—essentially asking whether the evidence supports finding three unique signals in the accused product.

**But the Federal Circuit addressed a different legal issue: prosecution history estoppel.**

On appeal, AT&T framed its argument around **claim scope surrender**: that Finesse "surrendered claim scope over two signals when it amended the claims during prosecution to recite 'three signals,' instead of 'two or three signals,' to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference." 

This is a legal argument about the **meaning and scope of the claim language**, not a factual challenge to the sufficiency of evidence. The court reviews:
- Claim construction de novo
- Whether prosecution history constitutes a "clear and unambiguous disavowal" of claim scope de novo

**The court explicitly addressed this as a claim scope issue.**

The Federal Circuit analyzed whether Finesse's amendment during prosecution rose to the level of "clear and unambiguous disavowal" under *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), concluding: "At most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

**The practical result.**

Even though the Federal Circuit reviewed this claim-scope argument de novo, it ultimately rejected AT&T's position on the merits. The court then granted JMOL on the independent ground that "no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications"—a point the district court had addressed and that was properly preserved.

In short: the waiver finding stood, but the Federal Circuit could still review the **separate legal question** of whether prosecution history narrowed the claim's scope as a matter of law.

### How specifically must an expert map accused product features to claim limitations?
Question:
For the '775 patent, the expert identified three multiplications from a Nokia document but never explained how they mapped to the seven multiplications claimed. Finesse tried to provide a mapping on appeal, but the court said this evidence was never presented to the jury. What level of mapping detail is actually required? Does an expert have to walk through each claim limitation element-by-element, or is it enough to identify generally corresponding features? At what point does the gap between evidence and claim elements become legally fatal?
Answer:
### Answer

The expert must provide a clear, specific mapping from the accused product's features to each claim limitation—and that mapping must be presented to the jury at trial. General correspondence is not enough when the claim requires specific elements or quantities.

**The Mapping Requirement**

An infringement expert's role is to bridge the gap between claim language and the accused product. The expert must do more than identify features that seem related; they must explain *how* those features satisfy each claim limitation. The opinion illustrates this principle in two contexts:

1. **'775 Patent (Quantitative Mismatch):** The claim required "seven" specific multiplications of three signals. Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications from the Nokia document and "did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications." The court held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications" without this explanatory bridge.

2. **'134 Patent (Contradictory Identification):** Dr. Wells consistently mapped "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path. When confronted with the fact that this path is generated *after* sampling (making his mapping impossible), he pivoted confusingly but "never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals.'" The court rejected Finesse's attempt to "piece together parts of Dr. Wells' testimony over ninety pages apart in the record" to construct a mapping the expert never articulated.

**The Legal Standard**

The court applied the substantial evidence standard, asking whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." The expert's failure to provide a coherent mapping meant no reasonable jury could find infringement. Critically, citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, the court held that "self-contradictory testimony" from the party's own expert cannot satisfy the burden of proof.

**When Gaps Become Fatal**

The gap becomes legally fatal when:
- The expert identifies features that don't match the claim's specific requirements (seven multiplications vs. three);
- The expert's testimony is contradictory or unclear about what maps to what; or
- The party attempts to supply the missing mapping for the first time on appeal rather than at trial.

The court explicitly rejected Finesse's appellate attempt to clarify the mapping: "Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury."

**Bottom Line**

An expert must walk through the claim limitations and clearly connect each to specific features of the accused product. The mapping must be articulated at trial, not constructed post-hoc from scattered testimony or argued for the first time on appeal.

### Why vacate damages rather than remand for a new trial?
Question:
The Federal Circuit reversed JMOL on noninfringement and simply 'vacated' the damages award. Since the infringement verdict is gone, what happens to the damages? Does 'vacated' mean the damages issue is moot, or does it leave open the possibility of a new damages trial if there's a new infringement finding on remand? And why didn't the court need to address the defendants' alternative argument for a new trial on damages?
Answer:
### Answer

The Federal Circuit vacated the damages award because once the court reversed JMOL and found noninfringement as a matter of law, there was no longer any basis for damages. This is a straightforward consequence of the court's holding on liability.

**What "vacated" means here**

When a court vacates a damages award after reversing a finding of liability, it nullifies the award entirely. The damages issue becomes moot because damages are purely derivative of liability—there can be no compensation for infringement where there is no infringement.

This differs from a remand for a new trial, which would be appropriate if the court found a defect in the damages proceeding itself (such as erroneous jury instructions or prejudicial evidence) but left the underlying liability finding intact.

**Why the court didn't reach the alternative damages arguments**

The court explicitly stated the basis for vacatur: "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, we vacate the damages award."

The court did not need to address Appellants' alternative argument for a new trial on damages because the noninfringement holding eliminated any possibility of recovery. Reaching the damages issues would have been unnecessary and would have constituted an advisory opinion. This is consistent with the principle of judicial economy—courts avoid deciding issues that are rendered moot by other holdings.

**What happens on remand**

On remand, there is no damages issue to retry. The reversal of JMOL on noninfringement means the defendants do not infringe as a matter of law. The vacatur of damages is not a determination that the damages calculation was erroneous; it simply reflects that the damages award cannot stand when the predicate finding of infringement has been eliminated. If Finesse cannot establish infringement on remand (and here, the Federal Circuit found the evidence legally insufficient to support any infringement finding), there can be no damages.
