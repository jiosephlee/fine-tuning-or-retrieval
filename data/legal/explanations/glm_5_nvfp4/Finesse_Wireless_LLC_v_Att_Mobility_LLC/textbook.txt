Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Overview of the Patents-in-Suit

This case involves two related patents owned by Finesse Wireless LLC: U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Both patents address a technical problem in radio communications—intermodulation product (IMP) interference. Understanding what the patents claim is essential because patent litigation centers on whether the accused product meets each limitation of the asserted claims. The claims define the legal boundaries of the patent owner's exclusive right.

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing IMP interference through a specific sequence of operations: oversampling a passband of received signals, isolating signals of interest and interference-generating signals using decimating filters, computing estimates of the intermodulation products, and canceling those products through phase and amplitude adjustment. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim for the '134 patent, containing six method steps that Finesse alleged the accused devices performed.

The '775 patent claims a different method for performing interference cancellation in a receiver co-located with a transmitter. Its key limitation requires generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific combinations to produce third-order cancellation signals. Claim 1 is again representative. The asserted claims of the '775 patent are claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36.

## Key Technical Concepts: IMPs and PIM

To understand the patents and the infringement arguments, you must grasp the underlying technology. Radios avoid signal interference by operating on different frequencies. However, when transmit signals encounter physical obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—they can combine to form new frequencies called "intermodulation products" or IMPs. These IMPs can interfere with the signals the radio is trying to receive.

When IMPs are caused by passive obstacles rather than active electronic components, they are specifically called "passive intermodulation products" or PIM. The opinion notes that internal PIM is caused by mechanical issues inside the radio, while external PIM is caused by sources outside the radio. This distinction matters because the '775 patent specifically addresses passive IMPs generated in passive transmitter and receiver components.

The technical details are important not for their own sake, but because the infringement analysis required mapping what the patents claim onto what the accused devices actually do. The expert testimony about how signals flow through the accused radios—and whether those signals correspond to the claimed "signals of interest," "interference generating signals," and "three signals S1, S2 and S3"—became central to the dispute.

## The Parties and Their Roles

Finesse Wireless LLC is the patent owner and served as the plaintiff in this litigation. As the patent owner, Finesse held the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence.

AT&T Mobility LLC was the original defendant. Finesse sued AT&T for allegedly infringing the asserted claims by using Nokia radios equipped with a feature called PIM cancellation (PIM-C). The theory of liability was that AT&T, as a user of the accused radios, directly infringed the method claims.

Nokia of America Corporation intervened in the lawsuit. Intervention is a procedural mechanism that allows a non-party with a significant interest in the litigation to join as a party. Nokia, as the manufacturer of the accused radios, had a clear stake in the outcome—both because its products were at issue and because AT&T might seek indemnification from Nokia for any damages award. Nokia and AT&T are collectively referred to as "Appellants" in the opinion because they jointly appealed the district court's adverse rulings.

## The Accused Instrumentation

The accused devices are Nokia radios that include a PIM cancellation feature called PIM-C. Finesse's infringement theory relied heavily on Nokia's own technical documents showing how the PIM-C feature operates. These documents depicted signal flow diagrams that Finesse's expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, attempted to map onto the claimed method steps.

Understanding what the accused radios actually do—and comparing that to what the patents claim—was the central technical dispute. The Federal Circuit's analysis focused heavily on whether Dr. Wells's testimony adequately connected the accused device's operation to each claim limitation. The court found his testimony insufficient, noting contradictions and lack of clarity that undermined the jury's infringement finding.

## Procedural History: From Trial to Appeal

This case followed a common procedural path in patent litigation. Finesse filed suit alleging infringement of multiple claims across both patents. The case proceeded to trial before a jury in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas.

The jury returned a verdict finding all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed. The jury also awarded $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the remaining life of the patents. A lump-sum damages award represents a one-time payment for a license covering the remaining patent term, rather than ongoing royalties.

After the verdict, Appellants filed several post-trial motions. They moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, arguing that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the evidence presented. They also moved for JMOL on damages and, alternatively, for a new trial on damages. JMOL is a motion asking the court to overturn a jury verdict because the evidence is legally insufficient to support it. A new trial motion asks the court to order a new trial due to legal errors that prejudiced the moving party.

The district court denied all post-trial motions. Appellants then appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

## Appellate Jurisdiction

The Federal Circuit noted its jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This statute grants the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from district court decisions in civil actions "arising under" patent law. Because Finesse's claims were for patent infringement—claims created by federal patent statute—the action arose under patent law, properly placing the appeal within the Federal Circuit's specialized jurisdiction.

Understanding jurisdiction is fundamental because courts must have authority to hear a case before they can rule on the merits. The Federal Circuit's exclusive jurisdiction over patent appeals creates uniformity in patent law interpretation nationwide, making it the de facto final arbiter of most patent law questions in the United States.

# Standards of Review for JMOL and Infringement Findings

Understanding how an appellate court reviews a lower court's decisions is essential to following patent litigation. This chapter explains the standards of review the Federal Circuit applied in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* and how these standards shaped the court's analysis of the noninfringement and damages issues.

## JMOL Denial Reviewed Under Regional Circuit Law (Fifth Circuit Standard)

The Federal Circuit's jurisdiction over patent appeals is exclusive, but that does not mean the court creates its own procedural rules for every aspect of litigation. For certain matters, including the review of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the Federal Circuit borrows the law of the regional circuit where the district court sits. In this case, because the litigation originated in the Eastern District of Texas, the Federal Circuit applied Fifth Circuit law to review the denial of JMOL.

This approach reflects a division of authority: the Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals "arising under" patent law under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), but it follows regional circuit precedent for non-patent-specific procedural issues. JMOL is one such issue. The Federal Circuit's role is not to substitute its own standard but faithfully to apply the standard the Fifth Circuit would apply.

## The De Novo Standard for Reviewing JMOL Denials

The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL de novo. This means the Federal Circuit gives no deference to the district court's decision on the motion. The appellate court examines the question anew, making its own determination about whether the motion should have been granted.

However, de novo review does not mean the court simply substitutes its own view of the evidence for the jury's. The standard is demanding: reversal is appropriate "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion."

Two principles are embedded in this standard. First, the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner—here, Finesse. The court does not weigh competing interpretations or assess witness credibility. Second, the evidence must be so one-sided that no reasonable jury could have reached the opposite result. This is a high bar, reserved for cases where the winning party's evidence is legally insufficient.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Jury Findings on Infringement

Separate from JMOL review, the Federal Circuit reviews a jury's finding of infringement for substantial evidence. This standard asks whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial."

Substantial evidence is more than a mere scintilla but less than a preponderance. It means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. The court does not ask whether it would have reached the same verdict, but only whether the jury's verdict falls within the range of reasonable conclusions from the evidence.

The relationship between JMOL review and substantial evidence review is important. When a party moves for JMOL, they are arguing that no reasonable jury could have found for the opposing party—that is, there is no substantial evidence to support the verdict. Thus, while framed as distinct standards, they operate in tandem: JMOL should be granted when substantial evidence does not support the jury's verdict.

## De Novo Review of Claim Construction with Clear Error Review of Underlying Factual Findings

Claim construction—the process of determining the meaning of patent claim terms—receives de novo review on appeal. This reflects the principle that claim construction is a question of law, though it may have factual underpinnings.

The Federal Circuit reviews any underlying factual findings supporting the district court's construction for clear error. Clear error is a deferential standard: a factual finding is clearly erroneous only when the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. This two-tiered approach means the court independently assesss the ultimate claim construction while deferring to the trial court's resolution of factual disputes, such as the credibility of expert testimony on technical meanings.

In *Finesse*, the district court had construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean "separately identifiable" but not "unique." This construction was not challenged as erroneous on appeal; rather, the dispute was whether substantial evidence showed the accused device met that construction.

## Abuse of Discretion Standard for Denial of a New Trial Under Fifth Circuit Law

The Federal Circuit also reviews a district court's denial of a motion for new trial under regional circuit law. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard, meaning the appellate court will reverse only if the district court's decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an error of law.

Abuse of discretion is a highly deferential standard. The district court has broad authority to manage its own proceedings, and the appellate court will not second-guess decisions that fall within the range of reasonable options. In *Finesse*, because the court reversed the JMOL denial on noninfringement, it vacated the damages award without reaching the new trial issue—there was no need for a new trial on damages when the underlying liability finding had been reversed.

## The Burden of Proof: Plaintiff's Obligation to Prove Infringement

Throughout patent litigation, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. This means Finesse had to show it was more likely than not that the accused devices practiced each element of the asserted claims.

The burden matters especially at the JMOL stage. When a defendant moves for JMOL of noninfringement, they are arguing that the plaintiff failed to meet its burden—that even viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, no reasonable jury could find infringement.

In *Finesse*, this principle proved decisive. Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Wells, gave testimony the court found "confusing and unclear." He mapped claim elements to signals in ways that contradicted the technical evidence—such as claiming the RF ADC samples a "modeled PIM path" when that path is generated downstream of sampling. When confronted with this contradiction, his explanations remained inconsistent. The court held that when "the party with the burden of proof . . . rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony," the evidence may be legally insufficient. The burden remained with Finesse, and the evidence it presented fell short of what substantial evidence requires.

# Noninfringement Analysis of the '134 Patent – Insufficient Expert Testimony

This chapter examines the Federal Circuit's analysis of the '134 patent claims, which provides an important lesson in how courts evaluate expert testimony in patent infringement cases. The opinion demonstrates that when an expert's testimony is internally contradictory, it cannot serve as substantial evidence to support a jury's infringement verdict, even when the jury has already rendered that verdict.

## Representative Claim 1 of the '134 Patent and its Six Method Steps

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. Intermodulation products are new frequencies formed when transmit signals combine upon encountering obstacles such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors. The patent addresses both the technical problem and its solution through a specific sequence of signal processing steps.

Claim 1 serves as the representative claim for the infringement analysis. The claim contains six method steps, labeled by the court as limitations [a] through [f]. The method begins with over-sampling a passband of received signals, then proceeds through isolation, computation, cancellation, and adjustment steps. Each limitation must be met for the accused device to infringe. The court's analysis focused primarily on the first limitation, as failure to meet this initial step meant the remaining limitations need not be addressed.

## The Critical Limitation 1[a]: Over-Sampling a Passband Containing Both "Signals of Interest" and "Interference Generating Signals"

Limitation 1[a] requires "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals, the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products inband of the signals of interest." This limitation establishes a foundational requirement: the receiver must sample a passband containing both categories of signals simultaneously.

As the patentee and plaintiff, Finesse bore the burden of proving that the accused Nokia radios sample a passband that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." This burden requires more than showing the accused device performs similar functions—it requires showing that the specific claim limitations are met. The "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" are distinct claim elements that must be identified in the accused device and shown to be sampled together at the receiver's analog-to-digital converter.

## Dr. Wells' Infringement Theory: Mapping "Signals of Interest" to the Downlink Transmit Reference and "Interference Generating Signals" to the Modeled PIM Path

Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringe Claim 1 by relying on a Nokia technical document depicting the radios' operation. Dr. Wells identified specific signals in the document and mapped them onto the claim elements. He testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) is the receiver that samples the claimed signals to create a bit stream.

Critically, Dr. Wells consistently mapped "signals of interest" to the downlink transmit reference (DL(TX) reference) and "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path. Both signals were depicted in a dashed red path in the Nokia document. Dr. Wells stated explicitly: "The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest" and "the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest." This mapping appeared throughout his testimony and in his expert report.

## The Fatal Timing Problem: The Modeled PIM Path is Generated Downstream of the RF ADC After Sampling Occurs

The appellants' cross-examination of Dr. Wells exposed a fundamental timing problem with his infringement theory. The modeled PIM path—the signal Dr. Wells identified as the "interference generating signals"—is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling occurs. This creates a logical impossibility: the RF ADC cannot sample a signal that has not yet been generated.

The sampling process occurs at the RF ADC. If the modeled PIM path is generated after the RF ADC performs its sampling function, then the RF ADC cannot possibly sample the modeled PIM path as part of the passband of received signals. As the court explained, "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated." This temporal problem meant that if the modeled PIM path is the claimed "interference generating signals," limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied because the accused radios' receiver does not sample a passband that includes interference generating signals.

## Dr. Wells' Contradictory and Confusing Testimony on Cross-Examination

When confronted with this timing problem on cross-examination, Dr. Wells' testimony became contradictory and confusing. Rather than acknowledging the problem or providing a clear alternative theory, Dr. Wells "pivoted" and suggested there were "two signals on that red path because we know that because there's the x1 and the x2."

The difficulty was that Dr. Wells never clearly testified that x1 and x2 map onto the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." He continued to testify that "the red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path" and that these were the two signals he had identified. When pressed on whether x1 was the modeled PIM path, he responded ambiguously: "I'm saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path."

The court found this testimony problematic. Dr. Wells "never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals.'" Instead, Finesse attempted to piece together this mapping by combining portions of testimony from nearly ninety pages apart in the record. Even if Dr. Wells did testify that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claimed signals, he had "repeatedly testified the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path correspond to the claimed signals, and offered no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

## The Court's Analysis: Expert Testimony Must Be Clear and Consistent, Not Self-Contradictory

The Federal Circuit rejected the district court's conclusion that "Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2" when discussing the two signals entering the RF ADC. The appellate court stated plainly: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony."

The court emphasized that Finesse's attempt to construct a coherent infringement theory required making inferences by "piecing together parts of Dr. Wells' testimony over ninety pages apart in the record." This approach falls short of what substantial evidence requires. Expert testimony must provide a clear and consistent basis for a jury to find infringement—not a puzzle that the jury or reviewing court must assemble from scattered, contradictory statements.

The court characterized Dr. Wells' testimony as a "confusing change of course" that cannot support a jury verdict. When expert testimony shifts ground in response to cross-examination without clearly explaining how the new testimony relates to the claim elements, the testimony becomes unreliable as evidence of infringement.

## Application of Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.: Insufficient Evidence When Expert Offers Contradictory Testimony on Claim Limitations

The court applied the precedent from *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), which addressed a similar problem of self-contradictory expert testimony. In that case, the expert had opined that the accused device contacted a vessel in three dimensions, but also testified that the device only contacted the vessel in two places. The Federal Circuit held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the [accused] device literally met this limitation based on [expert's] opinion, given his contradictory testimony."

The *Finesse* court applied this same principle. When the party bearing the burden of proof "rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony," the court may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that burden. The expert's inability to consistently identify which signals in the accused device correspond to the claimed "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" meant that no reasonable jury could find infringement based on his testimony.

This principle serves an important gatekeeping function. Expert testimony that contradicts itself on critical claim limitations provides no reliable basis for a jury to reach a verdict. Courts must not permit jury verdicts to stand when they rest on such shifting sands.

## Holding: No Substantial Evidence Supports the Jury's Infringement Verdict for the '134 Patent

The Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the '134 patent. The court held that the jury's infringement verdict "is not supported by substantial evidence." Dr. Wells' testimony—mapping "interference generating signals" to a signal generated after sampling occurs, then offering confusing, contradictory alternatives when confronted with this impossibility—could not sustain Finesse's burden of proof.

This holding illustrates a fundamental principle of patent litigation: expert testimony must clearly and consistently establish how each claim limitation is met in the accused device. When an expert's testimony is contradictory, unclear, or requires the court to assemble inferences from scattered portions of the record, it cannot constitute substantial evidence supporting a jury's verdict. The burden of proof remains with the patentee, and that burden cannot be satisfied through expert testimony that "pivots" rather than persuades.

# Noninfringement Analysis of the '775 Patent – The Seven Multiplications Requirement

## Representative Claim 1 of the '775 Patent and Its Method for Generating IMP Cancellation Signals

The '775 patent addresses a specific technical problem in radio communications: reducing interference caused by passive intermodulation products (PIM). While the '134 patent focused on isolating and canceling interference signals, the '775 patent claims a method for generating IMP cancellation signals through a particular mathematical approach. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim for the court's analysis.

The patent claims a method performed in a receiver that is co-located with a transmitter. The key limitation at issue in this appeal appears in limitation 1[d], which describes how the cancellation signals are generated. Specifically, the claim requires "generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering" seven specific combinations: "S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 and S1×S2×S3 and S1×S1×S3 and S2×S2×S3 and S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3." This limitation specifies that the method must perform all seven third-order multiplications using three signals. The phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" became the subject of both a claim construction dispute and a prosecution history estoppel argument on appeal.

## Claim Construction: "Three Signals S1, S2 and S3" Construed as "Separately Identifiable" but Not "Unique"

Before trial, the district court construed the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in a way that became central to the infringement analysis. The court held that the three signals must be "separately identifiable" but need not be "unique." This construction allowed for the possibility that one or more of the three signals could be copies of the same underlying signal.

The court reached this construction by examining both intrinsic and extrinsic evidence. Looking at the intrinsic evidence—the patent specification and prosecution history—the court concluded nothing limited "S1, S2 and S3" to unique input signals. The court also credited inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence, finding the inventor stated that "S1, S2 and S3" represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals. This construction was favorable to Finesse because it meant Finesse did not need to prove the accused radios used three entirely distinct signals—they could use copies or variations of fewer underlying signals.

This claim construction issue arose at JMOL in an unusual posture. The district court rejected Appellants' argument that "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique signals on procedural grounds, finding the argument had been waived because Appellants failed to raise it at the Rule 50(a) stage. The court then applied its construction, finding that using two signals plus a copy of one of those signals satisfied the "three separately identifiable signals" requirement.

## Appellants' Prosecution History Estoppel Argument: Claim Amendment from "Two or Three Signals" to "Three Signals"

On appeal, Appellants advanced a prosecution history estoppel argument based on amendments made during patent prosecution. The original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals," but Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals" instead. Appellants argued this amendment surrendered claim scope covering products that use only two signals.

The prosecution history reveals that the examiner rejected a claim reciting "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as obvious over the Filipovic prior art reference in combination with secondary references. Filipovic taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs. Finesse overcame this rejection by amending the claims to add the limitation requiring "three signals S1, S2 and S3" multiplied in seven specific combinations.

Appellants argued this amendment surrendered coverage over accused products like the Nokia radios, which use only two unique input signals (x1 and x2). Under prosecution history estoppel principles, when a patentee narrows claim scope to overcome prior art, the patentee may be estopped from asserting equivalent structures that fall within the surrendered territory.

## Court's Rejection of Surrender Argument: Amendment Did Not Constitute "Clear and Unambiguous" Disavowal Under Continental Circuits

The Federal Circuit rejected Appellants' prosecution history estoppel argument. The court emphasized that Finesse overcame the prior art rejection in part by explaining that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs" and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method addresses. This distinction—that the prior art addressed a different type of interference—provided an independent basis for patentability beyond the mere number of input signals.

The court applied the standard from *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, which requires "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope to establish surrender. The court held that amending claims to require three separately identifiable signals "does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals." The court concluded that "at most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

This holding illustrates an important principle: not every narrowing amendment during prosecution results in surrender of claim scope. The amendment must clearly and unambiguously disavow the subject matter the patentee now seeks to cover. Because Finesse distinguished the prior art on multiple grounds, and because the amendment's clearest effect was to require three separately identifiable signals rather than to exclude two-signal systems, the court found no surrender.

## The Seven Claimed Third-Order Multiplications: The Full Set Required by Limitation 1[d]

Having rejected the prosecution history estoppel argument, the court turned to the substantive question of infringement. The critical issue became whether the accused radios perform all seven multiplications specified in the claim.

Limitation 1[d] expressly lists seven specific third-order multiplications: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. These are all third-order multiplications because each involves multiplying three signals together (counting repeated signals). With three signals, there are mathematically exactly seven possible third-order combinations (3³ = 27 total combinations, but many are equivalent, and the seven listed represent the distinct combinations that generate different intermodulation products).

The claim language is explicit: the method must include "generating an n-th order ICS by... digitally multiplying and filtering" all seven of these combinations. The use of "and" between each multiplication, rather than "or," signals that all seven are required. This becomes critical when comparing what the accused radios actually do versus what the claims require.

## Evidence at Trial: Nokia Document Showing Only Three Distinct Multiplications Using Modulus and Complex Conjugate Operations

At trial, Finesse's infringement expert Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical document to show the accused radios perform the claimed multiplications. However, the document revealed a fundamental mismatch with the claim requirements.

The Nokia document showed the accused radios perform three distinct multiplications: x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|. These multiplications use mathematical operations not found in the claims: modulus operations (|x|) and complex conjugate operations (x2'). The claimed multiplications involve only straightforward multiplication of three signals (S1, S2, S3) without any modulus or complex conjugate operations.

More importantly, the document disclosed only three distinct multiplications, not seven. The court noted "there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed, as required by limitation 1[d]." The document simply did not show the seven multiplications the claim requires.

## Dr. Wells' Failure to Explain How Three Multiplications Map to the Seven Claimed Multiplications

The court found that Dr. Wells never explained how the three multiplications shown in the Nokia document correspond to the seven multiplications required by the claims. The expert testimony was silent on the critical mapping question.

Dr. Wells "did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications." This failure proved fatal to Finesse's infringement case. The burden was on Finesse to prove infringement, and the expert's testimony did not establish the necessary link between what the accused radios do and what the claims require.

The court emphasized that "Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury." This highlights a fundamental principle of appellate review: the court considers only what was presented to the jury, not new arguments or clarifications offered for the first time on appeal. The jury never heard an explanation of how three multiplications could satisfy a claim requiring seven.

## Finesse's Flawed Mapping Argument on Appeal Requiring S3 to Be Mapped onto x2, Its Complex Conjugate, and Its Modulus

On appeal, Finesse attempted to explain the mapping between the accused radios' operations and the claimed multiplications. The court found this attempted mapping fundamentally flawed.

Finesse's mapping would require mapping a single claimed signal S3 onto multiple different signals in the accused device: signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. The court noted "Finesse's mapping is flawed because it requires, for example, S3 to be mapped onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|."

This approach violates basic principles of claim mapping. A single claimed element must map consistently to a single structure or function in the accused device. Finesse cannot map S3 to x2 for one multiplication, to x2' for another, and to |x2| for a third. The claim requires three signals that are multiplied in seven specific combinations—each multiplication must use the same S1, S2, and S3. Finesse's proposed mapping was internally inconsistent and therefore could not support a finding of infringement.

At oral argument, Finesse tried a different approach, arguing the Nokia documents show the accused radios perform ten multiplications, not just three. The court rejected this as too little, too late: "Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications." Nor did they explain how the ten multiplications related to the three distinct multiplications Finesse had originally identified.

## Holding: No Substantial Evidence Supports the Jury's Finding That Accused Radios Perform the Seven Claimed Multiplications

The Federal Circuit held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications." This is a significant standard—when the court says "no reasonable jury could have found," it means the evidence was legally insufficient to support the verdict.

The court reviewed the jury's infringement finding for substantial evidence, defined as evidence from which "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." Applying this standard, the court found Finesse's evidence fell short. The expert testified to three multiplications; the Nokia document showed three multiplications using different mathematical operations than the claims require; and no evidence explained how three multiplications satisfy a claim requiring seven.

The court therefore reversed the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the '775 patent. Because the court found noninfringement, it vacated the damages award without needing to address the damages arguments. This illustrates the practical effect of a noninfringement finding—if the accused product does not infringe, any damages award must be vacated because there is no liability to support compensation.

The '775 patent analysis demonstrates how patent claims set precise technical boundaries that accused products must satisfy. Courts examine whether expert testimony actually maps claim limitations to the accused device, not merely whether an expert opines that infringement exists. When claims specify exact mathematical operations—in this case, seven specific multiplications—the patentee must prove those exact operations occur, not similar or substitute operations.

# Damages Award and Final Disposition

## The Lump-Sum Damages Award

The jury returned a substantial verdict against AT&T and Nokia, awarding Finesse $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages. This figure represented compensation for the remaining life of both the '134 and '775 patents. A lump-sum damages award differs from running royalty calculations in that it provides a single payment covering all past and future use of the patented technology, rather than periodic payments based on ongoing sales or usage. The jury had found all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed, and this finding of liability formed the foundation for the monetary award.

## Appellants' Post-Tial Motions

Following the adverse jury verdict, Appellants pursued multiple post-trial remedies. They moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on damages, challenging the legal sufficiency of the damages evidence. Recognizing that JMOL presents a high bar—requiring evidence so deficient that no reasonable jury could have reached such a conclusion—Appellants also filed an alternative motion for a new trial on damages. The new trial motion serves as a fallback position, arguing that even if the evidence technically supports some award, the verdict against the weight of the evidence or errors during trial warrant a fresh determination. The district court denied both motions, prompting this appeal.

## Effect of Noninfringement Reversal on Damages

The Federal Circuit's resolution of the noninfringement issues rendered the damages appeal largely academic. The court reviews a denial of a new trial under regional circuit law, here applying the Fifth Circuit's abuse of discretion standard. However, the court never reached the merits of either the JMOL or the new trial motion on damages. This is because damages in patent cases are consequential: they depend entirely on a finding of infringement. Without valid and infringed claims, there is no legal basis for compensation.

The court's reversal of JMOL on noninfringement meant that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the asserted claims. Once liability disappears, the damages award cannot stand. The logical consequence is vacatur—a legal term meaning the award is rendered void, as if it had never been entered. The court "vacated" rather than "reversed" the damages award because the proper remedy was to nullify the judgment rather than substitute a new determination. This distinction matters: reversal would imply the court reached a contrary conclusion on damages, while vacatur simply removes a judgment that no longer has a valid foundation.

## Final Disposition

The court's conclusion was straightforward: "we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award." The formal disposition appeared in capital letters: "REVERSED AND VACATED." This two-part disposition reflects the procedural posture. The court reversed the district court's denial of JMOL because the evidence was legally insufficient to support infringement—the opposite result from what the district court reached. The court vacated the damages award because that judgment, while once valid, could no longer persist without underlying liability.

## Costs Awarded to Defendants-Appellants

The opinion concluded with a costs determination: "Costs to Defendants-Appellants." Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 39, the prevailing party on appeal is generally entitled to recover costs. AT&T and Nokia, as the successful appellants, qualified for this recovery. Costs on appeal typically include filing fees, fees for printing or copying, and other expenses directly related to the appeal. While costs represent a modest recovery compared to the $166 million damages award that was vacated, they symbolize the court's recognition that Appellants were the successful parties in this litigation.
