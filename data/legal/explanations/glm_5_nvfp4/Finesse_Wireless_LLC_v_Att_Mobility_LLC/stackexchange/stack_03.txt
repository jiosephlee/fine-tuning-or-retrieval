Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the difference between 'separately identifiable' and 'unique' signals in patent claim construction?
Question:
The district court in Finesse Wireless construed 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to mean 'separately identifiable' but not 'unique' signals. The court said using two signals plus a copy of one could meet this construction. But doesn't 'separately identifiable' mean they have to be different from each other? If I have signal S1 and then make a copy of it, aren't those two things just the same signal twice? What exactly makes signals 'separately identifiable' versus 'unique'?
Answer:
The distinction matters for claim scope. Here's what the Federal Circuit explained:

## "Separately Identifiable" ≠ "Unique"

The district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean "separately identifiable" but **not** "unique." J.A. 115. Under this construction:

- **"Separately identifiable"** means you can distinguish and track each signal as a separate entity in the system
- **"Unique"** would require all three signals to be different from each other—no copies allowed

The court explicitly held that "using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals." J.A. 38.

## Why a Copy Counts as Separately Identifiable

A copy of S1 can be "separately identifiable" from S1 even though it carries the same information. Think of it this way: if you have a file on your computer and make a copy, you now have two files. They contain identical content, but you can identify each one separately—they have different locations, can be modified independently, and are processed separately.

The court credited inventor testimony that "S1, S2 and S3 represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals." J.A. 109–14. The signals need to be identifiable as distinct inputs to the multiplication process, not different in content.

## The Practical Consequence

Under this construction, if you have signals x1 and x2, you could designate:
- S1 = x1
- S2 = x2  
- S3 = x1 (a copy)

This satisfies "three separately identifiable signals" but would fail a "three unique signals" requirement.

---

**Note:** The Federal Circuit reversed the infringement verdict on other grounds—because Finesse's expert failed to show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–70. The claim construction itself wasn't overturned; the evidence simply didn't support infringement under any construction.
