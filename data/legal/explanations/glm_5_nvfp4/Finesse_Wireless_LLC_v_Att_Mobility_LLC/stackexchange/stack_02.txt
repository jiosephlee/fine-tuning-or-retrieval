Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### When does contradictory expert testimony become insufficient to support a jury verdict?
Question:
In Finesse Wireless, the expert Dr. Wells kept testifying that the 'modeled PIM path' was the 'interference generating signals,' but on cross-examination he seemed to pivot and say something about x1 and x2 signals instead. The Federal Circuit cited Johns Hopkins v. Datascope Corp. and held this 'confusing change of course' wasn't sufficient evidence. But isn't weighing conflicting testimony exactly what juries are supposed to do? At what point does contradictory expert testimony cross the line from 'credibility issue for the jury' to 'legally insufficient evidence'?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important doctrinal line. The short answer: expert testimony becomes legally insufficient when it is **internally contradictory on a dispositive issue** and the expert never resolves the contradiction, leaving no coherent opinion for a jury to credit.

## The Distinction Between Credibility and Sufficiency

Your instinct is correct that juries ordinarily weigh conflicting evidence. But there's a critical difference between:

1. **Conflicting testimony between witnesses** (classic jury question), and
2. **A single witness's self-contradictory testimony on the point they must prove** (potential legal insufficiency)

The first scenario asks jurors to decide *whom* to believe. The second presents jurors with nothing coherent to believe at all.

## What *Johns Hopkins* Actually Held

The Federal Circuit's citation to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), is the key. There, the patentee's expert testified the accused device contacted a vessel "along the length of the vessel"—suggesting three-dimensional contact. But on cross-examination, the same expert admitted the device only contacted the vessel "at two places." The court held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the device literally met this limitation based on [expert's] opinion, *given his contradictory testimony*." 543 F.3d at 1349.

The court didn't weigh credibility. It asked: what opinion did this witness actually offer? The answer was irreconcilable statements. A jury cannot "credit" a witness by picking whichever half of a contradiction helps their case when the witness never explains which half is correct.

## How Finesse Wireless Applied This Principle

Dr. Wells faced a similar problem. The claim required the radio to sample "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." His testimony went like this:

- **Direct examination**: He mapped "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path" (repeatedly and consistently)
- **Cross-examination**: Confronted with the fact that the modeled PIM path is generated *after* sampling (making it physically impossible to be the sampled signal), he mentioned "x1 and x2" but *never mapped them to the claim elements*
- **Subsequent testimony**: He continued stating the "red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path"

As the court emphasized, Dr. Wells "never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals'" and "offered no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

The court wasn't disbelieving Dr. Wells; it was observing that his testimony never coalesced into a complete opinion on infringement. Finesse attempted to construct an infringement theory by "piecing together parts of Dr. Wells' testimony over ninety pages apart in the record"—but no reasonable jury could connect those dots without speculation.

## The Practical Test

Contradictory expert testimony crosses from "jury question" to "legally insufficient" when:

1. The contradiction concerns an element the expert must establish (here, mapping the claim terms to the accused product)
2. The expert never resolves the contradiction by clearly explaining which statement is correct and why
3. The party with the burden cannot point to coherent testimony that, if credited, would support the verdict

If Dr. Wells had said, "I was wrong earlier—let me clarify: x1 is the signal of interest and x2 is the interference generating signal, and here's why," the jury could evaluate that clarified opinion. That would be a credibility question. But he never did.
