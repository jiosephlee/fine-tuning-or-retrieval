Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why didn't the waiver of the 'unique signals' argument preclude appellate review?
Question:
The district court found that AT&T waived its argument that 'three signals' must be unique by not raising it at the Rule 50(a) stage. But the Federal Circuit still analyzed the merits of whether the accused radios use three signals and perform seven multiplications. Did the appellate court implicitly reject the waiver finding, or is there some exception that allows arguments to be raised for the first time on appeal? I thought Rule 50(b) motions were strictly limited to arguments made in Rule 50(a) motions.
Answer:
### Why the Federal Circuit Could Review the 'Three Signals' Argument Despite Waiver

The Federal Circuit's ability to review this argument turns on the distinction between **factual sufficiency arguments** (subject to Rule 50's waiver rules) and **legal questions of claim scope** (reviewed de novo).

**The waiver finding was limited to a specific argument.**

The district court found AT&T waived the argument that "S1, S2 and S3 must be unique signals" by not raising it at the Rule 50(a) stage. This is a factual sufficiency argument—essentially asking whether the evidence supports finding three unique signals in the accused product.

**But the Federal Circuit addressed a different legal issue: prosecution history estoppel.**

On appeal, AT&T framed its argument around **claim scope surrender**: that Finesse "surrendered claim scope over two signals when it amended the claims during prosecution to recite 'three signals,' instead of 'two or three signals,' to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference." 

This is a legal argument about the **meaning and scope of the claim language**, not a factual challenge to the sufficiency of evidence. The court reviews:
- Claim construction de novo
- Whether prosecution history constitutes a "clear and unambiguous disavowal" of claim scope de novo

**The court explicitly addressed this as a claim scope issue.**

The Federal Circuit analyzed whether Finesse's amendment during prosecution rose to the level of "clear and unambiguous disavowal" under *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), concluding: "At most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

**The practical result.**

Even though the Federal Circuit reviewed this claim-scope argument de novo, it ultimately rejected AT&T's position on the merits. The court then granted JMOL on the independent ground that "no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications"—a point the district court had addressed and that was properly preserved.

In short: the waiver finding stood, but the Federal Circuit could still review the **separate legal question** of whether prosecution history narrowed the claim's scope as a matter of law.
