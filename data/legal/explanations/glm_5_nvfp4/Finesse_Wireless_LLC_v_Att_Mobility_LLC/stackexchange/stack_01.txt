Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the Federal Circuit's substantial evidence standard interact with the regional circuit's JMOL standard?
Question:
I'm reading Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility, and the court says it reviews JMOL denials under regional circuit law (Fifth Circuit here) but also reviews jury findings for 'substantial evidence.' The Fifth Circuit standard asks whether 'reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion,' while substantial evidence asks whether 'a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party.' These sound like opposite directions of the same question. Are they actually different standards, or are they just two ways of asking the same thing? How do courts apply both in practice?
Answer:
They are functionally the same standard, just framed from opposite perspectives.

**The Two Formulations**

The Fifth Circuit's JMOL standard asks whether "reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion" — that is, whether the evidence so overwhelmingly favors the movant that no reasonable jury could find for the opponent.

The substantial evidence standard asks whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party" — that is, whether evidence exists such that a reasonable jury *could* reach that conclusion.

These are mirror images of each other. If substantial evidence supports the verdict, then reasonable jurors *could* arrive at that conclusion, and JMOL should be denied. If substantial evidence does *not* support the verdict, then reasonable jurors *could not* arrive at that conclusion, and JMOL should be granted.

**How the Court Applies Them Together**

The Federal Circuit must honor both formulations because JMOL review combines procedural and substantive components:

1. **Procedural standard** (regional circuit): Sets the threshold for when JMOL is appropriate — the evidence must "point[] so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party" that reasonable jurors could not disagree.

2. **Evidentiary standard** (substantial evidence): Evaluates whether the jury's factual findings have adequate support — whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party."

In practice, the court applies them as a single inquiry. Here, the court found Dr. Wells's testimony so "confusing" and self-contradictory that "no reasonable jury could have found" infringement. That single conclusion satisfied *both* standards simultaneously — the evidence was insufficient (no substantial evidence), meaning reasonable jurors could not have found for Finesse (JMOL should have been granted).

**The Key Point**

The court reverses JMOL denials when "the jury's infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence" — exactly what happened here for both patents. The standards converge: if there's no substantial evidence for the verdict, the evidence points so strongly the other way that JMOL must be granted.
