Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What constitutes a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope during prosecution?
Question:
Finesse Wireless originally claimed 'digitally multiplying two or three signals' but amended to 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to overcome prior art. AT&T argued this surrendered claim scope over two-signal products, but the Federal Circuit held there was no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal under Continental Circuits v. Intel. I'm confused about where the line is drawn. If changing 'two or three' to just 'three' isn't a clear disavowal of two-signal products, what would be? How explicit does the prosecution history need to be?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit's holding in *Finesse Wireless* illustrates an important principle: **claim amendment alone does not automatically constitute a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal.** The prosecution history must contain explicit statements that would lead a reasonable competitor to understand that the patentee has surrendered particular subject matter.

## What Happened in *Finesse Wireless*

The original claim language recited "digitally multiplying **two or three** signals." During prosecution, Finesse amended this to "**three signals S1, S2 and S3**" in order to overcome a prior art rejection based on *Filipovic*, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs.

AT&T argued that by deleting "two" from the claim language, Finesse had clearly disavowed products using only two signals. The Federal Circuit disagreed, holding that this "does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products... with two unique input signals."

## Why the Amendment Alone Was Insufficient

The critical fact is that Finesse overcame the prior art rejection **primarily on other grounds.** As the court explained:

> "Finesse overcame that rejection in part by explaining 'Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs' and not passive IMPs that the claimed method also addresses."

The amendment to "three signals" was secondary. The patentee never argued that the invention required three *unique* input signals, nor did they explicitly disclaim two-signal embodiments. The court observed:

> "At most, Finesse's amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

Because "three signals S1, S2 and S3" can be satisfied by two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals (making them "separately identifiable" but not "unique"), there was no clear surrender of two-signal products.

## What Would Constitute a Clear Disavowal

Under *Continental Circuits* and *Finesse Wireless*, a clear and unambiguous disavowal typically requires one of the following:

1. **Explicit disclaimer language.** Statements such as "the present invention does not include..." or "we are disclaiming embodiments that..."

2. **Arguments distinguishing the invention from prior art on a specific basis that requires surrendering claim scope.** For example, if Finesse had argued: "The prior art teaches two signals, but our invention *requires* three distinct input signals to achieve the claimed result," that would likely constitute disavowal.

3. **Surrender made necessary to overcome the rejection.** If the only way to distinguish the prior art was to require three *unique* signals, that would signal a clear disavowal.

In *Finesse Wireless*, none of these occurred. The primary distinction was about active versus passive IMPs—not the number of signals—and the claim language itself ("three signals") was not inherently limited to three unique inputs.

## The Practical Line

The takeaway is that **prosecution history estoppel for disavowal purposes focuses on what the patentee *said*, not merely what they *did*.** Amending claim language from "two or three" to "three" narrows the literal scope, but without accompanying statements clearly disavowing two-signal products, competitors cannot rely on that amendment alone to establish surrender.

A reasonable competitor reading Finesse's prosecution history would see that Finesse distinguished *Filipovic* primarily on the active/passive IMP distinction, not on the number of signals. That is not enough to raise a clear and unambiguous disavowal under *Continental Circuits*.
