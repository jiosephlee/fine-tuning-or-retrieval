Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Expert Testimony Crumbles: The Federal Circuit Reinforces Limits on Contradictory Infringement Opinions

Patent litigation lives and dies on expert testimony. Juries rely on technical experts to translate complex engineering concepts into intelligible opinions on infringement and validity. But the Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that there are limits to what contradictory expert testimony can support—and that when an expert's opinion collapses under cross-examination without coherent explanation, no reasonable jury can sustain a verdict based on it.

## The Stakes

The case involved two patents relating to methods for mitigating signal interference in cellular radios—a technical problem that plagues wireless networks when transmit signals combine upon encountering obstacles like metal fences or corroded connectors, creating unwanted "intermodulation products" that interfere with received signals. Finesse Wireless sued AT&T and Nokia, and a jury in the Eastern District of Texas returned a $166 million verdict finding both patents infringed.

The Federal Circuit reversed, finding that Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, offered testimony so internally inconsistent that no reasonable jury could have found infringement on either patent. In doing so, the court reaffirmed a critical but sometimes overlooked principle: when the party bearing the burden of proof rests its case on self-contradictory expert testimony, judgment as a matter of law is appropriate.

## The '134 Patent: An Impossible Mapping

The first patent claimed a method for reducing interference by sampling received signals that include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," then using computational techniques to cancel out the interference. The critical limitation required the radio to sample a passband containing both types of signals simultaneously.

Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical diagram to opine on infringement. He consistently testified that the diagram's "downlink TX reference" was the "signal of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" was the "interference generating signal." He repeated this mapping in his expert report and throughout direct examination.

But on cross-examination, defense counsel exposed a fundamental problem: the modeled PIM path is generated *downstream* of the radio's analog-to-digital converter, after sampling occurs. It is physically impossible for a receiver to sample a signal that doesn't yet exist. When confronted with this reality, Dr. Wells pivoted to vague testimony about "two signals on that red path" referenced as "x1 and x2" in the diagram, without ever clearly mapping x1 or x2 to the claimed "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals."

The district court attempted to salvage the testimony, finding that "Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2." Chief Judge Moore, writing for the Federal Circuit, would have none of it: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony." The expert never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claim elements, and his continued insistence that the modeled PIM path was the interference generating signal contradicted any such reading. The court cited *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, where it similarly held that contradictory expert testimony cannot sustain a jury verdict when the expert offers no coherent explanation for the inconsistency.

## The '775 Patent: Missing Multiplications

The second patent required generating interference cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific combinations—a precise mathematical formula set forth in the claims. The question was whether the accused Nokia radios performed all seven claimed multiplications.

Dr. Wells identified only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia documentation. When pressed on how three multiplications could satisfy a claim requiring seven, he offered no coherent mapping. Finesse's counsel attempted to supply the missing explanation on appeal, arguing that Nokia's documents actually showed ten multiplications and that the seven claimed multiplications were somehow a subset. But the court properly rejected this post-hoc rationalization: "Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications."

This is an important point of appellate practice. Parties cannot rescue inadequate expert testimony through appellate briefing. The jury's verdict must stand or fall on the evidence presented at trial.

## Doctrinal Implications

The decision reinforces an important boundary in patent litigation. Experts are permitted to change their opinions under cross-examination; indeed, clarifying or refining testimony in response to challenging questions is perfectly appropriate. But when an expert pivots mid-testimony without acknowledging the change or explaining the apparent contradiction, courts will not allow juries to rely on that testimony.

The *Johns Hopkins* precedent the court cited has been on the books since 2008, but *Finesse Wireless* demonstrates its continuing vitality. That case involved an expert who testified that an accused device contacted a blood vessel in three dimensions, then contradicted himself by acknowledging it only made two points of contact. The Federal Circuit held that such self-contradiction cannot support a jury verdict. *Finesse Wireless* extends this principle to the patent context where an expert's technical mapping becomes incoherent under scrutiny.

## Practical Takeaways for Litigators

For trial lawyers, the decision offers several lessons. First, when preparing expert witnesses, ensure they can withstand the inevitable confrontation with contrary evidence without resorting to confusing or contradictory pivots. If the facts don't support the opinion, the opinion won't survive cross-examination—or appellate review.

Second, when opposing experts offer self-contradictory testimony, preserve the issue properly. AT&T and Nokia moved for JMOL at the close of evidence and renewed the motion after verdict, giving the Federal Circuit a clean record to review. The standard is demanding: the evidence must "point[] so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that... reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." But as *Finesse Wireless* shows, contradictory expert testimony can meet that threshold.

Third, don't rely on appellate counsel to explain what trial testimony meant. The Federal Circuit rejected Finesse's attempt to piece together a coherent infringement theory from scattered testimony separated by "ninety pages" in the record. If the expert cannot articulate a clear, consistent opinion at trial, no amount of appellate advocacy can fix it.

The $166 million verdict is vacated. The patents remain valid but unenforced against AT&T and Nokia. And the lesson endures: expert testimony that crumbles under scrutiny cannot sustain a jury verdict, no matter how favorable that verdict might have been.
