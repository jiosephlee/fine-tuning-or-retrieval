Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Standards of Review for JMOL and Infringement Findings

Understanding how an appellate court reviews a lower court's decisions is essential to following patent litigation. This chapter explains the standards of review the Federal Circuit applied in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* and how these standards shaped the court's analysis of the noninfringement and damages issues.

## JMOL Denial Reviewed Under Regional Circuit Law (Fifth Circuit Standard)

The Federal Circuit's jurisdiction over patent appeals is exclusive, but that does not mean the court creates its own procedural rules for every aspect of litigation. For certain matters, including the review of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the Federal Circuit borrows the law of the regional circuit where the district court sits. In this case, because the litigation originated in the Eastern District of Texas, the Federal Circuit applied Fifth Circuit law to review the denial of JMOL.

This approach reflects a division of authority: the Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals "arising under" patent law under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), but it follows regional circuit precedent for non-patent-specific procedural issues. JMOL is one such issue. The Federal Circuit's role is not to substitute its own standard but faithfully to apply the standard the Fifth Circuit would apply.

## The De Novo Standard for Reviewing JMOL Denials

The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL de novo. This means the Federal Circuit gives no deference to the district court's decision on the motion. The appellate court examines the question anew, making its own determination about whether the motion should have been granted.

However, de novo review does not mean the court simply substitutes its own view of the evidence for the jury's. The standard is demanding: reversal is appropriate "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion."

Two principles are embedded in this standard. First, the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner—here, Finesse. The court does not weigh competing interpretations or assess witness credibility. Second, the evidence must be so one-sided that no reasonable jury could have reached the opposite result. This is a high bar, reserved for cases where the winning party's evidence is legally insufficient.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Jury Findings on Infringement

Separate from JMOL review, the Federal Circuit reviews a jury's finding of infringement for substantial evidence. This standard asks whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial."

Substantial evidence is more than a mere scintilla but less than a preponderance. It means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. The court does not ask whether it would have reached the same verdict, but only whether the jury's verdict falls within the range of reasonable conclusions from the evidence.

The relationship between JMOL review and substantial evidence review is important. When a party moves for JMOL, they are arguing that no reasonable jury could have found for the opposing party—that is, there is no substantial evidence to support the verdict. Thus, while framed as distinct standards, they operate in tandem: JMOL should be granted when substantial evidence does not support the jury's verdict.

## De Novo Review of Claim Construction with Clear Error Review of Underlying Factual Findings

Claim construction—the process of determining the meaning of patent claim terms—receives de novo review on appeal. This reflects the principle that claim construction is a question of law, though it may have factual underpinnings.

The Federal Circuit reviews any underlying factual findings supporting the district court's construction for clear error. Clear error is a deferential standard: a factual finding is clearly erroneous only when the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. This two-tiered approach means the court independently assesss the ultimate claim construction while deferring to the trial court's resolution of factual disputes, such as the credibility of expert testimony on technical meanings.

In *Finesse*, the district court had construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean "separately identifiable" but not "unique." This construction was not challenged as erroneous on appeal; rather, the dispute was whether substantial evidence showed the accused device met that construction.

## Abuse of Discretion Standard for Denial of a New Trial Under Fifth Circuit Law

The Federal Circuit also reviews a district court's denial of a motion for new trial under regional circuit law. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard, meaning the appellate court will reverse only if the district court's decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an error of law.

Abuse of discretion is a highly deferential standard. The district court has broad authority to manage its own proceedings, and the appellate court will not second-guess decisions that fall within the range of reasonable options. In *Finesse*, because the court reversed the JMOL denial on noninfringement, it vacated the damages award without reaching the new trial issue—there was no need for a new trial on damages when the underlying liability finding had been reversed.

## The Burden of Proof: Plaintiff's Obligation to Prove Infringement

Throughout patent litigation, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. This means Finesse had to show it was more likely than not that the accused devices practiced each element of the asserted claims.

The burden matters especially at the JMOL stage. When a defendant moves for JMOL of noninfringement, they are arguing that the plaintiff failed to meet its burden—that even viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, no reasonable jury could find infringement.

In *Finesse*, this principle proved decisive. Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Wells, gave testimony the court found "confusing and unclear." He mapped claim elements to signals in ways that contradicted the technical evidence—such as claiming the RF ADC samples a "modeled PIM path" when that path is generated downstream of sampling. When confronted with this contradiction, his explanations remained inconsistent. The court held that when "the party with the burden of proof . . . rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony," the evidence may be legally insufficient. The burden remained with Finesse, and the evidence it presented fell short of what substantial evidence requires.
