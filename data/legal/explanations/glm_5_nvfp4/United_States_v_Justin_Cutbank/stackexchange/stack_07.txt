Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Does the presumption of reasonableness for within-guidelines sentences conflict with § 3553(a)'s requirement that sentences be 'not greater than necessary'?
Question:
Cutbank argued that the four-level enhancement resulted in a sentence that 'conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)' that a sentence be 'sufficient, but not greater than necessary.' The court applied a presumption of reasonableness to the within-guidelines sentence and affirmed. But if the guidelines themselves can produce sentences that are 'greater than necessary,' how does presuming those sentences are reasonable square with the statutory mandate? Does the presumption effectively shift the burden to the defendant to prove the sentence is excessive, and is that consistent with the statutory text? I'm confused about the relationship between appellate presumption and the district court's obligation to impose a sentence that is 'not greater than necessary.'
Answer:
**Answer**

The presumption of reasonableness and the statutory mandate that sentences be "not greater than necessary" operate at different levels of the sentencing process. The short answer is that the presumption is an **appellate standard of review**, not a substitute for the district court's independent obligation under § 3553(a).

Here's how the two fit together:

### 1. The District Court's Obligation Remains Intact

The presumption of reasonableness does not relieve the district court of its duty under § 3553(a). As the opinion explains, quoting *Gall v. United States*, "a district court should begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range" and then consider whether the § 3553(a) factors "warrant a departure or variance from the advisory guidelines range sentence."

The district court here reviewed the PSR, sentencing memoranda, and the § 3553(a) factors. The appellate court presumed "that the district court considered and rejected" Cutbank's arguments for a lower sentence. The statutory analysis happens at sentencing; the presumption operates only on appeal.

### 2. The Presumption Is a Rebuttable Appellate Standard

The presumption of reasonableness means that on appeal, a within-guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable, and the **burden shifts to the defendant** to demonstrate substantive unreasonableness. This is what Cutbank encountered: he argued that the enhancement-produced sentence was "greater than necessary," but the court applied the presumption and found he had not rebutted it.

Critically, the presumption does not mean the guidelines are inherently "not greater than necessary"—it means the appellant must show why the particular within-range sentence is unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors.

### 3. The Statutory Text Is Preserved, Not Contradicted

The statute requires the district court to impose a sentence "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The presumption of reasonableness does not alter that command. Rather, it reflects appellate deference to the district court's exercise of its sentencing discretion. As the opinion notes, "The district court did not abuse its substantial sentencing discretion and impose a substantively unreasonable sentence."

In other words, § 3553(a) constrains the sentencing court; the presumption constrains the reviewing court. A defendant remains free to argue that a within-guidelines sentence is "greater than necessary"—and if the district court agrees, it can vary downward. On appeal, the defendant must overcome the presumption that the within-range sentence is reasonable.

### Summary

The presumption of reasonableness is consistent with § 3553(a) because it governs appellate review, not the sentencing court's initial determination. The district court still must calculate the Guidelines range and weigh the § 3553(a) factors. A within-guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal, but that presumption can be rebutted by showing the sentence is substantively unreasonable—i.e., greater than necessary to achieve the purposes of sentencing.
