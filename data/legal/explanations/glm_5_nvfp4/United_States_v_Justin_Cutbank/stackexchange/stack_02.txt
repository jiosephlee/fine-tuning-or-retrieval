Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Does the Confrontation Clause require a limiting instruction when officers testify about a victim's out-of-court statements before the victim testifies?
Question:
Cutbank requested a limiting instruction to prevent the jury from considering D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence until she testified. The court denied this, saying the statements were admitted for context and that D.F. eventually testified, satisfying the Confrontation Clause. But I'm confused about the timing issue—if officers testify about what D.F. told them before D.F. takes the stand, hasn't the jury already heard hearsay statements that could be used substantively? Does the Confrontation Clause only care about whether the declarant testifies at some point, or does the sequence matter? And when should a limiting instruction be required?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is no—the Confrontation Clause does not require a limiting instruction based on the *timing* of when the declarant testifies. What matters is that the declarant appears and testifies at some point during trial.

**The Confrontation Clause Standard**

The opinion directly addresses your question about sequence. The court held that "the Confrontation Clause is satisfied 'when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person,'" citing *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). The Clause does not require that the declarant testify *before* the out-of-court statements are introduced. It only requires that the declarant be available for cross-examination at trial. Here, D.F. testified, so Cutbank had the opportunity to confront her about the statements officers recounted.

**Limiting Instructions Are Discretionary**

On the limiting instruction question, the court applied an abuse-of-discretion standard and quoted *United States v. Bennett*: "The court 'need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.'" 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014). So even if a limiting instruction were appropriate, the district court had discretion to give it later rather than immediately.

**Context vs. Substantive Evidence**

The court also noted that the statements were admitted for a non-hearsay purpose—"to establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time"—not as substantive evidence of Cutbank's guilt. This is a common hearsay exception: out-of-court statements offered not for their truth but to explain why law enforcement took certain actions. Since D.F. later testified and was subject to cross-examination, the Confrontation Clause was satisfied regardless of the order of proof.

**Summary**

The timing you're concerned about doesn't violate the Confrontation Clause. The Clause guarantees confrontation, not a particular sequence of testimony. As long as D.F. testified and was available for cross-examination, the requirement was met.
