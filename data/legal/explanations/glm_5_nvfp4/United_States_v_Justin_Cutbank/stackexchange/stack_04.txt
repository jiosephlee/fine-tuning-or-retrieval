Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What foundation is required to admit a police report for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement when the witness didn't author the report?
Question:
The court sustained the government's objection to defense counsel reading portions of a police report to impeach C.F., noting the report was not authored by C.F. and lacked proper foundation. But I thought the rule against extrinsic evidence for collateral matters (Rule 608(b)) was the main limit on impeachment by prior inconsistent statement. If C.F. gave a statement to police that was reduced to writing by an officer, can the defense use that report at all? Does C.F. need to authenticate that the report accurately reflects what she said, or does the officer need to testify? What's the proper way to get a prior inconsistent statement from a police interview before the jury?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

The police report requires authentication by someone with knowledge—either the witness (C.F.) confirming the report accurately reflects what she said, or the officer who authored the report testifying to its accuracy. Without that foundation, the report is inadmissible as extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement.

**Explanation**

You're correct that Rule 608(b) limits extrinsic evidence for impeachment on *collateral matters*, but a prior inconsistent statement under Rule 613 is not a collateral matter—extrinsic evidence *is* permitted. However, as the Eighth Circuit explained, the evidence "must still be authenticated."

The opinion reveals the critical distinction between two impeachment techniques the defense attempted:

1. **Cross-examination about prior statements**: The court *allowed* defense counsel to ask C.F. what she said to police. This required no foundation—you may cross-examine a witness about prior statements without introducing any document.

2. **Reading the police report into the record**: This constitutes extrinsic evidence. The court sustained the objection because the report "was not authored by C.F. and lacked proper foundation."

The problem was authentication. A police report documenting C.F.'s statement, written by an officer, is hearsay when offered to prove what C.F. said. To admit it for impeachment, the proponent must authenticate it—showing it accurately reflects the witness's statement. As the court quoted from *United States v. Cumbie*: "Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated."

**Practical Approach**

To get a prior inconsistent statement from a police interview before the jury via the report itself, you would need either:

- **The witness's authentication**: Ask C.F. on cross whether she gave a statement, whether she reviewed the report, and whether it accurately reflects what she said. If she admits it, you may read it. If she denies making the statement or denies the report's accuracy, you cannot use it through her.

- **The officer's testimony**: Call the officer who took the statement to authenticate the report and testify to what C.F. said. This provides the necessary foundation for the extrinsic evidence.

The defense here apparently did neither, so the court properly excluded the report.
