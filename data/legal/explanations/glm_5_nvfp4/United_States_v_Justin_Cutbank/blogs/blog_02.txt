Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# No Charge Required: Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and Sentencing Based on Uncharged Felony Conduct

The federal sentencing guidelines have long permitted judges to consider conduct that was never charged, let alone proven to a jury. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank* offers a stark illustration of this principle in action—and a reminder of how significantly uncharged conduct can drive a Guidelines sentence.

## The Enhancement That Didn't Require a Charge

Justin Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The Armed Career Criminal Act mandated a minimum fifteen-year sentence given his prior violent felonies. But the sentencing calculation that produced his 292-month term included something more: a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm "in connection with another felony offense."

The "other felony" was never charged. According to trial testimony, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint for hours, threatened her life, struck her with the firearm, and refused to leave a stranger's garage while claiming to be armed. These facts, recited in the Presentence Investigation Report, formed the basis for the enhancement. No kidnapping charge. No assault charge. No jury verdict on whether those offenses occurred.

Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 explicitly permits this approach. The enhancement applies, the Note states, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." The Eighth Circuit had little difficulty affirming the district court's adoption of the PSR's factual statements, which Cutbank did not contest at sentencing.

## The Broader Framework: Uncharged Conduct and the Guidelines

Cutbank's case is unremarkable in the sense that it follows well-established doctrine. Federal sentencing has long operated on a "real offense" model, where judges consider the defendant's actual conduct, not merely the elements of the crime of conviction. Watts, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), held that acquitted conduct can even support a sentencing enhancement, so long as the standard of proof at sentencing—preponderance of the evidence—is met.

Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sits comfortably within this framework. Courts across the circuits have applied it to uncharged state-law felonies, uncharged federal offenses, and conduct that prosecutors declined to pursue. The logic is straightforward: if Congress has directed that possessing a firearm in connection with another felony warrants greater punishment, the formal decision to charge that underlying felony should not control the sentencing outcome.

But the practical consequences can be dramatic. In Cutbank's case, the four-level enhancement raised his total offense level from 31 to 35. Combined with a Criminal History Category of VI, this produced a Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months. Without the enhancement, his range would have been 188 to 235 months—roughly seven to ten years less. The uncharged conduct nearly doubled the floor of his recommended sentence.

## Charging Discretion and Sentencing Severity

The case raises a structural question about the relationship between prosecutorial discretion and sentencing outcomes. Prosecutors decide which charges to bring. They chose § 922(g) here, not kidnapping or assault. Perhaps the evidence on those charges presented proof problems. Perhaps plea negotiations or resource constraints drove the decision. But the sentencing court then used that same uncharged conduct to significantly increase Cutbank's sentence—without the procedural protections that would have attached had the government brought those charges.

To be sure, Cutbank did not object to the PSR's factual statements at sentencing, and the evidence supporting the underlying conduct came in at trial through the victim's testimony. This is not a case where the sentencing court relied on dubious allegations. But the structure of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) would permit enhancement based on facts found by a judge by a preponderance, even if a jury would have required proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

## Downstream Implications

The decision also illustrates an important interaction with the Armed Career Criminal Act. Cutbank argued that applying the enhancement produced a Guidelines range that "conflicts with the core principle" that sentences be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The court rejected this as a challenge to the Guidelines calculation itself—not the substantive reasonableness of the sentence. Within-range sentences are presumed reasonable on appeal, and the district court's consideration of the § 3553(a) factors was sufficient.

Practically, defendants facing § 922(g) charges where underlying felony conduct exists should expect the government to seek this enhancement. Defense counsel would be wise to scrutinize the factual basis for any "other felony offense" carefully. The preponderance standard means that contested facts at sentencing can still support the enhancement—but the defense may have opportunities to challenge the factual predicate if the government's evidence is thin.

## Conclusion

*Cutbank* represents business as usual in federal sentencing. The Guidelines expressly permit enhancement based on uncharged conduct, and courts routinely affirm such applications. But the magnitude of the sentence increase here—nearly a decade of additional recommended imprisonment—shows why these provisions matter. The decision about what to charge remains with the prosecutor. The decision about what conduct warrants enhanced punishment rests with the judge, applying a standard that favors the government. For defendants like Cutbank, that combination can be decisive.
