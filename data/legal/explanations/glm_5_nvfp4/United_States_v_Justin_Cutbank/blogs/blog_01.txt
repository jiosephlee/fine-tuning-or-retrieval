Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic or Propensity? The Eighth Circuit's Prior Possession Doctrine in Felon-in-Possession Cases

The line between intrinsic evidence and impermissible propensity evidence is one of the most contested terrains in federal criminal practice. Nowhere does this tension surface more regularly than in felon-in-possession prosecutions, where courts must decide whether—and how—evidence of a defendant's prior possession of the same firearm can be presented to the jury. In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week, the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed its straightforward answer: prior possession of the same weapon is intrinsic evidence, not governed by Rule 404(b) at all. The decision offers a useful window into a circuit split that continues to divide courts on the proper boundaries of the "prior bad acts" prohibition.

## The Facts and the Legal Question

Justin Cutbank's conviction arose from a terrifying domestic violence incident. After using methamphetamine, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint for hours, threatened her life, and struck her with the firearm. When he finally fled, police located the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle along his probable route. DNA on the weapon matched Cutbank.

Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from two witnesses—his girlfriend and her housemate—that they had seen him with this same rifle on multiple occasions before the incident. He argued this was classic propensity evidence: the government was trying to show that because he possessed the gun before, he must have possessed it on the night in question. The district court rejected the argument, holding that testimony about prior possession of the *same* firearm was intrinsic to the charged offense.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed. Quoting its 2014 decision in *United States v. Battle*, the court stated that "[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." The panel treated this as an established principle requiring no further analysis—indeed, it devoted just one paragraph to the issue.

## The Doctrinal Divide

The Eighth Circuit's approach reflects one side of an enduring circuit split. Courts generally agree that Rule 404(b) does not apply to evidence that is "intrinsic" to the charged offense—that is, evidence that forms part of the factual background of the case or is directly at issue. But courts disagree about where that line should be drawn in felon-in-possession cases.

The Eighth Circuit, like the Fifth and Seventh Circuits, treats prior possession of the *same* firearm as per se intrinsic. The theory is straightforward: if the government must prove that the defendant possessed a particular weapon at a particular time, evidence that he possessed that same weapon before is directly relevant to establishing ownership, knowledge, and control. It is not evidence of some "other" act; it is evidence of continuous possession of the same contraband item.

Other circuits take a more restrictive view. The Fourth Circuit, for instance, has held that prior possession evidence is properly analyzed under Rule 404(b) because it involves acts separate in time from the charged offense. Under this approach, the evidence may still be admissible—but it must satisfy the 404(b) framework, including the requirement that the prosecution articulate a proper non-propensity purpose. The distinction matters because 404(b) carries procedural and substantive safeguards that the "intrinsic" label bypasses entirely.

## Why the Distinction Matters

The circuit split is not merely academic. Treating prior possession as intrinsic evidence means trial judges need not conduct the familiar 404(b) analysis—no need to identify a permissible purpose, no need to weigh probative value against unfair prejudice under Rule 403, and no need to give a limiting instruction. The evidence comes in as part of the government's direct case.

In theory, this should not dramatically alter trial outcomes. Even under 404(b), prior possession of the same firearm would almost always satisfy the "intent" or "knowledge" purposes, and its probative value would typically outweigh any prejudicial effect. But the doctrinal categorization matters for at least two reasons.

First, the intrinsic label changes the evidentiary landscape before trial. Defense counsel cannot use 404(b) motions to force the government to articulate its theory of relevance or to obtain pretrial rulings on admissibility. The evidence is simply part of the government's case.

Second, the distinction affects appellate review. When evidence is deemed intrinsic, courts apply ordinary abuse-of-discretion review without the structured analysis that 404(b) demands. That difference was visible in *Cutbank* itself—the Eighth Circuit needed only a single paragraph to reject the defendant's argument, citing *Battle* as dispositive.

## The Eighth Circuit's Prior Possession Doctrine

The Eighth Circuit's approach has the virtue of clarity and practical simplicity. The court's reasoning in *Battle* and now *Cutbank* rests on a commonsense intuition: if the government charges possession of a specific firearm, and the defendant disputes that he possessed it, evidence that he possessed that same weapon before is directly relevant to proving who controlled it. The evidence does not rely on an impermissible inference of character or propensity; it relies on the logical inference that a person who possessed a gun before is more likely to have possessed it at the time in question.

But this logic has its limits. Prior possession evidence can still carry significant prejudicial force. In *Cutbank*, for example, the witnesses testified that they had seen the defendant with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the incident. That evidence did more than establish control—it painted a picture of a defendant who routinely armed himself with an illegal weapon. The danger is that jurors will convict not because they are persuaded of possession on the night in question, but because they conclude the defendant is a dangerous person who deserves to be incarcerated.

To be sure, the Eighth Circuit has not abandoned 404(b) entirely. The court would presumably still apply the rule to prior possession of a *different* firearm, or to evidence of other bad acts by the defendant. But when the same weapon is at issue, the court has drawn a clear line.

## Downstream Implications

*Cutbank* reinforces the Eighth Circuit's position in an area where the Supreme Court has shown little interest in granting certiorari. That means the circuit split will likely persist, with practitioners facing different evidentiary regimes depending on venue.

The decision also highlights a broader trend in the Eighth Circuit's criminal jurisprudence: a pragmatic, prosecution-friendly approach to evidentiary questions that emphasizes probative value over formal categorization. Whether that approach serves the interests of justice in close cases remains an open question. In *Cutbank*, where the evidence of guilt was overwhelming—including DNA evidence and independent witness testimony—the prior possession evidence was largely cumulative. But the principle will apply equally in weaker cases, where the government's evidence of possession may hinge on disputed eyewitness identification or circumstantial inference.

For defense practitioners in the Eighth Circuit, *Cutbank* confirms that 404(b) motions to exclude prior possession evidence face an uphill battle. The better strategy may be to focus on Rule 403 arguments—that even if admissible, the evidence should be excluded because its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. The court's brief analysis suggests it would not have found such an argument persuasive here, but the door remains open in future cases where the prejudicial effect is more pronounced.

The Eighth Circuit's approach represents a defensible if controversial resolution to a genuine doctrinal puzzle. Whether the Supreme Court will eventually step in to resolve the circuit split remains to be seen. Until then, practitioners must navigate a patchwork of rules that treat the same evidence differently depending on geography.
