Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic or Propensity? The Eighth Circuit's Prior Possession Doctrine in Felon-in-Possession Cases

The line between intrinsic evidence and impermissible propensity evidence is one of the most contested terrains in federal criminal practice. Nowhere does this tension surface more regularly than in felon-in-possession prosecutions, where courts must decide whether—and how—evidence of a defendant's prior possession of the same firearm can be presented to the jury. In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week, the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed its straightforward answer: prior possession of the same weapon is intrinsic evidence, not governed by Rule 404(b) at all. The decision offers a useful window into a circuit split that continues to divide courts on the proper boundaries of the "prior bad acts" prohibition.

## The Facts and the Legal Question

Justin Cutbank's conviction arose from a terrifying domestic violence incident. After using methamphetamine, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint for hours, threatened her life, and struck her with the firearm. When he finally fled, police located the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle along his probable route. DNA on the weapon matched Cutbank.

Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from two witnesses—his girlfriend and her housemate—that they had seen him with this same rifle on multiple occasions before the incident. He argued this was classic propensity evidence: the government was trying to show that because he possessed the gun before, he must have possessed it on the night in question. The district court rejected the argument, holding that testimony about prior possession of the *same* firearm was intrinsic to the charged offense.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed. Quoting its 2014 decision in *United States v. Battle*, the court stated that "[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." The panel treated this as an established principle requiring no further analysis—indeed, it devoted just one paragraph to the issue.

## The Doctrinal Divide

The Eighth Circuit's approach reflects one side of an enduring circuit split. Courts generally agree that Rule 404(b) does not apply to evidence that is "intrinsic" to the charged offense—that is, evidence that forms part of the factual background of the case or is directly at issue. But courts disagree about where that line should be drawn in felon-in-possession cases.

The Eighth Circuit, like the Fifth and Seventh Circuits, treats prior possession of the *same* firearm as per se intrinsic. The theory is straightforward: if the government must prove that the defendant possessed a particular weapon at a particular time, evidence that he possessed that same weapon before is directly relevant to establishing ownership, knowledge, and control. It is not evidence of some "other" act; it is evidence of continuous possession of the same contraband item.

Other circuits take a more restrictive view. The Fourth Circuit, for instance, has held that prior possession evidence is properly analyzed under Rule 404(b) because it involves acts separate in time from the charged offense. Under this approach, the evidence may still be admissible—but it must satisfy the 404(b) framework, including the requirement that the prosecution articulate a proper non-propensity purpose. The distinction matters because 404(b) carries procedural and substantive safeguards that the "intrinsic" label bypasses entirely.

## Why the Distinction Matters

The circuit split is not merely academic. Treating prior possession as intrinsic evidence means trial judges need not conduct the familiar 404(b) analysis—no need to identify a permissible purpose, no need to weigh probative value against unfair prejudice under Rule 403, and no need to give a limiting instruction. The evidence comes in as part of the government's direct case.

In theory, this should not dramatically alter trial outcomes. Even under 404(b), prior possession of the same firearm would almost always satisfy the "intent" or "knowledge" purposes, and its probative value would typically outweigh any prejudicial effect. But the doctrinal categorization matters for at least two reasons.

First, the intrinsic label changes the evidentiary landscape before trial. Defense counsel cannot use 404(b) motions to force the government to articulate its theory of relevance or to obtain pretrial rulings on admissibility. The evidence is simply part of the government's case.

Second, the distinction affects appellate review. When evidence is deemed intrinsic, courts apply ordinary abuse-of-discretion review without the structured analysis that 404(b) demands. That difference was visible in *Cutbank* itself—the Eighth Circuit needed only a single paragraph to reject the defendant's argument, citing *Battle* as dispositive.

## The Eighth Circuit's Prior Possession Doctrine

The Eighth Circuit's approach has the virtue of clarity and practical simplicity. The court's reasoning in *Battle* and now *Cutbank* rests on a commonsense intuition: if the government charges possession of a specific firearm, and the defendant disputes that he possessed it, evidence that he possessed that same weapon before is directly relevant to proving who controlled it. The evidence does not rely on an impermissible inference of character or propensity; it relies on the logical inference that a person who possessed a gun before is more likely to have possessed it at the time in question.

But this logic has its limits. Prior possession evidence can still carry significant prejudicial force. In *Cutbank*, for example, the witnesses testified that they had seen the defendant with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the incident. That evidence did more than establish control—it painted a picture of a defendant who routinely armed himself with an illegal weapon. The danger is that jurors will convict not because they are persuaded of possession on the night in question, but because they conclude the defendant is a dangerous person who deserves to be incarcerated.

To be sure, the Eighth Circuit has not abandoned 404(b) entirely. The court would presumably still apply the rule to prior possession of a *different* firearm, or to evidence of other bad acts by the defendant. But when the same weapon is at issue, the court has drawn a clear line.

## Downstream Implications

*Cutbank* reinforces the Eighth Circuit's position in an area where the Supreme Court has shown little interest in granting certiorari. That means the circuit split will likely persist, with practitioners facing different evidentiary regimes depending on venue.

The decision also highlights a broader trend in the Eighth Circuit's criminal jurisprudence: a pragmatic, prosecution-friendly approach to evidentiary questions that emphasizes probative value over formal categorization. Whether that approach serves the interests of justice in close cases remains an open question. In *Cutbank*, where the evidence of guilt was overwhelming—including DNA evidence and independent witness testimony—the prior possession evidence was largely cumulative. But the principle will apply equally in weaker cases, where the government's evidence of possession may hinge on disputed eyewitness identification or circumstantial inference.

For defense practitioners in the Eighth Circuit, *Cutbank* confirms that 404(b) motions to exclude prior possession evidence face an uphill battle. The better strategy may be to focus on Rule 403 arguments—that even if admissible, the evidence should be excluded because its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. The court's brief analysis suggests it would not have found such an argument persuasive here, but the door remains open in future cases where the prejudicial effect is more pronounced.

The Eighth Circuit's approach represents a defensible if controversial resolution to a genuine doctrinal puzzle. Whether the Supreme Court will eventually step in to resolve the circuit split remains to be seen. Until then, practitioners must navigate a patchwork of rules that treat the same evidence differently depending on geography.



# No Charge Required: Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and Sentencing Based on Uncharged Felony Conduct

The federal sentencing guidelines have long permitted judges to consider conduct that was never charged, let alone proven to a jury. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank* offers a stark illustration of this principle in action—and a reminder of how significantly uncharged conduct can drive a Guidelines sentence.

## The Enhancement That Didn't Require a Charge

Justin Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The Armed Career Criminal Act mandated a minimum fifteen-year sentence given his prior violent felonies. But the sentencing calculation that produced his 292-month term included something more: a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm "in connection with another felony offense."

The "other felony" was never charged. According to trial testimony, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint for hours, threatened her life, struck her with the firearm, and refused to leave a stranger's garage while claiming to be armed. These facts, recited in the Presentence Investigation Report, formed the basis for the enhancement. No kidnapping charge. No assault charge. No jury verdict on whether those offenses occurred.

Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 explicitly permits this approach. The enhancement applies, the Note states, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." The Eighth Circuit had little difficulty affirming the district court's adoption of the PSR's factual statements, which Cutbank did not contest at sentencing.

## The Broader Framework: Uncharged Conduct and the Guidelines

Cutbank's case is unremarkable in the sense that it follows well-established doctrine. Federal sentencing has long operated on a "real offense" model, where judges consider the defendant's actual conduct, not merely the elements of the crime of conviction. Watts, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), held that acquitted conduct can even support a sentencing enhancement, so long as the standard of proof at sentencing—preponderance of the evidence—is met.

Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sits comfortably within this framework. Courts across the circuits have applied it to uncharged state-law felonies, uncharged federal offenses, and conduct that prosecutors declined to pursue. The logic is straightforward: if Congress has directed that possessing a firearm in connection with another felony warrants greater punishment, the formal decision to charge that underlying felony should not control the sentencing outcome.

But the practical consequences can be dramatic. In Cutbank's case, the four-level enhancement raised his total offense level from 31 to 35. Combined with a Criminal History Category of VI, this produced a Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months. Without the enhancement, his range would have been 188 to 235 months—roughly seven to ten years less. The uncharged conduct nearly doubled the floor of his recommended sentence.

## Charging Discretion and Sentencing Severity

The case raises a structural question about the relationship between prosecutorial discretion and sentencing outcomes. Prosecutors decide which charges to bring. They chose § 922(g) here, not kidnapping or assault. Perhaps the evidence on those charges presented proof problems. Perhaps plea negotiations or resource constraints drove the decision. But the sentencing court then used that same uncharged conduct to significantly increase Cutbank's sentence—without the procedural protections that would have attached had the government brought those charges.

To be sure, Cutbank did not object to the PSR's factual statements at sentencing, and the evidence supporting the underlying conduct came in at trial through the victim's testimony. This is not a case where the sentencing court relied on dubious allegations. But the structure of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) would permit enhancement based on facts found by a judge by a preponderance, even if a jury would have required proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

## Downstream Implications

The decision also illustrates an important interaction with the Armed Career Criminal Act. Cutbank argued that applying the enhancement produced a Guidelines range that "conflicts with the core principle" that sentences be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The court rejected this as a challenge to the Guidelines calculation itself—not the substantive reasonableness of the sentence. Within-range sentences are presumed reasonable on appeal, and the district court's consideration of the § 3553(a) factors was sufficient.

Practically, defendants facing § 922(g) charges where underlying felony conduct exists should expect the government to seek this enhancement. Defense counsel would be wise to scrutinize the factual basis for any "other felony offense" carefully. The preponderance standard means that contested facts at sentencing can still support the enhancement—but the defense may have opportunities to challenge the factual predicate if the government's evidence is thin.

## Conclusion

*Cutbank* represents business as usual in federal sentencing. The Guidelines expressly permit enhancement based on uncharged conduct, and courts routinely affirm such applications. But the magnitude of the sentence increase here—nearly a decade of additional recommended imprisonment—shows why these provisions matter. The decision about what to charge remains with the prosecutor. The decision about what conduct warrants enhanced punishment rests with the judge, applying a standard that favors the government. For defendants like Cutbank, that combination can be decisive.



# Presumed Reasonable: The Eighth Circuit's Deferential Approach to Within-Guidelines ACCA Sentences

The Armed Career Criminal Act has long been a source of doctrinal complexity in federal sentencing, creating mandatory minimums that interact in sometimes awkward ways with the advisory guidelines system. Last week, in *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit added another chapter to this ongoing story, affirming a 292-month sentence for a felon in possession and rejecting the defendant's novel argument that overlapping guideline enhancements conflict with the statutory command that sentences be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary."

The case presents a fact pattern that has become familiar in ACCA prosecutions. Justin Cutbank, a felon with an extensive criminal history, held his girlfriend at gunpoint during a drug-fueled episode of paranoia, threatening her life and striking her with the sawed-off rifle. When police finally apprehended him after a standoff, he bit an officer and attempted to grab the officer's weapon. A jury convicted Cutbank under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), triggering ACCA's fifteen-year mandatory minimum.

## The Sentencing Calculation Problem

What makes Cutbank's case interesting from a doctrinal perspective is not the underlying facts but the mathematical puzzle his sentencing presented. The Presentence Investigation Report recommended a base offense level of 26 under USSG § 2K2.1(a)(1), applicable because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm and Cutbank had multiple prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR then applied several enhancements: three levels for the obliterated serial number, two levels for recklessly creating a risk of death during flight, and four levels under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This last enhancement is where Cutbank's argument gained traction. The four-level bump produced a total offense level of 35, which, combined with a Criminal History Category of VI, yielded an advisory range of 292 to 365 months. But the ACCA guideline provision, USSG § 4B1.4, would have produced an offense level of 34 on its own. Under § 4B1.4(b), the greater offense level controls. Cutbank argued that this layering of enhancements—particularly the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) increase for conduct already accounted for in his ACCA status—produced a sentence that "conflicts with the core principle" of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that sentences not be greater than necessary.

## The Parsimony Principle Meets Guideline Calculations

Cutbank's argument is not frivolous. The parsimony principle—the requirement that district courts impose sentences that are sufficient but not greater than necessary—sits at the heart of federal sentencing law. Congress codified it in § 3553(a) as a check on excessive punishment. The question Cutbank raises is whether the mechanical application of guideline enhancements on top of ACCA's already severe framework can produce results that violate this statutory mandate.

The Eighth Circuit was unmoved. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Loken noted that the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range and was "aware of Cutbank's arguments" regarding the § 3553(a) factors. The court applied its standard presumption: "we presume that the district court considered and rejected them." This formulation, drawn from recent Eighth Circuit precedent, reflects a highly deferential stance toward sentencing judges who impose within-range sentences.

The court's reasoning highlights an important feature of post-*Gall* appellate review. Once a district court correctly calculates the guidelines range and considers the § 3553(a) factors, a within-range sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal. The burden shifts to the defendant to demonstrate substantive unreasonableness—a heavy lift when the sentence falls within the advisory range. Cutbank's argument that the sentence violated the parsimony principle was thus treated as a variance argument that the district court implicitly rejected, rather than as a legal error requiring correction.

## The Broader Context: Layered Enhancements in ACCA Cases

Cutbank's case illustrates a persistent tension in federal sentencing for armed career criminals. ACCA mandates a fifteen-year minimum, but the guidelines then pile additional offense level increases on top of this floor. The sawed-off rifle enhancement, the serial number enhancement, the flight enhancement—all reflect congressional and Sentencing Commission judgments about particular offense characteristics. But they can combine to produce sentences that seem to punish the same conduct multiple times over.

The Fourth-Level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) exemplifies this problem. The provision applies when a defendant possesses a firearm "in connection with another felony offense," regardless of whether charges are brought for that underlying offense. In Cutbank's case, the "other felony" was presumably his conduct toward D.F.—the assault, the kidnapping, the domestic violence. But that same conduct also informed his criminal history score and his ACCA status. The question is whether § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is double-counting what ACCA already accounts for.

The Eighth Circuit did not engage with this question directly. Instead, it treated Cutbank's argument as a straightforward challenge to the substantive reasonableness of a within-guidelines sentence. This approach is consistent with circuit precedent but leaves unresolved a deeper question: whether certain guideline enhancements, when combined with ACCA's framework, can produce sentences that systematically exceed what § 3553(a) permits.

## A Presumption That Bites

The most notable aspect of *Cutbank* is how thoroughly the presumption of reasonableness foreclosed meaningful appellate review. Cutbank did not challenge the guidelines calculation itself; he argued that the result of that calculation—producing an offense level of 35 rather than 34—conflicted with statutory sentencing principles. But the Eighth Circuit's deferential standard meant that once the district court imposed a within-range sentence, the substantive reasonableness challenge faced an uphill battle that Cutbank could not climb.

This deference reflects a broader trend in the Eighth Circuit's sentencing jurisprudence. The court has consistently held that within-range sentences need not be justified on the record and that district courts are presumed to have considered and rejected variance arguments even when they do not explicitly address them. For defendants like Cutbank, who receive substantial sentences for serious offenses, this presumption creates a significant barrier to appellate relief.

The decision also underscores the limited utility of parsimony principle arguments in the Eighth Circuit when the sentence falls within the guidelines range. Courts in this circuit treat the guidelines calculation as the starting point for the § 3553(a) analysis, not as a potential source of error that might itself violate the statute's commands. Cutbank's attempt to weaponize the parsimony principle against the guideline enhancements failed because the court viewed the guidelines range as presumptively compliant with § 3553(a)—a presumption that is effectively irrebuttable in the absence of a variance.

## Conclusion

*United States v. Cutbank* offers a clear statement of the Eighth Circuit's approach to within-guidelines ACCA sentences: calculate correctly, consider the statutory factors, and the presumption of reasonableness will do the rest. The decision provides little guidance for defendants seeking to challenge the layering of enhancements that may produce sentences exceeding what the parsimony principle allows. For practitioners in the Eighth Circuit, the message is clear—challenges to within-range ACCA sentences face a presumption that has become, for all practical purposes, conclusive.
