Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Factual and Procedural Framework

## The November 2020 Incident: Domestic Violence, Kidnapping, and Firearm Possession

The events giving rise to this prosecution occurred during the night of November 19-20, 2020. Justin Cutbank, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, engaged in a violent episode at the home of his girlfriend, D.F. The conduct began when Cutbank awakened D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. Over the ensuing hours, Cutbank made paranoid accusations about "listening devices" in the home and people outside waiting to harm him. He held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint, threatened her life, and struck her across the face with the firearm.

The incident escalated when Cutbank ordered D.F. to wake others sleeping in the house, including her two teenage sons and their housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank fled the residence, taking D.F.'s cell phone and the gun with him. D.F. called the police.

Shortly thereafter, police received a second call from a residence approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home. The caller reported that a man was in the garage and refused to leave. When officers arrived, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage. Over several hours, he made erratic statements and claimed to possess a weapon. When officers attempted to detain him, Cutbank resisted violently, biting one officer in the face and neck and grabbing for the officer's gun.

Officers searching the area between D.F.'s home and the garage discovered three items along what appeared to be Cutbank's likely path of flight: D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA analysis confirmed that Cutbank's DNA was on the rifle. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified this weapon as one they had observed in Cutbank's possession before the incident.

## The Felon in Possession Charge Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)

The federal government charged Cutbank with being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). This statute prohibits any person who has been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment exceeding one year from knowingly possessing a firearm that has traveled in or affecting interstate commerce. The offense is a foundational federal gun crime, frequently prosecuted in federal district courts.

Section 922(g)(1) establishes the basic prohibition, while § 924 sets forth the penalties for violations. Section 924(e), known as the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), provides enhanced mandatory minimum sentences for defendants who violate § 922(g) and have three or more previous convictions for "violent felonies" or "serious drug offenses." The ACCA transforms what would otherwise be a offense with a maximum ten-year sentence into one carrying a mandatory minimum fifteen-year term and a maximum of life imprisonment.

The charging documents in this case alleged that Cutbank had an extensive criminal history, including multiple prior felony convictions. The government sought ACCA enhanced penalties based on at least three prior violent felony convictions.

## The Armed Career Criminal Act and Enhanced Penalties for Violent Felons

The Armed Career Criminal Act, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), represents Congress's determination that certain repeat offenders pose heightened danger to public safety and warrant substantially increased sentences. To trigger ACCA penalties, the government must prove that the defendant committed the § 922(g) offense and had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, serious drug offenses, or a combination thereof, committed on occasions different from one another.

A "violent felony" under ACCA is defined as any crime punishable by imprisonment exceeding one year that either: (1) has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against another; or (2) is burglary, arson, or extortion, involves use of explosives, or otherwise involves conduct presenting a serious potential risk of physical injury to another. This second category, known as the "residual clause," has been the subject of significant Supreme Court litigation, though it plays no role in the present appeal.

In this case, the jury found that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby triggering ACCA's enhanced penalty provisions. This finding was essential to the ultimate sentence imposed.

## Trial Proceedings and Jury Verdict

Cutbank proceeded to trial on the felon-in-possession charge. Before trial, he filed motions in limine seeking to exclude certain testimony, including testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle. The district court denied these motions, ruling that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence relevant to proving possession and demonstrating continuous possession before the incident.

During trial, the government presented testimony from D.F. and C.F., police officers who responded to the scene, and forensic evidence establishing Cutbank's DNA on the recovered firearm. The defense cross-examined government witnesses and challenged the reliability of certain identifications. The jury convicted Cutbank of the charged offense and found that he had the requisite three or more prior violent felony convictions to trigger ACCA penalties.

## Sentencing Outcome and Calculation of the Advisory Guidelines Range

Following the conviction, the United States Probation Office prepared a Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) calculating Cutbank's advisory sentencing range under the United States Sentencing Guidelines. The PSR determined a base offense level of 26, which applied because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR then added several specific offense characteristics: a three-level increase because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number; a two-level increase because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police; and a four-level increase because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These adjustments yielded a total offense level of 35.

The PSR calculated Cutbank's Criminal History Category as VI, the highest category under the Guidelines. Combined with offense level 35, this produced an advisory sentencing range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, the bottom of the advisory range. The sentence fell within the statutory range required by ACCA, which mandates a minimum fifteen-year term for armed career criminals.

## Issues Raised on Appeal and Applicable Standards of Review

Cutbank appealed both his conviction and his sentence, raising multiple arguments. With respect to his conviction, he challenged several evidentiary and procedural rulings by the district court, claiming these errors deprived him of a fair trial. Specifically, he argued that the district court improperly admitted propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), failed to give necessary limiting instructions regarding hearsay, improperly admitted certain testimony, and restricted his ability to cross-examine a government witness.

With respect to his sentence, Cutbank challenged the application of the four-level sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm in connection with another felony offense. He argued that applying this enhancement resulted in a guidelines range that conflicted with the statutory directive under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary."

The Eighth Circuit applied different standards of review to these categories of claims. Evidentiary rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion, meaning the appellate court gives substantial deference to the trial court's determinations and reverses only if that court made a clear error of judgment. Even if an error occurred, the court applies harmless error review, affirming unless the error substantially influenced the jury's verdict. Sentencing decisions are also reviewed for abuse of discretion, with a within-guidelines sentence presumed reasonable on appeal. Procedural challenges to sentencing, such as claims that the district court miscalculated the guidelines range, are reviewed for clear error as to factual findings and de novo as to legal interpretations. Substantive reasonableness challenges are reviewed for abuse of discretion, with the appellate court considering the totality of the circumstances.

# Rule 404(b) and the Intrinsic Evidence Doctrine

## Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) and the Prohibition on Propensity Evidence

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) embodies a fundamental principle of evidence law: a defendant may not be convicted based on evidence that he has a bad character or a propensity to commit crimes. The rule prohibits evidence of a defendant's other crimes, wrongs, or acts when offered to prove that the defendant acted in accordance with that character trait. This prohibition guards against the substantial risk that a jury will convict a defendant not because the prosecution proved the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but because the jury concludes the defendant is a bad person who deserves punishment. The concern is particularly acute in criminal trials, where the stakes are high and the temptation to rely on character reasoning is strong.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the defendant sought to exclude testimony from two witnesses—his girlfriend D.F. and housemate C.F.—that they had previously observed him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle that formed the basis of the felon-in-possession charge. Cutbank argued that this evidence constituted prohibited "propensity evidence" under Rule 404(b). If the jury heard that he had possessed this same firearm on prior occasions, Cutbank reasoned, they might infer that he possessed it on the night in question simply because he was the type of person who carries guns. The district court rejected this argument, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, concluding that the evidence was not governed by Rule 404(b) at all.

## The Intrinsic Evidence Exception: Evidence That Is Part of the Charged Offense

The key to understanding this evidentiary dispute is the distinction between "extrinsic" and "intrinsic" evidence. Rule 404(b) governs evidence of "other acts"—conduct that is extrinsic to the charged offense. But evidence that is intrinsic to the charged offense falls outside the scope of Rule 404(b) entirely. Intrinsic evidence is evidence that directly proves an element of the charged offense or provides necessary context for understanding the offense. Such evidence is admissible not because it fits within a Rule 404(b) exception, but because it is not evidence of "other acts" at all—it is evidence of the very conduct for which the defendant is on trial.

The line between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence can be subtle. Courts generally treat evidence as intrinsic when it forms an integral part of the factual narrative of the charged offense. For example, evidence that completes the story of the crime, explains the sequence of events, or establishes an essential element of the offense typically qualifies as intrinsic. By contrast, evidence of separate, distinct acts that are not part of the same transaction or course of conduct generally falls within Rule 404(b)'s prohibition. The determination turns on whether the evidence describes conduct that is part of the same event or course of conduct as the charged offense, or whether it describes truly separate acts.

## Prior Possession of the Same Firearm as Proof of Ownership or Control

In a felon-in-possession prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm charged in the indictment. Possession is the gravamen of the offense. This creates a unique evidentiary landscape: evidence that the defendant possessed the same firearm on occasions before the charged offense date can be directly relevant to proving possession on the charged date, not as character evidence, but as substantive proof of an element of the offense.

The logic is straightforward. If the defendant possessed a particular firearm on multiple occasions before the charged date, that evidence tends to establish that the defendant had ownership or control over that specific weapon. This is not propensity reasoning—it is not an inference from "he possessed guns in the past" to "he possessed a gun on this occasion." Rather, it is direct evidence that the defendant had an ongoing relationship with the specific firearm at issue. The evidence helps the jury answer the central question: was this the defendant's gun? Prior possession of the same weapon provides affirmative evidence that the defendant owned or controlled it.

## The Battle Precedent: United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014)

The Eighth Circuit's analysis in *Cutbank* relied directly on its prior precedent in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014). In *Battle*, the court articulated the governing principle: "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." This statement captures the essential distinction between admissible evidence of prior possession of the *same* firearm and inadmissible evidence of prior possession of *other* firearms.

The *Battle* court recognized that when the evidence concerns the same weapon charged in the indictment, it serves a legitimate evidentiary purpose: it tends to prove that the defendant possessed that weapon on the charged date. The evidence is offered not to show that the defendant has a general propensity to possess firearms, but to show that this specific firearm was in his possession. This reasoning distinguishes cases involving the same weapon from those involving different weapons or other bad acts, which would raise the specter of forbidden propensity reasoning. *Battle* thus established a bright-line principle in the Eighth Circuit: testimony about prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic evidence admissible to prove the possession element of a § 922(g) charge.

## Pretrial Ruling on the Admissibility of Prior Possession Testimony

Cutbank's challenge to this evidence began before trial, when he filed a motion in limine to prevent D.F. and C.F. from testifying about their prior observations of him with the sawed-off rifle. This procedural posture is significant. A motion in limine allows parties to seek advance rulings on evidentiary issues before the jury is empaneled, promoting trial efficiency and preventing prejudicial evidence from being mentioned before a ruling can be obtained. The district court considered Cutbank's Rule 404(b) objection and rejected it, adopting the government's position that the testimony would constitute intrinsic evidence relevant to proving possession.

The district court's pretrial ruling was straightforward. The court agreed with the government that the evidence was "intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident." This reasoning tracked *Battle* directly. The court understood that the witnesses' testimony about seeing Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before November 19-20, 2020, was not evidence of "other acts" requiring Rule 404(b) analysis—it was evidence of the same act of possession that formed the basis of the charge. By ruling before trial, the district court provided clarity for both parties and allowed the trial to proceed without interruption on this point.

## Trial Testimony from D.F. and C.F. Regarding Prior Observations of the Firearm

At trial, the contested testimony unfolded as the district court had permitted. D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the November 19-20 incident. C.F. similarly testified that she had observed the gun "once or twice" prior to that night. This testimony provided the jury with evidence from which it could infer that Cutbank had an established pattern of possession of this specific weapon. The witnesses' testimony was consistent with the government's theory that the rifle found along the path between D.F.'s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself was the same rifle Cutbank had possessed on prior occasions.

Cutbank renewed his objection to this testimony during trial, but the district court overruled the objection and adhered to its pretrial ruling. On appeal, Cutbank cited no authority challenging the court's determination that this evidence was intrinsic and admissible under *Battle*. The Eighth Circuit noted this failure and affirmed the trial court's judgment, reiterating that prior possession of the same firearm is admissible to prove later possession of that weapon. The panel explicitly cited *Battle* and applied its reasoning to uphold the admission of both witnesses' testimony.

## Abuse of Discretion Standard for Evidentiary Rulings

The standard of review applied by the Eighth Circuit is critical to understanding why Cutbank's challenge failed. Federal courts of appeal review district court evidentiary rulings for "abuse of discretion." This highly deferential standard reflects the principle that trial judges are best positioned to make real-time decisions about the admissibility of evidence. Under this standard, an appellate court will not reverse a district court's evidentiary ruling unless the court's decision was clearly unreasonable, arbitrary, or based on an erroneous view of the law.

The abuse of discretion standard creates a substantial hurdle for defendants challenging evidentiary rulings on appeal. The appellate court does not ask whether it would have ruled the same way, but only whether the district court's ruling fell within the range of reasonable options available to the trial judge. In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit's review was straightforward: the district court applied *Battle* correctly, and its ruling that the prior possession testimony was intrinsic evidence fell well within the bounds of its discretion. Moreover, the court noted that even if there had been error, the error would have been harmless given the substantial evidence of Cutbank's guilt, including DNA evidence matching Cutbank on the recovered rifle. Thus, both the substantive legal analysis and the deferential standard of review converged to affirm the conviction.

# Confrontation Clause, Hearsay, and Cross-Examination Issues

This chapter examines the procedural and constitutional challenges Cutbank raised regarding trial testimony. The defendant raised several issues on appeal concerning the admission of evidence, jury instructions, and limitations on cross-examination, but provided minimal legal support for his arguments. The Eighth Circuit's analysis demonstrates how courts evaluate these types of challenges and the standards that govern them.

## The Request for a Limiting Instruction on D.F.'s Statements to Police

Before trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed that it could not consider D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence of Cutbank's actions until D.F. herself testified about the events. This request was aimed at preventing hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems. The government responded that D.F.'s statements would serve only to establish context for the police officers' actions and their knowledge at the time—purposes that do not violate hearsay rules.

The district court denied Cutbank's request. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit applied an abuse of discretion standard to review the district court's decision not to give a limiting instruction. The court held that a district court "need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial." This principle recognizes that juries receive evidence over the course of a trial, and limiting instructions are often more effective when given as part of the complete set of jury instructions at the end, rather than piecemeal as evidence is admitted.

## Confrontation Clause Requirements When the Hearsay Declarant Testifies at Trial

Cutbank's concern about Confrontation Clause violations stemmed from D.F.'s statements to police being introduced through officer testimony. The Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment guarantees criminal defendants the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." This protection bars the admission of testimonial hearsay statements unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination.

However, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant "actually appear[s] in court and testify[s] in person." Because D.F. testified at trial, Cutbank had the opportunity to cross-examine her about her statements to police and her account of the events. The court found this sufficient to resolve any Confrontation Clause concerns. This holding illustrates an important principle: the Confrontation Clause does not prohibit the admission of out-of-court statements so long as the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination at trial.

## Cross-Examination Impeachment and Authentication Requirements for Police Reports

During trial, the defense sought to undermine C.F.'s testimony by cross-examining her about prior inconsistent statements she made to a police investigator. The defense attempted to read portions of the police report into the record. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. said to police but sustained the government's objection to reading the police report itself. The court's ruling was based on lack of proper foundation because the report was not authored by C.F.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed this limitation. While extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach a witness through prior inconsistent statements, "such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." A police report is a document created by the officer, not by the witness being cross-examined. The witness cannot authenticate a document she did not create. The proper method was to ask the witness about her statements, which the court permitted. This ruling demonstrates that trial courts have discretion to require proper authentication before documents are read into evidence.

## The Testimony About D.F.'s Sister Being Killed by a Gun and Rule 403 Balancing

During cross-examination, D.F. was questioned about prior inconsistent statements regarding the gun. She explained that some of her statements were influenced by the fact that she was "not familiar with guns" and "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." Cutbank moved to strike the testimony about her sister, but the district court denied the motion.

On appeal, Cutbank did not identify any unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony. Federal Rule of Evidence 403 permits a court to exclude relevant evidence "if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of... unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." The court found no abuse of discretion. The testimony was responsive to cross-examination about why D.F. made prior inconsistent statements—it provided context for her hesitation and confusion about guns. Moreover, C.F. later mentioned the sister's death without objection from the defense, further undercutting any claim of prejudice.

## Harmless Error Analysis: Whether Errors Substantially Swayed the Verdict

Even if a trial court commits an evidentiary error, an appellate court will not reverse if the error was harmless. The Eighth Circuit applies this principle rigorously: it "will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless." The standard asks whether the jury "may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence."

The court found that Cutbank could not meet this standard. The government's evidence was described as "substantial, both testimonial and physical." This included D.F.'s testimony about the terrifying events of the night, C.F.'s corroboration, and physical evidence including D.F.'s cell phone and the sawed-off rifle found along the path Cutbank took, with DNA matching Cutbank on the weapon. Given the strength of this evidence, the court concluded that any alleged errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict."

## Cumulative Error Doctrine and Substantial Prejudice Standard

Cutbank raised multiple evidentiary and procedural challenges. Even when individual errors might not warrant reversal, the cumulative error doctrine allows a defendant to argue that the combined effect of multiple errors deprived him of a fair trial. However, the standard is demanding: courts "will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant."

The Eighth Circuit found that Cutbank could not demonstrate substantial prejudice. The court systematically addressed each of his arguments and found them lacking in merit or support. Where he cited no authority, provided no analysis, or pointed to no prejudice, the court rejected his claims. The court also noted that Cutbank provided "no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments" on appeal. The strong evidence of guilt, combined with the lack of demonstrated error or prejudice, led the court to affirm the conviction.

# The Sentencing Enhancement Under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## Presentence Investigation Report and the Calculation of Total Offense Level

Following Cutbank's conviction for being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), the sentencing process began with the preparation of a Presentence Investigation Report (PSR). The PSR is a document prepared by a probation officer that compiles information about the defendant's offense, criminal history, and personal background. It serves as the foundation for calculating the applicable advisory Guidelines range and provides the sentencing court with essential information for determining an appropriate sentence.

In this case, the PSR calculated Cutbank's advisory sentencing range through a series of offense level adjustments. The report started with a base offense level of 26, selected because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. From there, the PSR added a three-level increase because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and a four-level increase under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These enhancements produced a total offense level of 35. Combined with a Criminal History Category of VI—the highest category—this yielded an advisory Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment.

The PSR also noted that the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) applied because Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions. The ACCA Guideline provision, USSG § 4B1.4, would have produced a total offense level of 34 on its own. However, the Guidelines instruct that when multiple provisions could apply, the greater offense level controls. Thus, the PSR's calculation under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) resulted in the higher offense level and became the governing figure.

## USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): The Four-Level Enhancement for Possession in Connection with Another Felony

The specific enhancement at issue in Cutbank's appeal was the four-level increase under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision applies when a defendant "used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense." The enhancement recognizes that firearm possession combined with separate criminal conduct poses heightened danger and warrants more severe punishment.

The term "another felony offense" is defined in the Guidelines commentary to mean "any federal, state, or local offense punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, whether or not a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." This definition is critical because it extends the enhancement's reach beyond offenses that result in separate charges or convictions. The sentencing court need only find that the defendant possessed the firearm in connection with some qualifying felony offense—the offense itself need not be prosecuted.

In Cutbank's case, the district court adopted the PSR's factual statements regarding Cutbank's prior felony convictions without objection. The underlying conduct supporting the enhancement was the terrifying domestic incident described throughout the opinion: Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint, kept her in a closet for hours, threatened her life, struck her with the gun, and created danger for others in the household. This conduct clearly constituted felony offenses such as kidnapping, assault, and domestic abuse. Because these acts were felonies under applicable law, and because Cutbank possessed the sawed-off rifle in connection with them, the four-level enhancement properly applied.

## Application of the Enhancement Without Requiring a Separate Criminal Charge or Conviction

A key feature of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is that the "other felony offense" triggering the enhancement need not result in a separate criminal charge or conviction. Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 expressly states that the enhancement applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." This principle significantly expands the sentencing court's authority to consider conduct that was not—and perhaps will never be—prosecuted separately.

This feature of the Guidelines reflects a broader theme in federal sentencing: the court's consideration of "relevant conduct" under USSG § 1B1.3. At sentencing, the court may take into account all acts that were part of the same course of conduct or common scheme as the offense of conviction, as well as acts that the defendant committed in preparation for or during the offense. The sentencing court's inquiry is not limited to the narrow elements of the crime of conviction; rather, the court examines the full scope of the defendant's conduct to determine an appropriate punishment.

For Cutbank, this meant that the absence of separate charges for kidnapping, assault, or domestic violence was irrelevant to whether the enhancement applied. The sentencing court properly looked to the actual conduct that occurred during the November 2020 incident—the gunpoint confinement, the threats, the physical violence—and determined that this conduct qualified as "another felony offense" under the Guideline. The factual basis was uncontested, as the district court adopted the PSR's findings without objection from Cutbank.

## The Defendant's Argument That the Enhancement Conflicts with § 3553(a)'s Parsimony Principle

On appeal, Cutbank mounted a different challenge to the enhancement. Rather than disputing whether the factual predicate for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was satisfied, Cutbank argued that applying the enhancement produced a within-range sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" that a sentence "be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection."

This argument invoked what is sometimes called the "parsimony principle"—the statutory directive that sentences should be no longer than necessary to achieve the purposes of sentencing, which include just punishment, deterrence, protection of the public, and rehabilitation. Cutbank's theory was essentially that the four-level enhancement, stacked on top of the ACCA's already severe penalties, produced a sentence greater than necessary.

The appellate court rejected this challenge. As the opinion explains, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline alone would have produced an offense level of 34. The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement produced a higher offense level of 35, and under USSG § 4B1.4(b), the greater offense level applies. The Guidelines themselves therefore accounted for this overlap by taking the higher calculation. More fundamentally, Cutbank's argument misapprehended the sentencing process. A district court first calculates the correct Guidelines range, then considers whether the § 3553(a) factors warrant a departure or variance from that range. The fact that a within-range sentence might seem harsh does not render its calculation erroneous.

## Procedural Reasonableness: Correct Calculation of the Guidelines Range Under Gall v. United States

Appellate review of federal sentences proceeds in two stages: procedural reasonableness and substantive reasonableness. The Supreme Court established this framework in *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38 (2007). Procedural review examines whether the district court committed any significant procedural error in determining the sentence. Common procedural errors include failing to calculate the correct Guidelines range, treating the Guidelines as mandatory, failing to consider the § 3553(a) factors, selecting a sentence based on clearly erroneous facts, or failing to adequately explain the chosen sentence.

Before reaching substantive reasonableness, the appellate court must first ensure that no significant procedural error occurred. Here, the district court correctly calculated the applicable Guidelines range. The court adopted the PSR's factual findings without objection, applied the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) based on uncontested facts showing that Cutbank possessed the firearm in connection with other felony offenses, and correctly determined that this offense level (35) exceeded the ACCA Guideline level (34). The Guidelines explicitly provide that when multiple provisions apply, the greater offense level governs. The district court committed no procedural error in this calculation.

## Substantive Reasonableness and the Presumption for Within-Range Sentences

Once procedural reasonableness is established, the appellate court turns to substantive reasonableness—whether the sentence itself, in light of all the circumstances, is unreasonably long or short. For sentences within the applicable Guidelines range, appellate courts apply a presumption of reasonableness. This presumption is not binding, but it reflects the reality that a sentence falling within a properly calculated Guidelines range is more likely to be reasonable than one falling outside that range.

Cutbank's 292-month sentence fell at the very bottom of the advisory range of 292 to 365 months. The appellate court therefore presumed this sentence was reasonable. The burden shifted to Cutbank to rebut this presumption by demonstrating that the sentence was substantively unreasonable given the § 3553(a) factors. This he could not do.

The opinion recites the aggravating factors that supported the sentence: Cutbank had "nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms." The offense of conviction itself was profoundly serious, involving "domestic abuse, kidnaping, assault in the victim's home in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer while resisting arrest." These facts amply justified a sentence at the bottom of the Guidelines range, if not above it. The appellate court found that Cutbank could not overcome the presumption of reasonableness.

## The District Court's Consideration of § 3553(a) Factors and Sentencing Discretion

Cutbank's final argument was that the district court failed to give adequate weight to the § 3553(a) factors that might have warranted a below-range sentence. The sentencing court is required to consider all the factors set forth in § 3553(a), which include: the nature and circumstances of the offense; the history and characteristics of the defendant; the need for the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense, promote respect for the law, and provide just punishment; the need for deterrence; the need to protect the public; and the need to provide the defendant with needed educational or vocational training, medical care, or other correctional treatment.

The record demonstrated that the district court fulfilled this obligation. The court "noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance." Under established Eighth Circuit precedent, when the record shows that the district court was aware of the defendant's arguments, the appellate court presumes that the district court considered and rejected them. The district court need not provide a "mechanical recitation" of each factor or explain in detail why it rejected arguments for a lower sentence. It is sufficient if the record makes clear that the court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors.

The sentencing court has substantial discretion in determining an appropriate sentence, particularly when the sentence falls within the Guidelines range. The appellate court's role is not to reweigh the § 3553(a) factors or to substitute its judgment for that of the district court. Rather, the court of appeals asks whether the sentencing court abused its discretion by imposing a sentence that was substantively unreasonable. Given Cutbank's extensive criminal history and the serious nature of the offense conduct, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion. The within-range sentence of 292 months was both procedurally and substantively reasonable.
