Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Confrontation Clause, Hearsay, and Cross-Examination Issues

This chapter examines the procedural and constitutional challenges Cutbank raised regarding trial testimony. The defendant raised several issues on appeal concerning the admission of evidence, jury instructions, and limitations on cross-examination, but provided minimal legal support for his arguments. The Eighth Circuit's analysis demonstrates how courts evaluate these types of challenges and the standards that govern them.

## The Request for a Limiting Instruction on D.F.'s Statements to Police

Before trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed that it could not consider D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence of Cutbank's actions until D.F. herself testified about the events. This request was aimed at preventing hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems. The government responded that D.F.'s statements would serve only to establish context for the police officers' actions and their knowledge at the time—purposes that do not violate hearsay rules.

The district court denied Cutbank's request. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit applied an abuse of discretion standard to review the district court's decision not to give a limiting instruction. The court held that a district court "need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial." This principle recognizes that juries receive evidence over the course of a trial, and limiting instructions are often more effective when given as part of the complete set of jury instructions at the end, rather than piecemeal as evidence is admitted.

## Confrontation Clause Requirements When the Hearsay Declarant Testifies at Trial

Cutbank's concern about Confrontation Clause violations stemmed from D.F.'s statements to police being introduced through officer testimony. The Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment guarantees criminal defendants the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." This protection bars the admission of testimonial hearsay statements unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination.

However, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant "actually appear[s] in court and testify[s] in person." Because D.F. testified at trial, Cutbank had the opportunity to cross-examine her about her statements to police and her account of the events. The court found this sufficient to resolve any Confrontation Clause concerns. This holding illustrates an important principle: the Confrontation Clause does not prohibit the admission of out-of-court statements so long as the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination at trial.

## Cross-Examination Impeachment and Authentication Requirements for Police Reports

During trial, the defense sought to undermine C.F.'s testimony by cross-examining her about prior inconsistent statements she made to a police investigator. The defense attempted to read portions of the police report into the record. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. said to police but sustained the government's objection to reading the police report itself. The court's ruling was based on lack of proper foundation because the report was not authored by C.F.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed this limitation. While extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach a witness through prior inconsistent statements, "such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." A police report is a document created by the officer, not by the witness being cross-examined. The witness cannot authenticate a document she did not create. The proper method was to ask the witness about her statements, which the court permitted. This ruling demonstrates that trial courts have discretion to require proper authentication before documents are read into evidence.

## The Testimony About D.F.'s Sister Being Killed by a Gun and Rule 403 Balancing

During cross-examination, D.F. was questioned about prior inconsistent statements regarding the gun. She explained that some of her statements were influenced by the fact that she was "not familiar with guns" and "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." Cutbank moved to strike the testimony about her sister, but the district court denied the motion.

On appeal, Cutbank did not identify any unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony. Federal Rule of Evidence 403 permits a court to exclude relevant evidence "if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of... unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." The court found no abuse of discretion. The testimony was responsive to cross-examination about why D.F. made prior inconsistent statements—it provided context for her hesitation and confusion about guns. Moreover, C.F. later mentioned the sister's death without objection from the defense, further undercutting any claim of prejudice.

## Harmless Error Analysis: Whether Errors Substantially Swayed the Verdict

Even if a trial court commits an evidentiary error, an appellate court will not reverse if the error was harmless. The Eighth Circuit applies this principle rigorously: it "will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless." The standard asks whether the jury "may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence."

The court found that Cutbank could not meet this standard. The government's evidence was described as "substantial, both testimonial and physical." This included D.F.'s testimony about the terrifying events of the night, C.F.'s corroboration, and physical evidence including D.F.'s cell phone and the sawed-off rifle found along the path Cutbank took, with DNA matching Cutbank on the weapon. Given the strength of this evidence, the court concluded that any alleged errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict."

## Cumulative Error Doctrine and Substantial Prejudice Standard

Cutbank raised multiple evidentiary and procedural challenges. Even when individual errors might not warrant reversal, the cumulative error doctrine allows a defendant to argue that the combined effect of multiple errors deprived him of a fair trial. However, the standard is demanding: courts "will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant."

The Eighth Circuit found that Cutbank could not demonstrate substantial prejudice. The court systematically addressed each of his arguments and found them lacking in merit or support. Where he cited no authority, provided no analysis, or pointed to no prejudice, the court rejected his claims. The court also noted that Cutbank provided "no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments" on appeal. The strong evidence of guilt, combined with the lack of demonstrated error or prejudice, led the court to affirm the conviction.
