Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Rule 404(b) and the Intrinsic Evidence Doctrine

## Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) and the Prohibition on Propensity Evidence

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) embodies a fundamental principle of evidence law: a defendant may not be convicted based on evidence that he has a bad character or a propensity to commit crimes. The rule prohibits evidence of a defendant's other crimes, wrongs, or acts when offered to prove that the defendant acted in accordance with that character trait. This prohibition guards against the substantial risk that a jury will convict a defendant not because the prosecution proved the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but because the jury concludes the defendant is a bad person who deserves punishment. The concern is particularly acute in criminal trials, where the stakes are high and the temptation to rely on character reasoning is strong.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the defendant sought to exclude testimony from two witnesses—his girlfriend D.F. and housemate C.F.—that they had previously observed him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle that formed the basis of the felon-in-possession charge. Cutbank argued that this evidence constituted prohibited "propensity evidence" under Rule 404(b). If the jury heard that he had possessed this same firearm on prior occasions, Cutbank reasoned, they might infer that he possessed it on the night in question simply because he was the type of person who carries guns. The district court rejected this argument, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, concluding that the evidence was not governed by Rule 404(b) at all.

## The Intrinsic Evidence Exception: Evidence That Is Part of the Charged Offense

The key to understanding this evidentiary dispute is the distinction between "extrinsic" and "intrinsic" evidence. Rule 404(b) governs evidence of "other acts"—conduct that is extrinsic to the charged offense. But evidence that is intrinsic to the charged offense falls outside the scope of Rule 404(b) entirely. Intrinsic evidence is evidence that directly proves an element of the charged offense or provides necessary context for understanding the offense. Such evidence is admissible not because it fits within a Rule 404(b) exception, but because it is not evidence of "other acts" at all—it is evidence of the very conduct for which the defendant is on trial.

The line between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence can be subtle. Courts generally treat evidence as intrinsic when it forms an integral part of the factual narrative of the charged offense. For example, evidence that completes the story of the crime, explains the sequence of events, or establishes an essential element of the offense typically qualifies as intrinsic. By contrast, evidence of separate, distinct acts that are not part of the same transaction or course of conduct generally falls within Rule 404(b)'s prohibition. The determination turns on whether the evidence describes conduct that is part of the same event or course of conduct as the charged offense, or whether it describes truly separate acts.

## Prior Possession of the Same Firearm as Proof of Ownership or Control

In a felon-in-possession prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm charged in the indictment. Possession is the gravamen of the offense. This creates a unique evidentiary landscape: evidence that the defendant possessed the same firearm on occasions before the charged offense date can be directly relevant to proving possession on the charged date, not as character evidence, but as substantive proof of an element of the offense.

The logic is straightforward. If the defendant possessed a particular firearm on multiple occasions before the charged date, that evidence tends to establish that the defendant had ownership or control over that specific weapon. This is not propensity reasoning—it is not an inference from "he possessed guns in the past" to "he possessed a gun on this occasion." Rather, it is direct evidence that the defendant had an ongoing relationship with the specific firearm at issue. The evidence helps the jury answer the central question: was this the defendant's gun? Prior possession of the same weapon provides affirmative evidence that the defendant owned or controlled it.

## The Battle Precedent: United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014)

The Eighth Circuit's analysis in *Cutbank* relied directly on its prior precedent in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014). In *Battle*, the court articulated the governing principle: "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." This statement captures the essential distinction between admissible evidence of prior possession of the *same* firearm and inadmissible evidence of prior possession of *other* firearms.

The *Battle* court recognized that when the evidence concerns the same weapon charged in the indictment, it serves a legitimate evidentiary purpose: it tends to prove that the defendant possessed that weapon on the charged date. The evidence is offered not to show that the defendant has a general propensity to possess firearms, but to show that this specific firearm was in his possession. This reasoning distinguishes cases involving the same weapon from those involving different weapons or other bad acts, which would raise the specter of forbidden propensity reasoning. *Battle* thus established a bright-line principle in the Eighth Circuit: testimony about prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic evidence admissible to prove the possession element of a § 922(g) charge.

## Pretrial Ruling on the Admissibility of Prior Possession Testimony

Cutbank's challenge to this evidence began before trial, when he filed a motion in limine to prevent D.F. and C.F. from testifying about their prior observations of him with the sawed-off rifle. This procedural posture is significant. A motion in limine allows parties to seek advance rulings on evidentiary issues before the jury is empaneled, promoting trial efficiency and preventing prejudicial evidence from being mentioned before a ruling can be obtained. The district court considered Cutbank's Rule 404(b) objection and rejected it, adopting the government's position that the testimony would constitute intrinsic evidence relevant to proving possession.

The district court's pretrial ruling was straightforward. The court agreed with the government that the evidence was "intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident." This reasoning tracked *Battle* directly. The court understood that the witnesses' testimony about seeing Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before November 19-20, 2020, was not evidence of "other acts" requiring Rule 404(b) analysis—it was evidence of the same act of possession that formed the basis of the charge. By ruling before trial, the district court provided clarity for both parties and allowed the trial to proceed without interruption on this point.

## Trial Testimony from D.F. and C.F. Regarding Prior Observations of the Firearm

At trial, the contested testimony unfolded as the district court had permitted. D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the November 19-20 incident. C.F. similarly testified that she had observed the gun "once or twice" prior to that night. This testimony provided the jury with evidence from which it could infer that Cutbank had an established pattern of possession of this specific weapon. The witnesses' testimony was consistent with the government's theory that the rifle found along the path between D.F.'s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself was the same rifle Cutbank had possessed on prior occasions.

Cutbank renewed his objection to this testimony during trial, but the district court overruled the objection and adhered to its pretrial ruling. On appeal, Cutbank cited no authority challenging the court's determination that this evidence was intrinsic and admissible under *Battle*. The Eighth Circuit noted this failure and affirmed the trial court's judgment, reiterating that prior possession of the same firearm is admissible to prove later possession of that weapon. The panel explicitly cited *Battle* and applied its reasoning to uphold the admission of both witnesses' testimony.

## Abuse of Discretion Standard for Evidentiary Rulings

The standard of review applied by the Eighth Circuit is critical to understanding why Cutbank's challenge failed. Federal courts of appeal review district court evidentiary rulings for "abuse of discretion." This highly deferential standard reflects the principle that trial judges are best positioned to make real-time decisions about the admissibility of evidence. Under this standard, an appellate court will not reverse a district court's evidentiary ruling unless the court's decision was clearly unreasonable, arbitrary, or based on an erroneous view of the law.

The abuse of discretion standard creates a substantial hurdle for defendants challenging evidentiary rulings on appeal. The appellate court does not ask whether it would have ruled the same way, but only whether the district court's ruling fell within the range of reasonable options available to the trial judge. In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit's review was straightforward: the district court applied *Battle* correctly, and its ruling that the prior possession testimony was intrinsic evidence fell well within the bounds of its discretion. Moreover, the court noted that even if there had been error, the error would have been harmless given the substantial evidence of Cutbank's guilt, including DNA evidence matching Cutbank on the recovered rifle. Thus, both the substantive legal analysis and the deferential standard of review converged to affirm the conviction.
