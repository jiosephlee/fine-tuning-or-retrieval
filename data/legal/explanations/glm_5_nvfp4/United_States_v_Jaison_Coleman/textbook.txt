Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Factual Foundation: The Domestic Violence Call and Police Encounter

## The 911 Call and Officer Kizer's Arrival at the Coleman Residence

The events giving rise to this case began in the early morning hours of April 20, 2023, when the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department received a 911 call reporting domestic violence. The caller—Lisa Coleman's stepdaughter—reported that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill Lisa and was still inside the family home. Such early morning domestic violence calls, the court notes at the outset, represent "one of the most difficult responsibilities of police officers," setting the stage for the tense encounter that would follow.

Officer Jamie Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence just after 4:00 a.m. The time of day is legally significant: courts recognize that nighttime encounters in domestic violence contexts often involve heightened tensions and require officers to make rapid assessments under uncertain conditions. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa Coleman responded by stepping onto the porch, initiating what would become a multi-stage interaction captured on body camera footage.

## Initial Interactions: Lisa's Hesitation About Her Children's Safety

From the moment Lisa stepped outside, Officer Kizer's primary concern was the welfare of the children inside the home. He asked whether her kids were okay, but Lisa's responses were marked by hesitation and equivocation. She first stated only that the children were in her bedroom. When pressed again whether they would be okay, Lisa waffled, saying she "hope[d] so." This pattern of evasive answers—an initial non-answer followed by an expression of hope rather than assurance—naturally heightened Officer Kizer's concerns.

The court's recitation of these facts demonstrates how an officer's reasonable suspicion can develop through a series of ambiguous responses. Each exchange provided additional information that shaped Officer Kizer's approach. When asked where her husband was located inside the home, Lisa responded that she did not know—a seemingly improbable answer that further suggested something was amiss.

## Lisa's Initial Refusal When Asked to Enter: 'I'd Prefer You Not'

A critical moment in the encounter came when Officer Kizer directly asked Lisa if he could enter the home. Lisa, who had just turned back toward the door, paused before responding: "um ... I'd prefer you not." This response is legally significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Lisa understood she had the authority to grant or deny entry—she was capable of asserting her Fourth Amendment rights. Second, it shows that Officer Kizer's initial request was not coercive: Lisa felt able to refuse.

The court emphasizes that Lisa was not in custody at this point, and the entire conversation had lasted only a few minutes. After Lisa's refusal, she told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to get her shoes. When she did not promptly return, Officer Kizer grew concerned, cracked the door, and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. Approximately one minute later, Lisa returned to the doorway, and the conversation resumed.

## The Pivotal Exchange: Officer Kizer's Statement of Need and Lisa's 'Okay'

The encounter reached its decisive moment when Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was "more calm right now" and whether the children were okay. Lisa again hesitated: "I ... I ... I don't ... They're ... They're okay right this second." Officer Kizer then made the statement that would become central to the legal dispute: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that."

Lisa responded with a single word: "Okay."

This exchange lies at the heart of the Fourth Amendment consent analysis. The government would argue that Lisa's "Okay" constituted voluntary consent to enter; Coleman would argue that Officer Kizer's use of the word "need"—particularly combined with his statement about "sensing" the need to enter—made the request coercive, rendering any apparent consent involuntary.

## Lisa's Active Facilitation: Providing Information About Children, Dogs, and Coleman's Location

The court's factual analysis looks beyond the single word "Okay" to examine Lisa's subsequent conduct. Immediately after her verbal assent, Lisa provided Officer Kizer with specific, practical information facilitating the officers' entry. She told him the children were in her bedroom. When Officer Kizer asked whether the dogs would bite, Lisa assured him the dogs would only jump, not bite. She indicated she believed Coleman was in the back bedroom.

Most significantly, Lisa then "opened the door for Officer Kizer and his colleagues and facilitated their entry by brushing her dogs away." She led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were located. This pattern of active assistance—opening the door, managing the dogs, guiding officers through the home—provided the district court with substantial factual support for its finding that Lisa had voluntarily consented to entry.

## Events Following Entry: Locating Coleman, Speaking with Children, and Discovery of Firearms

Once inside, the officers' stated purpose was to check on the children's welfare and ensure Coleman posed no danger. They did not initially find Coleman in the bedroom Lisa had indicated. When asked where he was, Lisa again said she did not know. A quick canvas of the home revealed Coleman in a separate bedroom. Officer Kizer conducted a protective pat-down, which Coleman permitted.

The critical evidence emerged from conversations with the two children. They reported that Lisa and Coleman had been fighting, with Coleman being "loud, aggressive, and mean." Most damning, the children stated that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her and set the house on fire. The children confirmed they had seen the gun both that night and previously, though they did not know its current location.

Based on this information, officers sought and secured a warrant to search the home. The search yielded several firearms, leading to Coleman's federal indictment for possessing a firearm as a convicted felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The legal question on appeal was whether Lisa's consent—disputed from the moment Officer Kizer said "need"—validly authorized the initial entry that set this chain of events in motion.

# Procedural History: From Indictment to Appeal

## Federal Indictment Charging Coleman with Violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)

The procedural journey of United States v. Coleman began with a federal indictment. After officers searched the Coleman home and recovered several firearms, the government charged Jaison Coleman with one count of possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). This statute makes it a federal crime for any person who has been convicted of a felony to possess a firearm. The offense is serious, carrying significant potential penalties, and it represents one of the most commonly charged federal gun crimes.

It is important to understand how the evidence led to this charge. The firearms were not discovered during the initial entry; rather, the officers' entry led to information—particularly from the children—that prompted them to seek a search warrant. The warrant-authorized search then turned up the guns that formed the basis of the indictment. Coleman's challenge on appeal does not contest the validity of the warrant itself, but rather targets the initial entry that set the evidentiary chain in motion.

## Coleman's Motion to Suppress the Firearms Recovered from the Home

Once indicted, Coleman responded by filing a motion to suppress. A motion to suppress is a pretrial request that the court exclude evidence from trial on the grounds that it was obtained in violation of the defendant's constitutional rights. Here, Coleman argued that the firearms should be suppressed because they were fruits of an unlawful entry. The Fourth Amendment generally requires police to obtain a warrant before entering a home, and evidence obtained through a warrantless entry that violates the Fourth Amendment is typically subject to exclusion.

Coleman's theory was straightforward: if Lisa never consented to the officers' entry, then the entry violated the Fourth Amendment, and any evidence discovered as a result—including the children's statements that led to the warrant, and ultimately the firearms themselves—should be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree." The motion thus put Lisa's consent at the center of the case. Importantly, Coleman did not need to be the one who consented; as the court explains, a third party with common authority over the premises can give valid consent, and a defendant can challenge the validity of that consent.

## The Magistrate Judge's Report Finding Valid Consent and Recommending Denial

The district court referred the motion to a magistrate judge for an evidentiary hearing. Federal magistrate judges are judicial officers who assist district judges with various matters, including conducting hearings and issuing reports and recommendations on dispositive motions. After holding a hearing, the magistrate judge issued a report finding that Lisa had in fact consented to the officers' entry and recommending that the district court deny Coleman's motion to suppress.

This finding turned on the magistrate judge's assessment of the facts, particularly the body camera footage that captured the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa. The magistrate judge concluded that Lisa's "Okay" in response to Officer Kizer's statement that he "almost need[ed] to come inside" constituted voluntary consent. This factual finding would prove central to the appeal, as appellate courts give substantial deference to lower court factual determinations.

## The District Court's Adoption of the Magistrate's Findings and Rejection of Scope Arguments

The district court reviewed the magistrate judge's report and adopted its findings. In doing so, the court concluded that Lisa consented to the officers' entry. The district court went further, separately addressing Coleman's argument that even if consent were given, the officers exceeded the scope of that consent once inside. The court rejected this argument as well, finding that the officers' actions—checking on the children's welfare, locating Coleman, and asking basic questions—remained within the bounds of what a reasonable person would have understood Lisa's consent to permit.

This two-part ruling matters because Coleman raised both arguments on appeal. The district court's rejection of both the consent validity challenge and the scope challenge meant that the firearms would not be suppressed. With the motion to suppress denied, the path was cleared for the case to proceed toward trial.

## The Conditional Guilty Plea Preserving the Right to Appeal the Suppression Ruling

Rather than proceed to trial, Coleman entered a conditional guilty plea. A conditional guilty plea allows a defendant to plead guilty while expressly reserving the right to appeal a specified pretrial ruling—typically the denial of a motion to suppress. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2) authorizes this procedure, which serves an important practical function: it allows defendants to challenge constitutional violations without forcing them to roll the dice at trial and face potentially harsher sentences if convicted.

Coleman's conditional plea meant he could proceed directly to the Seventh Circuit to challenge the district court's suppression ruling, even though he had pleaded guilty. On appeal, Coleman argued that Lisa's consent was not voluntary and that the officers exceeded the scope of whatever consent she gave. The court of appeals reviews such challenges under a deferential "clear error" standard for factual findings, meaning the appellate court will not reverse simply because it might have weighed the evidence differently—it must find that the district court made a clear mistake. This standard of review shapes how the appellate court approaches the case, as we see in the opinion's careful deference to the district court's factual assessments.

# Governing Law: The Fourth Amendment Consent Framework

## Consent as a Recognized Exception to the Fourth Amendment Warrant Requirement

The Fourth Amendment generally requires police to obtain a warrant before searching a home. However, the Supreme Court has recognized several exceptions to this warrant requirement, and consent is among the most significant. As the Seventh Circuit explains in this opinion, "consent is an exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement for searching a home." This principle is deeply embedded in Fourth Amendment jurisprudence, and both parties in Coleman's appeal accepted it as the starting point for their arguments.

The consent exception operates on a straightforward premise: when a person voluntarily agrees to a search, the Fourth Amendment's warrant and probable cause requirements are satisfied. The government bears the burden of proving that valid consent was given. In this case, the government argued that Lisa Coleman's "Okay" in response to Officer Kizer's statement about needing to come inside constituted valid consent, thereby eliminating any Fourth Amendment violation. If the consent was valid, the firearms discovered in the subsequent search could properly be used as evidence against Jaison Coleman.

## Third-Party Consent Under United States v. Matlock

A unique feature of consent doctrine is that the person giving consent need not be the defendant. The opinion cites *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974), the foundational Supreme Court case establishing that "a third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy." This rule recognizes that individuals who share control over property can authorize searches of that property.

In *Coleman*, this principle was central to the dispute. Lisa Coleman, not Jaison Coleman, was the one who allegedly consented to the officers' entry. Yet Jaison Coleman sought to challenge the validity of that consent on appeal. The Seventh Circuit confirmed that he had standing to do so: "the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party." This aspect of *Matlock* means that defendants are not foreclosed from raising Fourth Amendment challenges simply because a co-occupant granted permission. The defendant retains the right to argue that the third party's consent was invalid, and if successful, any evidence discovered as a result may be suppressed.

## The "Freely and Voluntarily" Standard from Bumper v. North Carolina

Not all consent satisfies the Fourth Amendment. For consent to be valid, it must be "freely and voluntarily" given. The opinion cites *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968), the Supreme Court case that articulated this standard. *Bumper* established that consent obtained through coercion or duress does not validate a warrantless search. The voluntariness inquiry protects individuals from being pressured into waiving their constitutional rights.

In *Coleman*, the central factual dispute was whether Lisa's consent was truly voluntary. Jaison Coleman argued that Officer Kizer's statement—"I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside"—was coercive, particularly given the early hour and the domestic violence context. The government countered that Lisa's response was freely given, pointing to her ability to decline the initial request and the officer's calm demeanor. The district court found that Lisa's consent was voluntary, and the Seventh Circuit reviewed that finding for clear error.

## Totality of Circumstances Factors

Courts do not assess voluntariness based on any single factor in isolation. Instead, they consider the "totality of the circumstances." The opinion lists several factors drawn from Seventh Circuit precedent, including *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*: "(1) the person's age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent."

These factors guide the court's analysis but are not exhaustive. The court considers the overall situation, including the dynamics between the officer and the individual, the setting, and any indicia of pressure or coercion. In *Coleman*, several factors cut against a finding of coercion: Lisa was not in custody, the encounter lasted only a few minutes, and Officer Kizer used no physical coercion. He asked twice for permission to enter, but repeated requests alone do not establish coercion, particularly when the encounter was brief.

Importantly, the court noted that Officer Kizer did not explicitly advise Lisa that she could refuse consent. While informing a person of the right to refuse is a factor weighing in favor of voluntariness, the absence of such a warning does not automatically render consent involuntary. The court found that Officer Kizer's request for permission itself implied that Lisa had a choice, and her initial refusal demonstrated she understood she could say no.

## Scope of Consent and the Objective Reasonableness Inquiry

Even when consent is valid, it has limits. The court must determine whether the police remained within the "scope of consent." The opinion cites *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991), which established that the proper inquiry is "objective reasonableness"—what a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent.

In *Coleman*, Officer Kizer stated his purpose: to check on the welfare of the children inside. The Seventh Circuit found that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent by scanning the home, locating everyone present, and asking basic questions. These actions were directly related to the stated purpose of confirming the children's safety. The information the officers gathered during this welfare check—including the children's statements about seeing a gun—formed the basis for the subsequent search warrant. The court concluded that the officers did not exceed the scope of consent because their actions were consistent with what a reasonable person would have understood Lisa to be authorizing.

## Clear Error Standard of Review for Consent Findings on Appeal

The standard of review significantly shapes how appellate courts evaluate consent determinations. The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court's finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for "clear error." This is a deferential standard. As the opinion explains, the appellate court's role is "more limited."

Under the clear error standard, the appellate court does not ask whether it would have reached the same conclusion as the district court. Rather, it asks whether the district court's finding was clearly erroneous—whether the reviewing court is left with a "definite and firm conviction" that a mistake was made. This deference reflects the district court's superior position to assess witness credibility and evaluate the nuances of the encounter.

In *Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit viewed the body camera footage and concluded there was no clear error. The court highlighted two observations from the video: Officer Kizer's concerned (not coercive) tone, and Lisa's demonstrated ability to deny consent when she initially refused entry. While Coleman argued that the word "need" made Officer Kizer's request coercive, the court found that the district court "could reasonably" reject that argument based on the totality of circumstances. The court acknowledged that Coleman's argument was "plenty fair," but under the clear error standard, reasonableness was sufficient to affirm. The district court's findings stood because they were supported by the record, not because they were the only permissible conclusions.

# The Court's Voluntary Consent Analysis

## The district court's finding of voluntary consent and appellate deference

The Seventh Circuit's analysis begins with a fundamental principle of appellate review: the court was not asked to make an independent determination about whether Lisa consented to the officers' entry, but rather to review the district court's finding for clear error. This standard of review shapes everything that follows. Under the clear error standard, the appellate court does not substitute its own judgment for that of the trial court. Instead, it asks only whether the district court's finding was reasonable given the evidence. The court frames its role explicitly: "As a court of review, our role is more limited."

This deferential standard reflects the practical reality that district courts are better positioned to assess witness credibility and weigh evidence. The district court here had reviewed the body camera footage and adopted a magistrate judge's findings that Lisa consented to the officers' entry. On appeal, Coleman bore the burden of showing that these findings were clearly erroneous—meaning the Seventh Circuit would affirm unless the record compelled a contrary conclusion. The court signals early that it views the video evidence as dispositive, citing *United States v. Norville* for the proposition that video evidence can "evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist."

## Officer Kizer's concerned tone versus coercive conduct

The court's analysis of voluntariness centers significantly on Officer Kizer's demeanor throughout the encounter. The opinion emphasizes that "Officer Kizer's tone and demeanor throughout was that of someone concerned, not someone coercing." This observation matters because coercive police conduct is a key factor in determining whether consent was truly voluntary or instead the product of official pressure. The court draws a careful distinction between an officer expressing appropriate concern for child safety and an officer who oversteps by exerting undue pressure on a resident.

The court notes that Officer Kizer "spoke calmly and respectfully to Lisa" and that his request for permission to enter "implied" that she had the right to refuse. Importantly, the opinion explicitly states that "at no point was Lisa in custody," which eliminates one potential source of coercion. The court also observes that the entire conversation lasted only a few minutes, weighing against any claim of prolonged pressure. The absence of physical coercion and the brief duration of the encounter are cited as factors supporting voluntariness. While acknowledging that Lisa may have felt "some degree of pressure," the court draws a critical line: feeling pressure from circumstances is not the same as being subjected to coercion by police conduct. This distinction preserves the meaningfulness of consent in situations that are inherently stressful—like a domestic violence call—without allowing the stress of the situation itself to invalidate otherwise valid consent.

## Lisa's demonstrated capacity to refuse consent through her initial denial

Perhaps the most significant factual finding in the court's voluntariness analysis is Lisa's initial refusal to consent. When Officer Kizer first asked if he could enter, Lisa responded, "um … I'd prefer you not." The court treats this as powerful evidence that Lisa both understood her right to refuse and was capable of exercising it. The opinion states directly: "Lisa showed that she had the ability to deny consent to entry, as she did just that in response to Officer Kizer's initial request."

This factual detail serves multiple analytical functions. First, it rebuts any suggestion that Lisa was so intimidated or overwhelmed that she could not assert her rights. Second, it demonstrates that the encounter was not a one-sided exercise of police authority; Lisa actively participated in the conversation and made her preferences known. Third, it provides context for interpreting Lisa's later "Okay" as a genuine change of mind rather than mere submission to authority. The court reasons that because Lisa had already demonstrated her willingness to say no, her subsequent assent can more readily be understood as a voluntary choice. The opinion notes that Lisa "chose to let Officer Kizer enter after he asked again, with the second request being unaccompanied by any coercion." This progression—from refusal to consent—supports the court's conclusion that Lisa's final decision was her own.

## Coleman's argument: the significance of the word 'need' in Officer Kizer's statement

Coleman's central challenge on appeal focused on the specific language Officer Kizer used when he made his second request to enter: "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay]." Coleman argued that the word "need" transformed the officer's statement from a request into a command, thereby rendering any subsequent consent involuntary. This argument has logical force: if an officer frames entry as something they "need" to do, a reasonable person might understand the statement as an assertion of authority rather than a genuine request for permission.

The court acknowledges Coleman's position as a "plenty fair argument," conceding that the language could be read as making the interaction more coercive. This acknowledgment demonstrates that the court did not dismiss Coleman's argument lightly. The word "need" does carry an implication of necessity that could, in other circumstances, undermine the voluntariness of consent. Courts have recognized that consent obtained through assertions of lawful authority is not truly voluntary; an officer's statement that they "need" to enter could communicate that entry is inevitable regardless of the resident's wishes. Coleman's argument asked the court to focus closely on this single word and find that it crossed the line from request to demand.

## The court's rejection of a narrow focus in favor of totality analysis

The court ultimately rejects Coleman's argument, but not by disputing his reading of the word "need" in isolation. Instead, the court applies the governing legal standard—totality of the circumstances—and concludes that "Coleman's focus is too narrow." The voluntariness inquiry requires examining all relevant factors together, not scrutinizing individual words detached from their context. The court cites *United States v. Hicks* for the proposition that even an officer's statement with some urgency or intensity does not necessarily amount to coercion when viewed in context.

Applying this framework, the court finds that Officer Kizer "asked for permission to enter by expressing concern for the safety of Lisa's children and relayed that concern with some urgency and intensity." The word "need" must be understood in light of the officer's overall purpose—checking on children's welfare in a potential domestic violence situation—and his consistent demeanor throughout the encounter. The statement expressed concern with "some urgency," which the court treats as natural and appropriate given the circumstances, but it did not overstep into coercion. The district court's finding that Officer Kizer "did so without overstepping and coercing her consent" was therefore not clearly erroneous.

## Lisa's conduct following her 'Okay' as confirmation of consent

The court's analysis concludes by examining Lisa's behavior after she said "Okay" to Officer Kizer's request. Her subsequent words and actions provide additional support for the district court's finding of voluntary consent. Immediately after her assent, Lisa provided the officers with practical information: she told them where the children were located inside the home and reassured them about her dogs, explaining that the dogs "were friendly and would not bite." The court views this as evidence that "Lisa heard Officer Kizer's request" as exactly what it purported to be—a request—and that she understood herself as granting permission.

Lisa's conduct went beyond mere verbal acquiescence. The opinion notes that she "opened the door for Officer Kizer and his colleagues and facilitated their entry by brushing her dogs away." She then led the officers through the home and pointed out where the children were located. These affirmative acts of assistance confirm that Lisa was not merely tolerating an intrusion she believed was inevitable, but actively cooperating with the officers' welfare check. The court treats this post-consent conduct as confirmatory evidence: "All of this leads us to affirm the district court's finding that Lisa consented to the police entering her home." By examining Lisa's words and actions after the critical exchange, the court provides a complete picture of the encounter and bolsters its conclusion that consent was both given and genuine.

# Scope of Consent, Alternative Arguments, and Resolution

## Objective Reasonableness of Scope: What Lisa Authorized by Consenting

Even when consent to enter a home is validly given, the Fourth Amendment requires that police respect the boundaries of that consent. The Supreme Court has established that the scope of consent is measured by "objective reasonableness." Under this standard, courts ask: what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent? The inquiry is not subjective—courts do not ask what the particular individual actually intended or thought. Instead, they examine what a reasonable person in the same situation would have understood the consent to permit.

In this case, the court had to determine whether Officer Kizer and his colleagues exceeded the scope of Lisa's consent once they were inside the Coleman home. The district court found they did not, and the Seventh Circuit reviewed that finding for clear error—a deferential standard that requires the appellate court to accept the lower court's factual determinations unless they are unsupported by the record.

## The Welfare Check Purpose: Locating Children and Assessing Danger from Coleman

The court's analysis of the scope issue was straightforward and anchored in the context of Lisa's consent. Recall that Officer Kizer had stated his concern in fairly specific terms: "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay]." Lisa's consent, therefore, was properly understood as authorization to enter the home for the limited purpose of conducting a welfare check on the children. The court emphasized that this purpose carried with it certain necessary implications: the officers needed to scan the home, locate everyone present, and ask basic questions to confirm that no one was in danger.

This included locating Coleman, who Lisa indicated was "in the back bedroom." The officers' quick canvas of the home to find him, followed by a brief discussion and a protective pat-down, fell within what a reasonable person would have understood Lisa to be permitting. The court noted that the officers were acting on their stated purpose—to confirm the children's safety—and assessing whether Coleman presented any danger to them or anyone else in the residence. The court found no infirmity in the district court's determination that the officers remained within the bounds of Lisa's consent throughout their time in the home.

## Information Gathering from Children That Led to the Search Warrant

The welfare check quickly yielded information that transformed the nature of the police investigation. After locating Coleman, the officers spoke with the two children, who provided disturbing accounts of the evening's events. The children reported that Coleman had been "loud, aggressive, and mean" during a fight with Lisa. More significantly, they stated that Coleman had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her and set the house on fire. The children confirmed that they had seen the gun, both that night and on prior occasions, though they did not know its current location.

This information was critical because it provided the factual basis for the officers to seek and obtain a search warrant. The warrant, in turn, authorized the search that recovered several firearms from the Coleman home. The subsequent federal indictment charged Coleman with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The entire chain of evidence depended on the initial entry being lawful—either because Lisa consented or because some other Fourth Amendment exception applied.

## The Government's Alternative Theories Not Reached: Exigent Circumstances and Inevitable Discovery

The government had argued alternative theories to support the legality of the officers' entry: exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. These are recognized exceptions to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement, and the government urged the court to affirm Coleman's conviction on either basis if it rejected the consent theory.

The court declined to address these alternative arguments. Because the court affirmed the district court's finding that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers' entry, and because it likewise found that the officers acted within the scope of that consent, there was no need to consider whether exigent circumstances existed or whether the inevitable discovery doctrine would have independently validated the search. This approach reflects a common judicial practice: courts generally resolve cases on the narrowest available ground and avoid reaching constitutional questions or alternative theories when the primary basis for decision is sufficient.

It is worth noting what these alternative theories would have required. The exigent circumstances doctrine permits warrantless entry when police have an objectively reasonable basis for believing that an occupant is in imminent danger. The inevitable discovery doctrine allows evidence obtained through an unlawful search to be admitted if the government can prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the evidence would have been discovered lawfully anyway. The court's decision not to reach these theories should not be read as endorsing or rejecting them—it simply means they were unnecessary to the disposition.

## The Affirmance and the Court's Closing Observations on Police Conduct in Domestic Violence Cases

The court concluded by affirming Coleman's conviction. The Seventh Circuit found no clear error in the district court's determination that Lisa consented to the officers' entry, nor in the finding that the officers respected the scope of that consent. Having reviewed the body camera footage, the appellate court agreed with the district court's assessment of the interaction between Officer Kizer and Lisa.

The opinion closed with observations that went beyond the technical legal analysis. The court noted that responding to 911 calls involving domestic violence is "one of the most difficult responsibilities of police officers." It described the circumstances here—arriving at 4:00 a.m. to a report of a death threat, facing uncertainty about the safety of children inside—as "delicate and difficult." In the court's view, Officer Kizer and his colleagues exhibited "sound and balanced decision making" throughout the encounter. This closing observation serves as a reminder that Fourth Amendment jurisprudence operates in real-world contexts where officers must make quick judgments under pressure. The court's approval of the officers' conduct in this case underscores that consent determinations are not made in the abstract, but with an appreciation for the challenges inherent in domestic violence investigations.
