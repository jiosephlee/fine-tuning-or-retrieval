Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # Factual Foundation: The Domestic Violence Call and Police Encounter

## The 911 Call and Officer Kizer's Arrival at the Coleman Residence

The events giving rise to this case began in the early morning hours of April 20, 2023, when the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department received a 911 call reporting domestic violence. The caller—Lisa Coleman's stepdaughter—reported that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill Lisa and was still inside the family home. Such early morning domestic violence calls, the court notes at the outset, represent "one of the most difficult responsibilities of police officers," setting the stage for the tense encounter that would follow.

Officer Jamie Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence just after 4:00 a.m. The time of day is legally significant: courts recognize that nighttime encounters in domestic violence contexts often involve heightened tensions and require officers to make rapid assessments under uncertain conditions. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa Coleman responded by stepping onto the porch, initiating what would become a multi-stage interaction captured on body camera footage.

## Initial Interactions: Lisa's Hesitation About Her Children's Safety

From the moment Lisa stepped outside, Officer Kizer's primary concern was the welfare of the children inside the home. He asked whether her kids were okay, but Lisa's responses were marked by hesitation and equivocation. She first stated only that the children were in her bedroom. When pressed again whether they would be okay, Lisa waffled, saying she "hope[d] so." This pattern of evasive answers—an initial non-answer followed by an expression of hope rather than assurance—naturally heightened Officer Kizer's concerns.

The court's recitation of these facts demonstrates how an officer's reasonable suspicion can develop through a series of ambiguous responses. Each exchange provided additional information that shaped Officer Kizer's approach. When asked where her husband was located inside the home, Lisa responded that she did not know—a seemingly improbable answer that further suggested something was amiss.

## Lisa's Initial Refusal When Asked to Enter: 'I'd Prefer You Not'

A critical moment in the encounter came when Officer Kizer directly asked Lisa if he could enter the home. Lisa, who had just turned back toward the door, paused before responding: "um ... I'd prefer you not." This response is legally significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Lisa understood she had the authority to grant or deny entry—she was capable of asserting her Fourth Amendment rights. Second, it shows that Officer Kizer's initial request was not coercive: Lisa felt able to refuse.

The court emphasizes that Lisa was not in custody at this point, and the entire conversation had lasted only a few minutes. After Lisa's refusal, she told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to get her shoes. When she did not promptly return, Officer Kizer grew concerned, cracked the door, and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. Approximately one minute later, Lisa returned to the doorway, and the conversation resumed.

## The Pivotal Exchange: Officer Kizer's Statement of Need and Lisa's 'Okay'

The encounter reached its decisive moment when Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was "more calm right now" and whether the children were okay. Lisa again hesitated: "I ... I ... I don't ... They're ... They're okay right this second." Officer Kizer then made the statement that would become central to the legal dispute: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that."

Lisa responded with a single word: "Okay."

This exchange lies at the heart of the Fourth Amendment consent analysis. The government would argue that Lisa's "Okay" constituted voluntary consent to enter; Coleman would argue that Officer Kizer's use of the word "need"—particularly combined with his statement about "sensing" the need to enter—made the request coercive, rendering any apparent consent involuntary.

## Lisa's Active Facilitation: Providing Information About Children, Dogs, and Coleman's Location

The court's factual analysis looks beyond the single word "Okay" to examine Lisa's subsequent conduct. Immediately after her verbal assent, Lisa provided Officer Kizer with specific, practical information facilitating the officers' entry. She told him the children were in her bedroom. When Officer Kizer asked whether the dogs would bite, Lisa assured him the dogs would only jump, not bite. She indicated she believed Coleman was in the back bedroom.

Most significantly, Lisa then "opened the door for Officer Kizer and his colleagues and facilitated their entry by brushing her dogs away." She led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were located. This pattern of active assistance—opening the door, managing the dogs, guiding officers through the home—provided the district court with substantial factual support for its finding that Lisa had voluntarily consented to entry.

## Events Following Entry: Locating Coleman, Speaking with Children, and Discovery of Firearms

Once inside, the officers' stated purpose was to check on the children's welfare and ensure Coleman posed no danger. They did not initially find Coleman in the bedroom Lisa had indicated. When asked where he was, Lisa again said she did not know. A quick canvas of the home revealed Coleman in a separate bedroom. Officer Kizer conducted a protective pat-down, which Coleman permitted.

The critical evidence emerged from conversations with the two children. They reported that Lisa and Coleman had been fighting, with Coleman being "loud, aggressive, and mean." Most damning, the children stated that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her and set the house on fire. The children confirmed they had seen the gun both that night and previously, though they did not know its current location.

Based on this information, officers sought and secured a warrant to search the home. The search yielded several firearms, leading to Coleman's federal indictment for possessing a firearm as a convicted felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The legal question on appeal was whether Lisa's consent—disputed from the moment Officer Kizer said "need"—validly authorized the initial entry that set this chain of events in motion.
