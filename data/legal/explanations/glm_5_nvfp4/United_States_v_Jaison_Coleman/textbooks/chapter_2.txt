Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 2: # Procedural History: From Indictment to Appeal

## Federal Indictment Charging Coleman with Violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)

The procedural journey of United States v. Coleman began with a federal indictment. After officers searched the Coleman home and recovered several firearms, the government charged Jaison Coleman with one count of possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). This statute makes it a federal crime for any person who has been convicted of a felony to possess a firearm. The offense is serious, carrying significant potential penalties, and it represents one of the most commonly charged federal gun crimes.

It is important to understand how the evidence led to this charge. The firearms were not discovered during the initial entry; rather, the officers' entry led to information—particularly from the children—that prompted them to seek a search warrant. The warrant-authorized search then turned up the guns that formed the basis of the indictment. Coleman's challenge on appeal does not contest the validity of the warrant itself, but rather targets the initial entry that set the evidentiary chain in motion.

## Coleman's Motion to Suppress the Firearms Recovered from the Home

Once indicted, Coleman responded by filing a motion to suppress. A motion to suppress is a pretrial request that the court exclude evidence from trial on the grounds that it was obtained in violation of the defendant's constitutional rights. Here, Coleman argued that the firearms should be suppressed because they were fruits of an unlawful entry. The Fourth Amendment generally requires police to obtain a warrant before entering a home, and evidence obtained through a warrantless entry that violates the Fourth Amendment is typically subject to exclusion.

Coleman's theory was straightforward: if Lisa never consented to the officers' entry, then the entry violated the Fourth Amendment, and any evidence discovered as a result—including the children's statements that led to the warrant, and ultimately the firearms themselves—should be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree." The motion thus put Lisa's consent at the center of the case. Importantly, Coleman did not need to be the one who consented; as the court explains, a third party with common authority over the premises can give valid consent, and a defendant can challenge the validity of that consent.

## The Magistrate Judge's Report Finding Valid Consent and Recommending Denial

The district court referred the motion to a magistrate judge for an evidentiary hearing. Federal magistrate judges are judicial officers who assist district judges with various matters, including conducting hearings and issuing reports and recommendations on dispositive motions. After holding a hearing, the magistrate judge issued a report finding that Lisa had in fact consented to the officers' entry and recommending that the district court deny Coleman's motion to suppress.

This finding turned on the magistrate judge's assessment of the facts, particularly the body camera footage that captured the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa. The magistrate judge concluded that Lisa's "Okay" in response to Officer Kizer's statement that he "almost need[ed] to come inside" constituted voluntary consent. This factual finding would prove central to the appeal, as appellate courts give substantial deference to lower court factual determinations.

## The District Court's Adoption of the Magistrate's Findings and Rejection of Scope Arguments

The district court reviewed the magistrate judge's report and adopted its findings. In doing so, the court concluded that Lisa consented to the officers' entry. The district court went further, separately addressing Coleman's argument that even if consent were given, the officers exceeded the scope of that consent once inside. The court rejected this argument as well, finding that the officers' actions—checking on the children's welfare, locating Coleman, and asking basic questions—remained within the bounds of what a reasonable person would have understood Lisa's consent to permit.

This two-part ruling matters because Coleman raised both arguments on appeal. The district court's rejection of both the consent validity challenge and the scope challenge meant that the firearms would not be suppressed. With the motion to suppress denied, the path was cleared for the case to proceed toward trial.

## The Conditional Guilty Plea Preserving the Right to Appeal the Suppression Ruling

Rather than proceed to trial, Coleman entered a conditional guilty plea. A conditional guilty plea allows a defendant to plead guilty while expressly reserving the right to appeal a specified pretrial ruling—typically the denial of a motion to suppress. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2) authorizes this procedure, which serves an important practical function: it allows defendants to challenge constitutional violations without forcing them to roll the dice at trial and face potentially harsher sentences if convicted.

Coleman's conditional plea meant he could proceed directly to the Seventh Circuit to challenge the district court's suppression ruling, even though he had pleaded guilty. On appeal, Coleman argued that Lisa's consent was not voluntary and that the officers exceeded the scope of whatever consent she gave. The court of appeals reviews such challenges under a deferential "clear error" standard for factual findings, meaning the appellate court will not reverse simply because it might have weighed the evidence differently—it must find that the district court made a clear mistake. This standard of review shapes how the appellate court approaches the case, as we see in the opinion's careful deference to the district court's factual assessments.
