Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 3: # Governing Law: The Fourth Amendment Consent Framework

## Consent as a Recognized Exception to the Fourth Amendment Warrant Requirement

The Fourth Amendment generally requires police to obtain a warrant before searching a home. However, the Supreme Court has recognized several exceptions to this warrant requirement, and consent is among the most significant. As the Seventh Circuit explains in this opinion, "consent is an exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement for searching a home." This principle is deeply embedded in Fourth Amendment jurisprudence, and both parties in Coleman's appeal accepted it as the starting point for their arguments.

The consent exception operates on a straightforward premise: when a person voluntarily agrees to a search, the Fourth Amendment's warrant and probable cause requirements are satisfied. The government bears the burden of proving that valid consent was given. In this case, the government argued that Lisa Coleman's "Okay" in response to Officer Kizer's statement about needing to come inside constituted valid consent, thereby eliminating any Fourth Amendment violation. If the consent was valid, the firearms discovered in the subsequent search could properly be used as evidence against Jaison Coleman.

## Third-Party Consent Under United States v. Matlock

A unique feature of consent doctrine is that the person giving consent need not be the defendant. The opinion cites *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974), the foundational Supreme Court case establishing that "a third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy." This rule recognizes that individuals who share control over property can authorize searches of that property.

In *Coleman*, this principle was central to the dispute. Lisa Coleman, not Jaison Coleman, was the one who allegedly consented to the officers' entry. Yet Jaison Coleman sought to challenge the validity of that consent on appeal. The Seventh Circuit confirmed that he had standing to do so: "the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party." This aspect of *Matlock* means that defendants are not foreclosed from raising Fourth Amendment challenges simply because a co-occupant granted permission. The defendant retains the right to argue that the third party's consent was invalid, and if successful, any evidence discovered as a result may be suppressed.

## The "Freely and Voluntarily" Standard from Bumper v. North Carolina

Not all consent satisfies the Fourth Amendment. For consent to be valid, it must be "freely and voluntarily" given. The opinion cites *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968), the Supreme Court case that articulated this standard. *Bumper* established that consent obtained through coercion or duress does not validate a warrantless search. The voluntariness inquiry protects individuals from being pressured into waiving their constitutional rights.

In *Coleman*, the central factual dispute was whether Lisa's consent was truly voluntary. Jaison Coleman argued that Officer Kizer's statement—"I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside"—was coercive, particularly given the early hour and the domestic violence context. The government countered that Lisa's response was freely given, pointing to her ability to decline the initial request and the officer's calm demeanor. The district court found that Lisa's consent was voluntary, and the Seventh Circuit reviewed that finding for clear error.

## Totality of Circumstances Factors

Courts do not assess voluntariness based on any single factor in isolation. Instead, they consider the "totality of the circumstances." The opinion lists several factors drawn from Seventh Circuit precedent, including *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*: "(1) the person's age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent."

These factors guide the court's analysis but are not exhaustive. The court considers the overall situation, including the dynamics between the officer and the individual, the setting, and any indicia of pressure or coercion. In *Coleman*, several factors cut against a finding of coercion: Lisa was not in custody, the encounter lasted only a few minutes, and Officer Kizer used no physical coercion. He asked twice for permission to enter, but repeated requests alone do not establish coercion, particularly when the encounter was brief.

Importantly, the court noted that Officer Kizer did not explicitly advise Lisa that she could refuse consent. While informing a person of the right to refuse is a factor weighing in favor of voluntariness, the absence of such a warning does not automatically render consent involuntary. The court found that Officer Kizer's request for permission itself implied that Lisa had a choice, and her initial refusal demonstrated she understood she could say no.

## Scope of Consent and the Objective Reasonableness Inquiry

Even when consent is valid, it has limits. The court must determine whether the police remained within the "scope of consent." The opinion cites *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991), which established that the proper inquiry is "objective reasonableness"—what a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent.

In *Coleman*, Officer Kizer stated his purpose: to check on the welfare of the children inside. The Seventh Circuit found that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent by scanning the home, locating everyone present, and asking basic questions. These actions were directly related to the stated purpose of confirming the children's safety. The information the officers gathered during this welfare check—including the children's statements about seeing a gun—formed the basis for the subsequent search warrant. The court concluded that the officers did not exceed the scope of consent because their actions were consistent with what a reasonable person would have understood Lisa to be authorizing.

## Clear Error Standard of Review for Consent Findings on Appeal

The standard of review significantly shapes how appellate courts evaluate consent determinations. The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court's finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for "clear error." This is a deferential standard. As the opinion explains, the appellate court's role is "more limited."

Under the clear error standard, the appellate court does not ask whether it would have reached the same conclusion as the district court. Rather, it asks whether the district court's finding was clearly erroneous—whether the reviewing court is left with a "definite and firm conviction" that a mistake was made. This deference reflects the district court's superior position to assess witness credibility and evaluate the nuances of the encounter.

In *Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit viewed the body camera footage and concluded there was no clear error. The court highlighted two observations from the video: Officer Kizer's concerned (not coercive) tone, and Lisa's demonstrated ability to deny consent when she initially refused entry. While Coleman argued that the word "need" made Officer Kizer's request coercive, the court found that the district court "could reasonably" reject that argument based on the totality of circumstances. The court acknowledged that Coleman's argument was "plenty fair," but under the clear error standard, reasonableness was sufficient to affirm. The district court's findings stood because they were supported by the record, not because they were the only permissible conclusions.
