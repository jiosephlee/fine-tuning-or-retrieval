Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside": Parsing the Line Between Concern and Coercion

Domestic violence calls are among the most volatile and unpredictable situations police face. They are also among the most likely to generate Fourth Amendment disputes, precisely because officers must make split-second decisions about entering homes where violence may be ongoing. The Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Jaison Coleman* offers a revealing look at how courts evaluate whether an officer's expression of concern crosses into coercion that invalidates consent.

The case arises from a 4:00 a.m. call to police in Marshfield, Wisconsin. A woman reported that her stepfather had threatened to kill her mother and was still inside the home. When Officer Jamie Kizer arrived and spoke with Lisa Coleman on the porch, the situation was fluid. Lisa's responses to questions about her children's safety were equivocal—she said she "hoped" they were okay. When Officer Kizer first asked directly if he could enter, Lisa paused and said, "um … I'd prefer you not."

That initial refusal is what makes this case significant. When an officer asks again and uses language that sounds less like a request and more like an assertion of authority, courts must decide whether the subsequent "okay" represents genuine consent or mere acquiescence to a claim of lawful authority—the very distinction the Supreme Court drew in *Bumper v. North Carolina*.

## The Critical Exchange

After Lisa's initial refusal, circumstances changed. Lisa went inside briefly to get shoes, and when she returned, Officer Kizer tried a different approach. He asked about her husband's demeanor and the children's safety. When Lisa gave another hesitant response—"I … I … I don't … They're … They're okay right this second"—Officer Kizer said the words that became the crux of the appeal: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that."

Lisa responded: "Okay." She then proceeded to tell the officer where the children and her husband were located and reassured him that the dogs would not bite. She opened the door and even brushed the dogs aside to facilitate the officers' entry.

The question before the Seventh Circuit was whether that "Okay" constituted voluntary consent or whether Officer Kizer's use of the word "need"—even when softened by "almost" and "sensing that"—transformed the exchange into something coercive.

## The Totality of Circumstances Test

Judge Scudder's opinion applies the familiar *Figueroa-Espana* factors: the person's age, intelligence, and education; whether she was advised of her constitutional rights; the length of detention before consent; whether consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; physical coercion; and whether the individual was in custody. No single factor is dispositive, and courts examine the "totality of the circumstances."

Two aspects of the interaction proved decisive for the court. First, Officer Kizer's tone throughout was calm and respectful—conveying concern rather than authority. Second, and more importantly, Lisa had already demonstrated her ability to refuse consent when she told Officer Kizer she would "prefer [he] not" enter. That initial refusal, the court reasoned, showed she understood she could say no, which made her subsequent "Okay" more likely a genuine change of heart than submission to claimed authority.

The court acknowledged Coleman's argument that the word "need" could be read as an assertion of lawful authority—the kind of language that, under *Bumper*, would render consent involuntary. But the court found that reading too narrow. In context, Officer Kizer was expressing concern for children's safety, not claiming a legal right to enter. The "urgency and intensity" of that concern did not, in the court's view, overstep into coercion.

## Why the Initial Referral Mattered

The opinion underscores a point that warrants more attention in consent jurisprudence: the evidentiary value of an initial refusal. When a person has already said no, courts seem more willing to accept that a subsequent yes reflects a decision rather than a surrender. This makes intuitive sense, but it also creates something of a safe harbor for officers. If they ask once, get a refusal, and then use more pointed language on a second attempt, the initial refusal may paradoxically insulate the later consent from challenge.

That dynamic raises a practical question: does the law effectively encourage officers to manufacture evidence of voluntariness by asking first, getting rejected, and then escalating the pressure? The Seventh Circuit's analysis suggests not, so long as the escalation stops short of actual coercion. But the line between "expressing urgency" and "exerting pressure" remains fuzzy.

## Domestic Violence and the Fourth Amendment

The opinion opens with an acknowledgment that domestic violence calls are "one of the most difficult responsibilities of police officers." That framing matters. Courts evaluating consent in this context may be subtly influenced by the stakes involved—children potentially in danger, a volatile suspect, a frightened victim. Judge Scudder is careful to ground the analysis in the facts and law, but the court's willingness to read Officer Kizer's statement as concern rather than coercion reflects, at some level, an understanding that officers responding to domestic violence cannot be expected to phrase their requests with the precision of constitutional scholars.

The opinion closes by commending Officer Kizer's "sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." That's not legally material to the Fourth Amendment analysis, but it signals that the court saw a conscientious officer trying to protect children, not an overreach disguised as concern.

## The Scope Question

The court also upheld the district court's finding that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent once inside. The stated purpose was to check on the children's welfare and ensure Coleman posed no danger. That required a "scan of the home" and basic questions. The information gathered led to a warrant, which produced the firearms that formed the basis for Coleman's conviction.

This aspect of the opinion is straightforward but worth noting. Once consent is valid, the scope is measured by objective reasonableness—what a typical person would understand the consent to authorize. Checking on children's safety reasonably entails looking around the home for those children and for anyone who might harm them. The court had little trouble concluding the officers stayed within those bounds.

## Downstream Implications

*Jaison Coleman* does not break new doctrinal ground, but it illustrates how courts grapple with language at the margins of coercion. The decision suggests that officers may express urgency—even use words like "need"—so long as the overall context shows a request, not a command, and so long as the person giving consent has shown some capacity to refuse.

For defendants challenging consent, the case is a reminder that an initial refusal may cut both ways: it establishes the voluntariness of a subsequent yes as much as it highlights the pressure that led to the change. And for courts, the opinion demonstrates that the "totality of the circumstances" test remains sufficiently flexible to account for the realities of domestic violence calls without abandoning the protections of the Fourth Amendment.

The line between concern and coercion will remain contested. But in the Seventh Circuit, at least, a calm officer who asks rather than demands—and who accepts an initial refusal—will have room to express urgency when children's safety is at stake.



# From Welfare Check to Warrant: The Expansive Scope of Third-Party Consent

Domestic violence calls are among the most volatile situations police face, and the Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Jaison Coleman* illustrates just how readily a welfare check can transform into a full-fledged criminal investigation. The case offers a revealing window into how courts evaluate third-party consent—and specifically, what happens when that consent opens a door that officers can walk through and then use to gather information supporting a warrant for a broader search.

The factual backdrop is familiar to anyone who studies policing. At 4:00 a.m., Officer Jamie Kizer responded to a 911 call reporting that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife and was still inside the home. When Kizer arrived, he spoke with Lisa Coleman on the porch. Her responses to his questions were equivocal—she said she hoped her children were okay, that she didn't know where her husband was. When Kizer first asked if he could enter, Lisa said, "I'd prefer you not."

That should have been the end of it, at least temporarily. But Lisa then went inside to get her shoes, and when she returned, the dynamic shifted. Kizer asked again about the children's safety, and when Lisa gave another halting response, he said: "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa replied, "Okay," and proceeded to tell the officers where everyone was located and that the dogs wouldn't bite. She then opened the door and led them inside.

Once there, the officers asked the children some basic questions. Those answers—describing their father's threats and the gun he had pointed at their mother—provided the probable cause for a search warrant. The warrant turned up firearms, and Jaison Coleman was charged as a felon in possession.

## The Consent Analysis: Voluntariness Under the Totality Test

Coleman's challenge focused on whether his wife's "Okay" constituted valid consent at all. The Seventh Circuit affirmed that it did, applying the familiar voluntariness standard from *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* and its progeny. Judge Scudder's opinion emphasizes two points: first, that Kizer's tone was "concerned, not coercive," and second, that Lisa had already demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent when she declined his initial request.

The second observation is significant. Courts often look to whether a person knew they could say no as evidence that consent was voluntary. Here, Lisa's initial refusal—"I'd prefer you not"—shows she understood the nature of the exchange. The court reasoned that when she said "Okay" the second time, she made a choice.

But Coleman's argument about Kizer's use of the word "need" deserves serious attention. When an officer says "we almost *need* to come inside," is that a request or a statement of intent? The court acknowledged that Kizer's use of "need" carried "some urgency and intensity," but concluded that the totality of circumstances showed no coercion. The opinion notes that Kizer never claimed legal authority to enter and that his tone remained calm throughout.

This is a close call. The linguistic move from "may I" to "I'm sensing we need to" represents exactly the kind of subtle shift that can convert a request into something that sounds more like a demand. *Bumper v. North Carolina* held that consent given only after an officer claims lawful authority is no consent at all. Kizer didn't claim lawful authority, but he did invoke necessity—and the distinction between "we need to" and "we're going to" may be too fine for a frightened person at 4:00 a.m. to parse.

## The Scope Problem: When Welfare Checks Become Investigations

The more troubling aspect of *Coleman* concerns the scope of consent. Even assuming Lisa voluntarily consented, what precisely did she authorize? Under *Florida v. Jimeno*, the question is what a "typical reasonable person" would understand from the exchange. Lisa agreed to let officers enter to "make sure" her children were okay. The court concluded this "necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions."

That formulation merits scrutiny. The officers' stated purpose was a welfare check on the children. But a reasonable person might understand that as looking at the children to confirm they're unharmed—not as permission to canvass the home, locate the husband, and conduct substantive interviews with the kids. The court's reasoning effectively expands "checking on children" into a general sweep of the premises combined with investigative questioning.

And that's exactly what happened. The officers found Coleman in a bedroom, conducted a protective pat-down, and then interviewed the children. Those interviews produced the information that supported the warrant application. Without the children's statements about the gun and the threats, the warrant might not have issued.

This is where the consent analysis threatens to become circular. Officers enter on consent for a limited purpose. Once inside, they conduct lawful observations and questioning that produce new information. That information supports a warrant for a broader search. The warrant is valid because it rests on probable cause developed during the consent entry—and the consent entry was valid because it was limited to checking on welfare.

The court correctly notes that it didn't need to reach the government's alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. But those doctrines might have provided a more honest analytical framework. If there was genuine reason to believe children were in danger, exigent circumstances could have justified entry. Instead, the consent analysis does the work while obscuring the slippery slope from welfare check to criminal investigation.

## Downstream Implications

The practical implications of *Coleman* matter. Domestic violence situations present genuine emergencies, and officers should be able to verify that children are safe. But the scope-of-consent analysis here gives officers significant latitude to convert a narrowly tailored permission into an opportunity for general investigation. The "objective reasonableness" standard from *Jimeno* already gives courts room to find broad consent; *Coleman* suggests that consent to enter for a welfare check carries with it authorization to conduct a protective sweep, locate all occupants, and interview household members.

The decision also highlights the pressure domestic violence victims may feel in these encounters. Lisa Coleman had been threatened by her husband. She was standing on her porch at 4:00 a.m., speaking to police, while he remained inside. The court found her consent voluntary because she had initially refused. But that initial refusal may simply reflect uncertainty about what would happen next, not a confident exercise of autonomy. The subsequent "Okay" came in a context of fear, not free choice.

Police officers face genuinely difficult decisions in these situations, and the Seventh Circuit rightly recognized that Kizer acted with restraint and good faith. But the legal framework matters, and *Coleman* illustrates how readily the consent exception can expand to accommodate investigative purposes that the original consent never contemplated.

The case also serves as a reminder that Fourth Amendment doctrine in this area is shaped by facts on the ground. Body camera footage allowed the court to assess tone, demeanor, and timing with precision. That transparency is valuable—but it shouldn't obscure the underlying tension between limited consent and expansive police authority once that threshold is crossed.



# Domestic Violence Responses and Fourth Amendment Flexibility: The Court's Acknowledged Reality

The Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Jaison Coleman* arrives at an unremarkable destination—affirming a district court's denial of a suppression motion—but travels there through noteworthy terrain. The case highlights how domestic violence scenarios test the Fourth Amendment's consent doctrine in ways that more conventional criminal cases do not. Judge Scudder's opinion implicitly acknowledges what practitioners and scholars have long understood: the realities of DV calls require calibrated legal analysis, even if courts resist creating explicit doctrinal carve-outs.

## The Consent Analysis: Standard Framework, Contextual Application

The facts read like a training scenario. Officer Jamie Kizer responded to a 4:00 a.m. domestic violence call. The caller reported that her stepfather had threatened to kill her mother. When Officer Kizer arrived, Lisa Coleman stepped onto the porch but gave inconsistent answers about her children's safety. She initially refused entry, saying "I'd prefer you not" when asked if the officer could come inside. Minutes later, after Officer Kizer expressed concern—saying "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—Lisa responded "Okay," provided information about the children's location, and reassured officers about the dogs.

The defendant argued that Officer Kizer's use of "need" rendered Lisa's consent coercive. The Seventh Circuit rejected this argument, emphasizing two factors: Officer Kizer's non-coercive tone and Lisa's demonstrated capacity to refuse consent when first asked. The court applied the standard voluntariness test from *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* and *Bumper v. North Carolina*, examining the totality of circumstances without announcing any DV-specific modification.

Yet the opinion's framing matters. Judge Scudder opens by noting that "responding to 911 calls of domestic violence is one of the most difficult responsibilities of police officers." He closes by praising Officer Kizer and his colleagues for "sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." These bookends suggest that the domestic violence context exercised meaningful influence on the court's analysis, even as the legal framework remained formally unchanged.

## The Victim's Dual Role: Caller and Consenting Party

Domestic violence cases create a distinctive dynamic that standard consent doctrine struggles to capture. Lisa Coleman was both the victim of the reported threat and the third-party consent giver. She called 911 seeking protection, yet hesitated when officers arrived—a pattern domestic violence experts recognize as common. Victims often oscillate between seeking intervention and fearing its consequences, caught between immediate danger and the disruptions that police involvement brings.

This ambivalence complicates the voluntariness inquiry. When Lisa initially refused entry, she exercised genuine agency. When she later consented, was she responding to the officer's legitimate concern for her children, or was she acquiescing to perceived pressure? The Seventh Circuit found the former, emphasizing that Lisa "chose to let Officer Kizer enter after he asked again, with the second request being unaccompanied by any coercion."

The analysis feels correct as an empirical matter. Officer Kizer did not threaten, did not assert authority, and did not misrepresent his legal rights. He expressed concern in urgent terms appropriate to circumstances where children's safety was genuinely uncertain. That Lisa had just demonstrated her ability to say "no" strengthens the inference that her subsequent "Okay" reflected voluntary choice rather than coerced submission.

## Doctrinal Implications: Context Without Carve-Outs

The Seventh Circuit's approach—applying standard consent doctrine while acknowledging contextual realities—represents a sensible middle ground. Courts have resisted creating categorical rules for domestic violence encounters, and understandably so. Any rule that automatically validates warrantless entries in DV cases would risk eroding Fourth Amendment protections precisely in situations where victims' privacy interests remain acute. Conversely, rules requiring officers to obtain explicit, uncoerced consent before entering could endanger victims and children in volatile situations.

The *Coleman* opinion navigates this tension through careful fact-specific analysis rather than doctrinal innovation. The court found sufficient indicia of voluntariness—Lisa's initial refusal, the brief duration of the encounter, the absence of custodial pressure, and Officer Kizer's calm demeanor—to satisfy constitutional requirements. This approach preserves flexibility while demanding genuine judicial scrutiny.

The opinion notably declines to address the government's alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery, finding consent dispositive. This reluctance to endorse broader justifications for warrantless entry suggests appropriate judicial restraint. Courts should resolve Fourth Amendment questions on the narrowest available grounds, particularly in domestic violence cases where the facts are often messy and the stakes are high.

## Looking Forward: Contextual Competence

*United States v. Coleman* illustrates how context can permeate legal analysis without destabilizing doctrinal frameworks. The Seventh Circuit applied standard consent principles but did so with clear awareness of domestic violence's distinctive pressures. Officers responding to DV calls must make rapid decisions in ambiguous circumstances; courts reviewing those decisions must balance deference to police judgment against rigorous protection of constitutional rights.

Judge Scudder's opinion achieves this balance. It recognizes that domestic violence responses present "delicate and difficult circumstances" without suggesting that such circumstances excuse constitutional shortcuts. For practitioners, the decision reaffirms that third-party consent remains viable in domestic violence investigations, but that consent's validity will depend on demonstrated voluntariness—not merely the fact that a victim initially sought help. The Fourth Amendment's flexibility, properly understood, can accommodate the realities of domestic violence response without sacrificing its essential protections.



# The Road Not Taken: Why the Seventh Circuit Avoided Exigent Circumstances

When a court resolves a Fourth Amendment case on one ground, it necessarily leaves other questions unanswered. That reality is on full display in *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, where the Seventh Circuit affirmed a firearm conviction based on third-party consent while explicitly declining to address the government's alternative theories of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. The decision reaches the right result, but it also represents something of a missed opportunity to provide much-needed guidance to law enforcement officers navigating one of the most fraught situations they face: early-morning domestic violence calls.

## The Consent Framework

Judge Scudder's opinion for the court walks carefully through the consent analysis. The facts are about as borderline as they come. At 4:00 a.m., Officer Jamie Kizer responded to a report that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife and was still inside the home. When Kizer asked Lisa Coleman if he could enter, she initially demurred, saying she would "prefer you not." A few minutes later, after Kizer expressed concern about the children's safety—"I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—Lisa said "Okay" and proceeded to provide helpful information about the home's layout, her dogs' temperament, and her husband's location.

The court found this exchange sufficient to establish voluntary consent. Two factors proved decisive. First, Kizer's tone throughout was "concerned, not coercive." Second, and more importantly, Lisa had already demonstrated her capacity to say no by initially refusing entry. The fact that she changed her mind after Kizer pressed the issue did not render her consent involuntary, the court concluded, because the officer's persistence never crossed into coercion.

This is a sensible, fact-bound resolution. The court properly emphasizes that consent determinations are inherently context-dependent, and the body camera footage allowed for a reliable reconstruction of the interaction. One can quibble with whether "I'm sensing that we almost *need* to come inside" tips too far toward pressure rather than persuasion. But the Seventh Circuit's approach—focusing on Lisa's demonstrated ability to refuse and Kizer's calm demeanor—provides a workable framework for trial courts evaluating similar interactions.

## What the Court Didn't Say

What's striking, however, is what the opinion does not address. The government urged the court to affirm on alternative grounds: exigent circumstances justified the entry, and even if they didn't, the firearms would have been inevitably discovered through lawful means. The court declined to reach either argument, noting that its consent finding made them unnecessary.

This is standard judicial practice. Courts routinely resolve cases on the narrowest available ground, avoiding constitutional questions when possible. The approach serves judicial economy and respects the principle that courts should not issue advisory opinions on issues not necessary to the judgment.

But there are costs to this parsimony. Domestic violence calls represent a uniquely challenging context for Fourth Amendment analysis. Officers arrive at scenes that are volatile and unpredictable, often with limited information about what dangers await. The exigent circumstances doctrine is designed precisely for these situations—when the time required to obtain a warrant would create an imminent threat to safety or risk destruction of evidence.

The facts here would have supported a straightforward exigent circumstances analysis. A 911 caller reported that Coleman had threatened to kill his wife and light the house on fire. He was still inside. The children were present. Lisa's hesitant and contradictory responses—she "hoped" the kids would be okay, didn't know where her husband was—would reasonably heighten an officer's concern. And the hour, 4:00 a.m., hardly suggested that waiting for a warrant was a realistic option.

The court could have used this case to clarify how the exigent circumstances doctrine applies to domestic violence responses, where the Supreme Court's decision in *Brigham City v. Stuart* already recognized that officers may enter a home without a warrant when they have "an objectively reasonable basis for believing" that someone inside is in imminent danger. Instead, we're left with a consent analysis that, while sound, turns heavily on idiosyncratic facts that will be difficult to apply in future cases.

## Guidance Left on the Table

This raises a practical concern. Law enforcement officers need clear rules. When they respond to domestic violence calls, they need to know what they can do and what they can't. The consent framework, while doctrinally sound, offers limited guidance because it turns so heavily on the specific words and tone used in a particular interaction. An officer trying to determine whether consent was voluntary in the next case won't learn much from the Seventh Circuit's fact-intensive analysis here.

The exigent circumstances framework, by contrast, would have given officers something more concrete. The court could have held that early-morning domestic violence calls involving reported threats of deadly violence create inherent exigencies, or at least elaborated on what factors counsel in favor of finding such exigencies. Such an opinion would have given law enforcement a more reliable roadmap.

To be fair, courts are not in the business of issuing advisory opinions, and there are good reasons to resolve cases on the narrowest available ground. But when the narrowest ground is also the most fact-specific, the doctrinal value of the decision diminishes. Future courts will have to work through the same questions about what constitutes coercive police questioning and how to evaluate allegedly "voluntary" consent in high-pressure situations.

## A Final Note on Police Judgment

Judge Scudder closes with praise for Officer Kizer's "sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." The compliment is well-deserved. Kizer showed patience and persistence without crossing into intimidation. He expressed concern rather than issuing commands. And the result—a lawful entry that led to the discovery of firearms and protection of victims—suggests that good police work can coexist with constitutional limits.

But it's worth noting that even excellent police work would benefit from clearer legal frameworks. The Seventh Circuit's decision affirms that consent can be found in borderline circumstances. It tells us less about what happens when consent can't be obtained—which is precisely when officers most need guidance about what other tools the Fourth Amendment provides.
