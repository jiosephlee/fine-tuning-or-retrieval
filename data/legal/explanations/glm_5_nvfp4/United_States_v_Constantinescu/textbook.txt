Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## The Defendants and Their Social Media Following

The case involves eight defendants who cultivated substantial followings on social media platforms by presenting themselves as successful and skilled stock traders. Edward Constantinescu, Perry "PJ" Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight each built audiences that looked to them for trading advice and information. Through their posts, they established credibility with their followers, who relied on their representations about trading positions and market opportunities. This digital influence became the foundation for the government's allegations of criminal conduct.

One defendant, Daniel Knight, pleaded guilty before the case reached the appellate stage. The remaining seven defendants moved to dismiss the indictment, setting up the legal challenge that reached the Fifth Circuit.

## Mechanics of the Alleged Pump-and-Dump Scheme

The government's indictment describes a classic "pump and dump" scheme adapted for the social media age. According to the allegations, the defendants followed a three-step pattern. First, they would purchase a security for themselves. Second, they would "pump" the price by posting false and misleading information about that security on social media, inducing their followers to purchase it and thereby artificially inflating its price. The misrepresentations included false statements about their own trading positions and predictions about potential price increases. Third, once the price had been artificially driven up by their followers' purchases, defendants would "dump" by secretly selling their own holdings at a profit.

The indictment alleges that defendants profited approximately $114 million through this scheme. The government's theory is that the defendants exploited their credibility and influence to manipulate their followers' trading decisions, all while secretly taking positions contrary to what they publicly recommended.

## The Superseding Indictment: Charges Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2

The government brought charges under federal securities fraud statutes. Count one charged conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, which criminalizes conspiracies to violate federal securities fraud provisions. The indictment also included multiple counts of substantive securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, which prohibits schemes to defraud any person in connection with securities or schemes to obtain money or property through false pretenses in connection with securities transactions. Additionally, the government invoked 18 U.S.C. § 2, the general aiding and abetting statute, which extends criminal liability to those who aid, abet, counsel, command, induce, or procure the commission of an offense.

Section 1348, enacted as part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, was Congress's response to major corporate fraud scandals. The Fifth Circuit notes that there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," but the statute "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," allowing courts to draw on that well-developed body of law.

## District Court's Dismissal Based on Failure to Alleged Deprivation of a Traditional Property Interest

The defendants moved to dismiss the indictment, arguing that it failed to state an offense. The district court agreed and dismissed the indictment. The court's reasoning centered on recent Supreme Court precedent interpreting the scope of federal fraud statutes, particularly *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023). In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, which had allowed convictions based on a defendant scheming to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. The Court held that federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests," and the "right to valuable economic information" does not qualify.

The district court concluded that the indictment in this case similarly failed to allege that defendants schemed to deprive victims of any traditional property interest. Instead, the court viewed the indictment as alleging only that defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information"—essentially the same theory the Supreme Court had rejected in *Ciminelli*.

## Government's Timely Appeal

The government exercised its right to appeal the dismissal. Under federal criminal procedure, the government may appeal certain pretrial rulings, including the dismissal of an indictment. The government timely appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, arguing that the district court had mischaracterized the indictment and that the charges were sufficient under the Supreme Court's recent guidance. The Fifth Circuit would review the sufficiency of the indictment de novo, meaning it would consider the question anew without deference to the district court's legal conclusions.

The central question on appeal was whether the indictment adequately alleged a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud"—the essential elements of securities fraud under § 1348—or whether, as the district court concluded, it relied on an impermissible theory of fraud that the Supreme Court had rejected.

# The Legal Framework for Securities Fraud Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348

## The Text and Structure of 18 U.S.C. § 1348

The securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, serves as the statutory foundation for the charges against the defendants in this case. The statute prohibits two distinct types of misconduct. First, it criminalizes any "scheme to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security." Second, it prohibits schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security." The statute thus provides two complementary theories of prosecution: one focused on fraudulent schemes affecting securities transactions generally, and another requiring the specific intent to obtain money or property through deception.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* were charged under both theories. The indictment alleged conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, which prohibits conspiracies to violate § 1348, as well as multiple counts of securities fraud under § 1348 itself. Understanding the statutory language is essential because the district court's dismissal turned on whether the indictment adequately alleged the deprivation of "money or property"—a phrase that lies at the heart of what constitutes a cognizable fraud offense.

## Element One: Scheme to Defraud Defined

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* articulates the first element of securities fraud as a "scheme to defraud." This element requires a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived." The court draws this definition from its wire fraud precedent, illustrating how fraud concepts translate across statutory contexts.

Crucially, the "scheme to defraud" element is not satisfied by deception alone. The deception must be aimed at obtaining something of value. In *Constantinescu*, the court explains that a defendant commits securities fraud when he "'engaged in deception'" and "[o]btaining the victim's money or property" was "'an object' of his fraud." This formulation, drawn from Supreme Court precedent, emphasizes the instrumental relationship between the deception and the sought-after gain.

The distinction between valid and invalid theories of fraud becomes critical here. The court contrasts the "right-to-control theory" rejected by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* with the "fraudulent-inducement theory" the government pursued in *Constantinescu*. Under the right-to-control theory, the government had attempted to establish fraud based solely on depriving victims of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Supreme Court rejected this theory because the fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." By contrast, the fraudulent-inducement theory alleged in *Constantinescu*—"whereby a defendant 'uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property'"—properly targets the deprivation of money or property, not mere information.

## Element Two: Intent to Defraud Defined

The second element of securities fraud is "intent to defraud." The Fifth Circuit defines this as "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." This two-part definition captures both the defendant's deceptive purpose and the anticipated consequences of that deception.

A critical question in *Constantinescu* was whether the "harm" element requires proof that the defendant intended to cause the victim economic loss. The defendants argued that because they intended only to profit from their scheme—not to harm their followers—the indictment failed to allege the requisite intent to defraud. The court rejected this argument based on Supreme Court precedent holding that "the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme."

The Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis* clarified that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraudulent-inducement theory thus permits conviction "regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." This principle directly refutes the defendants' contention that their followers' receipt of market-value securities negated the fraud charges. The indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped to gain a profit" demonstrated that they "had money in mind," which suffices to establish the intent element.

## De Novo Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency

The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. This standard means the appellate court considers the question anew, giving no deference to the district court's conclusions. De novo review is appropriate here because determining whether an indictment states an offense is a question of law.

Under this standard, the court "is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated." This approach serves a practical function at the pretrial stage: the court does not weigh evidence or assess credibility but instead assumes the government can prove what it has alleged. The question is purely legal—whether those allegations, if proven, would establish each element of the charged offense.

## Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud Jurisprudence

A notable aspect of the Fifth Circuit's analysis is its reliance on mail and wire fraud precedent to interpret § 1348. The court acknowledges that "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." However, because § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," courts have given the terms similar treatment, "often relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges."

This approach reflects a broader pattern in federal criminal law. When Congress enacts a new fraud statute using familiar terminology, courts naturally look to existing interpretations of similar language. The mail and wire fraud statutes—18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 1343—have generated extensive jurisprudence defining "scheme to defraud," "intent to defraud," and related concepts. By incorporating these well-developed definitions, courts provide consistency and predictability in fraud law.

The Supreme Court's decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, though arising under the wire fraud statute, thus directly inform the securities fraud analysis. Both cases addressed the property requirement in federal fraud statutes, and their holdings apply equally to § 1348's prohibition on schemes "to obtain . . . any money or property."

## Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment

Beyond the substantive elements of the offense, the court addresses the formal requirements for a sufficient indictment. An indictment must "(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." These requirements serve distinct but related functions in the criminal process.

The first requirement—enumerating each element—ensures that the defendant and the court can verify that the prosecution has a valid legal theory. The second requirement—fairly informing the defendant—enables effective preparation of a defense. The third requirement—enabling a double jeopardy defense—protects against successive prosecutions for the same conduct. Together, these requirements reflect the constitutional and procedural safeguards inherent in the grand jury system.

In *Constantinescu*, the district court concluded that the indictment failed the first requirement because it did not allege the deprivation of a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment properly alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory under which defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." Because the indictment adequately alleged both a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud," it met the standard for sufficiency and the case was remanded for further proceedings.

# Supreme Court Fraud Jurisprudence: Ciminelli and Kousisis

## Ciminelli v. United States: rejecting the right-to-control theory

To understand why the district court dismissed the indictment in *Constantinescu*—and why the Fifth Circuit reversed that dismissal—we must first examine two recent Supreme Court decisions that define the boundaries of federal fraud liability. The first, *Ciminelli v. United States*, addressed whether the government could base a fraud conviction on what prosecutors called the "right-to-control" theory.

In *Ciminelli*, a construction company owner named Louis Ciminelli paid a lobbyist to help secure state-funded construction contracts. The government prosecuted him for wire fraud, but notably did not allege that Ciminelli defrauded the state of money or property. Instead, prosecutors relied solely on the theory that Ciminelli had deprived the state of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." Under this right-to-control theory, the victim's property interest was defined as "the right to control [its] assets"—essentially, the right to make informed decisions.

The Supreme Court rejected this approach. Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Gorsuch held that the federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests." The "right to valuable economic information," the Court explained, "is not a traditional property interest." Accordingly, the right-to-control theory "cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction."

This holding matters deeply for our case. The district court in *Constantinescu* read *Ciminelli* to mean that the indictment was deficient because it merely alleged that defendants deprived their followers of valuable economic information about securities. But the Fifth Circuit disagreed with this reading, explaining that the indictment in *Constantinescu* alleged something different.

## The limitation to 'traditional property interests'

* Ciminelli* establishes a clear boundary: federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, not intangible rights or informational interests. This limitation flows from the text of the fraud statutes, which prohibit schemes to "defraud" or to "obtain money or property." The Court emphasized that these terms must be given their ordinary meaning—property in the traditional sense, not creative expansions that would effectively federalize all manner of deceptive conduct.

The right-to-control theory failed precisely because it converted a non-property interest—the right to make fully informed decisions—into a property interest. Under that theory, any deception that denied a victim information could become fraud, even if the victim lost no money or property. The Court refused to endorse such an expansion, noting that accepting the right-to-control theory would "invite federal courts to develop a common-law crime covering a wide swath of previously state-law fraud offenses."

This principle directly informed the district court's reasoning in *Constantinescu*. The district court believed the indictment relied on a theory similar to the one *Ciminelli* rejected—that defendants had deprived their followers of valuable information about the securities being promoted. But the Fifth Circuit read the indictment differently, distinguishing *Ciminelli* on the ground that the indictment here alleged a more traditional form of fraud.

## Kousisis v. United States: confirming the fraudulent-inducement theory

The second key decision, *Kousisis v. United States*, was decided alongside *Ciminelli* and clarifies what type of fraud prosecution *remains* valid after *Ciminelli*. Together, these cases draw a sharp line between two fraud theories—one rejected, one affirmed.

In *Kousisis*, the defendant sought government contracts that required subcontracting with "disadvantaged business enterprises." Kousisis falsely represented that his company would use a qualifying disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. In reality, he used that business merely as a "pass-through" entity—a paper arrangement that satisfied the technical requirement without providing actual disadvantaged-business participation. When the deception came to light, the government prosecuted Kousisis for fraud.

Kousisis argued that his conviction could not stand because he had not sought to cause the government economic harm. He had performed the contract work, the government received what it paid for, and there was no evidence the government suffered any net financial loss. He contended that fraud requires an intent to harm the victim economically, not merely to deceive.

The Supreme Court rejected this argument. Justice Kavanaugh, writing for the majority, held that a defendant commits fraud when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The Court described this as the "fraudulent-inducement theory"—a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

Critically, the Court confirmed that this fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." Unlike the discredited right-to-control theory, which focused on deprivation of information, the fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property"—the traditional property interests that *Ciminelli* held are the proper concern of fraud law.

## Obtaining money or property as an object of the fraud

*Kousisis* clarifies that the object of the fraud—the thing the defendant seeks to obtain—must be money or property. This requirement ensures that federal fraud statutes remain anchored in property law rather than becoming general prohibitions on deception.

The fraudulent-inducement theory satisfies this requirement because the defendant obtains the victim's money or property through deception. In *Kousisis*, the defendant obtained government contract payments through false representations. In *Constantinescu*, the defendants allegedly obtained their followers' money through false representations that induced them to purchase securities. In both cases, the defendants "had money in mind"—they sought to obtain property from the victims through deception.

The Fifth Circuit emphasized this point in its analysis. The indictment alleged that defendants "pump[ed]" securities by posting false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security," after which defendants would "dump" by selling for profit. This factual allegation describes exactly what *Kousisis* confirmed as valid fraud: using deception to obtain money or property.

## Why fraud liability does not require victim economic loss

One of the most important holdings in *Kousisis*—and one directly relevant to *Constantinescu*—is that fraud liability does not require proof that the defendant intended to cause the victim economic harm. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that they intended only to profit from their scheme, not to harm their followers. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument by relying directly on *Kousisis*.

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* explicitly held that a defendant can commit fraud even when he "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return." The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the victim's money or property through material misrepresentations. The defendant's intent to cause economic loss—or lack thereof—is irrelevant.

This principle refutes the defendants' argument in *Constantinescu* that they merely intended to profit, not to harm anyone. As the Fifth Circuit explained, "that is a distinction without a difference." The indictment alleged that defendants obtained money from their followers by deceiving them into purchasing securities. That the followers received securities of equal market value does not negate the fraud. The "fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

The Fifth Circuit supported this point by citing its own precedent in *United States v. Baker*, which explains that fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must equal the defendant's gain. The fraud lies in the deception used to obtain money or property, not in the ultimate economic outcome for the victim.

## The critical distinction between the two theories

Understanding the distinction between the rejected right-to-control theory and the affirmed fraudulent-inducement theory is essential to understanding *Constantinescu*. This distinction determined whether the indictment properly stated an offense.

The right-to-control theory, rejected in *Ciminelli*, treats the deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information" as the property interest protected by fraud law. Under this theory, a defendant commits fraud by denying a victim information necessary to make informed decisions, even if the victim never parts with money or property. The Supreme Court rejected this because the "right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest."

The fraudulent-inducement theory, affirmed in *Kousisis*, requires that the defendant deceive the victim into parting with money or property. Under this theory, the property interest protected is the money or property itself—the traditional property interest that *Ciminelli* held fraud statutes protect. The deception causes the deprivation of property; the information denied is the means of the fraud, not the object.

In *Constantinescu*, the district court read the indictment as invoking the right-to-control theory—alleging that defendants deprived followers of valuable information. The Fifth Circuit disagreed. The indictment alleged that defendants deceived followers into purchasing securities—that is, into parting with money. This is the fraudulent-inducement theory, which *Kousisis* confirmed is valid. The defendants' followers were not merely denied information; they were induced to transfer their money to purchase securities. The property interest allegedly obtained through deception was the followers' money—a traditional property interest.

This distinction explains why the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court. While defendants argued the indictment merely alleged deprivation of economic information, the court read the indictment more narrowly and traditionally: it alleged that defendants obtained followers' money through false representations. That allegation, the court held, states a valid fraud offense under *Kousisis*.

# The Fifth Circuit's Analysis and Holding

## Why the indictment alleges a scheme to defraud under fraudulent-inducement theory

The Fifth Circuit began its analysis by identifying the legal framework governing securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The statute prohibits schemes to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security" and schemes to obtain money or property through false pretenses. The court explained that securities fraud requires two elements: (1) a "scheme to defraud" and (2) "intent to defraud." A scheme to defraud involves a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money."

The court concluded that the indictment properly alleged a scheme to defraud under what the Supreme Court has termed the "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The indictment alleged that defendants posted false and misleading information about securities on social media to induce their followers to purchase those securities. By alleging that defendants tricked followers into parting with their money to buy securities, the indictment properly stated a scheme to defraud. The court emphasized that the fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property"—traditional property interests that federal fraud statutes were designed to protect.

## Why this case is distinguishable from Ciminelli's right-to-control theory

The defendants argued that the indictment was insufficient under the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, which allowed convictions based solely on a defendant scheming to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and because "[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest," the right-to-control theory could not support a fraud conviction.

The Fifth Circuit distinguished *Ciminelli* decisively. The indictment in this case did not rely on the right-to-control theory at all—it did not allege that defendants schemed to deprive followers of valuable information needed to make economic decisions. Instead, the indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities, depriving them of money or property directly. The court explained that the fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." While the right-to-control theory was defective because it sought to protect a non-traditional property interest (valuable economic information), the fraudulent-inducement theory protects the traditional property interests of money and property that victims hand over due to deception. Because the indictment alleged defendants obtained their followers' money through deception, it did not run afoul of *Ciminelli*.

## Why the indictment sufficiently alleges intent to defraud

The intent-to-defraud element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." The defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception, but they challenged whether it alleged intent to cause harm. They argued that the indictment only showed they intended to profit from their scheme, not to injure their followers.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument based on the Supreme Court's recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Kousisis*, the defendant obtained government contracts by falsely representing that his company would subcontract with a disadvantaged business. The Court held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss.

Applying *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit found the indictment's allegations sufficient. The indictment alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." By fraudulently inducing followers to purchase securities, defendants allegedly intended to deceive them and cause the harm that results from depriving someone of money or property through deception. The followers' subsequent receipt of market-value securities did not negate the fraud allegations, because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm to the victim.

## Defendants' profit motive versus victim harm: a distinction without a difference

The defendants attempted to draw a distinction between their intent to profit and an intent to harm their followers. They contended that the indictment merely showed they sought to enrich themselves, not to cause injury. The Fifth Circuit rejected this framing as "a distinction without a difference."

The court explained that a fraud is complete when a defendant "has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss." The indictment's allegation that defendants pumped and dumped securities to gain a profit demonstrated that they "had money in mind." Their object was to obtain money from followers through deception. That defendants may have profited without causing their followers to suffer net losses was immaterial—the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must equal the defendant's gain. A defendant may be liable for fraud even when providing something of equal value in return.

This principle makes sense in the context of a pump-and-dump scheme. Defendants allegedly induced followers to buy securities at artificially inflated prices. Even if followers received securities worth what they paid at the moment of purchase, the transaction was tainted by fraud. The defendants obtained money through deception, and that is sufficient to allege intent to defraud.

## The court's holding and disposition: reversal and remand

The Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged both elements of securities fraud: a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud. The court held that the indictment properly charged defendants under the fraudulent-inducement theory, which protects traditional property interests in money and property, distinguishing this case from *Ciminelli*'s rejection of the right-to-control theory. The court further held that the indictment sufficiently alleged intent to defraud because it showed defendants schemed to obtain their followers' money through deception, and the fraud statutes do not require intent to cause net economic harm.

Accordingly, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's dismissal of the indictment and remanded the case for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The decision means that the government's charges against the defendants will proceed, and the case will return to the district court for further litigation. The defendants will face trial on the conspiracy and securities fraud charges unless they succeed on other grounds.

# Significance and Implications

## Clarifying the Boundary Between Valid and Invalid Fraud Theories Post-Ciminelli

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Constantinescu* provides crucial clarification on the boundary between valid and invalid fraud theories following the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. Understanding this boundary is essential for properly analyzing fraud charges under federal statutes, including the securities fraud provision at issue here.

In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, which had allowed convictions based solely on a defendant's scheme to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that such information is not a "traditional property interest" and therefore cannot serve as the basis for a fraud conviction. This narrowing of fraud doctrine raised questions about whether traditional fraud prosecutions—particularly those involving deceptive schemes—could survive scrutiny.

*Constantinescu* answers that question decisively. The Fifth Circuit explains that *Ciminelli* did not eliminate fraud liability; it merely rejected one specific theory. The opinion draws a clear line: a fraud theory is invalid when it relies solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information, but a fraud theory remains valid when it involves a defendant fraudulently inducing a victim to part with money or property. The latter scenario constitutes a "fraudulent-inducement theory," which the Supreme Court explicitly distinguished from the discredited right-to-control theory in *Kousisis v. United States*.

This distinction is critical for practitioners and students alike. The fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property" in the traditional sense—assets that fraud statutes have long protected. When a defendant makes material misrepresentations to trick someone into handing over money or property, the fraud is complete, regardless of whether the victim also lost control over information or decision-making authority. *Constantinescu* thus confirms that *Ciminelli* did not upend fraud law; it simply required prosecutors to focus on the traditional property interests that have always animated fraud statutes.

## Implications for Social-Media-Based Fraud Prosecutions

The *Constantinescu* opinion arises from a modern fact pattern: a "pump and dump" scheme executed through social media platforms. Defendants with large online followings allegedly posted false information about securities to inflate prices artificially, then sold their holdings for profit. This fact pattern tests whether established fraud principles apply with equal force to technologically novel schemes.

The Fifth Circuit's analysis confirms that the medium of fraud does not alter the legal analysis. Whether defendants make misrepresentations through traditional channels—mail, telephone, in-person meetings—or through posts on social media platforms, the fundamental question remains the same: did the defendant scheme to obtain the victim's money or property through deception? The indictment here alleged that defendants used social media posts to "pump" securities by posting "false and misleading information" that "induced" followers to purchase those securities. This straightforward fraudulent-inducement narrative survives *Ciminelli* because the alleged scheme targeted followers' money, not merely their information or decision-making capacity.

The opinion thus provides important guidance for future prosecutions involving social media. Prosecutors need not develop novel legal theories to address technology-driven fraud. Instead, they can rely on time-tested fraudulent-inducement theories, focusing on how defendants used deception to cause victims to part with money or property. The technology may be new, but the fraud is familiar.

## The Government's Burden at the Indictment Stage

*Constantinescu* reinforces important principles regarding the standard for evaluating the sufficiency of an indictment. The court reviews de novo whether an indictment states an offense, taking all allegations as true. At this stage, the court does not weigh evidence or assess whether the government can prove its case at trial. Rather, the court asks a more limited question: assuming the indictment's allegations are true, does the document state each element of the charged offense?

The court's analysis demonstrates how this standard operates in practice. The government alleged that defendants posted false information to induce followers to purchase securities and that defendants profited $114 million from this scheme. Accepting these allegations as true, the court concluded that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. The court did not ask whether the securities purchased by followers had value, whether followers suffered actual losses, or whether the government could prove the falsity of defendants' statements. Those are questions for trial.

This procedural posture matters significantly. Defendants sought dismissal before trial, arguing that the indictment failed to allege a cognizable fraud theory. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, emphasizing that the indictment's allegations—read generously—satisfied the elements of securities fraud. The opinion thus illustrates how the indictment stage provides defendants with a meaningful opportunity to challenge legally deficient charges while preserving the government's ability to prove its case at trial.

## Relationship Between Defendants' Gain and Victims' Deprivation

One of the most significant aspects of *Constantinescu* is its treatment of the relationship between defendants' gain and victims' deprivation. Defendants argued that they intended only to profit—not to harm their followers. In their view, followers who purchased securities received something of value and therefore suffered no injury that could support a fraud conviction. The Fifth Circuit, relying on *Kousisis*, rejected this argument.

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* held that fraud statutes "do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme." A defendant violates fraud law when he schemes to obtain the victim's money or property through deception, "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraudulent-inducement theory focuses on what the defendant sought to obtain through deception, not on whether the victim suffered net pecuniary harm. As the Court explained, a "fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return.

*Constantinescu* applies this principle to the securities fraud context. The indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities—that is, to part with money. The fact that followers received securities in exchange—securities that may have retained market value—does not negate the fraud. The defendants' object was to obtain money, and they accomplished this through deception. The Fifth Circuit also cited *United States v. Baker* for the proposition that fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain. The two inquiries—defendant's gain and victim's deprivation—are related but distinct.

This principle has significant practical consequences. In many fraud cases, particularly investment frauds, victims may receive something of value that complicates loss calculations. *Constantinescu* confirms that such complications do not defeat fraud liability at the pleading stage. If the defendant obtained the victim's money or property through material deception, the fraud elements are satisfied.

## Guidance for Future Securities Fraud Charging Decisions

The *Constantinescu* opinion offers valuable guidance for prosecutors and defense attorneys navigating securities fraud charges in the post-*Ciminelli* landscape. For prosecutors, the decision underscores the importance of grounding fraud charges in fraudulent-inducement theories that clearly identify how victims were deprived of money or property through deception.

The opinion notes that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Practitioners should therefore look to mail and wire fraud precedent when analyzing securities fraud charges, as courts have given these terms "similar treatment." This cross-referencing provides a substantial body of caselaw to draw upon when constructing or challenging fraud theories.

Prosecutors should also note that actual economic harm to victims is not an element of securities fraud. The focus remains on the defendant's scheme and intent—specifically, whether the defendant engaged in deception with the object of obtaining the victim's money or property. An indictment that clearly alleges these elements will survive a motion to dismiss, even if victims ultimately received something of value or did not suffer demonstrable losses.

For defense attorneys, *Constantinescu* illustrates the limits of *Ciminelli*. The Supreme Court's rejection of the right-to-control theory does not provide a blanket defense to fraud charges. Arguments that the indictment merely alleges deprivation of information or economic decision-making authority will fail if the indictment also alleges that victims parted with money or property through fraudulent inducement. Defense challenges must instead focus on the adequacy of allegations regarding deception, scheme, and intent—or await trial to contest the government's evidence.

Finally, the opinion's treatment of social media fraud confirms that emerging technologies do not create new defenses or require novel legal frameworks. Practitioners on both sides should focus on the fundamental questions: What did the defendant represent? Were those representations materially false? Did the defendant intend to deceive? Did the defendant scheme to obtain money or property through that deception? If these questions can be answered affirmatively based on the indictment's allegations, the fraud charge will survive.
