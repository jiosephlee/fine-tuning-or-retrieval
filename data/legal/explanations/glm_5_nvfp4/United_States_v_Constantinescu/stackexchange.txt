Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the "right-to-control theory" that the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli v. United States?
Question:
I'm reading United States v. Constantinescu and the court discusses Ciminelli's rejection of the 'right-to-control theory.' The opinion says this theory attempted to 'establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.' But I'm confused - isn't the right to control your own assets a property interest? Why did the Supreme Court say this isn't a 'traditional property interest' protected by fraud statutes? And why can't the government base a fraud conviction on depriving someone of information they need to make informed decisions?
Answer:
**The right-to-control theory attempted to redefine "property" to include the right to make informed economic decisions.**

The Supreme Court rejected it because the federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests"—and the right to valuable economic information is not one of them.

---

### What the right-to-control theory was

Under this theory, the government could establish wire fraud by showing that a defendant "schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The victim's "property" under this theory was defined as "the right to control [his] assets."

In *Ciminelli*, a construction company paid a lobbyist to obtain state-funded contracts. The government's theory was that the state was deprived of its "right to control" its assets because it made contracting decisions without complete information about how the process had been manipulated.

### Why the Court rejected it

The fraud statutes (mail, wire, and by extension, securities fraud) protect only "traditional property interests." The Court held that "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest."

The theory attempted to stretch fraud liability too far by treating the deprivation of information itself as the property harm. But not every deprivation of information that affects decisionmaking constitutes a property interest protected by fraud statutes. The Court explained that the right-to-control theory "cannot form the basis for a conviction" because it "divorces the concept of property from its traditional meaning."

### Why this doesn't mean the right to control your assets isn't protected

Your question is understandable: don't you have a property right to control your own assets?

Yes—but that's not what the right-to-control theory actually targeted. The theory wasn't about depriving someone of their assets. It was about depriving them of *information* they needed to make decisions *about* those assets. The "property" under the theory was the information itself, not the underlying assets. The victim might have received full value for their money—they just did so without all the facts.

### The contrast with fraudulent inducement

The Fifth Circuit's opinion helpfully contrasts this with the "fraudulent-inducement theory," which *is* valid. Under fraudulent inducement, a defendant uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into handing over money or property. The property interest is the money or property itself—not information, and not an abstract right to make informed decisions.

In *Constantinescu*, the court found the indictment alleged fraudulent inducement: defendants allegedly lied to induce followers to purchase securities (i.e., part with money). That's a traditional property interest—the followers' money. The "right to valuable information" was not the property the defendants were charged with scheming to obtain.

---

**Bottom line:** The right-to-control theory treated informational deprivation as the property harm. The Supreme Court rejected that because federal fraud statutes protect traditional property—money, goods, land—not the right to make fully informed economic decisions.

### How does the "fraudulent-inducement theory" differ from the "right-to-control theory" in federal fraud law?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the Fifth Circuit says the indictment properly alleges a 'fraudulent-inducement theory' and distinguishes it from the 'right-to-control theory' rejected in Ciminelli. The court cites Kousisis for the proposition that fraudulent inducement 'is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory' and that it 'protects money and property.' But I'm struggling to see the practical difference. In both theories, the defendant deceives the victim to get something of value. Why is one valid and the other not? What exactly makes fraudulent inducence different from depriving someone of the ability to make an informed economic decision?
Answer:
### Answer

The distinction turns on **what the defendant seeks to deprive the victim of**—not how the deception is accomplished.

**The Right-to-Control Theory (Invalid)**

Under the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli*, the government attempted to define the victim's property interest as "the right to control [his] assets" or "valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Supreme Court held this theory invalid because federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests," and "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest."

The problem is not the deception itself, but that the alleged deprivation is of something intangible—the ability to make an informed choice—rather than money or property. The theory "cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction" because it expands fraud law beyond its statutory property requirement.

**The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Valid)**

Under fraudulent inducement, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The deception is the means, but the object of the scheme is to obtain the victim's money or property directly.

The Court in *Kousisis* confirmed that this theory "protects money and property" and "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the victim's money or property through material misrepresentations—regardless of whether the victim suffers net economic loss.

**The Practical Difference**

Both theories involve deception. But:

| Right-to-Control | Fraudulent Inducement |
|------------------|----------------------|
| Defendant deprives victim of **information** needed to make an economic decision | Defendant deprives victim of **money or property** by tricking them into a transaction |
| The "property" alleged is the right to informed decision-making | The property alleged is traditional: money, securities, etc. |
| Invalid—no traditional property interest | Valid—money or property is actually transferred |

**In Constantinescu**

The indictment alleged that defendants "induced" followers "to purchase securities" through misrepresentations, causing them to "part with their money." Followers weren't just denied information; they were tricked into handing over actual property (money paid for securities). This is classic fraudulent inducement—the scheme's object was obtaining the followers' money, not merely controlling their decisions.

### Does federal fraud law require proof that the defendant intended to cause economic harm to the victim?
Question:
The defendants in Constantinescu argued that the indictment fails to allege intent to defraud because it only shows they intended to profit - not that they intended to harm their followers. The court cites Kousisis holding that fraud statutes 'do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss' and that a defendant can be liable 'regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.' This seems counterintuitive to me - I thought fraud required an intent to deceive AND cause harm. If the victims received market-value securities, where's the harm element? How can you have 'intent to defraud' without intent to cause economic injury?
Answer:
### Answer

No, federal fraud law does not require proof that the defendant intended to cause economic harm to the victim. The confusion arises from conflating two different concepts: the required "harm" element of fraud, and "net economic loss."

**The Legal Standard**

As the Fifth Circuit explains, the "intent to defraud" element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." However, this "harm" is satisfied by the **deprivation of money or property through deception**—not by an intent to leave the victim economically worse off.

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), directly addressed this question. The Court held that a defendant violates federal fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." Under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," a defendant uses material misrepresentations "to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss."

**Application to Constantinescu**

The defendants argued they merely intended to profit, not to harm their followers. The court rejected this as "a distinction without a difference." The indictment alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." This is sufficient because:

1. Defendants intended to deceive their followers about their trading positions; and
2. The intended "harm" was obtaining followers' money through this deception.

That followers may have received market-value securities is irrelevant. As the court emphasizes, quoting *Kousisis*, "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss." The fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" relationship where the victim's loss equals the defendant's gain.

**The Key Distinction**

The "harm" element in fraud law addresses the *deprivation*—the victim parting with money or property due to deception. It does not require proof that the defendant intended the victim to suffer *net economic injury*. The wrong is in obtaining property through deceit, not in the ultimate economic outcome for the victim.

### What constitutes a "traditional property interest" versus "valuable economic information" under federal fraud statutes?
Question:
Ciminelli says federal fraud statutes protect only 'traditional property interests' and that 'the right to valuable economic information...is not a traditional property interest.' But then the Constantinescu court says inducing followers to purchase securities IS depriving them of a protected property interest (money). I'm confused about where the line is drawn. When I buy a stock, I'm voluntarily parting with money in exchange for something - how is that 'deprivation' of property? And why is depriving someone of 'valuable economic information' (like knowing a stock promoter is secretly selling) not protected, but depriving them of money is? What makes something a 'traditional' property interest?
Answer:
The distinction turns on **what the defendant's scheme aims to obtain from the victim**—not on whether the victim suffers a net economic loss.

## Traditional Property vs. Valuable Economic Information

**Traditional property interests** are concrete, tangible assets: money, land, goods, or contractual rights to receive such things. The fraud statutes protect against schemes designed to *take* these assets from victims through deception.

**Valuable economic information**—such as the right to make an informed financial decision—is not itself a property interest. As the Fifth Circuit explains, citing *Ciminelli*, "the right to valuable economic information...is not a traditional property interest." A fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the theory that the defendant deprived victims of information they needed to make better choices.

## Why the "Deprivation" Analysis Differs

You're right that buying stock is voluntary. But the *manner* of obtaining that money matters. The court distinguishes two theories:

| Theory | What Defendant Seeks | Protected? |
|--------|---------------------|------------|
| **Right-to-control** | Deprive victim of information for decisionmaking | No |
| **Fraudulent-inducement** | Obtain victim's money/property through misrepresentation | Yes |

Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the money through deception—**even if the victim receives something of equal value in return**.

## The Key Distinction in This Case

The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued the indictment merely alleged they deprived followers of "valuable economic information" (i.e., knowing the promoters were secretly selling). The court rejected this characterization because the indictment went further:

> "[I]t alleges that defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property."

The scheme's *object* was obtaining followers' money. The deception (hiding their true trading positions) was the *means*, not the property interest itself. This distinguishes the case from *Ciminelli*, where the government's theory rested entirely on depriving victims of information.

## Why Money Is Protected but Economic Information Is Not

Money is a traditional property interest because it is a discrete, transferable asset. When fraud induces someone to hand over money, that money is "obtained" by the fraudster.

Economic information—the opportunity to make a fully informed choice—is too intangible and abstract. A victim deprived only of information still retains all their property; they've merely been denied knowledge. The fraud statutes do not convert every dishonest business dealing into property theft.

The line thus depends on what the defendant sought to *extract* from the victim. If the scheme aims at the victim's money or tangible property, it falls within the fraud statutes. If it aims only at manipulating the victim's decisionmaking process without obtaining their property, it does not.

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) relate to mail and wire fraud statutes?
Question:
The court in Constantinescu mentions that § 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes' and that courts 'have given the terms similar treatment.' I'm trying to understand the relationship between these statutes. Does this mean that when interpreting securities fraud under § 1348, courts should look to mail fraud (§ 1341) and wire fraud (§ 1343) precedent? Why would Congress create a separate securities fraud statute if it essentially mirrors the existing fraud statutes? And what concepts exactly are 'borrowed' - is it just the 'scheme to defraud' language, or something broader?
Answer:
### Answer

Your intuition is correct: courts interpreting § 1348 do look to mail and wire fraud precedent. The opinion explicitly states that because § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," courts "have given the terms similar treatment," often relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges.

**What concepts are borrowed?**

The opinion identifies two core elements borrowed from §§ 1341 and 1343:

1. **"Scheme to defraud"** — requiring a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money."

2. **"Intent to defraud"** — requiring "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit."

These definitions come from mail and wire fraud precedent, and the Fifth Circuit applies them directly to § 1348.

**How the borrowing works in practice**

The opinion demonstrates this borrowing by applying Supreme Court precedent from wire fraud cases—*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*—to resolve the § 1348 claims. For example:

- The court adopts *Kousisis*'s formulation that a defendant commits securities fraud when he "engaged in deception" and "[o]btaining the victim's money or property" was "an object' of his fraud."

- The court uses *Ciminelli* to determine what constitutes a protected property interest, rejecting the "right-to-control theory" and distinguishing it from the "fraudulent-inducement theory" properly alleged here.

This shows that interpretive questions—such as what qualifies as "property" or what "intent to defraud" requires—are resolved by looking to established mail and wire fraud jurisprudence.

**Why a separate statute?**

The opinion doesn't directly address Congress's rationale for enacting § 1348 separately. However, two points are evident from the opinion:

1. § 1348 applies specifically to fraud "in connection with . . . any security"—providing a targeted charging option when the fraud involves securities markets.

2. The statute has "scant caselaw construing" it in the Fifth Circuit, suggesting it remains a relatively newer tool compared to the well-developed mail and wire fraud jurisprudence Congress expected courts to draw upon.

In short: Congress created a specialized fraud offense for securities contexts but intentionally adopted the familiar concepts and standards from §§ 1341 and 1343, allowing courts to apply decades of accumulated precedent.

### What standard do appellate courts use when reviewing the sufficiency of an indictment?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu says it reviews 'the sufficiency of an indictment de novo' and must 'take the allegations of the indictment as true.' The court also lays out three requirements: the indictment must '(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.' But I'm confused about the practical application. If the court takes allegations 'as true,' isn't it just rubber-stamping the indictment? At what point does an indictment become insufficient - does it need to include evidentiary details, or just legal conclusions?
Answer:
### The Standard: Legal Sufficiency, Not Evidentiary Weight

Your confusion is understandable, but the key is recognizing what "taking allegations as true" actually means in practice. The court isn't rubber-stamping anything—it's applying a specific analytical framework.

**What "Taking Allegations as True" Means**

When the court says it takes allegations as true, it means that at the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court accepts the indictment's *factual allegations* as given. The court does not assess whether those facts can be proven, whether they're credible, or whether evidence supports them. Those are questions for trial.

But—and this is critical—the court independently evaluates whether those alleged facts, if true, satisfy each *legal element* of the charged offense. This is where the rubber meets the road.

**When Indictments Fail**

An indictment becomes insufficient when, even accepting all factual allegations as true, it fails to allege facts that would constitute each material element of the offense. The opinion makes this clear: *"In sum, to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense."*

The Fifth Circuit's three-part test essentially asks:
1. Did the indictment list each element? (Legal adequacy)
2. Does the defendant understand what they're charged with? (Notice)
3. Could the defendant plead double jeopardy if prosecuted again? (Protection)

**Application in Constantinescu**

This case illustrates the distinction perfectly. The defendants argued the indictment failed to allege a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud"—two statutory elements under § 1348. They characterized the allegations as merely depriving victims of "valuable economic information."

The court rejected this not by deferring to the indictment's characterization, but by *independently analyzing* whether the factual allegations satisfied the legal elements. The indictment alleged defendants:
- Made misrepresentations about securities
- Induced followers to purchase those securities
- Sold for profit (the "dump")

The court determined these facts, if true, constituted a "fraudulent-inducement theory"—distinct from the legally insufficient "right-to-control theory" rejected in *Ciminelli*. Under *Kousisis*, obtaining money through material misrepresentations is fraud regardless of whether the victim suffers net economic loss.

**Evidentiary Details vs. Factual Allegations**

Indictments need not include evidentiary detail—witnesses, documents, dates, or the government's proof. They must, however, allege *facts* (not mere legal conclusions) that satisfy each element. An indictment saying "Defendant committed securities fraud" would be insufficient. An indictment alleging "Defendant posted false information to induce purchases, then sold for profit" states facts that, if true, satisfy the elements.

The district court's error was mischaracterizing what the indictment alleged; the Fifth Circuit corrected that legal determination through de novo review, not deference.

### Why does fraudulently inducing someone to purchase securities count as depriving them of "money or property" under fraud statutes?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the defendants allegedly convinced followers to buy securities through misrepresentations about their trading positions. The court says this sufficiently alleges deprivation of 'money or property' because followers were induced 'to part with their money.' But the followers received the securities they paid for - they weren't outright stolen from. In a typical pump-and-dump, victims buy shares at inflated prices, but they still receive actual shares. How does this constitute 'deprivation' of money or property under fraud statutes? Is the theory that any transaction induced by fraud counts as deprivation, even if the victim receives something in return? And does the victim need to suffer a net loss for there to be 'deprivation'?
Answer:
The question touches on a fundamental issue in fraud law that the Fifth Circuit addresses directly in this opinion. The short answer is that under the **fraudulent-inducement theory**, a defendant commits fraud when they use material misrepresentations to trick a victim into parting with money or property—**regardless of whether the victim receives something in return or suffers a net economic loss**.

## The Distinguishing Theories of Fraud

The opinion draws a critical distinction between two fraud theories:

1. **Right-to-control theory** (rejected in *Ciminelli*): This theory attempted to treat "valuable economic information" or the "right to control assets" as protected property. The Supreme Court rejected this because fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests"—and the right to make informed economic decisions isn't one.

2. **Fraudulent-inducement theory** (affirmed in *Kousisis*): This theory applies when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." This *is* a valid basis for fraud liability because it involves actual money or property, not mere information.

## Why Victims' Receipt of Securities Doesn't Defeat the Fraud Allegation

The defendants argued (as you suggest in your question) that followers received the securities they paid for, so no "deprivation" occurred. The court rejects this on two grounds:

**First, the fraud statutes target obtaining money or property by deception—not just causing net harm.** The court quotes *Kousisis* directly: a defendant may be liable for fraud "even when he 'provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.'" The fraud is complete once the defendant induces the victim to part with money or property through material deception. The subsequent transfer of securities doesn't negate the initial wrongful deprivation.

**Second, no showing of net economic loss is required.** The court explicitly holds that "the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme." The focus is on the defendant's objective—to obtain the victim's money—and the deceptive means used to achieve it. As the court states: "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

## Why This Makes Sense

The rationale is straightforward: fraud statutes criminalize obtaining property through deception. If defendants could avoid liability simply by providing *something* of value in return, fraud would be extremely difficult to prosecute. A con artist who sells fake watches but delivers actual (worthless) watches would escape liability. The law instead focuses on whether the victim was induced to part with money or property based on material misrepresentations—not whether the victim came out ahead or behind in the transaction.
