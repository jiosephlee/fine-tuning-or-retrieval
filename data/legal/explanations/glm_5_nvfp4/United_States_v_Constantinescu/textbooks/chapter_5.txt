Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # Significance and Implications

## Clarifying the Boundary Between Valid and Invalid Fraud Theories Post-Ciminelli

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Constantinescu* provides crucial clarification on the boundary between valid and invalid fraud theories following the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. Understanding this boundary is essential for properly analyzing fraud charges under federal statutes, including the securities fraud provision at issue here.

In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, which had allowed convictions based solely on a defendant's scheme to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that such information is not a "traditional property interest" and therefore cannot serve as the basis for a fraud conviction. This narrowing of fraud doctrine raised questions about whether traditional fraud prosecutions—particularly those involving deceptive schemes—could survive scrutiny.

*Constantinescu* answers that question decisively. The Fifth Circuit explains that *Ciminelli* did not eliminate fraud liability; it merely rejected one specific theory. The opinion draws a clear line: a fraud theory is invalid when it relies solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information, but a fraud theory remains valid when it involves a defendant fraudulently inducing a victim to part with money or property. The latter scenario constitutes a "fraudulent-inducement theory," which the Supreme Court explicitly distinguished from the discredited right-to-control theory in *Kousisis v. United States*.

This distinction is critical for practitioners and students alike. The fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property" in the traditional sense—assets that fraud statutes have long protected. When a defendant makes material misrepresentations to trick someone into handing over money or property, the fraud is complete, regardless of whether the victim also lost control over information or decision-making authority. *Constantinescu* thus confirms that *Ciminelli* did not upend fraud law; it simply required prosecutors to focus on the traditional property interests that have always animated fraud statutes.

## Implications for Social-Media-Based Fraud Prosecutions

The *Constantinescu* opinion arises from a modern fact pattern: a "pump and dump" scheme executed through social media platforms. Defendants with large online followings allegedly posted false information about securities to inflate prices artificially, then sold their holdings for profit. This fact pattern tests whether established fraud principles apply with equal force to technologically novel schemes.

The Fifth Circuit's analysis confirms that the medium of fraud does not alter the legal analysis. Whether defendants make misrepresentations through traditional channels—mail, telephone, in-person meetings—or through posts on social media platforms, the fundamental question remains the same: did the defendant scheme to obtain the victim's money or property through deception? The indictment here alleged that defendants used social media posts to "pump" securities by posting "false and misleading information" that "induced" followers to purchase those securities. This straightforward fraudulent-inducement narrative survives *Ciminelli* because the alleged scheme targeted followers' money, not merely their information or decision-making capacity.

The opinion thus provides important guidance for future prosecutions involving social media. Prosecutors need not develop novel legal theories to address technology-driven fraud. Instead, they can rely on time-tested fraudulent-inducement theories, focusing on how defendants used deception to cause victims to part with money or property. The technology may be new, but the fraud is familiar.

## The Government's Burden at the Indictment Stage

*Constantinescu* reinforces important principles regarding the standard for evaluating the sufficiency of an indictment. The court reviews de novo whether an indictment states an offense, taking all allegations as true. At this stage, the court does not weigh evidence or assess whether the government can prove its case at trial. Rather, the court asks a more limited question: assuming the indictment's allegations are true, does the document state each element of the charged offense?

The court's analysis demonstrates how this standard operates in practice. The government alleged that defendants posted false information to induce followers to purchase securities and that defendants profited $114 million from this scheme. Accepting these allegations as true, the court concluded that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. The court did not ask whether the securities purchased by followers had value, whether followers suffered actual losses, or whether the government could prove the falsity of defendants' statements. Those are questions for trial.

This procedural posture matters significantly. Defendants sought dismissal before trial, arguing that the indictment failed to allege a cognizable fraud theory. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, emphasizing that the indictment's allegations—read generously—satisfied the elements of securities fraud. The opinion thus illustrates how the indictment stage provides defendants with a meaningful opportunity to challenge legally deficient charges while preserving the government's ability to prove its case at trial.

## Relationship Between Defendants' Gain and Victims' Deprivation

One of the most significant aspects of *Constantinescu* is its treatment of the relationship between defendants' gain and victims' deprivation. Defendants argued that they intended only to profit—not to harm their followers. In their view, followers who purchased securities received something of value and therefore suffered no injury that could support a fraud conviction. The Fifth Circuit, relying on *Kousisis*, rejected this argument.

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* held that fraud statutes "do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme." A defendant violates fraud law when he schemes to obtain the victim's money or property through deception, "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraudulent-inducement theory focuses on what the defendant sought to obtain through deception, not on whether the victim suffered net pecuniary harm. As the Court explained, a "fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return.

*Constantinescu* applies this principle to the securities fraud context. The indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities—that is, to part with money. The fact that followers received securities in exchange—securities that may have retained market value—does not negate the fraud. The defendants' object was to obtain money, and they accomplished this through deception. The Fifth Circuit also cited *United States v. Baker* for the proposition that fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain. The two inquiries—defendant's gain and victim's deprivation—are related but distinct.

This principle has significant practical consequences. In many fraud cases, particularly investment frauds, victims may receive something of value that complicates loss calculations. *Constantinescu* confirms that such complications do not defeat fraud liability at the pleading stage. If the defendant obtained the victim's money or property through material deception, the fraud elements are satisfied.

## Guidance for Future Securities Fraud Charging Decisions

The *Constantinescu* opinion offers valuable guidance for prosecutors and defense attorneys navigating securities fraud charges in the post-*Ciminelli* landscape. For prosecutors, the decision underscores the importance of grounding fraud charges in fraudulent-inducement theories that clearly identify how victims were deprived of money or property through deception.

The opinion notes that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Practitioners should therefore look to mail and wire fraud precedent when analyzing securities fraud charges, as courts have given these terms "similar treatment." This cross-referencing provides a substantial body of caselaw to draw upon when constructing or challenging fraud theories.

Prosecutors should also note that actual economic harm to victims is not an element of securities fraud. The focus remains on the defendant's scheme and intent—specifically, whether the defendant engaged in deception with the object of obtaining the victim's money or property. An indictment that clearly alleges these elements will survive a motion to dismiss, even if victims ultimately received something of value or did not suffer demonstrable losses.

For defense attorneys, *Constantinescu* illustrates the limits of *Ciminelli*. The Supreme Court's rejection of the right-to-control theory does not provide a blanket defense to fraud charges. Arguments that the indictment merely alleges deprivation of information or economic decision-making authority will fail if the indictment also alleges that victims parted with money or property through fraudulent inducement. Defense challenges must instead focus on the adequacy of allegations regarding deception, scheme, and intent—or await trial to contest the government's evidence.

Finally, the opinion's treatment of social media fraud confirms that emerging technologies do not create new defenses or require novel legal frameworks. Practitioners on both sides should focus on the fundamental questions: What did the defendant represent? Were those representations materially false? Did the defendant intend to deceive? Did the defendant scheme to obtain money or property through that deception? If these questions can be answered affirmatively based on the indictment's allegations, the fraud charge will survive.
