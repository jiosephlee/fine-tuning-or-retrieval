Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 4: # The Fifth Circuit's Analysis and Holding

## Why the indictment alleges a scheme to defraud under fraudulent-inducement theory

The Fifth Circuit began its analysis by identifying the legal framework governing securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The statute prohibits schemes to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security" and schemes to obtain money or property through false pretenses. The court explained that securities fraud requires two elements: (1) a "scheme to defraud" and (2) "intent to defraud." A scheme to defraud involves a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money."

The court concluded that the indictment properly alleged a scheme to defraud under what the Supreme Court has termed the "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The indictment alleged that defendants posted false and misleading information about securities on social media to induce their followers to purchase those securities. By alleging that defendants tricked followers into parting with their money to buy securities, the indictment properly stated a scheme to defraud. The court emphasized that the fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property"—traditional property interests that federal fraud statutes were designed to protect.

## Why this case is distinguishable from Ciminelli's right-to-control theory

The defendants argued that the indictment was insufficient under the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, which allowed convictions based solely on a defendant scheming to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and because "[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest," the right-to-control theory could not support a fraud conviction.

The Fifth Circuit distinguished *Ciminelli* decisively. The indictment in this case did not rely on the right-to-control theory at all—it did not allege that defendants schemed to deprive followers of valuable information needed to make economic decisions. Instead, the indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities, depriving them of money or property directly. The court explained that the fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." While the right-to-control theory was defective because it sought to protect a non-traditional property interest (valuable economic information), the fraudulent-inducement theory protects the traditional property interests of money and property that victims hand over due to deception. Because the indictment alleged defendants obtained their followers' money through deception, it did not run afoul of *Ciminelli*.

## Why the indictment sufficiently alleges intent to defraud

The intent-to-defraud element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." The defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception, but they challenged whether it alleged intent to cause harm. They argued that the indictment only showed they intended to profit from their scheme, not to injure their followers.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument based on the Supreme Court's recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Kousisis*, the defendant obtained government contracts by falsely representing that his company would subcontract with a disadvantaged business. The Court held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss.

Applying *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit found the indictment's allegations sufficient. The indictment alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." By fraudulently inducing followers to purchase securities, defendants allegedly intended to deceive them and cause the harm that results from depriving someone of money or property through deception. The followers' subsequent receipt of market-value securities did not negate the fraud allegations, because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm to the victim.

## Defendants' profit motive versus victim harm: a distinction without a difference

The defendants attempted to draw a distinction between their intent to profit and an intent to harm their followers. They contended that the indictment merely showed they sought to enrich themselves, not to cause injury. The Fifth Circuit rejected this framing as "a distinction without a difference."

The court explained that a fraud is complete when a defendant "has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss." The indictment's allegation that defendants pumped and dumped securities to gain a profit demonstrated that they "had money in mind." Their object was to obtain money from followers through deception. That defendants may have profited without causing their followers to suffer net losses was immaterial—the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must equal the defendant's gain. A defendant may be liable for fraud even when providing something of equal value in return.

This principle makes sense in the context of a pump-and-dump scheme. Defendants allegedly induced followers to buy securities at artificially inflated prices. Even if followers received securities worth what they paid at the moment of purchase, the transaction was tainted by fraud. The defendants obtained money through deception, and that is sufficient to allege intent to defraud.

## The court's holding and disposition: reversal and remand

The Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged both elements of securities fraud: a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud. The court held that the indictment properly charged defendants under the fraudulent-inducement theory, which protects traditional property interests in money and property, distinguishing this case from *Ciminelli*'s rejection of the right-to-control theory. The court further held that the indictment sufficiently alleged intent to defraud because it showed defendants schemed to obtain their followers' money through deception, and the fraud statutes do not require intent to cause net economic harm.

Accordingly, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's dismissal of the indictment and remanded the case for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The decision means that the government's charges against the defendants will proceed, and the case will return to the district court for further litigation. The defendants will face trial on the conspiracy and securities fraud charges unless they succeed on other grounds.
