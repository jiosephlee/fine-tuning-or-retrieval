Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # The Legal Framework for Securities Fraud Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348

## The Text and Structure of 18 U.S.C. § 1348

The securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, serves as the statutory foundation for the charges against the defendants in this case. The statute prohibits two distinct types of misconduct. First, it criminalizes any "scheme to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security." Second, it prohibits schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security." The statute thus provides two complementary theories of prosecution: one focused on fraudulent schemes affecting securities transactions generally, and another requiring the specific intent to obtain money or property through deception.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* were charged under both theories. The indictment alleged conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, which prohibits conspiracies to violate § 1348, as well as multiple counts of securities fraud under § 1348 itself. Understanding the statutory language is essential because the district court's dismissal turned on whether the indictment adequately alleged the deprivation of "money or property"—a phrase that lies at the heart of what constitutes a cognizable fraud offense.

## Element One: Scheme to Defraud Defined

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* articulates the first element of securities fraud as a "scheme to defraud." This element requires a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived." The court draws this definition from its wire fraud precedent, illustrating how fraud concepts translate across statutory contexts.

Crucially, the "scheme to defraud" element is not satisfied by deception alone. The deception must be aimed at obtaining something of value. In *Constantinescu*, the court explains that a defendant commits securities fraud when he "'engaged in deception'" and "[o]btaining the victim's money or property" was "'an object' of his fraud." This formulation, drawn from Supreme Court precedent, emphasizes the instrumental relationship between the deception and the sought-after gain.

The distinction between valid and invalid theories of fraud becomes critical here. The court contrasts the "right-to-control theory" rejected by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* with the "fraudulent-inducement theory" the government pursued in *Constantinescu*. Under the right-to-control theory, the government had attempted to establish fraud based solely on depriving victims of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Supreme Court rejected this theory because the fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." By contrast, the fraudulent-inducement theory alleged in *Constantinescu*—"whereby a defendant 'uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property'"—properly targets the deprivation of money or property, not mere information.

## Element Two: Intent to Defraud Defined

The second element of securities fraud is "intent to defraud." The Fifth Circuit defines this as "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." This two-part definition captures both the defendant's deceptive purpose and the anticipated consequences of that deception.

A critical question in *Constantinescu* was whether the "harm" element requires proof that the defendant intended to cause the victim economic loss. The defendants argued that because they intended only to profit from their scheme—not to harm their followers—the indictment failed to allege the requisite intent to defraud. The court rejected this argument based on Supreme Court precedent holding that "the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme."

The Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis* clarified that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraudulent-inducement theory thus permits conviction "regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." This principle directly refutes the defendants' contention that their followers' receipt of market-value securities negated the fraud charges. The indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped to gain a profit" demonstrated that they "had money in mind," which suffices to establish the intent element.

## De Novo Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency

The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. This standard means the appellate court considers the question anew, giving no deference to the district court's conclusions. De novo review is appropriate here because determining whether an indictment states an offense is a question of law.

Under this standard, the court "is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated." This approach serves a practical function at the pretrial stage: the court does not weigh evidence or assess credibility but instead assumes the government can prove what it has alleged. The question is purely legal—whether those allegations, if proven, would establish each element of the charged offense.

## Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud Jurisprudence

A notable aspect of the Fifth Circuit's analysis is its reliance on mail and wire fraud precedent to interpret § 1348. The court acknowledges that "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." However, because § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," courts have given the terms similar treatment, "often relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges."

This approach reflects a broader pattern in federal criminal law. When Congress enacts a new fraud statute using familiar terminology, courts naturally look to existing interpretations of similar language. The mail and wire fraud statutes—18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 1343—have generated extensive jurisprudence defining "scheme to defraud," "intent to defraud," and related concepts. By incorporating these well-developed definitions, courts provide consistency and predictability in fraud law.

The Supreme Court's decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, though arising under the wire fraud statute, thus directly inform the securities fraud analysis. Both cases addressed the property requirement in federal fraud statutes, and their holdings apply equally to § 1348's prohibition on schemes "to obtain . . . any money or property."

## Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment

Beyond the substantive elements of the offense, the court addresses the formal requirements for a sufficient indictment. An indictment must "(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." These requirements serve distinct but related functions in the criminal process.

The first requirement—enumerating each element—ensures that the defendant and the court can verify that the prosecution has a valid legal theory. The second requirement—fairly informing the defendant—enables effective preparation of a defense. The third requirement—enabling a double jeopardy defense—protects against successive prosecutions for the same conduct. Together, these requirements reflect the constitutional and procedural safeguards inherent in the grand jury system.

In *Constantinescu*, the district court concluded that the indictment failed the first requirement because it did not allege the deprivation of a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment properly alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory under which defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." Because the indictment adequately alleged both a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud," it met the standard for sufficiency and the case was remanded for further proceedings.
