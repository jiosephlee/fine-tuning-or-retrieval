Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Securities Fraud in the Age of Social Media: Applying Old Statutes to New Schemes

The Fifth Circuit's decision last week in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers a revealing glimpse into how federal courts are adapting venerable fraud statutes to the realities of modern financial markets. The case involves something that would have been unimaginable to the drafters of the mail and wire fraud statutes: a coordinated "pump and dump" scheme orchestrated through social media, in which influencers with hundreds of thousands of followers allegedly used their platforms to artificially inflate stock prices and cash out before the crash. But the legal questions the case presents are surprisingly traditional—questions about what counts as "property" under federal fraud law and what the government must prove to establish criminal intent.

The defendants—eight social media personalities with substantial followings—were indicted for conspiracy to commit securities fraud and multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349. According to the superseding indictment, they held themselves out as skilled traders, posted false and misleading information about securities to induce their followers to buy, and then secretly sold their own positions at a profit. The government alleged they made $114 million from the scheme. The district court, however, dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it failed to allege deprivation of a "traditional property interest" as required by the Supreme Court's recent decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*.

Judge Engelhardt's opinion for the Fifth Circuit reverses that dismissal, and in doing so, provides a useful roadmap for how lower courts should apply the Supreme Court's recent fraud jurisprudence to novel factual contexts. The opinion's significance lies less in breaking new doctrinal ground than in demonstrating how existing principles operate in the digital economy.

## The Right-to-Control Theory Versus Fraudulent Inducement

The defendants' core argument—and the district court's reasoning—rested on the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Ciminelli*. In that case, the Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, under which prosecutors had secured convictions by showing merely that defendants deprived victims of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests," not abstract rights to accurate information.

On its face, this might seem to help the defendants. After all, the indictment alleged that they deprived their followers of accurate information about stock prices. But as Judge Engelhardt correctly observes, the indictment went further. It alleged that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." This is not a right-to-control theory; it is a classic fraudulent-inducement theory, under which a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

The distinction matters. Under the right-to-control theory, the property interest is purely informational—the victim's right to make decisions with complete information. Under fraudulent inducement, the property interest is the money or property itself, which the victim surrenders based on deception. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* explicitly confirmed that fraudulent inducement "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory" because it "protects money and property."

The Fifth Circuit's application of this distinction is straightforward but important. Social media followers who bought securities based on allegedly false statements weren't merely deprived of information; they were induced to part with actual money. The property interest is traditional, even if the mechanism of fraud is thoroughly modern.

## Intent to Defraud Without Intent to Harm

The opinion's second significant holding concerns the intent element. The defendants argued that even if they induced purchases through misrepresentations, they intended only to profit themselves, not to harm their followers. The court rejects this argument, relying on the Supreme Court's holding in *Kousisis* that the fraud statutes "do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme."

This principle may seem counterintuitive—how can there be fraud without harm?—but it has deep roots in fraud law. A defendant who lies to obtain a contract may provide goods or services of equal value to what he receives. The victim may even be economically better off. But the fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the victim's money or property "under materially false pretenses."

The Fifth Circuit correctly applies this principle to the social-media context. Defendants argued that their followers received market-value securities in exchange for their money, so no fraud occurred. But as the court notes, that argument "is a distinction without a difference." The indictment alleged that defendants' objective was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing purchases—showing they "had money in mind," as *Kousisis* put it. That suffices for intent to defraud.

## Implications for Digital-Age Enforcement

The decision signals that federal fraud statutes remain flexible tools for prosecuting deceptive schemes, regardless of the technological medium. The Fifth Circuit's analysis suggests that courts will look past novel factual contexts to the underlying economic reality: Did defendants use deception to obtain money or property? If so, traditional fraud principles apply.

This has significant implications for enforcement in the digital marketplace. Social media influencers who promote financial products, cryptocurrencies, or securities should understand that their medium does not immunize them from fraud liability. The fact that followers "voluntarily" choose to purchase securities based on influencer recommendations does not defeat fraud charges if those recommendations were materially false and intended to manipulate prices for the influencer's benefit.

The decision also illustrates how the Supreme Court's recent fraud decisions, while constraining certain prosecutorial theories, leave ample room for traditional fraud prosecutions. *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* did not revolutionize fraud law; they clarified its boundaries. Prosecutors can still bring fraud charges when defendants use deception to obtain money or property—they simply cannot rely on theories that treat intangible informational harms as property deprivations.

For practitioners and scholars, *Constantinescu* demonstrates that old statutes can reach new schemes when courts focus on the essential elements: deception, property, and intent. The social media age has created new mechanisms for fraud, but the legal framework for addressing it remains remarkably stable.
