Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What standard do appellate courts use when reviewing the sufficiency of an indictment?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu says it reviews 'the sufficiency of an indictment de novo' and must 'take the allegations of the indictment as true.' The court also lays out three requirements: the indictment must '(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.' But I'm confused about the practical application. If the court takes allegations 'as true,' isn't it just rubber-stamping the indictment? At what point does an indictment become insufficient - does it need to include evidentiary details, or just legal conclusions?
Answer:
### The Standard: Legal Sufficiency, Not Evidentiary Weight

Your confusion is understandable, but the key is recognizing what "taking allegations as true" actually means in practice. The court isn't rubber-stamping anything—it's applying a specific analytical framework.

**What "Taking Allegations as True" Means**

When the court says it takes allegations as true, it means that at the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court accepts the indictment's *factual allegations* as given. The court does not assess whether those facts can be proven, whether they're credible, or whether evidence supports them. Those are questions for trial.

But—and this is critical—the court independently evaluates whether those alleged facts, if true, satisfy each *legal element* of the charged offense. This is where the rubber meets the road.

**When Indictments Fail**

An indictment becomes insufficient when, even accepting all factual allegations as true, it fails to allege facts that would constitute each material element of the offense. The opinion makes this clear: *"In sum, to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense."*

The Fifth Circuit's three-part test essentially asks:
1. Did the indictment list each element? (Legal adequacy)
2. Does the defendant understand what they're charged with? (Notice)
3. Could the defendant plead double jeopardy if prosecuted again? (Protection)

**Application in Constantinescu**

This case illustrates the distinction perfectly. The defendants argued the indictment failed to allege a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud"—two statutory elements under § 1348. They characterized the allegations as merely depriving victims of "valuable economic information."

The court rejected this not by deferring to the indictment's characterization, but by *independently analyzing* whether the factual allegations satisfied the legal elements. The indictment alleged defendants:
- Made misrepresentations about securities
- Induced followers to purchase those securities
- Sold for profit (the "dump")

The court determined these facts, if true, constituted a "fraudulent-inducement theory"—distinct from the legally insufficient "right-to-control theory" rejected in *Ciminelli*. Under *Kousisis*, obtaining money through material misrepresentations is fraud regardless of whether the victim suffers net economic loss.

**Evidentiary Details vs. Factual Allegations**

Indictments need not include evidentiary detail—witnesses, documents, dates, or the government's proof. They must, however, allege *facts* (not mere legal conclusions) that satisfy each element. An indictment saying "Defendant committed securities fraud" would be insufficient. An indictment alleging "Defendant posted false information to induce purchases, then sold for profit" states facts that, if true, satisfy the elements.

The district court's error was mischaracterizing what the indictment alleged; the Fifth Circuit corrected that legal determination through de novo review, not deference.
