Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) relate to mail and wire fraud statutes?
Question:
The court in Constantinescu mentions that § 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes' and that courts 'have given the terms similar treatment.' I'm trying to understand the relationship between these statutes. Does this mean that when interpreting securities fraud under § 1348, courts should look to mail fraud (§ 1341) and wire fraud (§ 1343) precedent? Why would Congress create a separate securities fraud statute if it essentially mirrors the existing fraud statutes? And what concepts exactly are 'borrowed' - is it just the 'scheme to defraud' language, or something broader?
Answer:
### Answer

Your intuition is correct: courts interpreting § 1348 do look to mail and wire fraud precedent. The opinion explicitly states that because § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," courts "have given the terms similar treatment," often relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges.

**What concepts are borrowed?**

The opinion identifies two core elements borrowed from §§ 1341 and 1343:

1. **"Scheme to defraud"** — requiring a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money."

2. **"Intent to defraud"** — requiring "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit."

These definitions come from mail and wire fraud precedent, and the Fifth Circuit applies them directly to § 1348.

**How the borrowing works in practice**

The opinion demonstrates this borrowing by applying Supreme Court precedent from wire fraud cases—*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*—to resolve the § 1348 claims. For example:

- The court adopts *Kousisis*'s formulation that a defendant commits securities fraud when he "engaged in deception" and "[o]btaining the victim's money or property" was "an object' of his fraud."

- The court uses *Ciminelli* to determine what constitutes a protected property interest, rejecting the "right-to-control theory" and distinguishing it from the "fraudulent-inducement theory" properly alleged here.

This shows that interpretive questions—such as what qualifies as "property" or what "intent to defraud" requires—are resolved by looking to established mail and wire fraud jurisprudence.

**Why a separate statute?**

The opinion doesn't directly address Congress's rationale for enacting § 1348 separately. However, two points are evident from the opinion:

1. § 1348 applies specifically to fraud "in connection with . . . any security"—providing a targeted charging option when the fraud involves securities markets.

2. The statute has "scant caselaw construing" it in the Fifth Circuit, suggesting it remains a relatively newer tool compared to the well-developed mail and wire fraud jurisprudence Congress expected courts to draw upon.

In short: Congress created a specialized fraud offense for securities contexts but intentionally adopted the familiar concepts and standards from §§ 1341 and 1343, allowing courts to apply decades of accumulated precedent.
