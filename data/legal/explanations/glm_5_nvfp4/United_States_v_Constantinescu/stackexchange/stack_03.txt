Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Does federal fraud law require proof that the defendant intended to cause economic harm to the victim?
Question:
The defendants in Constantinescu argued that the indictment fails to allege intent to defraud because it only shows they intended to profit - not that they intended to harm their followers. The court cites Kousisis holding that fraud statutes 'do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss' and that a defendant can be liable 'regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.' This seems counterintuitive to me - I thought fraud required an intent to deceive AND cause harm. If the victims received market-value securities, where's the harm element? How can you have 'intent to defraud' without intent to cause economic injury?
Answer:
### Answer

No, federal fraud law does not require proof that the defendant intended to cause economic harm to the victim. The confusion arises from conflating two different concepts: the required "harm" element of fraud, and "net economic loss."

**The Legal Standard**

As the Fifth Circuit explains, the "intent to defraud" element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." However, this "harm" is satisfied by the **deprivation of money or property through deception**—not by an intent to leave the victim economically worse off.

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), directly addressed this question. The Court held that a defendant violates federal fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." Under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," a defendant uses material misrepresentations "to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss."

**Application to Constantinescu**

The defendants argued they merely intended to profit, not to harm their followers. The court rejected this as "a distinction without a difference." The indictment alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." This is sufficient because:

1. Defendants intended to deceive their followers about their trading positions; and
2. The intended "harm" was obtaining followers' money through this deception.

That followers may have received market-value securities is irrelevant. As the court emphasizes, quoting *Kousisis*, "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss." The fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" relationship where the victim's loss equals the defendant's gain.

**The Key Distinction**

The "harm" element in fraud law addresses the *deprivation*—the victim parting with money or property due to deception. It does not require proof that the defendant intended the victim to suffer *net economic injury*. The wrong is in obtaining property through deceit, not in the ultimate economic outcome for the victim.
