Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What constitutes a "traditional property interest" versus "valuable economic information" under federal fraud statutes?
Question:
Ciminelli says federal fraud statutes protect only 'traditional property interests' and that 'the right to valuable economic information...is not a traditional property interest.' But then the Constantinescu court says inducing followers to purchase securities IS depriving them of a protected property interest (money). I'm confused about where the line is drawn. When I buy a stock, I'm voluntarily parting with money in exchange for something - how is that 'deprivation' of property? And why is depriving someone of 'valuable economic information' (like knowing a stock promoter is secretly selling) not protected, but depriving them of money is? What makes something a 'traditional' property interest?
Answer:
The distinction turns on **what the defendant's scheme aims to obtain from the victim**—not on whether the victim suffers a net economic loss.

## Traditional Property vs. Valuable Economic Information

**Traditional property interests** are concrete, tangible assets: money, land, goods, or contractual rights to receive such things. The fraud statutes protect against schemes designed to *take* these assets from victims through deception.

**Valuable economic information**—such as the right to make an informed financial decision—is not itself a property interest. As the Fifth Circuit explains, citing *Ciminelli*, "the right to valuable economic information...is not a traditional property interest." A fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the theory that the defendant deprived victims of information they needed to make better choices.

## Why the "Deprivation" Analysis Differs

You're right that buying stock is voluntary. But the *manner* of obtaining that money matters. The court distinguishes two theories:

| Theory | What Defendant Seeks | Protected? |
|--------|---------------------|------------|
| **Right-to-control** | Deprive victim of information for decisionmaking | No |
| **Fraudulent-inducement** | Obtain victim's money/property through misrepresentation | Yes |

Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the money through deception—**even if the victim receives something of equal value in return**.

## The Key Distinction in This Case

The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued the indictment merely alleged they deprived followers of "valuable economic information" (i.e., knowing the promoters were secretly selling). The court rejected this characterization because the indictment went further:

> "[I]t alleges that defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property."

The scheme's *object* was obtaining followers' money. The deception (hiding their true trading positions) was the *means*, not the property interest itself. This distinguishes the case from *Ciminelli*, where the government's theory rested entirely on depriving victims of information.

## Why Money Is Protected but Economic Information Is Not

Money is a traditional property interest because it is a discrete, transferable asset. When fraud induces someone to hand over money, that money is "obtained" by the fraudster.

Economic information—the opportunity to make a fully informed choice—is too intangible and abstract. A victim deprived only of information still retains all their property; they've merely been denied knowledge. The fraud statutes do not convert every dishonest business dealing into property theft.

The line thus depends on what the defendant sought to *extract* from the victim. If the scheme aims at the victim's money or tangible property, it falls within the fraud statutes. If it aims only at manipulating the victim's decisionmaking process without obtaining their property, it does not.
