Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why does fraudulently inducing someone to purchase securities count as depriving them of "money or property" under fraud statutes?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the defendants allegedly convinced followers to buy securities through misrepresentations about their trading positions. The court says this sufficiently alleges deprivation of 'money or property' because followers were induced 'to part with their money.' But the followers received the securities they paid for - they weren't outright stolen from. In a typical pump-and-dump, victims buy shares at inflated prices, but they still receive actual shares. How does this constitute 'deprivation' of money or property under fraud statutes? Is the theory that any transaction induced by fraud counts as deprivation, even if the victim receives something in return? And does the victim need to suffer a net loss for there to be 'deprivation'?
Answer:
The question touches on a fundamental issue in fraud law that the Fifth Circuit addresses directly in this opinion. The short answer is that under the **fraudulent-inducement theory**, a defendant commits fraud when they use material misrepresentations to trick a victim into parting with money or property—**regardless of whether the victim receives something in return or suffers a net economic loss**.

## The Distinguishing Theories of Fraud

The opinion draws a critical distinction between two fraud theories:

1. **Right-to-control theory** (rejected in *Ciminelli*): This theory attempted to treat "valuable economic information" or the "right to control assets" as protected property. The Supreme Court rejected this because fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests"—and the right to make informed economic decisions isn't one.

2. **Fraudulent-inducement theory** (affirmed in *Kousisis*): This theory applies when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." This *is* a valid basis for fraud liability because it involves actual money or property, not mere information.

## Why Victims' Receipt of Securities Doesn't Defeat the Fraud Allegation

The defendants argued (as you suggest in your question) that followers received the securities they paid for, so no "deprivation" occurred. The court rejects this on two grounds:

**First, the fraud statutes target obtaining money or property by deception—not just causing net harm.** The court quotes *Kousisis* directly: a defendant may be liable for fraud "even when he 'provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.'" The fraud is complete once the defendant induces the victim to part with money or property through material deception. The subsequent transfer of securities doesn't negate the initial wrongful deprivation.

**Second, no showing of net economic loss is required.** The court explicitly holds that "the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme." The focus is on the defendant's objective—to obtain the victim's money—and the deceptive means used to achieve it. As the court states: "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

## Why This Makes Sense

The rationale is straightforward: fraud statutes criminalize obtaining property through deception. If defendants could avoid liability simply by providing *something* of value in return, fraud would be extremely difficult to prosecute. A con artist who sells fake watches but delivers actual (worthless) watches would escape liability. The law instead focuses on whether the victim was induced to part with money or property based on material misrepresentations—not whether the victim came out ahead or behind in the transaction.
