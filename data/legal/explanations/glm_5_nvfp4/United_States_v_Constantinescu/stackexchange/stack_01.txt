Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the "right-to-control theory" that the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli v. United States?
Question:
I'm reading United States v. Constantinescu and the court discusses Ciminelli's rejection of the 'right-to-control theory.' The opinion says this theory attempted to 'establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.' But I'm confused - isn't the right to control your own assets a property interest? Why did the Supreme Court say this isn't a 'traditional property interest' protected by fraud statutes? And why can't the government base a fraud conviction on depriving someone of information they need to make informed decisions?
Answer:
**The right-to-control theory attempted to redefine "property" to include the right to make informed economic decisions.**

The Supreme Court rejected it because the federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests"—and the right to valuable economic information is not one of them.

---

### What the right-to-control theory was

Under this theory, the government could establish wire fraud by showing that a defendant "schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The victim's "property" under this theory was defined as "the right to control [his] assets."

In *Ciminelli*, a construction company paid a lobbyist to obtain state-funded contracts. The government's theory was that the state was deprived of its "right to control" its assets because it made contracting decisions without complete information about how the process had been manipulated.

### Why the Court rejected it

The fraud statutes (mail, wire, and by extension, securities fraud) protect only "traditional property interests." The Court held that "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest."

The theory attempted to stretch fraud liability too far by treating the deprivation of information itself as the property harm. But not every deprivation of information that affects decisionmaking constitutes a property interest protected by fraud statutes. The Court explained that the right-to-control theory "cannot form the basis for a conviction" because it "divorces the concept of property from its traditional meaning."

### Why this doesn't mean the right to control your assets isn't protected

Your question is understandable: don't you have a property right to control your own assets?

Yes—but that's not what the right-to-control theory actually targeted. The theory wasn't about depriving someone of their assets. It was about depriving them of *information* they needed to make decisions *about* those assets. The "property" under the theory was the information itself, not the underlying assets. The victim might have received full value for their money—they just did so without all the facts.

### The contrast with fraudulent inducement

The Fifth Circuit's opinion helpfully contrasts this with the "fraudulent-inducement theory," which *is* valid. Under fraudulent inducement, a defendant uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into handing over money or property. The property interest is the money or property itself—not information, and not an abstract right to make informed decisions.

In *Constantinescu*, the court found the indictment alleged fraudulent inducement: defendants allegedly lied to induce followers to purchase securities (i.e., part with money). That's a traditional property interest—the followers' money. The "right to valuable information" was not the property the defendants were charged with scheming to obtain.

---

**Bottom line:** The right-to-control theory treated informational deprivation as the property harm. The Supreme Court rejected that because federal fraud statutes protect traditional property—money, goods, land—not the right to make fully informed economic decisions.
