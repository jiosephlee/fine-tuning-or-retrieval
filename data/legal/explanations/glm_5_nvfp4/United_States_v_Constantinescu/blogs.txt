Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Distinguishing 'Right-to-Control' from 'Fraudulent Inducement' After Ciminelli

When the Supreme Court decided *Ciminelli v. United States* in 2023, defense attorneys and prosecutors alike scrambled to assess the fallout. The Court had squarely rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud—the notion that deceiving someone about information necessary to make an economic decision could constitute fraud, even absent a traditional property deprivation. The decision left open a crucial question: how far does *Ciminelli* extend, and what fraud theories survive it?

The Fifth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers an early and instructive answer. In reversing the dismissal of a securities fraud indictment against social-media influencers accused of running a "pump and dump" scheme, the court drew a clear line between the discredited right-to-control theory and the perfectly valid "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The opinion provides a roadmap for prosecutors navigating post-*Ciminelli* pleading standards—and a warning for defendants who might read *Ciminelli* too broadly.

## The Scheme and the District Court's Dismissal

The defendants—social-media personalities with large followings—allegedly orchestrated a classic pump and dump. They would purchase a security, promote it to their followers through posts misrepresenting their own trading positions and the security's prospects, then secretly sell once the inflated price peaked. The government pegged their profits at $114 million.

The twist came when the district court dismissed the indictment. Relying on *Ciminelli*, the court reasoned that the indictment merely alleged defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information"—insight into the promoters' true positions and the artificially inflated prices—rather than a traditional property interest. If the Supreme Court had rejected fraud convictions based on the deprivation of intangible economic information, the district court concluded that this indictment suffered the same flaw.

## The Fifth Circuit's Correction

Judge Engelhardt's opinion methodically explains why the district court's analogy misfired. *Ciminelli* involved a right-to-control theory: the government charged that the defendant deprived victims of their right to make informed economic decisions by withholding material information. The Supreme Court rejected that theory because it "treated as property anything that is valuable," effectively collapsing the "property" requirement of the fraud statutes into an "economic-value" test.

But the indictment here doesn't rely on that theory at all. Instead, it alleges straightforward fraudulent inducement: defendants made material misrepresentations to trick followers into purchasing securities—that is, into parting with their money. As the Fifth Circuit explains, drawing on the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, fraudulent inducement "protects money and property" in the traditional sense. The theory does not treat "valuable economic information" as the property interest; the property interest is the victims' money, obtained through deception.

The distinction is subtle but significant. Under a right-to-control theory, the fraud is complete once the victim is deprived of information necessary to exercise control over her assets. Under fraudulent inducement, the fraud is complete when the victim is tricked into handing over money or property itself. The former collapses the property requirement; the latter honors it.

## Intent to Defraud: Profit as Sufficient

The Fifth Circuit also addresses a second argument from defendants: that the indictment failed to allege an "intent to defraud" because defendants sought only to profit, not to harm their followers. The court dispatches this argument by citing *Kousisis*'s rejection of a "net pecuniary loss" requirement. Under the fraud statutes, a defendant violates the law when he schemes to obtain the victim's money through deception—regardless of whether he intends to leave the victim economically worse off.

This holding has practical bite. The defendants argued that because their followers received securities at market value when they purchased them, no fraud occurred. The Fifth Circuit rejects this "mirror image" reasoning: the fraud is complete when the defendant obtains money through deception, even if the victim receives something of equivalent value in return. The defendants' admission that they "intended to profit" is effectively an admission that they "had money in mind"—the mental state the fraud statutes require.

## Implications Going Forward

*Constantinescu* is one of the first circuit opinions to apply *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* in tandem, and it suggests that the Supreme Court's fraud jurisprudence is more clarifying than revolutionary. The distinction between right-to-control and fraudulent inducement turns on what the defendant sought to obtain through deception: information (insufficient) or money and property (sufficient). Prosecutors who frame their indictments around the latter can survive *Ciminelli* challenges, while defendants who hope to extend *Ciminelli* to fraud involving actual monetary transfers will find little comfort in the Fifth Circuit's analysis.

The opinion also underscores that *Kousisis* resolved a lingering ambiguity about the harm requirement in fraud statutes. Defendants can no longer argue that the absence of intended economic loss defeats fraud charges when the defendant obtained money through deception. The property interest is the money; the intent is to obtain it. That is enough.

For practitioners in the Fifth Circuit and beyond, *Constantinescu* clarifies that *Ciminelli* did not rewrite fraud law—it merely policed its boundaries. Fraudulent inducement remains alive and well.



# The Myth of the "No Harm, No Foul" Defense: Kousisis and the Intent Requirement

The Fifth Circuit's decision last week in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers a striking illustration of how the Supreme Court's recent fraud jurisprudence is reshaping the boundaries of federal criminal law. At issue was whether a group of social media influencers accused of running a "pump and dump" scheme could escape prosecution simply because they claimed they never intended to harm their followers—only to profit themselves. The Fifth Circuit said no, and in doing so, it quietly confirmed something important: the "no harm, no foul" defense to fraud charges is effectively dead.

## The Social Media "Pump and Dump"

The defendants in *Constantinescu* were social media personalities who cultivated large followings by presenting themselves as skilled stock traders. According to the indictment, they would purchase a security, "pump" the price by posting false and misleading information to induce their followers to buy, and then "dump" their own shares at the artificially inflated price—profiting to the tune of $114 million.

The district court dismissed the indictment, accepting the defendants' argument that they had merely deprived followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed, but the more interesting portion of the opinion concerns the defendants' alternative argument: that they lacked the requisite *intent to defraud* because they intended only to profit, not to harm their victims.

## What Kousisis Clarified About Intent

The court's analysis on this point hinges squarely on the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. That case involved a contractor who lied about using disadvantaged-business subcontractors to obtain government contracts. The defendant argued—much like the *Constantinescu* defendants—that he couldn't be guilty of fraud because he never sought to cause the government "net pecuniary loss." The Court rejected that argument categorically.

Writing for the Court, the majority held that the fraud statutes are satisfied when a defendant "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraud is complete, in other words, "when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses."

The Fifth Circuit's application of *Kousisis* to the pump-and-dump context is straightforward but significant. The indictment alleged that defendants posted false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." That alleged induced deprivation is sufficient. As the court explained, the fact that followers received securities of some value in exchange for their money "does not obviate the fraud charges."

Indeed, the Fifth Circuit noted that the defendants' claimed distinction between "intending to profit" and "intending to harm" is "a distinction without a difference." When the object of the scheme is to obtain the victims' money by fraudulent means, the defendant has "had money in mind"—and that is enough.

## The Doctrinal Shift: Fraud Without Economic Harm

The implications of this reasoning extend well beyond securities fraud. For decades, defendants in fraud cases have attempted to argue that no fraud occurred because their victims received something of value in return, or because the defendant never affirmatively sought to cause economic harm. *Kousisis* and cases applying it like *Constantinescu* close that door.

Under the fraudulent-inducement theory now clearly endorsed by the Supreme Court, what matters is whether the defendant used material misrepresentations to trick the victim into parting with money or property. Whether the victim ultimately breaks even, profits, or loses everything is irrelevant to the fraud analysis. The injury element is satisfied by the induced transfer itself.

This represents a meaningful shift in how lower courts should approach the "intent to defraud" requirement. The Fifth Circuit's citation to *United States v. Baker* reinforces the point: the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must correspond to the defendant's gain.

## Downstream Effects

The *Constantinescu* decision will likely embolden prosecutors pursuing fraud cases in novel contexts—particularly those involving social media, cryptocurrency, and other modern investment vehicles where victims may not always suffer catastrophic losses. Defendants can no longer argue that their fraud was victimless simply because followers "got what they paid for" or because the defendant's profit motive didn't equate to an intent to cause harm.

Whether this expansion serves the interests of justice is a question better left to legislators. But as a matter of doctrine, the Fifth Circuit's reading of *Kousisis* is difficult to dispute: the Supreme Court has made clear that fraud is about the *means* of obtaining property, not the magnitude of the victim's ultimate loss. The "no harm, no foul" defense, already on life support, may now have met its end.



# Securities Fraud in the Age of Social Media: Applying Old Statutes to New Schemes

The Fifth Circuit's decision last week in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers a revealing glimpse into how federal courts are adapting venerable fraud statutes to the realities of modern financial markets. The case involves something that would have been unimaginable to the drafters of the mail and wire fraud statutes: a coordinated "pump and dump" scheme orchestrated through social media, in which influencers with hundreds of thousands of followers allegedly used their platforms to artificially inflate stock prices and cash out before the crash. But the legal questions the case presents are surprisingly traditional—questions about what counts as "property" under federal fraud law and what the government must prove to establish criminal intent.

The defendants—eight social media personalities with substantial followings—were indicted for conspiracy to commit securities fraud and multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349. According to the superseding indictment, they held themselves out as skilled traders, posted false and misleading information about securities to induce their followers to buy, and then secretly sold their own positions at a profit. The government alleged they made $114 million from the scheme. The district court, however, dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it failed to allege deprivation of a "traditional property interest" as required by the Supreme Court's recent decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*.

Judge Engelhardt's opinion for the Fifth Circuit reverses that dismissal, and in doing so, provides a useful roadmap for how lower courts should apply the Supreme Court's recent fraud jurisprudence to novel factual contexts. The opinion's significance lies less in breaking new doctrinal ground than in demonstrating how existing principles operate in the digital economy.

## The Right-to-Control Theory Versus Fraudulent Inducement

The defendants' core argument—and the district court's reasoning—rested on the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Ciminelli*. In that case, the Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud, under which prosecutors had secured convictions by showing merely that defendants deprived victims of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests," not abstract rights to accurate information.

On its face, this might seem to help the defendants. After all, the indictment alleged that they deprived their followers of accurate information about stock prices. But as Judge Engelhardt correctly observes, the indictment went further. It alleged that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." This is not a right-to-control theory; it is a classic fraudulent-inducement theory, under which a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

The distinction matters. Under the right-to-control theory, the property interest is purely informational—the victim's right to make decisions with complete information. Under fraudulent inducement, the property interest is the money or property itself, which the victim surrenders based on deception. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* explicitly confirmed that fraudulent inducement "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory" because it "protects money and property."

The Fifth Circuit's application of this distinction is straightforward but important. Social media followers who bought securities based on allegedly false statements weren't merely deprived of information; they were induced to part with actual money. The property interest is traditional, even if the mechanism of fraud is thoroughly modern.

## Intent to Defraud Without Intent to Harm

The opinion's second significant holding concerns the intent element. The defendants argued that even if they induced purchases through misrepresentations, they intended only to profit themselves, not to harm their followers. The court rejects this argument, relying on the Supreme Court's holding in *Kousisis* that the fraud statutes "do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant's scheme."

This principle may seem counterintuitive—how can there be fraud without harm?—but it has deep roots in fraud law. A defendant who lies to obtain a contract may provide goods or services of equal value to what he receives. The victim may even be economically better off. But the fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the victim's money or property "under materially false pretenses."

The Fifth Circuit correctly applies this principle to the social-media context. Defendants argued that their followers received market-value securities in exchange for their money, so no fraud occurred. But as the court notes, that argument "is a distinction without a difference." The indictment alleged that defendants' objective was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing purchases—showing they "had money in mind," as *Kousisis* put it. That suffices for intent to defraud.

## Implications for Digital-Age Enforcement

The decision signals that federal fraud statutes remain flexible tools for prosecuting deceptive schemes, regardless of the technological medium. The Fifth Circuit's analysis suggests that courts will look past novel factual contexts to the underlying economic reality: Did defendants use deception to obtain money or property? If so, traditional fraud principles apply.

This has significant implications for enforcement in the digital marketplace. Social media influencers who promote financial products, cryptocurrencies, or securities should understand that their medium does not immunize them from fraud liability. The fact that followers "voluntarily" choose to purchase securities based on influencer recommendations does not defeat fraud charges if those recommendations were materially false and intended to manipulate prices for the influencer's benefit.

The decision also illustrates how the Supreme Court's recent fraud decisions, while constraining certain prosecutorial theories, leave ample room for traditional fraud prosecutions. *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* did not revolutionize fraud law; they clarified its boundaries. Prosecutors can still bring fraud charges when defendants use deception to obtain money or property—they simply cannot rely on theories that treat intangible informational harms as property deprivations.

For practitioners and scholars, *Constantinescu* demonstrates that old statutes can reach new schemes when courts focus on the essential elements: deception, property, and intent. The social media age has created new mechanisms for fraud, but the legal framework for addressing it remains remarkably stable.
