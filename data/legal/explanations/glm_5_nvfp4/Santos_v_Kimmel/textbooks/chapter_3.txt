Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # Application of Fair Use Factors in Santos v. Kimmel

## Purpose and character of use: transformative use and commentary

The Second Circuit's fair use analysis in *Santos v. Kimmel* begins, as fair use analyses typically do, with the "purpose and character of the use" factor. This factor asks whether the allegedly infringing use is "transformative"—that is, whether the secondary work "merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." The court quoted its prior decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* for this formulation, emphasizing that transformative use lies at the heart of the first factor.

The court found that Kimmel's use of Santos's Cameo videos was plainly transformative. The purpose of the JKL segments, as the district court had found and Santos did not meaningfully dispute, was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." This purpose—critical commentary and satire—is fundamentally different from the purpose for which Santos created the videos. The court noted that fair use can be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, meaning the transformative nature of the use was evident from the face of Santos's own allegations. The court concluded that the first factor "strongly supports a finding of fair use."

## The reasonable observer standard versus subjective intent

Santos attempted to resist the transformative use finding by arguing that Kimmel had "instigated the creation of the original Works" and was therefore the "designer" of their original purpose. In essence, Santos claimed that because Kimmel requested the videos under false pretenses, Kimmel's purpose in using them was the same as Santos's purpose in creating them.

The Second Circuit rejected this argument by articulating a crucial principle: "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." The court cited the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, which explicitly held that "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use."

The court illustrated this principle by contrasting what Santos claimed his videos were meant to convey versus what Kimmel's segments communicated. According to Santos's own allegations, a reasonable observer of his original videos would perceive "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." By contrast, a reasonable observer of the JKL segments would perceive commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. Because these purposes are objectively different, the use is transformative regardless of Kimmel's role in requesting the videos or Santos's subjective intent in making them.

## Nature of the copyrighted work and publication timing

The second fair use factor directs courts to consider "the nature of the copyrighted work." The opinion addresses this factor briefly but meaningfully, focusing on a specific factual detail established through judicial notice: "the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created."

This timing matters because published works receive somewhat less protection under the second factor than unpublished works. The Copyright Act defines publication as distribution of copies to the public, and the court took judicial notice of the copyright registrations to establish this fact. The second factor thus weighed in favor of fair use, or at minimum did not weigh against it, because the works were already publicly available through the Cameo platform when Kimmel used them.

## Amount and substantiality of the portion used

The third fair use factor considers "the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole." The opinion does not analyze this factor in detail, instead noting that the court was "unpersuaded by Santos's challenges to the District Court's analysis of the remaining factors." The court's summary treatment of this factor suggests that Kimmel's use—airing the complete Cameo videos—did not weigh against fair use in light of the strongly transformative purpose. When a secondary use is highly transformative and serves purposes of commentary and criticism, copying the entire work may be appropriate and even necessary to achieve the secondary purpose.

## Effect on potential market: market harm versus market criticism

The fourth fair use factor examines "the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." This factor often receives significant weight, but the Second Circuit emphasized an important distinction: "[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

The court cited *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* for this principle, and also quoted the Supreme Court's decision in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*: "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." In other words, Santos could not claim market harm based on a lost licensing market for critical or satirical uses, because copyright holders do not typically license—and cannot be expected to license—the very criticism or lampooning of their own works.

The JKL segments did not offer a competing substitute for Santos's Cameo videos. Someone seeking to commission a personalized video from Santos would not purchase or watch a Kimmel segment instead. The fourth factor thus favored fair use because Kimmel's use did not usurp any legitimate market for Santos's original works.

## Bad faith argument and its rejection under Harper & Row

Santos raised a separate argument that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request the videos constituted bad faith, which Santos argued should nullify the fair use defense. Santos relied on the Supreme Court's statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing."

The Second Circuit rejected this argument on two grounds. First, the court observed that the Supreme Court "has since distanced itself from this statement"—suggesting that *Harper & Row*'s language on good faith is not controlling authority. The fair use doctrine does not categorically exclude bad faith conduct from its protection.

Second, and more importantly, the court found that Santos's complaint actually contradicted any claim of bad faith in the relevant sense. Citing *Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, the court explained that bad faith in the fair use context means a purpose to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work. Santos's complaint instead "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Quoting *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, the court confirmed that uses driven by criticism and commentary—even when the secondary user obtains the work through deception—do not constitute the kind of bad faith that weighs against fair use.

The court's rejection of Santos's bad faith argument underscores an important principle: deceptive conduct in obtaining a copyrighted work does not automatically defeat a fair use defense when the actual purpose of the use is legitimate commentary, criticism, or parody. The focus remains on the transformative character of the use and its effect on the market, not on the user's conduct in acquiring the work.
