Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## The "Instigation" Problem: When Secondary Users Manufacture the Original Work

What happens when a late-night comedian tricks a disgraced politician into recording embarrassing videos—but then the politician copyrights those videos and sues? The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* answers that question with a straightforward application of fair use doctrine, but along the way, it rejects a novel theory that could have created significant problems for commentators, journalists, and satirists.

The case arose from a prank that practically wrote itself. After former Congressman George Santos began selling personalized video messages on Cameo, Jimmy Kimmel created fictitious personas and requested videos asking Santos to say increasingly absurd things. Santos complied, recording videos with messages like "congratulations on successfully cloning a woolly mammoth" and "congratulations on being awarded Employee of the Month at Walt Disney World." Kimmel then aired these videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a segment titled "Will Santos Say It?"

Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state law claims. The district court dismissed the complaint, and the Second Circuit affirmed. The fair use holding itself is unremarkable—the court correctly found that Kimmel's use was transformative commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. What makes *Santos* worth examining is the plaintiff's creative attempt to sidestep settled doctrine.

## The "Instigation" Theory

Santos advanced an argument that, if accepted, would have created a new limitation on fair use. He contended that Kimmel could not claim his use was transformative because Kimmel had "instigated the creation of the original Works" and was therefore the "designer" of their original purpose. In other words, Kimmel shouldn't get fair use protection because he manufactured the very content he now claims to be commenting upon.

This is a clever theory. The transformative use inquiry traditionally asks whether the secondary use adds new meaning or purpose to the original work. Santos argued that when the secondary user orchestrates the original work's creation, that user cannot later claim to have transformed it—they merely realized their own intended purpose.

The Second Circuit rejected this argument, and properly so. Citing the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the court held that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."

This was the right call. The subjective motivations of either party are immaterial to the fair use analysis. What matters is how a reasonable observer would understand the relationship between the original work and the secondary use. Santos's own complaint alleged that his videos were meant to convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." Kimmel's segments, by contrast, were commentary on Santos's mercenary willingness to record messages about fictional achievements for a few hundred dollars. Those are fundamentally different purposes, regardless of who set the videos in motion.

## Subjective Intent and the Fair Use Analysis

The court's rejection of Santos's "instigation" theory reinforces an important principle: fair use is an objective inquiry. The court explicitly held that it does not matter whether Kimmel designed the original purpose of the videos or whether Santos subjectively intended a different purpose. The only question is what purpose a reasonable observer would perceive.

This holding aligns with the Supreme Court's guidance in *Warhol*. There, the Court rejected the argument that the purpose of Andy Warhol's silkscreen portraits of Prince was different from the purpose of Lynn Goldsmith's source photograph, even though Warhol was clearly commenting on celebrity and mass media. The Court emphasized that courts should not defer to the subjective intent of the user. *Santos* applies this principle in the opposite factual context—here, the secondary use was clearly transformative, and the court would not let Santos's subjective intent or Kimmel's "instigation" defeat that conclusion.

The court also addressed Santos's argument that Kimmel's deception—the use of fictitious names to request the videos—constituted bad faith that should preclude fair use. Santos relied on language from *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* suggesting that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." The Second Circuit noted that the Supreme Court has since "distanced itself from this statement."

This is an understatement. While *Harper & Row* suggested that bad faith could weigh against fair use, subsequent precedent has largely minimized this consideration. The *Santos* court correctly observed that the complaint showed Kimmel was motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary"—the very purpose the fair use doctrine is designed to protect. Deception in obtaining source material, while potentially relevant to other claims, does not undermine a transformative use defense.

## Market Harm and the Fourth Factor

The court's analysis of the fourth fair use factor—the effect on the potential market for the original work—was similarly sound. The court quoted *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* for the proposition that the question is not whether the secondary work damages the market for the original "by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism," but whether it "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

Kimmel's segments did not offer a substitute for Santos's Cameo videos. No one seeking to purchase a personalized video from Santos would decide to watch a late-night comedy segment instead. And as *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* observed, creators are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own work, so such uses fall outside "the very notion of a potential licensing market."

Santos's theory would have inverted this analysis. If Kimmel's "instigation" meant he could not claim transformative use, then the market harm analysis might have turned out differently—Kimmel's segments could be seen as exploiting content he engineered. The court's rejection of that theory preserves the objective market harm inquiry.

## State Law Claims and Preemption

The court also affirmed dismissal of Santos's state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. While the district court had found the contract claims preempted by the Copyright Act, the Second Circuit declined to reach that question, instead holding that the claims failed under state law.

The breach of contract claim failed because Santos was not a party to Cameo's Terms of Service, which users like Kimmel agreed to. Under Illinois law, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties," and nothing in the agreement suggested an intent to benefit Santos. The presence of third-party beneficiary language elsewhere in the agreement only underscored that the parties knew how to create such rights when they intended to.

The implied contract claim failed because Santos failed to plead the essential terms of any such contract or a "meeting of the minds" that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo's terms. The fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law—a particularly amusing deficiency given that Santos was paid for each video.

## Implications for Future Cases

*Santos* does not break new ground doctrinally, but it provides useful clarification on several points. First, the decision confirms that "instigation" is not a relevant factor in the transformative use analysis. A secondary user's role in bringing the original work into existence does not change the objective inquiry into whether the secondary use adds new meaning or purpose. This is an important holding for journalists and commentators who may obtain source material through their own initiative—undercover reporting, sting operations, or simply requesting materials that are later used in critical coverage.

Second, the decision reinforces that bad faith in obtaining copyrighted material does not preclude fair use when the use itself is transformative commentary. While courts may consider bad faith as part of the first factor, it does not carry independent weight sufficient to defeat an otherwise valid fair use defense.

Finally, the decision serves as a reminder that fair use can be established at the pleading stage. The court affirmed dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), holding that the fair use defense was "so clearly established by [the] complaint" that Santos's copyright claims could not survive. This is a useful precedent for defendants facing weak infringement claims that are clearly barred by fair use.

The *Santos* decision is ultimately a straightforward application of existing doctrine to a fact pattern tailor-made for late-night comedy. But the court's rejection of the "instigation" theory prevents a potentially significant expansion of copyright liability, preserving space for the very commentary and criticism that the fair use doctrine is meant to protect.



# Whatever Happened to Good Faith? The Continuing Erosion of Harper & Row's Moral Dimension

The Second Circuit's decision last week in *Santos v. Kimmel* will probably be remembered mostly for its celebrity litigants—the former congressman versus the late-night host. But buried in Judge Lohier's straightforward fair use analysis is a striking doctrinal observation that deserves more attention than the tabloid-ready facts might suggest. The court matter-of-factly notes that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from *Harper & Row*'s famous pronouncement that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." That single sentence speaks volumes about the trajectory of modern copyright law.

The facts are by now familiar. Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, submitted requests to George Santos through the Cameo platform for personalized videos. Santos fulfilled the requests, apparently believing he was making inspirational messages for fans. Kimmel then aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a segment titled "Will Santos Say It?"—a mocking commentary on Santos's apparent willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state law claims. The district court dismissed on fair use grounds, and the Second Circuit affirmed.

## The Good Faith Question

Santos raised a genuinely interesting argument. Even if Kimmel's use was transformative commentary, Santos contended, the deceptive manner in which Kimmel acquired the videos—using fake identities and fabricated backstories—constituted bad faith that should preclude fair use. The argument finds textual support in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, where the Supreme Court stated that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing" and treated bad faith as relevant to the first factor.

The Second Circuit's response is notable not for what it concludes about this specific dispute, but for what it says about the broader doctrine. Judge Lohier acknowledges that *Harper & Row* said what it said. But he then observes, without ceremony, that "the Court has since distanced itself from this statement."

He's right, though courts have been slow to grapple with the implications. The *Campbell* decision in 1994 reoriented the first factor analysis toward transformativeness, and more recent decisions have increasingly treated the good faith inquiry as marginal at best. The Supreme Court's 2023 *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* decision further emphasized that the first factor turns on "whether the use of a copyrighted work has a purpose or character that is distinct from the purpose of the original," not on the user's subjective intentions.

## The Doctrinal Drift

What's happening here is a fundamental reorientation of fair use away from moral judgment and toward market analysis. The old *Harper & Row* formulation suggested that fair use was, at least in part, an equitable doctrine that rewarded good behavior and punished bad actors. The modern approach treats it as a utilitarian instrument—a mechanism for determining whether secondary uses serve copyright's ultimate purpose of promoting creative progress.

The shift matters because it decouples the question of how you obtained a copyrighted work from what you did with it. Under a strict *Harper & Row* framework, Kimmel's deception might well have been disqualifying. He lied to get the videos made. He fabricated personas and backstories. That's precisely the kind of behavior *Harper & Row* seemed to contemplate when it suggested that bad faith undermines fair use.

But under the modern approach, that deception becomes largely irrelevant. What matters is that Kimmel used the videos for a different purpose—commentary and criticism—rather than as a market substitute. The court specifically notes that the complaint "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary," not by an intent to "supplant" Santos's commercial rights. The acquisition story fades; the use story dominates.

## Why This Matters Beyond Cameo

The doctrinal move is not necessarily wrong. There's something odd about treating fair use as a reward for virtuous behavior rather than as a structural feature of copyright law. And there are good reasons to worry about courts becoming arbiters of moral conduct in the creative sphere.

But the implications extend well beyond late-night comedy segments. The same logic that renders Kimmel's deception irrelevant to fair use could apply to far more consequential contexts. Consider AI companies that scrape copyrighted content from the web, sometimes in ways that circumvent technical protection measures or violate terms of service. If acquisition conduct doesn't matter to fair use, those companies may have a stronger argument that their subsequent uses—training language models, generating new content—are transformative even if the original acquisition was deceptive or unauthorized.

The *Santos* court didn't create this doctrinal drift, but it exemplifies it. By treating the "distancing" from *Harper & Row* as unremarkable, the decision reinforces a view of fair use that is increasingly agnostic about how secondary users obtain the works they transform.

## The Remaining Safeguards

To be fair, the Second Circuit's analysis doesn't leave bad actors entirely without consequence. Santos also brought state law claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement—claims that might have reached precisely the deception at issue. But those claims failed on their own terms. Santos couldn't enforce Cameo's Terms of Service because he wasn't a party to them or an intended third-party beneficiary. His fraudulent inducement claim foundered because he couldn't allege any out-of-pocket loss beyond what he would have earned from legitimate Cameo requests.

The latter point is particularly stark. Under New York fraud law, Santos needed to show he was worse off than he would have been but for the fraud. He got paid for the videos. The fact that he wouldn't have made them if he'd known Kimmel was the requestor doesn't count as a pecuniary loss. The fraud claim thus fails not because deception doesn't matter, but because Santos couldn't translate the deception into the kind of harm the law recognizes.

## What Comes Next

The Second Circuit's decision is probably correct as an application of current doctrine. The fair use analysis was straightforward: Kimmel used Santos's videos for commentary and criticism, not as a market substitute, and Santos could plausibly show no harm to any legitimate licensing market. That the deception feels distasteful doesn't change the doctrinal outcome.

But the case leaves open questions that will matter far more in other contexts. If AI companies obtain training data through deception or unauthorized scraping, will courts treat that conduct as irrelevant to fair use? Or will they find ways to distinguish *Santos*—a case about public figures, parody, and earned media—from the more systematic acquisition of protected works?

The Second Circuit's quiet burial of *Harper & Row*'s good faith requirement suggests that the answer will turn on what the defendant did with the content, not how they got it. For copyright holders worried about unauthorized use of their work, that's a sobering precedent.



# Dodging Preemption: Strategic Avoidance of the Copyright Preemption Question

When former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel for airing Cameo videos he had recorded under false pretenses, the case presented an interesting collision of copyright law, state contract principles, and the sometimes awkward relationship between the two. The Second Circuit's decision last week in *Santos v. Kimmel* resolved the dispute cleanly—but the path the court took to get there reveals something important about how federal courts approach the fraught question of copyright preemption.

## The Fair Use Question Was Straightforward

The facts are by now familiar. Kimmel, using fictitious identities, requested personalized videos from Santos through the Cameo platform. Santos obliged, recording messages that Kimmel then broadcast on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a segment cheekily titled "Will Santos Say It?" Santos cried copyright infringement; Kimmel answered with fair use.

The Second Circuit had little difficulty affirming the district court's dismissal on fair use grounds. Judge Lohier's analysis was methodical: the first factor—"purpose and character of the use"—strongly favored Kimmel because the segments commented on Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." This transformative purpose was clear to any reasonable observer, regardless of Santos's claimed subjective intent to convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance." The remaining factors posed no obstacle. The videos were published the same day they were created, and Santos could not show market harm in the form of a competing substitute. As the court noted, quoting *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose*, "[t]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market."

The fair use holding itself is unremarkable. What's more interesting is what the court did with Santos's state law claims.

## The Road Not Taken: Copyright Preemption

The district court had dismissed Santos's breach of contract and implied contract claims on preemption grounds, holding that the Copyright Act's preemptive sweep extended to these state-law theories. Copyright preemption under Section 301 of the Copyright Act is a thorny area. Courts must determine whether state law claims are "equivalent" to copyright claims—meaning they protect rights that fall within the scope of copyright's exclusive rights—or whether they embody "extra elements" that make them qualitatively different.

The Second Circuit did not take that path. Instead, Judge Lohier's opinion resolved each state law claim on state law grounds, explicitly sidestepping the preemption question: "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos's state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law."

This was prudent. The breach of contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service failed because Santos was not a party to that agreement. Under Illinois law, third-party beneficiary status requires an "express declaration"—something conspicuously absent from the provisions Santos invoked. The implied contract claim fared no better under New York law, as Santos failed to plausibly allege a "meeting of the minds" or even the essential terms of any putative agreement. The fraudulent inducement claim collapsed for want of out-of-pocket loss, a required element under New York law.

## Why Courts Avoid Preemption

The Second Circuit's strategic avoidance here illustrates a broader phenomenon in copyright litigation. Courts often prefer to resolve cases on state law grounds rather than wade into preemption analysis. There are sound institutional reasons for this preference.

First, preemption doctrine remains contested. The boundaries between rights "equivalent" to copyright and those that are "qualitatively different" are notoriously difficult to draw. The Second Circuit's own precedent—cases like *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio*—acknowledges this difficulty. By resolving claims on alternative grounds, courts avoid issuing pronouncements that might confuse the doctrine further.

Second, state law analysis can be more straightforward than the metaphysical inquiry preemption sometimes demands. Here, the contract claims failed for pedestrian reasons: lack of standing, failure to plead essential terms, absence of required damages. These are bread-and-butter issues that any first-year law student could identify, and they required no excursion into the murkier reaches of federal intellectual property law.

Third, there is a judicial conservatism at work. Courts generally prefer narrower grounds for decision. Why issue a sweeping ruling about the preemptive scope of the Copyright Act when state law disposes of the case cleanly?

## Implications for Litigants

The strategic lesson for practitioners is clear: when pleading state law claims that touch on copyrighted material, make sure those claims can stand on their own merits. It is not enough to invoke contract or fraud theories as end-runs around copyright doctrine if the underlying elements are unsatisfied. Santos attempted to use contract law to recover for the same conduct that formed his copyright claim, but he neglected to plead the basic elements of a contract claim under the governing state law.

This points to a deeper tension in copyright litigation. Plaintiffs often bring state law claims as backup theories, hoping to preserve recovery even if copyright claims fail. But *Santos v. Kimmel* demonstrates that this strategy requires careful attention to the elements of those state law claims. The court's refusal to reach preemption means that litigants cannot assume their state law claims will survive simply because they raise "extra elements" different from copyright infringement.

## Conclusion

*Santos v. Kimmel* resolves a distinctly modern dispute—the collision of social media platforms, late-night comedy, and intellectual property rights—through decidedly traditional legal analysis. The fair use holding reinforces that commentary and criticism remain at the heart of copyright's safety valve. But the more subtle lesson lies in what the court declined to decide. By avoiding preemption, the Second Circuit followed a well-worn path: resolving cases on the narrowest available grounds. Litigants would do well to remember that state law claims tied to copyrighted works must be independently viable, not merely creative attempts to circumvent copyright doctrine.



# Platform Terms of Service and the Third-Party Beneficiary Problem: Who Can Enforce the Contract?

The Second Circuit's recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* will be remembered primarily for its straightforward application of fair use doctrine to late-night comedy. But buried in the opinion is a contractual law holding that deserves attention from anyone interested in the growing economy of platform-mediated transactions. The court's analysis of third-party beneficiary status under the Cameo Terms of Service highlights a persistent problem for gig workers and platform users: when platforms draft their own rules, they rarely extend enforcement rights to the people who actually use them.

## The Factual Background

Former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and ABC after Kimmel, using pseudonyms, submitted requests for personalized videos through the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled these requests, apparently under the impression that the requesters were ordinary fans seeking inspirational messages. Kimmel then aired the videos on his show as part of a segment mocking Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money."

Santos's copyright claims were swiftly dismissed on fair use grounds. The Second Circuit agreed with the district court that Kimmel's use was transformative—commenting on Santos's character rather than merely supplanting the original videos—and that Santos suffered no market harm within the meaning of the Copyright Act.

But Santos also brought state law claims, including a breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service. That claim, and the court's rejection of it, reveals something important about how platforms structure their user agreements.

## The Third-Party Beneficiary Problem

Santos's breach of contract claim faced a threshold problem: he wasn't a party to the contract he was trying to enforce. The Terms of Service were an agreement between Cameo and its users—people like Kimmel, who had to click "I agree" before submitting video requests. Santos, as the talent fulfilling those requests, was never asked to agree to anything.

Under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The Second Circuit, quoting *Barry v. St. Mary's Hospital Decatur*, noted that courts will only recognize third-party beneficiary status when "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

Santos couldn't meet that standard. He pointed to no language in the Terms of Service suggesting that the parties intended to allow Cameo's talent to enforce the agreement. The court noted that this absence was particularly telling because other provisions of the Terms of Service *did* contain third-party beneficiary language—suggesting that when the parties intended to create enforceable rights for non-parties, they knew how to say so.

## What This Means for Platform Users

The court's reasoning is doctrinally sound but practically troubling for the growing class of workers who earn their living through platforms like Cameo, Uber, TaskRabbit, and DoorDash. These platforms typically impose Terms of Service on the consumers of their services—the people who request rides or order food or commission videos. But the workers providing those services often have no contractual relationship with those users at all, or at best have a separate agreement with the platform itself.

This creates an enforcement asymmetry. When a user violates the Terms of Service—by, for instance, using a fake name or misrepresenting their identity—workers have limited recourse. They can complain to the platform, which may or may not take action. But they typically cannot enforce the Terms of Service themselves, because they are not parties to that contract.

Santos tried to get around this problem by alleging an implied-in-fact contract between himself and Kimmel. That theory also failed. Under New York law, Santos needed to allege the essential terms of the alleged contract and demonstrate a "meeting of the minds" that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo's Terms of Service. The complaint contained only "conclusory" allegations about the supposed agreement and failed to identify specific provisions that were breached.

The contrast is instructive. Santos had no trouble identifying what Kimmel did wrong—he used fake names to request videos, then broadcast those videos on national television in violation of Cameo's policies. But he had no contractual hook to hang that claim on. The platform's rules, in other words, offered him no meaningful protection.

## Why This Matters Beyond George Santos

The obvious response is that Santos's case is an unusual one. Most Cameo performers aren't being pranked by late-night comedians, and most Terms of Service disputes don't involve public figures with their own axes to grind. But the doctrinal problem the case illustrates is more pervasive.

Platform workers regularly face harms that the platform's rules nominally prohibit—harassment, misrepresentation, non-payment, or misuse of their work product. Those rules often provide grounds for termination of the offending user's account. But they rarely provide a private right of action for the workers themselves.

This gap is unlikely to be accidental. Platforms have every incentive to draft Terms of Service that reserve enforcement power for themselves. Giving workers a direct contractual claim against users would expose platforms to litigation risks and complicate their business models. Better, from the platform's perspective, to maintain control over who gets to enforce the rules—and when.

The Second Circuit's opinion doesn't break new doctrinal ground here. It simply applies well-established principles of third-party beneficiary law to a fact pattern that exposes their limits. But for workers navigating the platform economy, *Santos v. Kimmel* is a reminder that the rules they rely on for protection may not be enforceable by them at all.

## The Bottom Line

The fair use holding in *Santos v. Kimmel* is unremarkable—commentary and criticism have long been protected under Section 107, and the court properly applied that framework to late-night satire. But the contractual claims reveal a structural problem that courts alone cannot solve. Until platforms are required or incentivized to give workers meaningful enforcement rights, the Terms of Service will remain primarily a tool for platform control rather than worker protection.
