Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can fair use be decided at the 12(b)(6) pleading stage before any discovery?
Question:
I'm confused about how the court could dismiss Santos's copyright claims based on fair use at the motion to dismiss stage. I thought fair use was a fact-intensive inquiry that usually requires developing a factual record. The court cites TCA Television Corp. for the proposition that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal." But how can a court determine transformative use, bad faith, or market harm without discovery? Is this unusual, or can fair use always be decided on the pleadings if the facts are clear enough?
Answer:
### Yes, fair use can be decided at the pleading stage when the complaint itself establishes the defense clearly.

You're right that fair use is generally a fact-intensive inquiry, but the Second Circuit has long held that it can be resolved on the pleadings in appropriate cases. The key precedent is *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 (2d Cir. 2016), which the court cites for the proposition that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim."

### How the court applied this here

The court determined that the complaint itself—including the videos and the JKL segments, which were incorporated by reference—provided everything needed to resolve fair use. Three points from the opinion illustrate why:

**1. Transformative use was evident from the face of the complaint.** The court didn't need discovery to compare the original purpose of Santos's videos with Kimmel's use. Santos's own complaint described his videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." Kimmel's purpose, as the district court found, was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." This contrast was sufficient to establish transformative purpose as a matter of law. Importantly, the court applied the objective standard from *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*: the question is what a "reasonable observer" would think, not the subjective intent of either party.

**2. Bad faith was refuted by the complaint's own allegations.** Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to solicit the videos demonstrated bad faith. But the court noted that the complaint itself "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary," not an attempt to "supplant" Santos's commercial rights. The court also clarified that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from the broad statement in *Harper & Row* that fair use "presupposes good faith."

**3. Market harm was absent as a matter of law.** The court cited *Hachette* and *Campbell* for the principle that the fourth factor asks whether the secondary use "usurps the market" by offering a competing substitute—not whether criticism or parody devalues the original. Since Kimmel's use was commentary, not a substitute, and since creators "will [not] license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions," there was no plausible market harm to investigate.

### When is this appropriate?

The court emphasized that it needed only to consider "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue" to resolve the question. When the comparison of the original and secondary works, together with the complaint's allegations, establishes fair use "clearly," no factual development through discovery is required.

This approach is consistent with Rule 12(b)(6) practice generally: if the facts alleged, taken as true, fail to state a claim because an affirmative defense is conclusively established, dismissal is appropriate. Fair use is not categorically exempt from this principle—it's just that the factual issues are usually too contested to resolve at the pleading stage. Here, they weren't.

### Why does the 'reasonable observer' standard control the transformative use analysis instead of the creator's intent?
Question:
The court rejects Santos's argument that his videos were created for the same purpose that Kimmel used them for (commentary on Santos saying absurd things for money). The court says that whether a use is transformative 'turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder.' But Santos claims he was 'instigating' the creation of the videos through his Cameo requests. If Kimmel effectively designed the purpose of the original works through his fake requests, why doesn't that matter? What exactly is the 'reasonable observer' looking at - the works themselves, or the context of their creation?
Answer:
### Why the Reasonable Observer Standard Controls

The short answer is that **transformative use is an objective inquiry**—it asks what the works actually communicate, not what either party subjectively intended them to communicate.

### The Legal Standard

Under the first fair use factor, courts ask whether the secondary use "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character" compared to the original. The Second Circuit, drawing from the Supreme Court's decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, held that this determination "turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."

This is a deliberate doctrinal choice. If subjective intent controlled, fair use analysis would devolve into a fact-intensive inquiry into the mental states of the parties—something ill-suited to early resolution of cases and something that would undermine the predictability of the fair use doctrine.

### How the Court Applied It Here

Santos argued that he and Kimmel shared the *same* purpose: commentary on "Santos saying absurd things for money." But the court rejected this on two levels:

1. **Santos's own allegations contradicted his claim.** The complaint described the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." To a reasonable observer, these are inspirational personal messages—the Cameo product itself—not commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things.

2. **Even if Santos subjectively intended commentary, it doesn't matter.** The works, as created and presented, communicated something different. The reasonable observer looks at what the work actually conveys, not the hidden intent behind its creation.

### Why the "Instigation" Argument Fails

Your question about Kimmel "designing" the purpose through his fake requests gets at why the subjective-intent approach is rejected. Kimmel's deceptive requests may have been his *method* of obtaining the content, but the resulting works still had to be evaluated based on what they actually showed. The court explains:

> "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks"

The observer looks at the **works themselves**—the original videos and the JKL segments—and asks whether the secondary use has a different purpose or character. The context of creation (including Kimmel's fake requests) is not irrelevant, but it does not change the fundamental question: what do the works communicate to an ordinary viewer?

### The Contrast That Matters

The reasonable observer sees:
- **Original works**: Personalized inspirational videos conveying hope and encouragement
- **Secondary use**: Satirical commentary mocking Santos's willingness to say anything for money

This contrast is what makes the use transformative. Santos's attempt to recast his purpose retroactively—claiming he was *also* commenting on his own absurdity—cannot change what the videos actually conveyed when he created them.

### Does bad faith or misrepresentation negate fair use after Harper & Row?
Question:
Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fake names to solicit the Cameo videos constituted bad faith that should nullify the fair use defense, citing Harper & Row's statement that 'fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.' But the court says the Supreme Court has 'since distanced itself from this statement.' I'm confused - what is the current status of bad faith in the fair use analysis? Can deliberate deception in obtaining copyrighted material ever preclude a fair use defense, or is the analysis now purely about the four statutory factors?
Answer:
**Short Answer: Bad faith is not an independent bar to fair use.** The Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* confirms that the Supreme Court has retreated from *Harper & Row*'s suggestion that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." Bad faith conduct—such as using fake names to obtain copyrighted material—is relevant only to the extent it informs the four statutory factors, particularly the first factor (purpose and character of the use). It cannot independently nullify a fair use defense.

---

**Background: The Harper & Row Statement**

In *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* (1985), the Supreme Court stated that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." 471 U.S. at 562. Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fake names to solicit Cameo videos constituted bad faith that should preclude fair use under this principle.

The Second Circuit rejected this argument, stating plainly that "the Court has since distanced itself from this statement." The opinion does not cite a specific Supreme Court case for this proposition, but the broader doctrinal shift is well-established: bad faith is not treated as an independent requirement or automatic bar to fair use.

---

**How Bad Faith Factors Into the Analysis Today**

The *Santos* court explained that even if bad faith were still relevant, the inquiry is folded into the first fair use factor—the purpose and character of the use. Specifically, the court looked to whether the defendant's purpose was to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work, citing *Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014).

Here, Kimmel's use was for "criticism and commentary"—the videos were aired to mock Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." This transformative purpose weighed in favor of fair use under the first factor, regardless of how Kimmel obtained the videos. As the court noted, Santos's own complaint "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary," citing *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015).

---

**Key Takeaways**

1. **Bad faith is not a fifth factor.** The four statutory factors in 17 U.S.C. § 107 remain the exclusive framework.

2. **Bad faith may inform Factor One, but only indirectly.** Deceptive conduct might be relevant to assessing the defendant's purpose, but the core question is whether the use is transformative and whether it usurps the market for the original—not the defendant's ethics.

3. **The focus remains on market harm.** The court emphasized that the fourth factor asks whether the defendant's use offers a "competing substitute" for the original, not whether criticism or parody causes reputational damage (citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994)).

In sum, deliberate deception in obtaining copyrighted material does not preclude a fair use defense. The analysis remains grounded in the four statutory factors, with the defendant's conduct at most serving as one data point in evaluating the purpose and character of the use.

### What distinguishes market 'usurpation' from market 'devaluation' under the fourth fair use factor?
Question:
The court says Santos suffered no market harm because the fourth factor asks 'not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.' This distinction confuses me. If Kimmel's use made Santos's Cameo videos less valuable by mocking them, isn't that market harm? What exactly makes something a 'competing substitute' versus merely critical commentary that might incidentally harm the market?
Answer:
The distinction turns on whether the secondary work serves the same market function as the original, or whether it serves an entirely different purpose that merely comments upon the original.

**A "competing substitute" usurps the market** when it fulfills the same demand as the original work—when a consumer would purchase the secondary work *instead of* the original to satisfy the same need. This is the type of economic harm the fourth factor is designed to capture.

**"Devaluation" through criticism or parody**, by contrast, describes reputational harm that flows from the *message* of the secondary work, not from market substitution. The secondary work doesn't compete with the original; it comments on it.

The court illustrates this distinction with Campbell v. Acuff-Rose, quoted in the opinion: creators of imaginative works are unlikely to "license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions," which "removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." A parody or critique may well reduce demand for the original by making it look foolish or unappealing, but that harm flows from the commentary's persuasive effect, not from the secondary work functioning as a market substitute.

Applied here: Kimmel's segments used Santos's videos to mock Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." The segments served a commentary function, not the same function as the original videos (which Santos alleged conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity"). Someone seeking a personalized inspirational video from Santos would not view Kimmel's mocking broadcast as a substitute. The market harm Santos feared—that his reputation would be damaged and his Cameo services less attractive—is precisely the kind of devaluation through criticism that the fourth factor does not recognize.

In short: **usurpation means functional substitution in the marketplace; devaluation means harm to reputation or appeal caused by the critical message itself.** Only the former counts against fair use.

### Why didn't Santos have standing as a third-party beneficiary to enforce Cameo's Terms of Service?
Question:
The court dismisses Santos's breach of contract claim because he wasn't a party to the Terms of Service between users (like Kimmel) and Cameo. The court notes there's a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties' under Illinois law, and that other provisions of the Terms did contain third-party beneficiary language. But Santos is clearly the 'talent' on Cameo and seems to be the intended beneficiary of user obligations. What exactly does a plaintiff need to show to overcome the presumption against third-party beneficiary status? Would explicit language always be required?
Answer:
### Answer

Under Illinois law, which governs the Cameo Terms of Service, a plaintiff must show that the contracting parties' intent to confer third-party beneficiary status was so clear that it amounts to "practically an express declaration." The court's analysis turns on two key points.

**First, the presumption is very difficult to overcome.** The court quotes *Barry v. St. Mary's Hospital Decatur* for the proposition that Illinois applies a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The standard is demanding: "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration." This is not merely a heightened burden—it sets a threshold that essentially requires the contract to speak directly to third-party enforcement rights.

**Second, the contract's structure defeated Santos's claim.** The court notes that "other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third-party beneficiary language." This observation is critical. When a contract includes express third-party beneficiary clauses in some sections but not others, the natural inference under contract interpretation principles (*expressio unius est exclusio alterius*) is that the parties deliberately chose not to confer such rights in the provisions at issue. The presence of explicit language elsewhere in the same agreement makes it far harder to argue that the parties intended third-party rights implicitly.

To answer your specific question: explicit language is not strictly required in every case, but the implication must be so strong that it functionally approximates one. If other provisions of the same contract *do* contain explicit third-party beneficiary language, a court will reasonably expect similarly clear language before finding that status in provisions lacking it. The fact that Santos was the "talent" and appears to benefit from user obligations does not overcome this textual hurdle. Being an intended beneficiary in a practical sense is not enough; the contract must clearly manifest an intent to grant enforcement rights.

### What must a plaintiff plead to establish an implied-in-fact contract under New York law?
Question:
The court rejects Santos's claim for breach of implied contract because he failed to 'allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract' and didn't plausibly allege 'an indication of a meeting of the minds.' I'm struggling to understand what would have been sufficient here. Santos and Kimmel did have interactions through Cameo - doesn't that imply some kind of agreement? What are the 'essential terms' that need to be pleaded for an implied contract claim, and how specific do those allegations need to be?
Answer:
Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract requires the same elements as an express contract: offer, acceptance, consideration, and a meeting of the minds. The difference is that the agreement is inferred from the parties' conduct rather than their words. But that doesn't make the pleading requirements any less demanding.

**What the complaint needed to allege**

The court identifies two fundamental deficiencies in Santos's implied contract claim:

1. **Essential terms**: The complaint must "allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." (quoting *Caniglia v. Chicago Tribune-New York News Syndicate, Inc.*). The plaintiff must identify what specific obligations each party undertook and what terms were allegedly breached.

2. **Meeting of the minds**: The complaint must plausibly allege "an indication of a meeting of the minds of the parties constituting an agreement." (quoting *DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass'n Compliance & Legal Div.*).

**Why the Cameo interactions weren't enough**

The mere fact that Santos and Kimmel interacted through Cameo did not establish an implied contract because Santos failed to identify what the terms of any such contract would be. The court notes that Santos did not plead "those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated" or identify what Kimmel allegedly agreed to do or refrain from doing.

Moreover, the "meeting of the minds" element was missing because Kimmel submitted requests under fictitious names for a comedy segment. A meeting of the minds requires mutual understanding of essential terms—something difficult to establish when one party is operating under false pretenses.

**What would have been sufficient**

To survive a motion to dismiss, Santos would have needed to allege specific, nonconclusory facts showing:
- What obligations each party agreed to undertake
- What specific terms Kimmel allegedly breached
- Facts demonstrating mutual assent to those terms

Conclusory assertions that an implied contract existed or that there was a "meeting of the minds" are insufficient. The plaintiff must plead factual content that permits the court to infer that both parties understood and agreed to specific contractual obligations.

### Why does New York's fraudulent inducement claim require 'out-of-pocket' loss rather than benefit-of-the-bargain damages?
Question:
The court dismisses Santos's fraudulent inducement claim because he 'failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss.' But Santos did provide services (the Cameo videos) that he wouldn't have provided but for Kimmel's alleged fraud. Isn't that a loss? What exactly is the difference between 'out-of-pocket loss' and 'benefit-of-the-bargain' damages in New York fraud law, and why does fraudulent inducement specifically require the former?
Answer:
### Answer

The distinction between "out-of-pocket" loss and "benefit-of-the-bargain" damages is foundational in New York fraud law, and the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion illustrates why it matters.

**Out-of-pocket loss** measures what the plaintiff actually lost—the difference between the value of what they parted with and what they received in return. Under New York law, this is the exclusive measure of damages for fraud and fraudulent inducement. The Court of Appeals held in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), that "the measure of damages for fraud is indemnity for the actual pecuniary loss sustained," i.e., out-of-pocket loss.

**Benefit-of-the-bargain damages**, by contrast, would measure the difference between what the plaintiff *was promised* and what they actually received—essentially compensating for the unrealized value of the bargain. New York deliberately rejects this measure for fraud claims, limiting recovery to actual losses.

**Why Santos's claim fails:** Santos was paid for each Cameo video he created. He received exactly the payment he bargained for. His grievance is that he would not have provided the videos at all had he known Kimmel's true identity and intentions—that is, he did not get the *benefit of his bargain* (restricting the videos to his understanding of the use and user). But Santos did not allege that he lost any money or parted with any value beyond what he was compensated for. He suffered no actual pecuniary loss. The videos themselves—the services he rendered—were compensated.

The opinion briefly affirms this principle by citation: fraudulent inducement requires "out-of-pocket loss," and Santos "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud," citing *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). The provision of services that one was fully paid for does not constitute an out-of-pocket loss under New York fraud law.
