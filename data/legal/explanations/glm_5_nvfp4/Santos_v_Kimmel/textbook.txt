Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Parties: George Santos, Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney

This case involves a copyright and contract dispute between former Congressman George Santos and several defendants associated with a late-night television program. The plaintiff-appellant is George Santos, who at the time of the underlying events was a former member of the United States House of Representatives. The defendants-appellees are Jimmy Kimmel, the host of *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), the network that airs Kimmel's show; and The Walt Disney Company, ABC's parent company. Understanding the identity of the parties is essential because Santos's claims arose from his interactions with Kimmel through a digital platform, and the corporate structure—ABC as the broadcaster and Disney as its parent company—determined who Santos named as defendants in his suit.

## The Cameo Platform and Personalized Video Request Mechanism

The dispute arose through the Cameo platform, a service that connects celebrities and public figures with users who pay for personalized video messages. The mechanics of Cameo are central to understanding both the copyright issues and the contract claims in this case. Through Cameo, users submit requests for personalized videos, specifying the message or occasion for which they want the video made. The celebrity or public figure then records a video fulfilling that request, and the user receives the completed video. Importantly, Cameo operates through its own Terms of Service, which govern the relationship between users (those requesting videos) and talent (those creating videos). This platform structure created the factual foundation for Santos's claims, as it was through this mechanism that Kimmel obtained the videos at issue.

## The Factual Dispute: Kimmel's Use of Fictitious Names and the "Will Santos Say It?" Segments

The core facts underlying this litigation involve a deliberate scheme by Kimmel to obtain videos from Santos. Kimmel, using various fictitious names, submitted multiple requests to Santos for personalized videos through the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled each request, presumably believing he was creating videos for ordinary users seeking encouraging messages. Kimmel then aired these videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" The segments were designed to comment on Santos's apparent willingness to say absurd things in exchange for payment.

This factual scenario raises important questions about the relationship between the creator's intent and the secondary user's purpose—a tension that became central to the fair use analysis. Santos alleged in his complaint that the videos were meant to convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." The district court, however, found that Kimmel's purpose was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." This divergence between Santos's subjective intent and the purpose of the secondary use became a key point of legal analysis on appeal.

## District Court Proceedings and Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)

Santos filed suit in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, asserting claims for copyright infringement and various state law claims including breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. Judge Cote dismissed Santos's complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. A motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) tests the legal sufficiency of a complaint, asking whether the plaintiff has alleged facts that, if true, would entitle the plaintiff to relief. The district court concluded that Santos's copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine and that his state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim under applicable state law. Santos timely appealed to the Second Circuit.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Review of a Motion to Dismiss

The Second Circuit begins its analysis by articulating the standard of review. The court reviews de novo a district court's dismissal of a complaint under Rule 12(b)(6). De novo review means the appellate court considers the legal questions anew, without deference to the district court's conclusions. This standard is appropriate because the legal sufficiency of a complaint presents a question of law, not fact. The appellate court thus stands in the same position as the district court when evaluating whether the complaint states a claim.

## Materials the Court May Consider on a 12(b)(6) Motion

In conducting this de novo review, the Second Circuit explained the materials it may properly consider. The court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint." This principle, drawn from *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, establishes the boundaries of the complaint's "four corners" on a motion to dismiss. Additionally, the court may take judicial notice of certain external facts. Specifically, the court noted it may take judicial notice of "copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office's registry." This procedural point proved relevant to the fair use analysis, as the copyright registrations established that Santos's videos were published on the same day they were created—a factual matter bearing on the fair use factors. Understanding what materials courts may consider on a motion to dismiss is essential for practitioners, as it defines both the opportunities and limitations of the pleading stage.

# Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Doctrine

## Elements of a Copyright Infringement Claim

Although the Santos opinion focuses primarily on the fair use defense, understanding the underlying claim helps contextualize why fair use matters. A copyright infringement claim has two basic elements: ownership of a valid copyright, and violation of one of the exclusive rights granted to copyright holders under the Copyright Act. The Act grants copyright owners exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, publicly perform, publicly display, and prepare derivative works based on their original works of authorship. When someone exercises one of these exclusive rights without permission, they have committed what would "otherwise be considered infringing behavior."

In this case, Santos alleged that Kimmel and ABC infringed his copyrights by broadcasting the Cameo videos on Jimmy Kimmel Live! without his authorization. The complaint sought to vindicate Santos's exclusive rights in the video works he had created. But the defendants raised fair use as an affirmative defense. As the court explains, fair use "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior," allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when doing so would "stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote." Thus, even if all elements of infringement are satisfied, a successful fair use defense defeats the claim entirely.

## Fair Use as an Affirmative Defense to Copyright Infringement

Fair use operates as an affirmative defense, meaning the defendant bears the burden of proving it applies. The doctrine serves a critical function in copyright law by creating breathing space for uses that serve broader societal interests, even when those uses would otherwise infringe on the copyright holder's exclusive rights. The Santos court frames fair use as a mechanism to "avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote." This reflects the underlying purpose of copyright law: to encourage creativity and the dissemination of knowledge, not merely to protect authors' economic interests.

The affirmative nature of fair use matters procedurally. The copyright holder need not disprove fair use in their prima facie case. Instead, the defendant must raise and establish the defense. However, as Santos demonstrates, fair use can sometimes be established so clearly from the face of the complaint that dismissal is appropriate even at the pleading stage, before any discovery or evidentiary hearing.

## Section 107 and the Four Statutory Fair Use Factors

Section 107 of the Copyright Act codifies the fair use doctrine and sets forth four non-exclusive factors that courts must consider. The Santos court articulates them as follows: "(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." These factors are not a rigid formula but rather provide a flexible framework for balancing competing interests.

The first factor—the purpose and character of the use—plays a central role in fair use analysis. Courts "focus chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative," asking "whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." In Santos, the district court found that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Santos argued this was also his original purpose in making the videos, but the court rejected this argument.

Critically, the court held that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." The court cited the Supreme Court's statement in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* that "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use." Applying this objective standard, the court found that Santos's own allegations described the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity"—not a willingness to say absurd things for money. This difference in perceived purpose supported a finding of transformative use.

Santos also argued that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request the videos demonstrated bad faith, which he claimed should nullify the fair use defense. The court acknowledged language in *Harper & Row* suggesting that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," but noted that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from this statement. More importantly, the court found that the complaint did not allege any purpose to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights in the videos. Instead, the complaint "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." The court thus concluded that the first factor "strongly supports a finding of fair use."

The fourth factor—the effect on the potential market—also favored fair use. The court emphasized the distinction articulated in *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*: "We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." Criticism and parody may well damage the market for the original by making it appear ridiculous or less desirable, but such harm does not count as market harm under the fourth factor. As the Supreme Court explained in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." The court found no market harm here because Kimmel's use did not offer a competing substitute for Santos's videos.

## Judicial Notice of Copyright Registrations

Procedurally, the Santos opinion touches on an important point about evidence at the pleading stage. When reviewing a motion to dismiss, the court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint." Additionally, courts "may also take judicial notice of copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office's registry." This principle allows courts to consider facts about copyright registration status even when not explicitly alleged in the complaint, since registration records are public and authoritative.

In Santos, the court relied on copyright registrations to establish that the videos "were published on the same day they were created." This factual finding bore on the fair use analysis—specifically the second factor, which concerns the nature of the copyrighted work. The ability to take judicial notice of registration records thus serves a practical function in resolving copyright disputes efficiently at the pleading stage.

## Fair Use Resolution at the Pleading Stage

A key lesson from Santos is that fair use can sometimes be resolved on a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6). The court explicitly holds that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage." This principle allows defendants to avoid the burden of full litigation when the complaint itself demonstrates that fair use applies.

The court emphasized that it needed only to "consider the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue" to decide "the question of fair use." By comparing the original works and the allegedly infringing use directly, the court could determine that the use was transformative and that no market harm existed. This approach reflects the practical reality that some fair use questions are so straightforward they can be resolved by examining the works themselves, without needing factual development through discovery.

This holding has important strategic implications. Defendants facing weak infringement claims can seek early dismissal by demonstrating fair use on the face of the complaint. Conversely, plaintiffs drafting copyright complaints should anticipate fair use defenses and consider whether their factual allegations might inadvertently support such a defense. Santos illustrates that even at the pleading stage, courts will engage meaningfully with the fair use factors, applying an objective standard to determine whether the secondary use serves a transformative purpose distinct from the original work.

# Application of Fair Use Factors in Santos v. Kimmel

## Purpose and character of use: transformative use and commentary

The Second Circuit's fair use analysis in *Santos v. Kimmel* begins, as fair use analyses typically do, with the "purpose and character of the use" factor. This factor asks whether the allegedly infringing use is "transformative"—that is, whether the secondary work "merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." The court quoted its prior decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* for this formulation, emphasizing that transformative use lies at the heart of the first factor.

The court found that Kimmel's use of Santos's Cameo videos was plainly transformative. The purpose of the JKL segments, as the district court had found and Santos did not meaningfully dispute, was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." This purpose—critical commentary and satire—is fundamentally different from the purpose for which Santos created the videos. The court noted that fair use can be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, meaning the transformative nature of the use was evident from the face of Santos's own allegations. The court concluded that the first factor "strongly supports a finding of fair use."

## The reasonable observer standard versus subjective intent

Santos attempted to resist the transformative use finding by arguing that Kimmel had "instigated the creation of the original Works" and was therefore the "designer" of their original purpose. In essence, Santos claimed that because Kimmel requested the videos under false pretenses, Kimmel's purpose in using them was the same as Santos's purpose in creating them.

The Second Circuit rejected this argument by articulating a crucial principle: "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." The court cited the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, which explicitly held that "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use."

The court illustrated this principle by contrasting what Santos claimed his videos were meant to convey versus what Kimmel's segments communicated. According to Santos's own allegations, a reasonable observer of his original videos would perceive "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." By contrast, a reasonable observer of the JKL segments would perceive commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. Because these purposes are objectively different, the use is transformative regardless of Kimmel's role in requesting the videos or Santos's subjective intent in making them.

## Nature of the copyrighted work and publication timing

The second fair use factor directs courts to consider "the nature of the copyrighted work." The opinion addresses this factor briefly but meaningfully, focusing on a specific factual detail established through judicial notice: "the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created."

This timing matters because published works receive somewhat less protection under the second factor than unpublished works. The Copyright Act defines publication as distribution of copies to the public, and the court took judicial notice of the copyright registrations to establish this fact. The second factor thus weighed in favor of fair use, or at minimum did not weigh against it, because the works were already publicly available through the Cameo platform when Kimmel used them.

## Amount and substantiality of the portion used

The third fair use factor considers "the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole." The opinion does not analyze this factor in detail, instead noting that the court was "unpersuaded by Santos's challenges to the District Court's analysis of the remaining factors." The court's summary treatment of this factor suggests that Kimmel's use—airing the complete Cameo videos—did not weigh against fair use in light of the strongly transformative purpose. When a secondary use is highly transformative and serves purposes of commentary and criticism, copying the entire work may be appropriate and even necessary to achieve the secondary purpose.

## Effect on potential market: market harm versus market criticism

The fourth fair use factor examines "the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." This factor often receives significant weight, but the Second Circuit emphasized an important distinction: "[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

The court cited *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* for this principle, and also quoted the Supreme Court's decision in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*: "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." In other words, Santos could not claim market harm based on a lost licensing market for critical or satirical uses, because copyright holders do not typically license—and cannot be expected to license—the very criticism or lampooning of their own works.

The JKL segments did not offer a competing substitute for Santos's Cameo videos. Someone seeking to commission a personalized video from Santos would not purchase or watch a Kimmel segment instead. The fourth factor thus favored fair use because Kimmel's use did not usurp any legitimate market for Santos's original works.

## Bad faith argument and its rejection under Harper & Row

Santos raised a separate argument that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request the videos constituted bad faith, which Santos argued should nullify the fair use defense. Santos relied on the Supreme Court's statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing."

The Second Circuit rejected this argument on two grounds. First, the court observed that the Supreme Court "has since distanced itself from this statement"—suggesting that *Harper & Row*'s language on good faith is not controlling authority. The fair use doctrine does not categorically exclude bad faith conduct from its protection.

Second, and more importantly, the court found that Santos's complaint actually contradicted any claim of bad faith in the relevant sense. Citing *Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, the court explained that bad faith in the fair use context means a purpose to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work. Santos's complaint instead "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Quoting *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, the court confirmed that uses driven by criticism and commentary—even when the secondary user obtains the work through deception—do not constitute the kind of bad faith that weighs against fair use.

The court's rejection of Santos's bad faith argument underscores an important principle: deceptive conduct in obtaining a copyrighted work does not automatically defeat a fair use defense when the actual purpose of the use is legitimate commentary, criticism, or parody. The focus remains on the transformative character of the use and its effect on the market, not on the user's conduct in acquiring the work.

# State Law Contract Claims

## Direct Breach of Contract Claim under the Cameo Terms of Service

Santos's first state law claim was for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. The factual foundation for this claim was straightforward: Kimmel, using fictitious names, had agreed to Cameo's Terms of Service when submitting his video requests. Santos alleged that Kimmel breached those terms by using the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* without permission. The critical flaw in this claim, however, was that Santos himself was not a party to the contract between Kimmel and Cameo.

The court explained that under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, there exists a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This principle reflects the fundamental contract law concept that contracts generally create rights and obligations only between the parties who agree to them. A non-party typically cannot enforce a contract to which they did not consent. The court quoted Illinois precedent stating that "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

## Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine and Illinois Law's Strong Presumption against Third-Party Enforcement

The third-party beneficiary doctrine provides a narrow exception to the general rule that only contracting parties may enforce a contract. Under this doctrine, a third party may enforce a contract if the contracting parties intended to confer enforceable rights upon that third party. The court's analysis illustrates how courts apply this doctrine in practice and how demanding the standard can be.

Illinois law creates a strong presumption against third-party enforcement. This presumption places a heavy burden on any non-party seeking to enforce a contract. The party claiming beneficiary status must show that the contracting parties clearly intended to grant enforcement rights to third parties. Mere incidental benefits are insufficient. The court emphasized that Illinois courts require something approaching an "express declaration" in the contract itself.

## Express Declaration Requirement for Third-Party Beneficiary Status

The court's analysis demonstrated how courts interpret contracts to determine whether third-party beneficiary status was intended. Santos "identified nothing close to an 'express declaration'" that the Terms of Service provisions could be enforced by him or other Cameo talent. This failure was fatal to his claim.

Perhaps most damaging to Santos's argument was the court's observation about other provisions within the same Terms of Service. The court noted that "other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third-party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly." This observation is significant for two reasons. First, it shows that the drafters of the Terms of Service knew how to create third-party rights when they wanted to do so. Second, under principles of contract interpretation, the presence of express third-party beneficiary language in some provisions suggests that its absence in other provisions was intentional. Courts often apply the canon *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others—to construe contracts.

## Implied Contract Claim under New York Law

Santos alternatively argued that an implied contract existed between himself and Kimmel, which Kimmel breached. Implied contracts differ from express contracts in that the terms are not explicitly stated by the parties but are instead inferred from their conduct and the circumstances surrounding their interactions. The court applied New York law to this claim, explaining that courts must consider "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine whether an implied contract was formed.

This claim faced its own pleading requirements. Under New York law, a plaintiff must allege "in nonconclusory language . . . the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." The complaint must do more than simply assert that a contract existed; it must identify what the terms of that contract were and how they were breached. The court found Santos's complaint inadequate because it "failing to plead the essential terms of the putative implied contract that the Defendants allegedly breached."

## Meeting of the Minds and Essential Terms Requirements

Contract formation, whether express or implied, requires a "meeting of the minds"—mutual assent to the same terms. The court held that Santos failed to plausibly allege such mutual agreement. Specifically, Santos did not allege facts showing that Kimmel had agreed to adhere to the Terms of Service in any direct contractual relationship with Santos himself.

The implied contract theory thus suffered from a fundamental problem: Santos and Kimmel had no direct communication or relationship. Kimmel submitted requests through the Cameo platform under a pseudonym, and Santos fulfilled those requests through the same platform. Nothing in these interactions suggested that Kimmel had directly agreed with Santos to any contractual terms. The court noted the absence of any "indication of a meeting of the minds of the parties constituting an agreement."

## Copyright Act Preemption as an Alternative Ground for Dismissal

The District Court had dismissed Santos's contract claims on preemption grounds, reasoning that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Copyright preemption prevents plaintiffs from circumventing federal copyright law by recasting copyright claims as state law claims. Under Section 301 of the Copyright Act, state law claims are preempted when: (1) the work falls within the subject matter of copyright, and (2) the state law claim asserts rights that are equivalent to the exclusive rights protected by copyright.

Santos argued that his contract claims were "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and therefore not preempted. The appellate court, however, found it unnecessary to resolve this question. Because Santos's contract claims failed to state a claim under state law, the court could affirm the dismissal on that basis alone without reaching the preemption issue.

This aspect of the opinion illustrates an important principle of judicial decision-making: courts often prefer to decide cases on narrower grounds rather than reaching constitutional or statutory questions that are unnecessary to the disposition. The court's approach also demonstrates that alternative holdings at the trial court level give appellate courts flexibility in affirming judgments. The District Court's preemption analysis provided an alternative ground that the Second Circuit could have relied upon, but the court instead chose the simpler path of affirming on the merits of the state law claims themselves.

# Fraudulent Inducement Claim

## Elements of Fraudulent Inducement under New York Law

Fraudulent inducement is a species of fraud claim that arises when a party is deceived into entering into a contract or transaction. Under New York law, which governs Santos's fraudulent inducement claim, a plaintiff must prove several elements: a material misrepresentation of fact, made with knowledge of its falsity and intent to deceive, reasonable reliance by the plaintiff on the misrepresentation, and resulting injury. The claim is grounded in the principle that no one should be permitted to profit from deliberate deception that causes harm to another.

The elements of fraudulent inducement track the general requirements for fraud claims in New York. The misrepresentation must be factual in nature—statements of opinion or puffery generally do not suffice. The plaintiff must demonstrate that the defendant acted with scienter, meaning knowledge that the representation was false or reckless disregard for its truth. Causation requires both actual reliance—meaning the plaintiff would not have acted but for the misrepresentation—and that such reliance was reasonable under the circumstances.

## The Out-of-Pocket Loss Requirement

Central to any fraud claim under New York law is the requirement that the plaintiff suffered actual damages. As the Second Circuit explains, citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, a plaintiff must allege "out-of-pocket loss" to state a claim for fraudulent inducement. This requirement serves a critical gatekeeping function: without actual financial harm traceable to the fraud, a plaintiff has no injury to redress.

The out-of-pocket loss requirement demands more than theoretical or speculative harm. A plaintiff must demonstrate that the deception caused concrete financial detriment—the difference between what was paid or given up and what was received in return. The measure of damages in fraud cases typically compares the value of what the plaintiff parted with against the value of what was obtained through the fraudulent transaction. Without such quantifiable loss, the fraud claim cannot proceed, regardless of how egregious the defendant's deceptive conduct may have been.

## Application to Santos's Allegations of Kimmel's Deception

Santos's fraudulent inducement claim arose from Kimmel's use of fictitious names and false identities when requesting personalized Cameo videos. Santos argued that Kimmel fraudulently induced him to create the videos by misrepresenting his identity and purpose. According to Santos, had he known the true identity of the requestor—Jimmy Kimmel—or the true purpose—mocking segments on a late-night television show—he would not have fulfilled the requests.

The factual allegations, accepted as true for purposes of the motion to dismiss, painted a picture of deliberate deception. Kimmel used pseudonyms to disguise his identity and submitted requests under false pretenses. The resulting videos were then broadcast on national television as part of the "Will Santos Say It?" segment, which mocked Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." Santos contended that this deception constituted fraudulent inducement warranting compensation.

## The Absence of Pleaded Actual Damages

The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court's dismissal because Santos failed to allege any out-of-pocket loss as required by New York law. The court cited *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* in support of this conclusion. This failure was fatal to the claim, regardless of the sufficiency of Santos's other allegations regarding misrepresentation, scienter, and reliance.

The deficiency in Santos's pleading becomes apparent upon examination of the transaction itself. For each Cameo video Santos created, he received payment—the fees he charged for his personalized video services. The deception, while arguably resulting in reputational harm and public embarrassment, did not cause Santos to part with money or property without receiving anything of value in return. He was paid for his work. The "injury" Santos suffered was not financial in the sense that fraudulent inducement doctrine requires. New York's out-of-pocket loss rule demands a showing that the plaintiff gave up something of value and received something of lesser or no value in return because of the fraud.

This outcome illustrates an important principle in fraud law: deceptive conduct, standing alone, does not establish liability. The plaintiff must suffer cognizable economic harm traceable to the deception. A fraud claim is not a vehicle for recovering damages for hurt feelings, damaged reputation, or emotional distress—those injuries, if actionable at all, belong to different legal theories such as defamation or intentional infliction of emotional distress. Santos's failure to plead actual out-of-pocket damages meant his fraudulent inducement claim could not survive a motion to dismiss, and the Second Circuit properly affirmed its dismissal.
