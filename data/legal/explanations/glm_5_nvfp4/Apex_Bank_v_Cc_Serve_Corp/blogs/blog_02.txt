Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Third-Party Marks and Commercial Strength: Clarifying the Scope of Factor Six After Apex Bank

When the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board evaluates likelihood of confusion, it applies the thirteen-factor test from *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* But as the Federal Circuit's decision last week in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* demonstrates, the Board must apply those factors consistently—and when it fails to do so, its findings cannot stand. The decision clarifies an important but underappreciated aspect of the sixth DuPont factor: the scope of "similar" goods for purposes of assessing third-party marks must align with the Board's own findings about the similarity of the parties' goods and services.

The case arose from a straightforward opposition proceeding. CC Serve, which offers credit card services under the mark ASPIRE, opposed Apex Bank's application to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. But the Federal Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Hughes, identified a critical analytical flaw in how the Board evaluated evidence of third-party marks—evidence that Apex offered to show that the ASPIRE mark is weak and entitled to only narrow protection.

## The Board's Inconsistent Approach

The opinion turns on a simple inconsistency. Under the second DuPont factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services, the Board found that credit card services and banking services are "highly similar"—indeed, "legally identical, in part." The Board reached this conclusion based on dictionary definitions showing that banking encompasses extending credit through credit cards, combined with evidence that financial institutions commonly offer both types of services under a single mark.

But when the Board turned to the sixth DuPont factor, which considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods, it abruptly narrowed its aperture. Rather than looking at third-party marks used across the banking and financing services that it had just deemed highly similar to credit card services, the Board considered only marks used specifically for credit card services. This meant excluding 42 third-party marks using ASPIRE or ASPIRE-formative elements for banking and financial services, considering only nine marks for credit card services. The Board then concluded that nine marks was insufficient to demonstrate that CC Serve's mark is commercially weak.

The Federal Circuit properly rejected this sleight of hand. As the court explained, the sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of marks used on "similar" goods—not identical goods. And critically, the Board cannot define "similar" one way for purposes of the second factor and another way for the sixth. When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services at issue are highly similar, it must "retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

This holding makes eminent sense. The purpose of the sixth factor is to assess whether consumers have been "educated to distinguish between marks" in a crowded field. That educational effect depends on what consumers encounter in the marketplace. If banking services and credit card services are highly similar—so similar that consumers might believe they come from the same source—then third-party marks used for banking services are part of the relevant commercial landscape. The Board cannot cabin off that evidence by pretending, for purposes of factor six, that the services are more distinct than it has already concluded they are.

## The Evidentiary Threshold Question

The opinion leaves unanswered a question that practitioners will nonetheless find significant: just how many third-party marks are enough to demonstrate commercial weakness? The Board, relying on the Federal Circuit's decisions in *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*, concluded that nine marks was not enough. In *Jack Wolfskin*, the court pointed to fourteen "notable examples" of third-party use; in *Juice Generation*, twenty-six marks was deemed "a considerable number."

The Board's analysis here suggests a rough threshold—somewhere north of nine but perhaps somewhere south of twenty-six. But this counting exercise has always been somewhat arbitrary. As the court noted in *Jack Wolfskin*, what matters is whether the evidence demonstrates that consumers have been "educated to distinguish" between similar marks. Nine marks might well be sufficient in some contexts; twenty-six might be inadequate in others. The Board's rigid approach—tallying registrations and stopping once it reaches a seemingly insufficient number—risks turning a contextual inquiry into a mechanical one.

On remand, the Board will have the opportunity to consider all 51 third-party marks that Apex identified. Whether that number crosses the evidentiary threshold remains to be seen. But the more important point is that the Board must actually consider them rather than excluding them at the threshold based on an artificially narrow definition of similarity.

## Downstream Implications

The decision has implications beyond the likelihood-of-confusion context. The court's insistence on analytical consistency should extend to other multi-factor tests in trademark law, including the distinctiveness analysis under Section 2(f) and the fame analysis under Section 43(c). Practitioners would do well to scrutinize Board decisions for similar inconsistencies—situations where the Board defines relevant markets or relevant consumers differently across different factors.

The court also vacated the Board's analysis of the first DuPont factor, which concerns the similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The commercial impression analysis, the court reasoned, depends in part on the commercial strength of the senior mark—and that strength assessment may change once the Board properly considers the full range of third-party marks. This cascade effect underscores an important structural point: the DuPont factors are not hermetically sealed. The Board's analysis under one factor can properly inform its analysis under others.

For CC Serve, the road ahead is uncertain. The Board's finding on the similarity of services survives, and that finding weighs heavily toward confusion. But if the Board now determines that the ASPIRE mark is commercially weak, that weakness may temper the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The commercial strength of a mark has long been recognized as a key variable in the confusion calculus—a strong mark is more likely to be associated with a single source, while a weak mark may be perceived as one among many in a crowded field.

*Apex Bank* is ultimately a case about analytical rigor. The Federal Circuit sent a clear message: the Board cannot reach a conclusion about likelihood of confusion by rigging the analysis. When the Board finds that goods are similar, it must give effect to that finding across the DuPont factors. Anything less is not just inconsistent—it's legally flawed.
