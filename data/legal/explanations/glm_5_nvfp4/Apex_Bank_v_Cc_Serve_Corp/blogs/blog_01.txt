Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## Federal Circuit Rejects Inconsistent Standards of 'Similarity' Across DuPont Factors

Multi-factor legal tests are designed to bring structure to nebulous questions, but they only work if the factors speak to one another. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit issued a useful corrective on this front, vacating a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board decision that applied inconsistent definitions of "similarity" across the famous *DuPont* factors. The decision reinforces a simple but vital principle: an administrative body cannot expand the scope of a market to find infringement while simultaneously narrowing that same market to ignore evidence of a crowded field.

The dispute arose from a straightforward opposition proceeding. CC Serve, owner of the mark ASPIRE for credit card services, opposed Apex Bank’s attempt to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar—a conclusion that usually spells doom for a junior user—but it took the unusual step of vacating the decision due to a logical inconsistency in how the Board defined the relevant universe of third-party marks.

The crux of the court’s analysis lies in the tension between the second and sixth *DuPont* factors. Under the second factor, which examines the similarity of goods and services, the Board found that credit card services and banking services are "legally identical, in part." The Board reached this conclusion by relying on dictionary definitions and third-party registrations showing that banks frequently issue credit cards. Having defined the services as highly similar, the Board then turned to the sixth factor, which considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. This factor is typically invoked by applicants to argue that a senior user’s mark is weak and deserves only narrow protection; if a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers are presumably more discerning.

Here, Apex presented evidence of 42 third-party marks using "Aspire" in connection with financial services. The Board, however, refused to consider the vast majority of these marks. It reasoned that because CC Serve’s registration was for credit card services, the relevant universe of third-party marks was limited only to those used for credit card services. By artificially narrowing the scope of the sixth factor, the Board found only nine relevant third-party marks and concluded that this number was insufficient to show that CC Serve’s mark was weak.

Judge Hughes, writing for the court, identified this analytical pivot as legal error. The Board cannot have it both ways. If credit card services and banking services are "highly similar" for the purpose of establishing a likelihood of confusion under factor two, they must also be "similar" for the purpose of evaluating the crowded field under factor six. The court correctly noted that the sixth factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. By restricting its analysis to identical services (credit cards) while simultaneously relying on a broader definition of similarity (banking and credit cards) to find a likelihood of confusion, the Board engaged in a jurisprudential sleight of hand.

This ruling has significant doctrinal implications for trademark prosecution and litigation. It prevents the Board—and by extension, trademark examiners—from using a one-way ratchet on the concept of similarity. Too often, parties (and tribunals) seek to define markets broadly when it helps establish a likelihood of confusion, but narrowly when evaluating the strength of the senior user’s mark. *Apex Bank* closes that door. The Federal Circuit made clear that once a factual finding regarding similarity is established, that finding must be carried through the rest of the analysis consistently.

The court also vacated the Board’s analysis of the first *DuPont* factor (similarity of the marks) and remanded for reconsideration. This was a necessary corollary to the sixth factor holding. As the court noted, the commercial impression of a mark—what the first factor measures—is inextricably linked to its strength. If, on remand, the Board considers the full scope of financial services marks and finds the ASPIRE mark to be commercially weak, that finding will necessarily inform the overall commercial impression of the mark. A weak mark requires a closer similarity to cause confusion; a strong mark tolerates less.

For practitioners, *Apex Bank* serves as a potent tool in oppositions and cancellations involving broad service descriptions. Applicants facing an opposition should scour the registry not just for identical services, but for all services the Board might deem "similar." If the Board finds those services similar enough to support a confusion analysis, *Apex Bank* dictates that those same services must be considered when evaluating whether the senior user’s mark is strong enough to warrant protection. The decision ensures that the *DuPont* factors remain a cohesive analytical framework rather than a collection of disjointed arguments.
