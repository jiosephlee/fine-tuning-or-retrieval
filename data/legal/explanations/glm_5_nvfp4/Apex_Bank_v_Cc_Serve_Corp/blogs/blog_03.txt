Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# From Credit Cards to Online Banking: TTAB's Finding of 'Legally Identical' Services Reflects Financial Services Convergence

The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* highlights a tension that trademark practitioners in the financial services sector have long recognized: the gap between how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board analyzes service similarity and how consumers actually experience the modern financial marketplace. While the court ultimately remanded the case for reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor—third-party use of similar marks—it affirmed the Board's critical finding that credit card services and "banking and financing services" are "legally identical, in part." That holding, and the analytical approach underpinning it, deserves close attention from trademark practitioners navigating an increasingly converged financial services landscape.

## The Board's Convergence Analysis

The second DuPont factor—the similarity of the parties' goods and services—proved to be the linchpin of the Board's likelihood-of-confusion analysis, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that finding. The Board's approach was methodical but revealing. Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for "credit card services" had been deleted after CC Serve's registration, the Board first had to define what those services encompass. It landed on a broad construction: "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores."

The Board then turned to dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance," and concluded that these terms encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." This interpretive move allowed the Board to find that the services were "legally identical, in part." The Board further supported this conclusion with evidence of third-party registrations covering both credit card services and banking services—registrations that demonstrate, in the Board's view, that such services "may emanate from a single source under one mark."

The Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supported this analysis. The court rejected Apex's argument that CC Serve merely "partners with banks" rather than providing banking services itself, noting that the Board had "carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services." The practical reality—that modern financial institutions commonly offer both credit cards and banking services under unified branding—carried significant weight.

## The Sixth Factor Problem

Where the Board faltered, according to the Federal Circuit, was in its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor. This factor considers the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods," and evidence of widespread third-party use can demonstrate that a mark is weak and entitled to only narrow protection. The theory is straightforward: when a marketplace is crowded with similar marks, consumers learn to distinguish among them more carefully, reducing the likelihood of confusion.

Apex submitted evidence of forty-two third-party uses of "Aspire" and Aspire-formative marks across credit card services and the broader financial services industry. But the Board, having defined the relevant public as "consumers of credit card services," considered only nine of those marks—the ones specifically tied to credit card services. The other marks, for banking and financing services, were deemed "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit properly identified the logical inconsistency. The Board had already determined, under the second factor, that credit card services and banking services are "highly similar"—indeed, "legally identical, in part." Having made that factual finding, the Board should have applied the same similarity standard when evaluating third-party marks under the sixth factor. As the court explained, "We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor." The Board cannot have it both ways: treating services as highly similar when analyzing confusion, but as meaningfully distinct when evaluating mark strength.

## Industry Reality Meets Legal Doctrine

The Board's second-factor analysis, affirmed by the Federal Circuit, reflects an important doctrinal shift in how trademark law treats the financial services sector. Traditional distinctions between banks, credit card issuers, and other financial services providers have become increasingly artificial. Major banks issue credit cards under their own marks. Credit card companies partner with banks to offer deposit accounts. Fintech platforms provide services that cut across all these categories. The Board's willingness to find legal identity between credit card services and banking services acknowledges this reality.

For trademark practitioners, the implications are significant. When drafting identifications of goods and services for financial services clients, the *Apex Bank* decision suggests that broad identifications may carry broader exposure to oppositions and citations than in other industries. A registration for "banking and financing services" may now be understood to sweep in credit card services, and vice versa. Practitioners should consider whether more specific identifications might provide clearer boundaries—or whether broader identifications are necessary to capture the full scope of a client's actual and anticipated services.

The decision also underscores the importance of sixth-factor evidence in financial services cases. If credit card services and banking services are "highly similar," then third-party registrations and uses across the broader financial services industry should be relevant to demonstrating that consumers have been educated to distinguish among similar marks. Practitioners opposing registration—or defending against opposition—should cast a wide evidentiary net.

## What Comes Next

On remand, the Board must reconsider both the sixth and first DuPont factors. The sixth-factor analysis will likely include a broader universe of third-party Aspire marks, potentially weakening CC Serve's claim to a broad scope of protection. If the Board finds that the financial services marketplace is sufficiently crowded with Aspire-formative marks, consumers may be more adept at distinguishing Apex's ASPIRE BANK from CC Serve's ASPIRE.

The first factor—the similarity of the marks themselves—must also be reconsidered because commercial strength informs the overall commercial impression analysis. A weaker senior mark may affect how similar a junior mark must be to cause confusion.

*Apex Bank* offers a useful reminder that the DuPont factors are not independent inquiries. They inform each other, and consistency across the analysis matters. The Federal Circuit's insistence that the Board apply the same similarity standard across factors is a sensible correction. Whether that correction ultimately changes the outcome for Apex Bank remains to be seen, but the decision marks a meaningful development in how trademark law grapples with the converged reality of modern financial services.
