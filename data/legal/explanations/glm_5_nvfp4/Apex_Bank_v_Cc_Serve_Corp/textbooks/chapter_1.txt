Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 1: # The Parties, Their Marks, and Procedural History

## CC Serve's Business Model and the ASPIRE Trademark for Credit Card Services

The party opposing registration in this case is CC Serve Corp., a company that provides credit card services to customers. Understanding CC Serve's business model is essential to understanding the scope of its trademark rights. CC Serve does not issue credit cards directly; rather, it operates through a partnership model. The company joins with banks that issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers. CC Serve and its affiliates then service those accounts. This arrangement—where a non-bank company handles the operational aspects of a credit card program while a bank issues the cards—is common in the financial services industry.

CC Serve's trademark rights derive from its registration of the ASPIRE mark with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). CC Serve owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE used in connection with credit card services. A "word mark," also called a "standard character mark," protects the textual element of a mark without claim to any particular font, style, size, or color. This form of protection is broad because it allows the trademark owner to use the mark in any visual presentation while maintaining rights to the underlying word.

The ASPIRE registration was issued in 1998 and carries an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Priority date is a critical concept in trademark law—the party that first uses a mark in commerce generally gains senior rights over later users. Here, CC Serve's priority date establishes its senior rights relative to Apex's later-filed applications, meaning that CC Serve's rights in ASPIRE predate Apex's attempted registration by more than two decades.

## Apex Bank's Business Operations and Intent-to-Use Applications for ASPIRE BANK Marks

The applicant in this case is Apex Bank, a retail bank chartered in Tennessee. Apex operates 18 branch locations and offers a range of traditional banking products: personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Importantly, Apex does not currently offer credit cards—a fact that becomes significant in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. Apex planned to expand its business by launching an internet bank under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

To secure trademark rights for this new venture, Apex filed "intent-to-use" applications with the USPTO in August 2019. An intent-to-use application, authorized under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act, allows an applicant to reserve trademark rights for a mark that the applicant has not yet used in commerce but has a bona fide intention to use in the future. This mechanism provides businesses with a way to establish priority dates before actually launching products or services. Apex sought to register both an ASPIRE BANK word mark and an ASPIRE BANK design mark for "[b]anking and financing services." The design mark would protect the visual elements—such as logo design, stylization, or graphic elements—while the word mark would protect the textual phrase "ASPIRE BANK."

## The Examination Process, Letter of Protest, and Publication of Apex's Marks

Trademark registration follows a specific procedural path that shapes this dispute. After an applicant files an application, a USPTO examining attorney reviews the application to determine whether the mark meets the requirements for registration under the Lanham Act. The examining attorney approved Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks for publication, meaning the attorney found no grounds for refusal.

Before that approval, however, CC Serve had attempted to alert the USPTO to potential problems. CC Serve submitted a "letter of protest" asserting that Apex's proposed marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve's existing ASPIRE mark. A letter of protest is a mechanism by which a third party can bring relevant information to the examining attorney's attention during the prosecution of a trademark application. Importantly, a letter of protest does not initiate an adversarial proceeding—it merely provides information that the examining attorney may consider. In this case, the examining attorney considered CC Serve's protest but nonetheless approved the marks for publication.

After approval, the marks were published in the USPTO's Official Gazette on December 17, 2019. Publication serves a notice function: it gives the public an opportunity to oppose registration of a mark before the mark registers. Once a mark publishes, third parties have 30 days to file an opposition or request an extension of time to oppose.

## CC Serve's Opposition Proceeding Before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board

CC Serve exercised its right to oppose registration by initiating an opposition proceeding in February 2020, within the statutory window after publication. An opposition proceeding is an adversarial administrative proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB or "Board"), a tribunal within the USPTO. In an opposition, the opposer (here, CC Serve) bears the burden of proving that the applicant's mark should not register. The parties may engage in discovery, file briefs, and present evidence, much like civil litigation but in an administrative forum.

CC Serve's opposition alleged that Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks would likely cause consumer confusion with CC Serve's prior ASPIRE mark. This claim falls under the traditional framework for analyzing likelihood of confusion, which has its origins in case law interpreting the Lanham Act. The opposition thus set the stage for the Board to apply the multi-factor test used to determine whether confusion is likely.

## The Board's Ruling Sustaining Opposition Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act

The TTAB sustained CC Serve's opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). Section 2(d) prohibits registration of a mark that so resembles a previously registered mark "as to be likely, when applied to the goods of the applicant, to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive." This statutory provision embodies the core policy of trademark law: protecting consumers from confusion about the source of goods or services.

The Board concluded that consumer confusion between Apex's proposed marks and CC Serve's ASPIRE mark was likely. In reaching this conclusion, the Board applied the framework established in *In re E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, a seminal decision from the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals (the Federal Circuit's predecessor court) that set forth thirteen factors for analyzing likelihood of confusion. The Board need not analyze all thirteen factors—only those relevant and supported by evidence. The Board's analysis focused heavily on the similarity of the parties' services and the similarity of the marks, ultimately finding in favor of CC Serve and refusing registration of Apex's marks.

## Jurisdiction and Appeal to the Federal Circuit

Apex appealed the Board's decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit has exclusive appellate jurisdiction over TTAB decisions under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). This specialized jurisdiction reflects the Federal Circuit's role in handling appeals involving intellectual property matters, including trademark registration decisions.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit applies specific standards of review. The court reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo—meaning it gives no deference to the Board's legal determinations. Factual findings, including findings on each relevant DuPont factor, are reviewed for "substantial evidence"—meaning the court asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the Board's conclusion. This framework creates a limited but meaningful scope for appellate review of TTAB decisions. The court may affirm the Board's findings if supported by substantial evidence, vacate findings that lack adequate evidentiary support, and remand for further proceedings consistent with its legal conclusions.
