Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 5: # The First DuPont Factor, Disposition, and Significance

## The first DuPont factor: similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression

The first DuPont factor directs the Board to consider "the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." This factor asks whether ordinary consumers, encountering both marks in the marketplace, would likely believe that the goods or services come from the same source. The analysis is not a mechanical comparison of individual elements. Rather, the court emphasizes that the focus must be on the "overall commercial impression" created by each mark and whether confusion as to source is likely to result.

In this case, the Board analyzed the similarity between CC Serve's word mark ASPIRE and Apex's word and design marks ASPIRE BANK. The Board concluded that the first factor weighed in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. However, the Federal Circuit did not reach the merits of this conclusion. Instead, the court identified a procedural flaw: the Board's analysis of the first factor depended in part on its assessment of the commercial strength of CC Serve's mark—a determination bound up with the sixth factor, which the court had already vacated.

## How commercial strength affects commercial impression analysis

The commercial strength or weakness of a mark plays a significant role in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. A commercially strong mark—one that is widely recognized by consumers—is more likely to be associated with a single source. Consumers encountering a similar mark on related goods may more readily assume a connection. Conversely, a commercially weak mark—particularly one in a crowded field of similar marks—receives a narrower scope of protection. Consumers in such markets are presumed to be more sophisticated in distinguishing between similar marks.

The court's decision highlights the relationship between commercial strength and commercial impression. The sixth DuPont factor addresses the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. When third-party marks are prevalent, consumers become "educated" to distinguish between them. This education affects how consumers perceive the commercial impression of the marks at issue. If CC Serve's ASPIRE mark is weak—surrounded by many similar marks in financial services—consumers would be less likely to confuse ASPIRE BANK with CC Serve's mark. The overall commercial impression analysis under the first factor thus depends, at least in part, on the sixth factor's assessment of mark strength.

## Interdependence of DuPont factors and why reconsideration is necessary

The Federal Circuit's treatment of the first DuPont factor illustrates an important principle: the DuPont factors are not wholly independent inquiries. The court recognized that its vacatur of the sixth factor required reconsideration of the first factor as well. Because the Board's commercial impression analysis was informed by its determination of commercial strength, and because the commercial strength determination was flawed by the Board's overly narrow restriction of third-party marks, the first factor analysis was necessarily incomplete.

The court explained that "reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." This reflects a practical reality of trademark law: factors that seem distinct can influence each other. If, on remand, the Board considers a broader universe of third-party marks (as the court directed for the sixth factor), it may conclude that CC Serve's mark is weaker than previously determined. A weaker mark could change the analysis of whether consumers are likely to be confused by the similarity of the marks under the first factor.

## The court's disposition: affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand

The Federal Circuit issued a mixed disposition. As to the second DuPont factor—the similarity of the parties' goods and services—the court affirmed the Board's finding. The Board had concluded that credit card services and banking and financing services were "legally identical, in part," and the Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supporting this conclusion.

However, as to the sixth and first DuPont factors, the court vacated the Board's findings. The sixth factor was vacated because the Board improperly limited its consideration of third-party marks to only those for credit card services, excluding marks for other banking and financial services. This limitation was inconsistent with the Board's own finding, under the second factor, that the parties' services were highly similar. The first factor was vacated because its analysis depended on a proper assessment of commercial strength.

The court remanded the case to the Board for reconsideration of both factors. On remand, the Board must apply a consistent standard of similarity: if the services are highly similar (as found under the second factor), the Board must consider third-party marks used on similarly related services under the sixth factor, and must reassess the commercial impression under the first factor in light of whatever commercial strength determination emerges.

## Award of costs to appellant

The court concluded with a brief but significant directive: "Costs to appellant." In federal appellate practice, costs are typically awarded to the prevailing party. Here, Apex Bank was the appellant—the party that appealed the Board's decision. Although Apex did not win on every issue (the second factor was affirmed against it), Apex succeeded in securing vacatur of two factors and a remand. The court thus treated Apex as the prevailing party for purposes of costs.

This award reflects the practical reality that Apex achieved a meaningful victory. While the ultimate question of likelihood of confusion remains unresolved, Apex succeeded in overturning the Board's refusal to register its marks on the specific grounds given. The case returns to the Board for further proceedings, and Apex will have the opportunity to argue that its ASPIRE BANK marks should be registrable.

## The significance of consistent similarity analysis across factors

The central teaching of this opinion concerns the need for consistency in how the Board defines similarity across different DuPont factors. The court articulated a clear principle: "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

This principle prevents the Board from employing a narrow definition of similarity when it favors a finding of likelihood of confusion, while simultaneously using a broader definition when considering evidence that might cut against confusion. The Board's approach was internally contradictory. Under the second factor, the Board found credit card services and banking services highly similar, which supported a finding of likely confusion. But under the sixth factor, the Board suddenly narrowed the definition of similarity to include only credit card services, excluding banking services from consideration—thereby limiting the number of third-party marks that could show the ASPIRE mark was weak.

The Federal Circuit rejected this inconsistency. A court or administrative body cannot manipulate legal standards to reach a preferred outcome. Once the Board determined that the services were highly similar, that determination had to carry through the entire analysis. This requirement for analytical consistency ensures that trademark law remains a principled inquiry rather than a results-oriented exercise. The decision serves as a reminder to practitioners and tribunals alike that the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, while multifactorial, must be coherent and internally consistent.
