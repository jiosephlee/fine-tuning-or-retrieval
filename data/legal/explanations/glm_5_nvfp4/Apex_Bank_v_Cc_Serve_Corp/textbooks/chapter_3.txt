Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 3: # The Second DuPont Factor: Similarity of Goods and Services

## The Legal Standard: Services Need Only Be Related, Not Identical

The second DuPont factor directs the Board to assess the similarity of the parties' goods and services. Importantly, the legal standard does not require identical services. As the Federal Circuit explained, quoting *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, "the respective products [need] be related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." This formulation establishes a relatively low threshold—the inquiry is whether the services are related enough that consumers might mistakenly believe they come from the same source. The focus is on the potential for consumer confusion, not on whether the services compete directly.

This standard reflects the underlying purpose of trademark law: preventing consumer confusion about source. When services are related, even if not identical, consumers encountering both marks may reasonably assume they come from the same provider. The Board's task under the second factor is therefore to examine the nature and relationship of the services in the marketplace, not to conduct a strict identity test.

## The Board's Interpretation of 'Credit Card Services' After Deletion from the Trademark ID Manual

A threshold issue in this case involved defining the scope of CC Serve's registered services. The registration identified "credit card services," but this entry had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve's registration issued. The ID Manual is a reference guide used by trademark practitioners and examiners to identify acceptable descriptions of goods and services. When an entry is deleted, it can create uncertainty about what the identified services actually encompass.

The Board addressed this uncertainty by conducting its own definitional analysis. It determined that "credit card services" includes: "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores." This comprehensive definition established the benchmark against which Apex's services would be compared.

## Use of Dictionary Definitions to Find Partial Legal Identity of Services

The Board then compared CC Serve's "credit card services" to Apex's identified "banking and financing services." The Board looked to dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" and found that these terms encompass extending credit and providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. Because these definitions overlapped with the Board's definition of credit card services, the Board concluded that the parties' services are "legally identical, in part."

The concept of "legal identity" in trademark law means that the services, as defined by their scope in the registration and application, overlap in a way that places them within the same commercial field. This is distinct from factual identity—the question is not whether the parties offer the exact same services in the real world, but whether the scope of their registered services overlaps. Here, because the dictionary definitions of banking and financing services include credit card activities, the Board found partial legal identity, which strongly supports a likelihood of confusion.

## Third-Party Registrations as Evidence That Services May Emanate from a Single Source

The Board bolstered its similarity finding by examining third-party trademark registrations. It found registrations that covered both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services under single marks. This evidence was significant because it demonstrated that "the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." When third-party registrants offer both types of services together, it indicates to consumers that such services are commonly provided by the same entity. This practice supports an inference that consumers would expect credit card services and banking services to come from the same source.

The Board's use of third-party registrations here is methodologically sound: the evidence demonstrates marketplace realities about how services are actually bundled and offered. If businesses commonly provide both types of services under one mark, consumers are more likely to be confused when they encounter similar marks used for these related services.

## Substantial Evidence Supporting the Board's Finding of High Similarity

On appeal, the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board's factual findings under the second DuPont factor for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This is a deferential standard—the court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment, but rather asks whether the Board's finding was supported by adequate evidence in the record.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's finding, noting that "the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services" and that "substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar." The court gave particular weight to the Board's definitional analysis and the third-party registration evidence. Based on this substantial evidence, the Board concluded that the second DuPont factor weighed "heavily" in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion.

## Apex's Argument That CC Serve Partners with Banks Rather Than Providing Banking Services

Apex challenged the Board's finding by arguing that the parties' services are "not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other." Apex emphasized that CC Serve's business model involves partnering with banks to manage credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself. According to Apex, this distinction meant the services were not similar enough to cause consumer confusion.

The Federal Circuit rejected this argument. The court's analysis suggests that Apex conflated the legal question under the second DuPont factor with the factual question of how the parties actually operate. The second factor examines the scope of the identified services in the registration and application, not necessarily the precise manner in which each party conducts its business. The Board had found partial legal identity based on the definitional overlap, and substantial evidence supported that finding. Apex's argument about the practical operation of CC Serve's business did not undermine the Board's conclusion that the services, as identified, were highly similar. The Federal Circuit thus affirmed the Board's finding on the second DuPont factor.
