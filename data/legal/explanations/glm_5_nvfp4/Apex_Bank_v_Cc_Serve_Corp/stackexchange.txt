Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What standard of review applies to TTAB likelihood of confusion determinations?
Question:
I'm reading this Federal Circuit opinion and trying to understand the standard of review. The court says it reviews legal conclusions de novo but findings of fact for substantial evidence. But then it says "likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors." And each DuPont factor is a question of fact tested for substantial evidence. So is likelihood of confusion ultimately a question of law or fact? And what does "substantial evidence" actually mean in practice?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer: **Likelihood of confusion is a question of law**, but it's what courts call a "hybrid" question—the legal conclusion depends on underlying factual findings about each DuPont factor. Those factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, while the ultimate legal conclusion is reviewed de novo.

Here's how the Federal Circuit frames it in the opinion (quoting *M2 Software*):

> "Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors."

And separately (quoting *Bose*):

> "Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal."

So in practice, the reviewing court takes each DuPont factor finding as a factual determination—if it's supported by substantial evidence, the court accepts it. Then the court performs its own de novo analysis of whether, given those facts, confusion is likely.

**What "substantial evidence" means in practice**

The opinion defines it (quoting *Consolidated Edison*):

> "Substantial evidence is 'such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.'"

This is a deferential standard. The court doesn't ask whether it would have reached the same conclusion, or even whether the Board's conclusion was the *best* conclusion—only whether *some* reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate. It's a low bar.

You can see this play out in the opinion's analysis of the second DuPont factor. The court affirms the Board's finding that the parties' services are "highly similar" because the Board "carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services" and substantial evidence supports that finding—even though Apex argued the services weren't identical or directly competitive.

**Why this matters in the opinion**

The distinction has real bite here. For the sixth DuPont factor, the court vacates not because the Board's factual findings lacked substantial evidence, but because the Board made a *legal error*—it applied an unduly narrow definition of "similarity" that was inconsistent with its own finding under the second factor. That's a legal issue the court can correct de novo.

So the practical upshot: The court will defer to the Board's factual findings under each DuPont factor if they're supported by substantial evidence, but it reserves the right to independently assess whether those facts add up to a likelihood of confusion.

### What does "legally identical" mean when the services aren't actually identical?
Question:
The Board found that credit card services and banking/financing services are "legally identical, in part." But CC Serve offers credit card services while Apex offers banking services—they're not the same thing. How can services be "legally identical" when they're different? What is the legal significance of this designation versus just saying they're "similar" or "related"? Does "legally identical" create a presumption of confusion?
Answer:
**Answer**

"Legally identical" is a term of art in trademark law that doesn't require the services to be the same in every respect. It means the identified services overlap sufficiently that they would be treated as identical for purposes of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

## What the Board Did Here

The Board looked at the *identification of services* in the trademark registrations, not the parties' actual commercial offerings. CC Serve's registration covers "credit card services," and the Board construed that term to include "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases" as well as account management and related services. J.A. 25. The Board then consulted dictionary definitions and found that "banking" and "finance" encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." J.A. 27–28. Because issuing credit cards falls within the dictionary meaning of banking and financing, the Board concluded the services are "legally identical, in part."

## Why "Legally Ident" Matters

This designation carries significant weight under the second DuPont factor. As the court explained, the standard doesn't require actual identity—the services need only be "related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012). When services are "legally identical," this factor weighs *heavily* in favor of finding likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35.

The Board also supported its finding with evidence that third-party registrations commonly cover both credit card services and banking/financing services, demonstrating that "the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." J.A. 33–34.

## The "In Part" Qualification

The phrase "in part" acknowledges that not every aspect of the parties' services overlaps. CC Serve doesn't offer checking accounts or mortgages, and Apex doesn't offer credit cards. But the identification of Apex's services—"banking and financing services"—is broad enough to encompass credit card issuance. Consumers encountering both services under similar marks could reasonably believe they come from the same source.

## Comparison to "Similar" or "Related"

"Legally identical" is a stronger designation than merely "similar" or "related." The latter terms still support finding likelihood of confusion, but "legally identical" establishes that the services are effectively the same *as identified in the registration*, which makes confusion more likely in the eyes of the law.

The court affirmed this finding, noting that "substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar"—using "highly similar" and "legally identical" interchangeably in this context.

### Why does evidence of third-party similar marks weaken a trademark's protection?
Question:
The court discusses how third-party uses of similar marks can show that a mark is "relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." The theory is that when a field is crowded with similar marks, "customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused." I don't understand this reasoning—if anything, wouldn't more similar marks in the marketplace cause MORE confusion, not less? Why does a "crowded field" mean the mark gets weaker protection?
Answer:
### Answer

Your intuition is understandable, but the doctrine works differently than you might expect. The key insight is that trademark law is concerned with **consumer psychology**, not abstract counting of similar marks.

**The Reasoning Explained**

The court articulates the theory directly: "When a field is crowded with similar marks, the theory is that customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused by similar marks." (quoting *Juice Generation*).

The logic works like this:

1. **Consumers learn from experience.** When shoppers repeatedly encounter multiple businesses using similar marks (e.g., multiple financial services using "Aspire" or variants), they learn that similarity doesn't automatically mean same source.

2. **They become more discerning.** Consumers in that market are "educated to distinguish between marks" (*Jack Wolfskin*). They learn to pay attention to the full mark, logos, trade dress, and other indicators of source.

3. **This reduces the likelihood of confusion.** Because consumers have been trained to look more carefully, another similar mark is less likely to trick them into thinking it comes from the same source.

4. **The mark is thus "commercially weak."** It still has protection—it's still inherently distinctive—but it gets a narrower scope. The owner can only block marks that are very similar, not marks that are merely somewhat similar.

**Why This Makes Sense**

Consider it from the opposite direction: if a market has *no* similar marks, and a consumer sees a mark that looks somewhat like the one they know, they might reasonably assume it's the same company branching out. But if that same consumer has seen ten different companies use similar-sounding marks, they've learned that "similar" ≠ "same source" in this industry.

**The Court's Application**

The Board found only nine third-party "Aspire" marks for credit card services, which it deemed insufficient to show a "crowded field." The Federal Circuit vacated this analysis, holding that the Board should have considered the 42+ other "Aspire" marks used in the broader financial services industry—since the Board had already found the parties' services were "highly similar." On remand, if the broader universe of marks shows a crowded field, CC Serve's ASPIRE mark would receive narrower protection, making confusion with Apex's ASPIRE BANK less likely.

### Why must the Board use consistent definitions of "similarity" across different DuPont factors?
Question:
The Federal Circuit held that the Board erred by using a narrow definition of "similar services" in the sixth DuPont factor analysis (limiting it to credit card services only) after finding the services were "highly similar" in the second factor. But aren't these different factors asking different questions? The second factor asks about similarity of the parties' goods to each other, while the sixth asks about third-party marks on similar goods. Why must the Board use the same scope of "similarity" for both factors? Couldn't there be a legitimate reason to define the relevant market differently for each factor?
Answer:
### Answer

The Federal Circuit's holding rests on a basic principle of administrative consistency: once the Board makes a factual finding about the relationship between goods or services, it must apply that finding coherently throughout the analysis. The court did not hold that the factors ask the same question, but rather that the Board cannot inconsistently define the same underlying facts.

**The Problem Was Inconsistency, Not Conflation of Factors**

The second and sixth DuPont factors do address different questions:

- **Factor two**: Are the parties' goods/services similar to each other?
- **Factor six**: Are there third-party marks using similar marks on similar goods?

But both factors require determining what counts as "similar" goods or services. Here, the Board made a factual finding under factor two that credit card services and banking/financing services are "highly similar"—in fact, "legally identical, in part." The Board supported this finding by looking at dictionary definitions and third-party registrations showing that such services "may emanate from a single source."

When the Board reached factor six, however, it abruptly narrowed the definition. It limited the relevant third-party marks to only those used for credit card services, excluding 42 other marks using "Aspire" in connection with other banking and financing services.

**Why This Was Error**

The court explained that this inconsistency was legally flawed for two related reasons:

1. **The standard for similarity doesn't change between factors**: The court stated, "We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor." The Board cannot apply one definition of "similar services" when comparing the parties' offerings, then apply a narrower definition when evaluating third-party use.

2. **The Board had already made a binding factual finding**: The court emphasized that "when the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

**The Practical Concern: Preventing Manipulation**

The underlying concern is that the Board should not be permitted to expand or contract definitions to reach a predetermined result. If banking services are similar enough to credit card services that consumers might believe they come from the same source (factor two), then third-party uses of "Aspire" for banking services are equally relevant to whether consumers have been "educated to distinguish between marks" (factor six). The Board cannot have it both ways—using breadth to find similarity when it favors one party, then using narrowness when it favors the other.

**The Proper Approach on Remand**

The court vacated and remanded so the Board can reconsider the sixth factor using the same definition of "similar services" it already found under factor two. This means the Board must consider whether third-party "Aspire" marks used for banking and financing services—not just credit card services—show that the mark is weak and entitled to narrower protection.

### Why was the first DuPont factor analysis vacated when the error was with the sixth factor?
Question:
The court vacated the Board's analysis of the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks themselves in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression) even though the legal error was with the sixth factor (third-party uses). The court says this is because "reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." But the first factor is about comparing the two marks' appearance, sound, and meaning—what does that have to do with third-party uses? How does the strength of a mark affect the first factor analysis?
Answer:
### Answer

You've identified an important point: the first DuPont factor facially concerns the marks' appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—not third-party uses. But the court's reasoning turns on how the *sixth factor's* analysis of commercial strength directly affects the *first factor's* commercial-impression inquiry.

**The key is that "commercial impression" under the first factor is not analyzed in isolation—it's evaluated in the context of the marketplace.**

The sixth factor determines whether a mark is commercially strong or weak based on the number of similar marks in use on similar goods. As the court explains, when "a field is crowded with similar marks," customers become "more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused by similar marks." This "educates" consumers to recognize differences.

**Why this matters for the first factor:** The "commercial impression" analysis asks whether consumers viewing the two marks would be confused about their source. If the sixth-factor analysis changes—say, the Board finds 42 additional third-party uses rather than just 9—the mark may be deemed weaker. A weaker mark means consumers are already accustomed to distinguishing it from similar marks in the field. That affects how consumers perceive the "commercial impression" of Apex's mark relative to CC Serve's mark.

The court put it succinctly: reconsideration of the sixth factor "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." If the mark is weaker than the Board found, consumers may be less likely to be confused by similarity in appearance, sound, or meaning—because they've learned to distinguish between similar marks in this crowded field.

**In short:** The first factor's commercial-impression analysis depends partly on the sixth factor's strength determination. Since the Board erred in defining which third-party marks to consider, its resulting strength finding—and thus its commercial-impression analysis—may change on remand. The court properly vacated both to ensure the Board reconsiders them together with consistent reasoning.

### How many third-party uses are needed to establish that a mark is weak under the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
The Board found that nine third-party Aspire-formative marks for credit card services weren't enough to show the mark is weak, citing cases that found 14 marks (Jack Wolfskin) and 26 marks (Juice Generation) to be sufficient. Is there an established threshold number? Does the number vary by industry or type of goods? The opinion says nine doesn't rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous"—but how should a practitioner know whether their evidence of third-party uses will be enough?
Answer:
### No Numerical Threshold Exists—It's a Totality-of-the-Circumstances Analysis

There is no established numerical threshold for how many third-party uses demonstrate that a mark is weak under the sixth DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit has not set a bright-line rule, and the numbers cited in *Jack Wolfskin* (14 marks) and *Juice Generation* (26 marks) are illustrative, not binding minimums.

### The Standard Is Qualitative, Not Quantitative

The sixth DuPont factor asks about "the number **and nature** of similar marks in use on similar goods." The Board's characterization of third-party use as needing to be "considerable" or "ubiquitous" comes from Federal Circuit precedent describing *how* the evidence functions, not from a counting exercise.

As the opinion explains, the theory is that when a field is crowded with similar marks, "customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused by similar marks." The question is whether the evidence demonstrates that consumers have been "educated to distinguish between marks" in this way—this is ultimately a fact-intensive inquiry.

### Consistency Across Factors Matters More Than Raw Numbers

The most important holding in *Apex Bank* for practitioners is not about counting, but about **definitional consistency**. The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's sixth-factor analysis because the Board:

1. Found the parties' services "highly similar" (even "partially legally identical") under the second factor; but
2. Then restricted the sixth-factor analysis only to marks for "credit card services"—excluding marks for other banking and financing services.

The court held this was legal error: "We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor."

The lesson for practitioners: if you argue goods are "similar" under one DuPont factor, you should be prepared to live with that breadth of similarity under other factors—and vice versa. The Board cannot narrow the universe of comparable third-party uses more restrictively than its own finding of similarity elsewhere in the analysis.

### Practical Guidance

Given the absence of a bright-line rule, practitioners should:

1. **Cast a wide net**: Gather as many third-party uses as possible within the scope of goods/services you can plausibly argue are "similar" under DuPont.

2. **Argue for consistency**: If the opponent's goods are similar to yours (factor two), that same similarity should expand the pool of relevant third-party uses (factor six).

3. **Focus on consumer perception**: The goal is showing consumers have been "educated" to distinguish similar marks. Quality matters—evidence of actual marketplace use may carry more weight than mere registrations.

4. **Understand the spectrum**: Nine may not be enough in some contexts, while fourteen was sufficient in *Jack Wolfskin*. The number interacts with the nature of the marks, the crowdedness of the field, and how similar the goods truly are.

On remand, the Board must now reconsider the sixth factor using a definition of "similar goods" consistent with its own finding that credit card services and banking/financing services are "highly similar"—which will expand the pool of relevant third-party Aspire-formative marks well beyond nine.

### Why were costs awarded to the appellant when they lost on one of three factors?
Question:
The court awarded costs to Apex as the appellant, even though the Board's opposition decision was affirmed in part (the second factor finding was affirmed). Apex won on two factors (vacatur and remand) but lost on one (the second factor was affirmed). I thought costs typically go to the prevailing party. Who is considered the "prevailing party" in a partially affirmed, partially vacated appeal like this? Does getting a vacatur and remand count as "prevailing" for costs purposes even though the underlying opposition was sustained?
Answer:
### Answer

You're right that costs generally go to the "prevailing party," but that determination is more nuanced when an appeal yields mixed results. Here, Apex was properly treated as the prevailing party because it obtained the primary relief it sought: vacatur of the Board's likelihood-of-confusion determination and a remand for reconsideration.

## The "Prevailing Party" Standard in Mixed-Result Appeals

Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 39(a)(2), the court has discretion to award costs "as justice may require" when the result is mixed. A party can be "prevailing" even without winning every issue if they secure the "primary relief sought" or achieve a "material alteration of the legal relationship of the parties" in their favor.

The key question is: what was the practical effect of the appeal?

## Apex Secured the Primary Relief It Sought

Apex appealed the Board's likelihood-of-confusion determination. While the court affirmed the second DuPont factor (similarity of services), it vacated two critical factors:

1. **Sixth factor (third-party marks):** The Board erred by defining "similar goods" too narrowly, excluding relevant third-party marks that could demonstrate commercial weakness.

2. **First factor (similarity of marks):** This was vacated because reconsideration of the sixth factor "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."

The court's remedy—vacatur and remand—means the Board's refusal to register Apex's marks is no longer a final decision. The matter must be reconsidered under the correct legal standard. That is a material alteration in Apex's legal position.

## Losing on One Factor Doesn't Undermine Prevailing Status

That Apex lost on the second factor doesn't change the analysis. The vacatur of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis as a whole, plus remand for reconsideration, means Apex accomplished its main objective: undoing the Board's decision and getting another opportunity for registration.

In mixed-result appeals, courts look to whether the appellant "substantially prevailed" on the central issues. See, e.g., *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983) (discussing prevailing party analysis where plaintiffs "succeeded on any significant issue"). Here, Apex succeeded on two of three challenged factors, and those successes directly led to vacatur of the Board's conclusion.

## Summary

The "costs to appellant" award reflects that Apex, while not winning every argument, achieved the principal relief it sought—vacatur of the opposition decision and a remand for proper reconsideration. That makes Apex the prevailing party under the applicable standard.
