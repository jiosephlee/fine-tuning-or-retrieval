Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Parties, Their Marks, and Procedural History

## CC Serve's Business Model and the ASPIRE Trademark for Credit Card Services

The party opposing registration in this case is CC Serve Corp., a company that provides credit card services to customers. Understanding CC Serve's business model is essential to understanding the scope of its trademark rights. CC Serve does not issue credit cards directly; rather, it operates through a partnership model. The company joins with banks that issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers. CC Serve and its affiliates then service those accounts. This arrangement—where a non-bank company handles the operational aspects of a credit card program while a bank issues the cards—is common in the financial services industry.

CC Serve's trademark rights derive from its registration of the ASPIRE mark with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). CC Serve owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE used in connection with credit card services. A "word mark," also called a "standard character mark," protects the textual element of a mark without claim to any particular font, style, size, or color. This form of protection is broad because it allows the trademark owner to use the mark in any visual presentation while maintaining rights to the underlying word.

The ASPIRE registration was issued in 1998 and carries an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Priority date is a critical concept in trademark law—the party that first uses a mark in commerce generally gains senior rights over later users. Here, CC Serve's priority date establishes its senior rights relative to Apex's later-filed applications, meaning that CC Serve's rights in ASPIRE predate Apex's attempted registration by more than two decades.

## Apex Bank's Business Operations and Intent-to-Use Applications for ASPIRE BANK Marks

The applicant in this case is Apex Bank, a retail bank chartered in Tennessee. Apex operates 18 branch locations and offers a range of traditional banking products: personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Importantly, Apex does not currently offer credit cards—a fact that becomes significant in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. Apex planned to expand its business by launching an internet bank under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

To secure trademark rights for this new venture, Apex filed "intent-to-use" applications with the USPTO in August 2019. An intent-to-use application, authorized under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act, allows an applicant to reserve trademark rights for a mark that the applicant has not yet used in commerce but has a bona fide intention to use in the future. This mechanism provides businesses with a way to establish priority dates before actually launching products or services. Apex sought to register both an ASPIRE BANK word mark and an ASPIRE BANK design mark for "[b]anking and financing services." The design mark would protect the visual elements—such as logo design, stylization, or graphic elements—while the word mark would protect the textual phrase "ASPIRE BANK."

## The Examination Process, Letter of Protest, and Publication of Apex's Marks

Trademark registration follows a specific procedural path that shapes this dispute. After an applicant files an application, a USPTO examining attorney reviews the application to determine whether the mark meets the requirements for registration under the Lanham Act. The examining attorney approved Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks for publication, meaning the attorney found no grounds for refusal.

Before that approval, however, CC Serve had attempted to alert the USPTO to potential problems. CC Serve submitted a "letter of protest" asserting that Apex's proposed marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve's existing ASPIRE mark. A letter of protest is a mechanism by which a third party can bring relevant information to the examining attorney's attention during the prosecution of a trademark application. Importantly, a letter of protest does not initiate an adversarial proceeding—it merely provides information that the examining attorney may consider. In this case, the examining attorney considered CC Serve's protest but nonetheless approved the marks for publication.

After approval, the marks were published in the USPTO's Official Gazette on December 17, 2019. Publication serves a notice function: it gives the public an opportunity to oppose registration of a mark before the mark registers. Once a mark publishes, third parties have 30 days to file an opposition or request an extension of time to oppose.

## CC Serve's Opposition Proceeding Before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board

CC Serve exercised its right to oppose registration by initiating an opposition proceeding in February 2020, within the statutory window after publication. An opposition proceeding is an adversarial administrative proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB or "Board"), a tribunal within the USPTO. In an opposition, the opposer (here, CC Serve) bears the burden of proving that the applicant's mark should not register. The parties may engage in discovery, file briefs, and present evidence, much like civil litigation but in an administrative forum.

CC Serve's opposition alleged that Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks would likely cause consumer confusion with CC Serve's prior ASPIRE mark. This claim falls under the traditional framework for analyzing likelihood of confusion, which has its origins in case law interpreting the Lanham Act. The opposition thus set the stage for the Board to apply the multi-factor test used to determine whether confusion is likely.

## The Board's Ruling Sustaining Opposition Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act

The TTAB sustained CC Serve's opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). Section 2(d) prohibits registration of a mark that so resembles a previously registered mark "as to be likely, when applied to the goods of the applicant, to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive." This statutory provision embodies the core policy of trademark law: protecting consumers from confusion about the source of goods or services.

The Board concluded that consumer confusion between Apex's proposed marks and CC Serve's ASPIRE mark was likely. In reaching this conclusion, the Board applied the framework established in *In re E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, a seminal decision from the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals (the Federal Circuit's predecessor court) that set forth thirteen factors for analyzing likelihood of confusion. The Board need not analyze all thirteen factors—only those relevant and supported by evidence. The Board's analysis focused heavily on the similarity of the parties' services and the similarity of the marks, ultimately finding in favor of CC Serve and refusing registration of Apex's marks.

## Jurisdiction and Appeal to the Federal Circuit

Apex appealed the Board's decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit has exclusive appellate jurisdiction over TTAB decisions under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). This specialized jurisdiction reflects the Federal Circuit's role in handling appeals involving intellectual property matters, including trademark registration decisions.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit applies specific standards of review. The court reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo—meaning it gives no deference to the Board's legal determinations. Factual findings, including findings on each relevant DuPont factor, are reviewed for "substantial evidence"—meaning the court asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the Board's conclusion. This framework creates a limited but meaningful scope for appellate review of TTAB decisions. The court may affirm the Board's findings if supported by substantial evidence, vacate findings that lack adequate evidentiary support, and remand for further proceedings consistent with its legal conclusions.

# The Legal Framework for Likelihood of Confusion Analysis

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act and the Refusal of Registration for Confusingly Similar Marks

The foundational statute governing this dispute is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). This provision prohibits the registration of a trademark that so resembles a mark already registered as to be likely, when used on or in connection with the goods of the applicant, to cause confusion, mistake, or deception. The statutory language reflects the core policy concern of trademark law: protecting consumers from confusion about the source of goods or services. When an examining attorney or the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board determines that confusion is likely between an applicant's mark and a prior registrant's mark, registration must be refused.

In this case, CC Serve opposed Apex Bank's registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, invoking Section 2(d) as the basis for its opposition. CC Serve was the owner of a prior registration for the word mark ASPIRE, used in connection with credit card services, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. When Apex filed intent-to-use applications for ASPIRE BANK marks in connection with "banking and financing services," CC Serve asserted that consumers would likely be confused into believing that Apex's services originated from or were connected with CC Serve. The Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d), concluding that consumer confusion was likely. Apex appealed, challenging the Board's analysis.

## The Thirteen DuPont Factors for Likelihood of Confusion Analysis

To determine whether a likelihood of confusion exists under Section 2(d), courts apply the analytical framework established in *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The Federal Circuit has held that likelihood of confusion requires registration refusal "when confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods." The *DuPont* framework identifies thirteen factors that may be relevant to this determination. These factors guide the analysis of whether the marks and the circumstances of their use are such that consumers might be confused about the source of the goods or services.

The factors include, among others: (1) the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression; (2) the similarity or dissimilarity of the goods or services as described in the application and registration; and (6) the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. The Board need not mechanically address all thirteen factors in every case. As the court reiterated, the Board must consider only those factors "that are relevant and of record." This principle recognizes that not every factor will be pertinent to every dispute, and the Board has discretion to focus its analysis on the considerations that actually bear on the likelihood of confusion.

In this appeal, Apex challenged the Board's analysis of three specific factors: the first factor (similarity of the marks), the second factor (similarity of the goods and services), and the sixth factor (third-party use of similar marks). The court addressed each in turn, demonstrating how the *DuPont* framework operates in practice.

## Likelihood of Confusion as a Question of Law Based on Underlying Factual Findings

A critical aspect of the *DuPont* framework is the distinction between law and fact. The court explained that "likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors." This formulation has important implications for how courts review Board decisions. The ultimate determination—whether confusion is likely—is a legal conclusion. However, that conclusion depends on factual findings about the marks, the goods, the channels of trade, the sophistication of purchasers, and other considerations enumerated in *DuPont*.

The court further clarified that "[e]ach of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact." This means that when the Board makes findings under any given factor—for example, determining that two marks are similar in appearance, or that the parties' goods are related—that finding is factual in nature. The legal conclusion about likelihood of confusion emerges from the aggregate of these factual findings. Understanding this structure is essential for grasping the court's analysis and the appropriate standard of review.

## De Novo Review of Legal Conclusions and Substantial Evidence Review of Factual Findings

The distinction between questions of law and questions of fact determines the standard of appellate review. The Federal Circuit stated that it reviews "the Board's legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." De novo review means that the court considers the legal question anew, without deference to the Board's conclusion. The court applies the law independently to the facts found. In contrast, substantial evidence review is deferential. Under this standard, the Board's factual findings must be upheld if "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion" exists in the record.

This bifurcated standard played a central role in the court's disposition of this appeal. When the court addressed the second *DuPont* factor—the similarity of the parties' services—it applied substantial evidence review. Apex argued that the services were not the same and were not directly competitive. The court rejected this argument, concluding that "substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar." The Board had examined dictionary definitions, considered the scope of "credit card services" and "banking and financing services," and looked at third-party registrations covering both types of services. The court deferred to these factual findings because they were supported by substantial evidence.

However, when the court identified a legal error in the Board's analysis of the sixth *DuPont* factor, it took a different approach. The Board had restricted its consideration of third-party marks to those used in connection with credit card services, excluding marks used for other banking and financing services. The court held that "the Board's definition of similarity [was] too narrow" and that this constituted legal error, not merely a factual finding subject to substantial evidence review. Because the Board had already found the services to be "highly similar" and even "partially legally identical" under the second factor, the court reasoned that the Board should have applied the same standard of similarity when analyzing the sixth factor. This legal error required vacatur and remand.

## The Board's Discretion to Consider Only Relevant Factors of Record

The *DuPont* framework grants the Board flexibility in conducting its analysis. The court reiterated that the Board "need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.'" This principle serves practical and analytical purposes. Not every factor will have bearing on every case, and the record may lack evidence on some factors. The Board properly focuses on the considerations that the parties have developed and that matter to the particular dispute.

However, this discretion does not permit the Board to apply inconsistent standards across the factors it does consider. The court's analysis of the sixth factor illustrates this limitation. The Board had discretion to determine which *DuPont* factors warranted analysis, but once it chose to analyze the sixth factor, it was required to do so correctly. The court found that the Board's restrictive definition of "similar goods" under the sixth factor was inconsistent with its prior finding that the parties' services were "highly similar" under the second factor. The Board could not adopt one standard of similarity for one factor and a different, narrower standard for another. The court thus vacated the Board's findings on the sixth factor and remanded for reconsideration using the proper legal standard.

The court also vacated the Board's finding on the first factor—the similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The court explained that "commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor," and because reconsideration of the sixth factor "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness," the first factor would need to be reassessed on remand as well. This interdependence among factors demonstrates how the *DuPont* framework functions as an integrated analysis: a change in one factual finding may affect the overall likelihood-of-confusion determination and require adjustment of other findings.

The court ultimately affirmed the Board's finding as to the second factor, vacated the Board's findings as to the first and sixth factors, and remanded for further proceedings. Costs were awarded to Apex, the appellant.

# The Second DuPont Factor: Similarity of Goods and Services

## The Legal Standard: Services Need Only Be Related, Not Identical

The second DuPont factor directs the Board to assess the similarity of the parties' goods and services. Importantly, the legal standard does not require identical services. As the Federal Circuit explained, quoting *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, "the respective products [need] be related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." This formulation establishes a relatively low threshold—the inquiry is whether the services are related enough that consumers might mistakenly believe they come from the same source. The focus is on the potential for consumer confusion, not on whether the services compete directly.

This standard reflects the underlying purpose of trademark law: preventing consumer confusion about source. When services are related, even if not identical, consumers encountering both marks may reasonably assume they come from the same provider. The Board's task under the second factor is therefore to examine the nature and relationship of the services in the marketplace, not to conduct a strict identity test.

## The Board's Interpretation of 'Credit Card Services' After Deletion from the Trademark ID Manual

A threshold issue in this case involved defining the scope of CC Serve's registered services. The registration identified "credit card services," but this entry had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve's registration issued. The ID Manual is a reference guide used by trademark practitioners and examiners to identify acceptable descriptions of goods and services. When an entry is deleted, it can create uncertainty about what the identified services actually encompass.

The Board addressed this uncertainty by conducting its own definitional analysis. It determined that "credit card services" includes: "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores." This comprehensive definition established the benchmark against which Apex's services would be compared.

## Use of Dictionary Definitions to Find Partial Legal Identity of Services

The Board then compared CC Serve's "credit card services" to Apex's identified "banking and financing services." The Board looked to dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" and found that these terms encompass extending credit and providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. Because these definitions overlapped with the Board's definition of credit card services, the Board concluded that the parties' services are "legally identical, in part."

The concept of "legal identity" in trademark law means that the services, as defined by their scope in the registration and application, overlap in a way that places them within the same commercial field. This is distinct from factual identity—the question is not whether the parties offer the exact same services in the real world, but whether the scope of their registered services overlaps. Here, because the dictionary definitions of banking and financing services include credit card activities, the Board found partial legal identity, which strongly supports a likelihood of confusion.

## Third-Party Registrations as Evidence That Services May Emanate from a Single Source

The Board bolstered its similarity finding by examining third-party trademark registrations. It found registrations that covered both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services under single marks. This evidence was significant because it demonstrated that "the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." When third-party registrants offer both types of services together, it indicates to consumers that such services are commonly provided by the same entity. This practice supports an inference that consumers would expect credit card services and banking services to come from the same source.

The Board's use of third-party registrations here is methodologically sound: the evidence demonstrates marketplace realities about how services are actually bundled and offered. If businesses commonly provide both types of services under one mark, consumers are more likely to be confused when they encounter similar marks used for these related services.

## Substantial Evidence Supporting the Board's Finding of High Similarity

On appeal, the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board's factual findings under the second DuPont factor for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This is a deferential standard—the court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment, but rather asks whether the Board's finding was supported by adequate evidence in the record.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's finding, noting that "the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services" and that "substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar." The court gave particular weight to the Board's definitional analysis and the third-party registration evidence. Based on this substantial evidence, the Board concluded that the second DuPont factor weighed "heavily" in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion.

## Apex's Argument That CC Serve Partners with Banks Rather Than Providing Banking Services

Apex challenged the Board's finding by arguing that the parties' services are "not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other." Apex emphasized that CC Serve's business model involves partnering with banks to manage credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself. According to Apex, this distinction meant the services were not similar enough to cause consumer confusion.

The Federal Circuit rejected this argument. The court's analysis suggests that Apex conflated the legal question under the second DuPont factor with the factual question of how the parties actually operate. The second factor examines the scope of the identified services in the registration and application, not necessarily the precise manner in which each party conducts its business. The Board had found partial legal identity based on the definitional overlap, and substantial evidence supported that finding. Apex's argument about the practical operation of CC Serve's business did not undermine the Board's conclusion that the services, as identified, were highly similar. The Federal Circuit thus affirmed the Board's finding on the second DuPont factor.

# The Sixth DuPont Factor: Third-Party Use and Commercial Strength

## Purpose of the Sixth Factor: Third-Party Use Demonstrates Mark Weakness

The sixth DuPont factor considers "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves an important function in likelihood-of-confusion analysis: evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods demonstrates that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The logic is straightforward. When consumers encounter numerous similar marks in a particular market, they become more sophisticated at distinguishing among them. A crowded marketplace teaches consumers to pay closer attention to subtle differences, reducing the likelihood that they will be confused by similar marks.

The Federal Circuit has recognized this principle across multiple cases. In *Palm Bay Imports, Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772*, the court explained that third-party use evidence is relevant specifically to show mark weakness. In *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enterprises LLC*, the court elaborated that when a field is crowded with similar marks, customers become more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused. Similarly, in *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.*, the court noted that evidence consumers have been educated to distinguish between marks tends to indicate a lack of commercial strength. Thus, the sixth factor operates as a reality check: even if marks are similar, confusion is less likely when consumers have learned to navigate a crowded field of similar marks.

## Apex's Evidence of Third-Party ASPIRE-Formative Marks in Financial Services

Apex recognized the potential of the sixth factor and submitted several exhibits to the Board showing third-party uses of marks incorporating the word "Aspire." These marks used "Aspire" in connection with credit card-related services and, more broadly, across the financial services industry. The evidence was designed to show that "Aspire" was a commonly used term in financial services, suggesting that CC Serve's mark was weak and entitled to only narrow protection. If successful, this argument would have undermined CC Serve's claim that consumers would likely confuse Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks with CC Serve's ASPIRE mark.

## The Board's Erroneous Restriction to Only Credit Card Services Marks

The Board made a critical error in how it defined the universe of relevant third-party marks. The Board first concluded that because of the overlap between Apex and CC Serve's services, the properly defined relevant public was "consumers of 'credit card services.'" Having defined the relevant public so narrowly, the Board then restricted its sixth-factor analysis to only those third-party marks used in connection with credit card services. The Board deemed marks using "Aspire" for other financial services to be "essentially irrelevant."

Under this narrowed scope, the Board found only nine ASPIRE-formative marks for credit card services. Citing *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*, the Board concluded that nine uses did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use necessary to demonstrate mark weakness. *Jack Wolfskin* had discussed "voluminous evidence" and highlighted fourteen "notable examples," while *Juice Generation* had referred to twenty-six third-party marks as "a considerable number." Finding that Apex's evidence fell short of these benchmarks, the Board concluded that Apex had not shown CC Serve's mark to be commercially or conceptually weak. The Board held that CC Serve's mark was entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks."

## The Legal Error: Imposing a Stricter Similarity Standard for Factor Six Than for Factor Two

The Federal Circuit identified a fundamental inconsistency in the Board's analysis. In analyzing the second DuPont factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services, the Board had determined that CC Serve's credit card services and Apex's banking and financing services were "highly similar"—in fact, "legally identical, in part." The Board reached this conclusion by looking at dictionary definitions showing that "banking" and "finance" encompass extending credit through credit cards, and by considering third-party registrations covering both types of services.

However, when the Board turned to the sixth factor, it abruptly narrowed its definition of similarity. The Board considered only third-party marks used for credit card services, excluding all marks used for other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit held that this was legal error. The sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—it requires only similar ones. The court cited *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy's, Inc.* and *Juice Generation* for this principle. By restricting its analysis to identical services, the Board imposed a stricter similarity standard for factor six than it had applied to factor two.

## The Principle of Consistent Scope Definitions Across DuPont Factors

The Federal Circuit's holding establishes an important principle: once the Board makes a factual finding about the degree of similarity between services, it must apply that finding consistently across all relevant DuPont factors. The court stated: "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

The court found no reason to impose "a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor." This inconsistency undermined the integrity of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The Federal Circuit therefore vacated the Board's findings as to both the sixth factor and the first factor (similarity of the marks in their entireties), and remanded for reconsideration. On remand, the Board must consider third-party ASPIRE-formative marks used on banking and financing services—not just credit card services—in light of its own finding that such services are highly similar to CC Serve's credit card services.

# The First DuPont Factor, Disposition, and Significance

## The first DuPont factor: similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression

The first DuPont factor directs the Board to consider "the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." This factor asks whether ordinary consumers, encountering both marks in the marketplace, would likely believe that the goods or services come from the same source. The analysis is not a mechanical comparison of individual elements. Rather, the court emphasizes that the focus must be on the "overall commercial impression" created by each mark and whether confusion as to source is likely to result.

In this case, the Board analyzed the similarity between CC Serve's word mark ASPIRE and Apex's word and design marks ASPIRE BANK. The Board concluded that the first factor weighed in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. However, the Federal Circuit did not reach the merits of this conclusion. Instead, the court identified a procedural flaw: the Board's analysis of the first factor depended in part on its assessment of the commercial strength of CC Serve's mark—a determination bound up with the sixth factor, which the court had already vacated.

## How commercial strength affects commercial impression analysis

The commercial strength or weakness of a mark plays a significant role in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. A commercially strong mark—one that is widely recognized by consumers—is more likely to be associated with a single source. Consumers encountering a similar mark on related goods may more readily assume a connection. Conversely, a commercially weak mark—particularly one in a crowded field of similar marks—receives a narrower scope of protection. Consumers in such markets are presumed to be more sophisticated in distinguishing between similar marks.

The court's decision highlights the relationship between commercial strength and commercial impression. The sixth DuPont factor addresses the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. When third-party marks are prevalent, consumers become "educated" to distinguish between them. This education affects how consumers perceive the commercial impression of the marks at issue. If CC Serve's ASPIRE mark is weak—surrounded by many similar marks in financial services—consumers would be less likely to confuse ASPIRE BANK with CC Serve's mark. The overall commercial impression analysis under the first factor thus depends, at least in part, on the sixth factor's assessment of mark strength.

## Interdependence of DuPont factors and why reconsideration is necessary

The Federal Circuit's treatment of the first DuPont factor illustrates an important principle: the DuPont factors are not wholly independent inquiries. The court recognized that its vacatur of the sixth factor required reconsideration of the first factor as well. Because the Board's commercial impression analysis was informed by its determination of commercial strength, and because the commercial strength determination was flawed by the Board's overly narrow restriction of third-party marks, the first factor analysis was necessarily incomplete.

The court explained that "reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." This reflects a practical reality of trademark law: factors that seem distinct can influence each other. If, on remand, the Board considers a broader universe of third-party marks (as the court directed for the sixth factor), it may conclude that CC Serve's mark is weaker than previously determined. A weaker mark could change the analysis of whether consumers are likely to be confused by the similarity of the marks under the first factor.

## The court's disposition: affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand

The Federal Circuit issued a mixed disposition. As to the second DuPont factor—the similarity of the parties' goods and services—the court affirmed the Board's finding. The Board had concluded that credit card services and banking and financing services were "legally identical, in part," and the Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supporting this conclusion.

However, as to the sixth and first DuPont factors, the court vacated the Board's findings. The sixth factor was vacated because the Board improperly limited its consideration of third-party marks to only those for credit card services, excluding marks for other banking and financial services. This limitation was inconsistent with the Board's own finding, under the second factor, that the parties' services were highly similar. The first factor was vacated because its analysis depended on a proper assessment of commercial strength.

The court remanded the case to the Board for reconsideration of both factors. On remand, the Board must apply a consistent standard of similarity: if the services are highly similar (as found under the second factor), the Board must consider third-party marks used on similarly related services under the sixth factor, and must reassess the commercial impression under the first factor in light of whatever commercial strength determination emerges.

## Award of costs to appellant

The court concluded with a brief but significant directive: "Costs to appellant." In federal appellate practice, costs are typically awarded to the prevailing party. Here, Apex Bank was the appellant—the party that appealed the Board's decision. Although Apex did not win on every issue (the second factor was affirmed against it), Apex succeeded in securing vacatur of two factors and a remand. The court thus treated Apex as the prevailing party for purposes of costs.

This award reflects the practical reality that Apex achieved a meaningful victory. While the ultimate question of likelihood of confusion remains unresolved, Apex succeeded in overturning the Board's refusal to register its marks on the specific grounds given. The case returns to the Board for further proceedings, and Apex will have the opportunity to argue that its ASPIRE BANK marks should be registrable.

## The significance of consistent similarity analysis across factors

The central teaching of this opinion concerns the need for consistency in how the Board defines similarity across different DuPont factors. The court articulated a clear principle: "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

This principle prevents the Board from employing a narrow definition of similarity when it favors a finding of likelihood of confusion, while simultaneously using a broader definition when considering evidence that might cut against confusion. The Board's approach was internally contradictory. Under the second factor, the Board found credit card services and banking services highly similar, which supported a finding of likely confusion. But under the sixth factor, the Board suddenly narrowed the definition of similarity to include only credit card services, excluding banking services from consideration—thereby limiting the number of third-party marks that could show the ASPIRE mark was weak.

The Federal Circuit rejected this inconsistency. A court or administrative body cannot manipulate legal standards to reach a preferred outcome. Once the Board determined that the services were highly similar, that determination had to carry through the entire analysis. This requirement for analytical consistency ensures that trademark law remains a principled inquiry rather than a results-oriented exercise. The decision serves as a reminder to practitioners and tribunals alike that the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, while multifactorial, must be coherent and internally consistent.
