Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## Federal Circuit Rejects Inconsistent Standards of 'Similarity' Across DuPont Factors

Multi-factor legal tests are designed to bring structure to nebulous questions, but they only work if the factors speak to one another. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit issued a useful corrective on this front, vacating a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board decision that applied inconsistent definitions of "similarity" across the famous *DuPont* factors. The decision reinforces a simple but vital principle: an administrative body cannot expand the scope of a market to find infringement while simultaneously narrowing that same market to ignore evidence of a crowded field.

The dispute arose from a straightforward opposition proceeding. CC Serve, owner of the mark ASPIRE for credit card services, opposed Apex Bank’s attempt to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar—a conclusion that usually spells doom for a junior user—but it took the unusual step of vacating the decision due to a logical inconsistency in how the Board defined the relevant universe of third-party marks.

The crux of the court’s analysis lies in the tension between the second and sixth *DuPont* factors. Under the second factor, which examines the similarity of goods and services, the Board found that credit card services and banking services are "legally identical, in part." The Board reached this conclusion by relying on dictionary definitions and third-party registrations showing that banks frequently issue credit cards. Having defined the services as highly similar, the Board then turned to the sixth factor, which considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. This factor is typically invoked by applicants to argue that a senior user’s mark is weak and deserves only narrow protection; if a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers are presumably more discerning.

Here, Apex presented evidence of 42 third-party marks using "Aspire" in connection with financial services. The Board, however, refused to consider the vast majority of these marks. It reasoned that because CC Serve’s registration was for credit card services, the relevant universe of third-party marks was limited only to those used for credit card services. By artificially narrowing the scope of the sixth factor, the Board found only nine relevant third-party marks and concluded that this number was insufficient to show that CC Serve’s mark was weak.

Judge Hughes, writing for the court, identified this analytical pivot as legal error. The Board cannot have it both ways. If credit card services and banking services are "highly similar" for the purpose of establishing a likelihood of confusion under factor two, they must also be "similar" for the purpose of evaluating the crowded field under factor six. The court correctly noted that the sixth factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. By restricting its analysis to identical services (credit cards) while simultaneously relying on a broader definition of similarity (banking and credit cards) to find a likelihood of confusion, the Board engaged in a jurisprudential sleight of hand.

This ruling has significant doctrinal implications for trademark prosecution and litigation. It prevents the Board—and by extension, trademark examiners—from using a one-way ratchet on the concept of similarity. Too often, parties (and tribunals) seek to define markets broadly when it helps establish a likelihood of confusion, but narrowly when evaluating the strength of the senior user’s mark. *Apex Bank* closes that door. The Federal Circuit made clear that once a factual finding regarding similarity is established, that finding must be carried through the rest of the analysis consistently.

The court also vacated the Board’s analysis of the first *DuPont* factor (similarity of the marks) and remanded for reconsideration. This was a necessary corollary to the sixth factor holding. As the court noted, the commercial impression of a mark—what the first factor measures—is inextricably linked to its strength. If, on remand, the Board considers the full scope of financial services marks and finds the ASPIRE mark to be commercially weak, that finding will necessarily inform the overall commercial impression of the mark. A weak mark requires a closer similarity to cause confusion; a strong mark tolerates less.

For practitioners, *Apex Bank* serves as a potent tool in oppositions and cancellations involving broad service descriptions. Applicants facing an opposition should scour the registry not just for identical services, but for all services the Board might deem "similar." If the Board finds those services similar enough to support a confusion analysis, *Apex Bank* dictates that those same services must be considered when evaluating whether the senior user’s mark is strong enough to warrant protection. The decision ensures that the *DuPont* factors remain a cohesive analytical framework rather than a collection of disjointed arguments.



# Third-Party Marks and Commercial Strength: Clarifying the Scope of Factor Six After Apex Bank

When the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board evaluates likelihood of confusion, it applies the thirteen-factor test from *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* But as the Federal Circuit's decision last week in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* demonstrates, the Board must apply those factors consistently—and when it fails to do so, its findings cannot stand. The decision clarifies an important but underappreciated aspect of the sixth DuPont factor: the scope of "similar" goods for purposes of assessing third-party marks must align with the Board's own findings about the similarity of the parties' goods and services.

The case arose from a straightforward opposition proceeding. CC Serve, which offers credit card services under the mark ASPIRE, opposed Apex Bank's application to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. But the Federal Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Hughes, identified a critical analytical flaw in how the Board evaluated evidence of third-party marks—evidence that Apex offered to show that the ASPIRE mark is weak and entitled to only narrow protection.

## The Board's Inconsistent Approach

The opinion turns on a simple inconsistency. Under the second DuPont factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services, the Board found that credit card services and banking services are "highly similar"—indeed, "legally identical, in part." The Board reached this conclusion based on dictionary definitions showing that banking encompasses extending credit through credit cards, combined with evidence that financial institutions commonly offer both types of services under a single mark.

But when the Board turned to the sixth DuPont factor, which considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods, it abruptly narrowed its aperture. Rather than looking at third-party marks used across the banking and financing services that it had just deemed highly similar to credit card services, the Board considered only marks used specifically for credit card services. This meant excluding 42 third-party marks using ASPIRE or ASPIRE-formative elements for banking and financial services, considering only nine marks for credit card services. The Board then concluded that nine marks was insufficient to demonstrate that CC Serve's mark is commercially weak.

The Federal Circuit properly rejected this sleight of hand. As the court explained, the sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of marks used on "similar" goods—not identical goods. And critically, the Board cannot define "similar" one way for purposes of the second factor and another way for the sixth. When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services at issue are highly similar, it must "retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

This holding makes eminent sense. The purpose of the sixth factor is to assess whether consumers have been "educated to distinguish between marks" in a crowded field. That educational effect depends on what consumers encounter in the marketplace. If banking services and credit card services are highly similar—so similar that consumers might believe they come from the same source—then third-party marks used for banking services are part of the relevant commercial landscape. The Board cannot cabin off that evidence by pretending, for purposes of factor six, that the services are more distinct than it has already concluded they are.

## The Evidentiary Threshold Question

The opinion leaves unanswered a question that practitioners will nonetheless find significant: just how many third-party marks are enough to demonstrate commercial weakness? The Board, relying on the Federal Circuit's decisions in *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*, concluded that nine marks was not enough. In *Jack Wolfskin*, the court pointed to fourteen "notable examples" of third-party use; in *Juice Generation*, twenty-six marks was deemed "a considerable number."

The Board's analysis here suggests a rough threshold—somewhere north of nine but perhaps somewhere south of twenty-six. But this counting exercise has always been somewhat arbitrary. As the court noted in *Jack Wolfskin*, what matters is whether the evidence demonstrates that consumers have been "educated to distinguish" between similar marks. Nine marks might well be sufficient in some contexts; twenty-six might be inadequate in others. The Board's rigid approach—tallying registrations and stopping once it reaches a seemingly insufficient number—risks turning a contextual inquiry into a mechanical one.

On remand, the Board will have the opportunity to consider all 51 third-party marks that Apex identified. Whether that number crosses the evidentiary threshold remains to be seen. But the more important point is that the Board must actually consider them rather than excluding them at the threshold based on an artificially narrow definition of similarity.

## Downstream Implications

The decision has implications beyond the likelihood-of-confusion context. The court's insistence on analytical consistency should extend to other multi-factor tests in trademark law, including the distinctiveness analysis under Section 2(f) and the fame analysis under Section 43(c). Practitioners would do well to scrutinize Board decisions for similar inconsistencies—situations where the Board defines relevant markets or relevant consumers differently across different factors.

The court also vacated the Board's analysis of the first DuPont factor, which concerns the similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The commercial impression analysis, the court reasoned, depends in part on the commercial strength of the senior mark—and that strength assessment may change once the Board properly considers the full range of third-party marks. This cascade effect underscores an important structural point: the DuPont factors are not hermetically sealed. The Board's analysis under one factor can properly inform its analysis under others.

For CC Serve, the road ahead is uncertain. The Board's finding on the similarity of services survives, and that finding weighs heavily toward confusion. But if the Board now determines that the ASPIRE mark is commercially weak, that weakness may temper the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The commercial strength of a mark has long been recognized as a key variable in the confusion calculus—a strong mark is more likely to be associated with a single source, while a weak mark may be perceived as one among many in a crowded field.

*Apex Bank* is ultimately a case about analytical rigor. The Federal Circuit sent a clear message: the Board cannot reach a conclusion about likelihood of confusion by rigging the analysis. When the Board finds that goods are similar, it must give effect to that finding across the DuPont factors. Anything less is not just inconsistent—it's legally flawed.



# From Credit Cards to Online Banking: TTAB's Finding of 'Legally Identical' Services Reflects Financial Services Convergence

The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* highlights a tension that trademark practitioners in the financial services sector have long recognized: the gap between how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board analyzes service similarity and how consumers actually experience the modern financial marketplace. While the court ultimately remanded the case for reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor—third-party use of similar marks—it affirmed the Board's critical finding that credit card services and "banking and financing services" are "legally identical, in part." That holding, and the analytical approach underpinning it, deserves close attention from trademark practitioners navigating an increasingly converged financial services landscape.

## The Board's Convergence Analysis

The second DuPont factor—the similarity of the parties' goods and services—proved to be the linchpin of the Board's likelihood-of-confusion analysis, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that finding. The Board's approach was methodical but revealing. Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for "credit card services" had been deleted after CC Serve's registration, the Board first had to define what those services encompass. It landed on a broad construction: "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores."

The Board then turned to dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance," and concluded that these terms encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." This interpretive move allowed the Board to find that the services were "legally identical, in part." The Board further supported this conclusion with evidence of third-party registrations covering both credit card services and banking services—registrations that demonstrate, in the Board's view, that such services "may emanate from a single source under one mark."

The Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supported this analysis. The court rejected Apex's argument that CC Serve merely "partners with banks" rather than providing banking services itself, noting that the Board had "carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services." The practical reality—that modern financial institutions commonly offer both credit cards and banking services under unified branding—carried significant weight.

## The Sixth Factor Problem

Where the Board faltered, according to the Federal Circuit, was in its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor. This factor considers the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods," and evidence of widespread third-party use can demonstrate that a mark is weak and entitled to only narrow protection. The theory is straightforward: when a marketplace is crowded with similar marks, consumers learn to distinguish among them more carefully, reducing the likelihood of confusion.

Apex submitted evidence of forty-two third-party uses of "Aspire" and Aspire-formative marks across credit card services and the broader financial services industry. But the Board, having defined the relevant public as "consumers of credit card services," considered only nine of those marks—the ones specifically tied to credit card services. The other marks, for banking and financing services, were deemed "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit properly identified the logical inconsistency. The Board had already determined, under the second factor, that credit card services and banking services are "highly similar"—indeed, "legally identical, in part." Having made that factual finding, the Board should have applied the same similarity standard when evaluating third-party marks under the sixth factor. As the court explained, "We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor." The Board cannot have it both ways: treating services as highly similar when analyzing confusion, but as meaningfully distinct when evaluating mark strength.

## Industry Reality Meets Legal Doctrine

The Board's second-factor analysis, affirmed by the Federal Circuit, reflects an important doctrinal shift in how trademark law treats the financial services sector. Traditional distinctions between banks, credit card issuers, and other financial services providers have become increasingly artificial. Major banks issue credit cards under their own marks. Credit card companies partner with banks to offer deposit accounts. Fintech platforms provide services that cut across all these categories. The Board's willingness to find legal identity between credit card services and banking services acknowledges this reality.

For trademark practitioners, the implications are significant. When drafting identifications of goods and services for financial services clients, the *Apex Bank* decision suggests that broad identifications may carry broader exposure to oppositions and citations than in other industries. A registration for "banking and financing services" may now be understood to sweep in credit card services, and vice versa. Practitioners should consider whether more specific identifications might provide clearer boundaries—or whether broader identifications are necessary to capture the full scope of a client's actual and anticipated services.

The decision also underscores the importance of sixth-factor evidence in financial services cases. If credit card services and banking services are "highly similar," then third-party registrations and uses across the broader financial services industry should be relevant to demonstrating that consumers have been educated to distinguish among similar marks. Practitioners opposing registration—or defending against opposition—should cast a wide evidentiary net.

## What Comes Next

On remand, the Board must reconsider both the sixth and first DuPont factors. The sixth-factor analysis will likely include a broader universe of third-party Aspire marks, potentially weakening CC Serve's claim to a broad scope of protection. If the Board finds that the financial services marketplace is sufficiently crowded with Aspire-formative marks, consumers may be more adept at distinguishing Apex's ASPIRE BANK from CC Serve's ASPIRE.

The first factor—the similarity of the marks themselves—must also be reconsidered because commercial strength informs the overall commercial impression analysis. A weaker senior mark may affect how similar a junior mark must be to cause confusion.

*Apex Bank* offers a useful reminder that the DuPont factors are not independent inquiries. They inform each other, and consistency across the analysis matters. The Federal Circuit's insistence that the Board apply the same similarity standard across factors is a sensible correction. Whether that correction ultimately changes the outcome for Apex Bank remains to be seen, but the decision marks a meaningful development in how trademark law grapples with the converged reality of modern financial services.
