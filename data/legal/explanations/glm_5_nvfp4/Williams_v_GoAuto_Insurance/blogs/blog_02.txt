Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Strict Adherence or Strict Construction? The Paradox of Louisiana's Insurance Cancellation Doctrine

When Louisiana courts say that insurance cancellation procedures require "strict adherence" to statutory requirements, they mean it literally—or so the doctrine has long suggested. Any defect in the cancellation process, Louisiana courts have held, renders the cancellation ineffective, leaving the insurer on the hook for coverage it thought it had terminated. But a recent Fifth Circuit decision exposes a fundamental tension in this "strict adherence" framework: does strict adherence mean courts should require everything the legislature might have contemplated, or only what the legislature actually wrote?

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit confronted a fact pattern that would have been unimaginable when Louisiana's premium-financing statute was enacted. An insurance premium finance company, APAC, used an automated computer system to request policy cancellations when insureds defaulted on their payment plans. APAC's computer sent GoAuto's computer an email at midnight; GoAuto's system automatically cancelled the policy; only later that morning did a human APAC employee affix a signature stamp to printed documents. The insureds, left without coverage after an accident, cried foul. They argued that "strict adherence" meant what it said: the finance company must *certify* the statutory prerequisites, and certification requires a signature. The statute's silence on signatures, they contended, didn't matter—the whole point of strict adherence is to protect consumers by ensuring every procedural step is properly executed.

The Fifth Circuit disagreed, and its reasoning reveals something important about how strict adherence doctrines operate in practice.

## The Signature Question

The court's analysis began with the statutory text. Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated facts: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that it is in uncured default, that notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to other interested parties. The statute says nothing about signatures. The court noted that no Louisiana appellate decision has squarely held that a signature is required, though one decision—*Benitez v. Elsayed*—mentioned the absence of a signature among several deficiencies in a cancellation notice.

The Fifth Circuit's approach here is notably textualist. The statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that must certify, the court observed, and nothing in the statutory language suggests that certification must come from a named individual. "Strict adherence," the court reasoned, "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." This is the heart of the doctrinal paradox: a doctrine designed to be protective of insureds becomes, in the court's hands, a constraint on judicial augmentation of statutory requirements. The court refused to treat "strict adherence" as synonymous with "hypertechnical compliance with everything a court thinks the statute should have required."

## Receipt in the Digital Age

The plaintiffs' second argument—that GoAuto never properly "received" the cancellation request—required the court to grapple with modern technology's interaction with statutory language drafted in an earlier era. The statute provides that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested." The plaintiffs argued that "receipt" requires human possession, not merely electronic transmission to a computer system.

The court looked to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines receipt as taking physical possession or coming into possession from an outside source. This definition, the court concluded, is satisfied when GoAuto's computer system receives the email. "This is a modern statute set in current times," the court wrote, "and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court declined to read the statute as requiring human involvement at the moment of receipt when the statutory language itself imposes no such requirement.

## What Strict Adherence Really Means

The doctrinal significance of *Williams* lies in its clarification of what "strict adherence" actually demands. Louisiana courts have long held that strict adherence serves two functions: providing a clear method for fixing the time and date of cancellation, and ensuring a minimum cure period for defaulted insureds. These are protective purposes, aimed at preventing insurers from playing fast and loose with cancellation procedures that leave consumers unexpectedly uninsured.

But the Fifth Circuit's decision suggests that strict adherence, properly understood, is a limit on parties' ability to deviate from statutory requirements—not a license for courts to supplement those requirements with additional procedural protections the legislature omitted. There's a difference between requiring insurers and finance companies to do exactly what the statute says and requiring them to do more than the statute says. The plaintiffs in *Williams* wanted the court to enforce not just the statute's explicit requirements, but also the statutory scheme's apparent consumer-protective purpose. The court declined that invitation.

This distinction matters beyond Louisiana insurance law. Strict adherence doctrines appear in various consumer protection contexts, and courts often face the same choice: enforce the statute's explicit mandates, or fill in gaps that seem consistent with legislative purpose. *Williams* suggests that at least in this context, strict adherence cuts against judicial supplementation. The doctrine's very strictness constrains courts as much as it constrains insurers.

## Certification Denied

Perhaps tellingly, the Fifth Circuit declined the plaintiffs' request to certify the signature and receipt questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Certification, the court noted, is reserved for compelling circumstances, and the mere absence of state supreme court precedent isn't enough. The decision suggests confidence in the court's Erie guess—and perhaps confidence that Louisiana's highest court would reach the same conclusion.

The result is a victory for efficiency in premium-financing arrangements. Insurance companies and their financing partners can continue using automated cancellation systems without worrying that a missing signature will invalidate thousands of cancellations. For consumers, the message is less welcome: if you want more procedural protections than the statute explicitly provides, take it up with the legislature. Courts enforcing strict adherence doctrines won't add requirements the statute left out.
