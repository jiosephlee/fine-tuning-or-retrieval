Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# When Computers 'Receive': Statutory Interpretation in the Age of Automation

The Fifth Circuit's recent decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* addresses a question that courts will increasingly face as business processes become more automated: what does it mean for a company to "receive" something when no human is involved? The court's pragmatic answer—that a computer system's automated receipt of an email satisfies statutory receipt requirements—offers a template for interpreting legacy statutory language in light of modern commercial practices. But the opinion also raises deeper questions about how courts should approach statutory terms that were written for a paper-and-people world.

## The Problem of Automated Compliance

The case arose from a straightforward scenario. Three insureds had financed their auto insurance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company (APAC). When they defaulted on payments, APAC's computer system automatically sent cancellation requests to GoAuto's computer system via email at midnight. GoAuto's system—designed to "listen" for such requests—automatically cancelled the policies. Only later that morning did any human become involved, when an APAC employee applied a signature stamp to printed documents.

The insureds argued this process violated Louisiana's premium finance statute, which requires that "upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested." They contended that "receipt" requires human possession, and that the lack of a signature on the certification before cancellation rendered the process defective.

Judge Southwick, writing for the panel, rejected both arguments. The court held that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the finance company's certification, and that GoAuto's computer system receiving the email satisfied the statutory receipt requirement. On the latter point, the court was direct: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did."

## A Pragmatic Approach to "Receipt"

The court's analysis of the receipt question is notable for its practicality. Drawing on Black's Law Dictionary definitions, the court concluded that "receipt" means "the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession." The key insight was that GoAuto's computer system does, in fact, take possession of the email—it "hears" the request and acts on it, even if no human reviews it until later.

The court's description of the technical process is worth quoting: "Basically, a web service is like a listener. It's always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it's called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity." This anthropomorphic framing—computers that "listen" and "hear"—suggests the court was comfortable analogizing automated processes to human ones.

This approach has significant implications. Countless statutes and regulations use terms like "receipt," "delivery," "notice," "filing," and "service." If courts consistently treat computer systems as capable of performing these acts on behalf of their corporate owners, businesses can design fully automated compliance systems without fear that human involvement is legally required at each step. The decision thus validates modern business practices while maintaining fidelity to statutory text.

## The Signature Question: Text Versus Tradition

The court's treatment of the signature requirement is equally instructive. The plaintiffs argued that the statutory term "certifying" necessarily implies a signature—a human act of attestation. The court acknowledged that one Louisiana appellate decision had noted the absence of a signature as a deficiency, but correctly observed that no court had held a signature independently required.

More fundamentally, the court resisted the invitation to add requirements to the statute. "Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures," Judge Southwick wrote. The statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the certifying entity, not any particular employee. The court's refusal to judicially legislate a signature requirement represents sound statutory interpretation.

But the underlying concern remains: what does it mean for a corporation to "certify" something? The traditional understanding involves a human agent making representations on behalf of the entity. GoAuto's process involves an automated email generated by one computer system and received by another. The certification language is pre-programmed text, not a contemporaneous attestation. The court's result is defensible, but it stretches the concept of corporate certification toward something closer to automated verification.

## Implications for Digital-Age Statutory Construction

*Williams* joins a growing body of case law grappling with how traditional legal concepts apply to automated processes. Courts have similarly wrestled with whether automated robocalls constitute "making a call" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, whether algorithmic recommendations create agency relationships, and whether AI-generated content can be "speech" for First Amendment purposes.

The Fifth Circuit's approach—focusing on functional equivalence rather than requiring human involvement—aligns with how many courts have treated electronic communications. The 2015 amendments to the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, for instance, clarified that electronically stored information can be "seized" and documents can be "produced" in digital form. Courts have also held that email can constitute "service" or "delivery" under various statutes.

But *Williams* goes further because the entire process is automated. No human initiated the cancellation request, reviewed it, or approved it before the policy was terminated. The court's blessing of this arrangement suggests that fully automated legal processes are permissible so long as the outcome matches what the statute envisions—even if the means are fundamentally different.

## Questions Left Open

The decision leaves important questions unanswered. What if the computer system had a bug that sent erroneous cancellations? Would GoAuto's lack of human oversight affect its good-faith defenses? What if a consumer claimed they never received the notice of default—would the automated process affect the analysis of whether proper notice was given?

Moreover, the court declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court, finding that the absence of state high court definition was insufficient to warrant certification. One might question whether an issue this consequential for Louisiana's insurance industry deserved clearer resolution from the state's highest court.

## Conclusion

*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* represents a sensible adaptation of statutory interpretation to commercial reality. Businesses have automated their processes, and courts must decide whether statutory language written for human actors can accommodate machine execution. The Fifth Circuit's answer is yes, so long as the functional result satisfies the statute's requirements.

As automation expands into more regulated domains—from contract formation to regulatory filings to legal service—courts will face similar questions. The Fifth Circuit's pragmatic approach provides a model: focus on what the statute requires functionally, resist adding procedural requirements not found in the text, and acknowledge that "company computers do much that individuals once did." Whether other courts follow this approach, or whether legislatures respond by updating statutes to explicitly address automation, remains to be seen. For now, *Williams* suggests that when a statute says "receipt," a computer can receive.
