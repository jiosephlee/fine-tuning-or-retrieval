Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Can a computer system's automated receipt satisfy a statutory "receipt" requirement?
Question:
The statute requires that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested." Here, GoAuto's computer system automatically received the email at midnight and immediately cancelled the policy, with no human involvement until hours later. The court says this satisfies "receipt" because "company computers do much that individuals once did." But the statute was presumably written with human receipt in mind. Is there a limit to this reasoning? Could any automated process satisfy statutory requirements meant for humans? What if the statute had said "upon receiving" instead of "upon receipt" - would that change the analysis?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is that **yes, an automated system's receipt can satisfy a statutory "receipt" requirement**, at least under the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of this Louisiana statute. The court's reasoning suggests that when a statute requires an entity (like an insurer) to "receive" something, receipt by that entity's automated systems counts.

---

### The Court's Reasoning

The court's analysis rests on three main points:

**1. Definition-based interpretation.** The court turned to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "receipt" as "the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Under these definitions, the court concluded that "GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation." The email arriving in GoAuto's computer system constituted possession.

**2. Entity-level receipt.** The statute requires receipt by "the insurer"—not by a specific human employee. The court treated GoAuto's computer system as the entity's agent for receiving communications: "once the e-mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto's computer system, that computer system cancels the policy." The receipt happened when the email was delivered to GoAuto's systems, regardless of whether a human had yet reviewed it.

**3. Modern context.** The court emphasized that "this is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court explicitly rejected the argument that automation defeats the statutory requirement.

---

### Limits to This Reasoning

The opinion suggests several implicit limits on treating automation as satisfying statutory requirements:

- **Older statutes may differ.** The court stressed that this is a "modern statute." A statute enacted when a function could only be performed by humans might be interpreted differently, perhaps under the presumption that legislatures legislate against the background of existing technology.

- **Receipt vs. further action.** The statute here required only "receipt"—mere possession or delivery. If a statute required something more—such as "upon *review* of," "upon *consideration* of," or "upon *determination* that"—courts might reach a different conclusion, since those terms could imply human judgment.

- **Statutory purpose matters.** The court noted that strict adherence to these procedures "serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured." Automated receipt arguably serves the first purpose well—it precisely fixes the moment of receipt.

---

### Would "Upon Receiving" Change the Analysis?

Probably not, based on the court's approach. The court defined both "receipt" and "receive" from Black's Law Dictionary, and the definitions are functionally equivalent: "receipt" is "the act of receiving," and "receive" is "to take... to come into possession of." Both focus on possession and delivery, not on human awareness or action.

The distinction you're drawing—between the noun "receipt" and the participle "receiving"—might matter if a court thought "receiving" implied an ongoing process requiring human involvement. But under this court's definition-centered approach, both terms point to the same concept: coming into possession. The key is what the entity (through its systems) has acquired, not what a human has done with it.

---

### Bottom Line

The Fifth Circuit's approach treats corporate computer systems as sufficient agents for receiving statutory notices. But this holding is anchored in (a) the statutory text's focus on mere "receipt" rather than further action, (b) the modern vintage of the statute, and (c) dictionary definitions emphasizing possession over awareness. Whether other statutes—particularly older ones or those requiring human judgment—would receive the same treatment remains an open question.
