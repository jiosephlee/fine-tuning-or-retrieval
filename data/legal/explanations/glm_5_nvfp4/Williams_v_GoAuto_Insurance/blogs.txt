Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# When Computers 'Receive': Statutory Interpretation in the Age of Automation

The Fifth Circuit's recent decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* addresses a question that courts will increasingly face as business processes become more automated: what does it mean for a company to "receive" something when no human is involved? The court's pragmatic answer—that a computer system's automated receipt of an email satisfies statutory receipt requirements—offers a template for interpreting legacy statutory language in light of modern commercial practices. But the opinion also raises deeper questions about how courts should approach statutory terms that were written for a paper-and-people world.

## The Problem of Automated Compliance

The case arose from a straightforward scenario. Three insureds had financed their auto insurance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company (APAC). When they defaulted on payments, APAC's computer system automatically sent cancellation requests to GoAuto's computer system via email at midnight. GoAuto's system—designed to "listen" for such requests—automatically cancelled the policies. Only later that morning did any human become involved, when an APAC employee applied a signature stamp to printed documents.

The insureds argued this process violated Louisiana's premium finance statute, which requires that "upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested." They contended that "receipt" requires human possession, and that the lack of a signature on the certification before cancellation rendered the process defective.

Judge Southwick, writing for the panel, rejected both arguments. The court held that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the finance company's certification, and that GoAuto's computer system receiving the email satisfied the statutory receipt requirement. On the latter point, the court was direct: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did."

## A Pragmatic Approach to "Receipt"

The court's analysis of the receipt question is notable for its practicality. Drawing on Black's Law Dictionary definitions, the court concluded that "receipt" means "the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession." The key insight was that GoAuto's computer system does, in fact, take possession of the email—it "hears" the request and acts on it, even if no human reviews it until later.

The court's description of the technical process is worth quoting: "Basically, a web service is like a listener. It's always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it's called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity." This anthropomorphic framing—computers that "listen" and "hear"—suggests the court was comfortable analogizing automated processes to human ones.

This approach has significant implications. Countless statutes and regulations use terms like "receipt," "delivery," "notice," "filing," and "service." If courts consistently treat computer systems as capable of performing these acts on behalf of their corporate owners, businesses can design fully automated compliance systems without fear that human involvement is legally required at each step. The decision thus validates modern business practices while maintaining fidelity to statutory text.

## The Signature Question: Text Versus Tradition

The court's treatment of the signature requirement is equally instructive. The plaintiffs argued that the statutory term "certifying" necessarily implies a signature—a human act of attestation. The court acknowledged that one Louisiana appellate decision had noted the absence of a signature as a deficiency, but correctly observed that no court had held a signature independently required.

More fundamentally, the court resisted the invitation to add requirements to the statute. "Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures," Judge Southwick wrote. The statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the certifying entity, not any particular employee. The court's refusal to judicially legislate a signature requirement represents sound statutory interpretation.

But the underlying concern remains: what does it mean for a corporation to "certify" something? The traditional understanding involves a human agent making representations on behalf of the entity. GoAuto's process involves an automated email generated by one computer system and received by another. The certification language is pre-programmed text, not a contemporaneous attestation. The court's result is defensible, but it stretches the concept of corporate certification toward something closer to automated verification.

## Implications for Digital-Age Statutory Construction

*Williams* joins a growing body of case law grappling with how traditional legal concepts apply to automated processes. Courts have similarly wrestled with whether automated robocalls constitute "making a call" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, whether algorithmic recommendations create agency relationships, and whether AI-generated content can be "speech" for First Amendment purposes.

The Fifth Circuit's approach—focusing on functional equivalence rather than requiring human involvement—aligns with how many courts have treated electronic communications. The 2015 amendments to the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, for instance, clarified that electronically stored information can be "seized" and documents can be "produced" in digital form. Courts have also held that email can constitute "service" or "delivery" under various statutes.

But *Williams* goes further because the entire process is automated. No human initiated the cancellation request, reviewed it, or approved it before the policy was terminated. The court's blessing of this arrangement suggests that fully automated legal processes are permissible so long as the outcome matches what the statute envisions—even if the means are fundamentally different.

## Questions Left Open

The decision leaves important questions unanswered. What if the computer system had a bug that sent erroneous cancellations? Would GoAuto's lack of human oversight affect its good-faith defenses? What if a consumer claimed they never received the notice of default—would the automated process affect the analysis of whether proper notice was given?

Moreover, the court declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court, finding that the absence of state high court definition was insufficient to warrant certification. One might question whether an issue this consequential for Louisiana's insurance industry deserved clearer resolution from the state's highest court.

## Conclusion

*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* represents a sensible adaptation of statutory interpretation to commercial reality. Businesses have automated their processes, and courts must decide whether statutory language written for human actors can accommodate machine execution. The Fifth Circuit's answer is yes, so long as the functional result satisfies the statute's requirements.

As automation expands into more regulated domains—from contract formation to regulatory filings to legal service—courts will face similar questions. The Fifth Circuit's pragmatic approach provides a model: focus on what the statute requires functionally, resist adding procedural requirements not found in the text, and acknowledge that "company computers do much that individuals once did." Whether other courts follow this approach, or whether legislatures respond by updating statutes to explicitly address automation, remains to be seen. For now, *Williams* suggests that when a statute says "receipt," a computer can receive.



# Strict Adherence or Strict Construction? The Paradox of Louisiana's Insurance Cancellation Doctrine

When Louisiana courts say that insurance cancellation procedures require "strict adherence" to statutory requirements, they mean it literally—or so the doctrine has long suggested. Any defect in the cancellation process, Louisiana courts have held, renders the cancellation ineffective, leaving the insurer on the hook for coverage it thought it had terminated. But a recent Fifth Circuit decision exposes a fundamental tension in this "strict adherence" framework: does strict adherence mean courts should require everything the legislature might have contemplated, or only what the legislature actually wrote?

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit confronted a fact pattern that would have been unimaginable when Louisiana's premium-financing statute was enacted. An insurance premium finance company, APAC, used an automated computer system to request policy cancellations when insureds defaulted on their payment plans. APAC's computer sent GoAuto's computer an email at midnight; GoAuto's system automatically cancelled the policy; only later that morning did a human APAC employee affix a signature stamp to printed documents. The insureds, left without coverage after an accident, cried foul. They argued that "strict adherence" meant what it said: the finance company must *certify* the statutory prerequisites, and certification requires a signature. The statute's silence on signatures, they contended, didn't matter—the whole point of strict adherence is to protect consumers by ensuring every procedural step is properly executed.

The Fifth Circuit disagreed, and its reasoning reveals something important about how strict adherence doctrines operate in practice.

## The Signature Question

The court's analysis began with the statutory text. Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated facts: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that it is in uncured default, that notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to other interested parties. The statute says nothing about signatures. The court noted that no Louisiana appellate decision has squarely held that a signature is required, though one decision—*Benitez v. Elsayed*—mentioned the absence of a signature among several deficiencies in a cancellation notice.

The Fifth Circuit's approach here is notably textualist. The statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that must certify, the court observed, and nothing in the statutory language suggests that certification must come from a named individual. "Strict adherence," the court reasoned, "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." This is the heart of the doctrinal paradox: a doctrine designed to be protective of insureds becomes, in the court's hands, a constraint on judicial augmentation of statutory requirements. The court refused to treat "strict adherence" as synonymous with "hypertechnical compliance with everything a court thinks the statute should have required."

## Receipt in the Digital Age

The plaintiffs' second argument—that GoAuto never properly "received" the cancellation request—required the court to grapple with modern technology's interaction with statutory language drafted in an earlier era. The statute provides that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested." The plaintiffs argued that "receipt" requires human possession, not merely electronic transmission to a computer system.

The court looked to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines receipt as taking physical possession or coming into possession from an outside source. This definition, the court concluded, is satisfied when GoAuto's computer system receives the email. "This is a modern statute set in current times," the court wrote, "and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court declined to read the statute as requiring human involvement at the moment of receipt when the statutory language itself imposes no such requirement.

## What Strict Adherence Really Means

The doctrinal significance of *Williams* lies in its clarification of what "strict adherence" actually demands. Louisiana courts have long held that strict adherence serves two functions: providing a clear method for fixing the time and date of cancellation, and ensuring a minimum cure period for defaulted insureds. These are protective purposes, aimed at preventing insurers from playing fast and loose with cancellation procedures that leave consumers unexpectedly uninsured.

But the Fifth Circuit's decision suggests that strict adherence, properly understood, is a limit on parties' ability to deviate from statutory requirements—not a license for courts to supplement those requirements with additional procedural protections the legislature omitted. There's a difference between requiring insurers and finance companies to do exactly what the statute says and requiring them to do more than the statute says. The plaintiffs in *Williams* wanted the court to enforce not just the statute's explicit requirements, but also the statutory scheme's apparent consumer-protective purpose. The court declined that invitation.

This distinction matters beyond Louisiana insurance law. Strict adherence doctrines appear in various consumer protection contexts, and courts often face the same choice: enforce the statute's explicit mandates, or fill in gaps that seem consistent with legislative purpose. *Williams* suggests that at least in this context, strict adherence cuts against judicial supplementation. The doctrine's very strictness constrains courts as much as it constrains insurers.

## Certification Denied

Perhaps tellingly, the Fifth Circuit declined the plaintiffs' request to certify the signature and receipt questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Certification, the court noted, is reserved for compelling circumstances, and the mere absence of state supreme court precedent isn't enough. The decision suggests confidence in the court's Erie guess—and perhaps confidence that Louisiana's highest court would reach the same conclusion.

The result is a victory for efficiency in premium-financing arrangements. Insurance companies and their financing partners can continue using automated cancellation systems without worrying that a missing signature will invalidate thousands of cancellations. For consumers, the message is less welcome: if you want more procedural protections than the statute explicitly provides, take it up with the legislature. Courts enforcing strict adherence doctrines won't add requirements the statute left out.



# Certification Reluctance and the Fifth Circuit's Erie Guesses

When federal courts sit in diversity jurisdiction, they routinely make predictive interpretations of state law—a practice that sits at the heart of the Erie doctrine's mandate that federal courts apply state law as it exists. But when state law is genuinely unsettled, federal courts face a choice: make their best guess about how the state supreme court would rule, or certify the question to that court directly. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit confronted this choice and, characteristically, chose to guess.

The case presents a straightforward factual dispute with potentially far-reaching implications for Louisiana's insurance industry. When insureds finance their premiums through a third-party company, Louisiana law permits that finance company to request cancellation of the policy upon default. But the statute requires strict adherence to procedural requirements designed to protect consumers—requirements that Louisiana courts have said must be followed "strictly" because they "fix[] the exact time and date of cancellation" and "grant[] a minimum time during which the default may be cured."

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's cancellation procedures fell short in two ways. First, they contended that the statutory requirement of a "statement certifying" certain information demanded a signature—a human attestation that the certification was authentic. GoAuto's automated system sent cancellation notices via email, with signature stamps affixed only after cancellation had already occurred. Second, they argued that the statute's "receipt" requirement meant GoAuto had to take physical possession of the cancellation request before acting on it. Instead, GoAuto's computer system automatically cancelled policies upon receiving the electronic notice.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both arguments. On the signature question, the court correctly observed that the statute says nothing explicit about signatures. The statute requires the premium finance company to certify certain information, and the court concluded that a corporate entity can "certify" through its automated systems without a human signature. On receipt, the court accepted Black's Law Dictionary definitions requiring "possession, delivery, or custody," but found that electronic receipt by GoAuto's computer system satisfied that requirement. The opinion takes pains to describe the technical process—"a web service is like a listener"—and concludes that modern commerce demands recognition of electronic receipt.

Maybe the Fifth Circuit got these questions right. The problem is that no Louisiana Supreme Court decision addresses either issue directly. The court candidly acknowledged that "no Louisiana judicial opinion considering insurance-premium finance companies' procedures under the statute has addressed the need for a signature." One intermediate appellate decision mentioned the absence of a signature in a notice that failed to certify anything at all, but that hardly establishes a rule. On the meaning of "receipt," the Louisiana Supreme Court has "not defined 'receipt' as used in the statute, and the statute itself has no definition."

This is precisely the scenario where certification makes sense. Louisiana's insurance industry operates under these statutes daily. The questions presented—whether automated certifications satisfy statutory requirements, and what "receipt" means in the age of electronic communication—are consequential for insurers, premium finance companies, and insureds throughout the state. The Louisiana Supreme Court is the proper body to resolve these ambiguities, not a federal appellate court making an Erie guess.

Yet when the plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify these questions, the court demurred. Quoting *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Ass'n*, the panel explained that certification is "an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often." The mere fact that "a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court" is "not sufficient to warrant certification."

This reluctance is not unique to the Fifth Circuit, but it reflects a problematic aspect of federal certification practice. The *Jefferson* formulation treats certification as a scarce resource to be husbanded rather than a tool for federalism—indeed, the court suggests that overuse might somehow cause the Louisiana Supreme Court to stop accepting certified questions. There is little evidence for this proposition. State supreme courts generally welcome certification in cases involving important questions of state law, and the certification process exists precisely to avoid the scenario the Fifth Circuit created here: a federal court resolving undecided questions of state law that affect the state's citizens and institutions.

The *Williams* decision also illustrates the risks of federal courts making predictive interpretations. The Fifth Circuit's analysis of "certifying" and "receipt" is reasonable, but it is ultimately a guess about how Louisiana courts would construe their own statute. If the Louisiana Supreme Court ultimately disagrees—if it holds that "strict adherence" requires human signatures, or that "receipt" means something more than a computer receiving an email—the parties here and others similarly situated will have been governed by the wrong rule. Certification would have avoided that risk.

To be sure, certification has costs. It delays resolution and can be used by litigants as a tactical tool to prolong litigation. But when genuinely novel questions of state law arise that affect significant state interests, the federalism values underlying Erie counsel restraint. Federal courts should resist the temptation to resolve unsettled questions of state law when the state's highest court is available and willing to do so.

The Fifth Circuit's approach to certification reflects a broader pattern of federal court confidence—or overconfidence—in predictive interpretation. That confidence may be warranted when state law is well-developed and the federal court can rely on clear precedent. But when, as here, the court admits that "no Louisiana judicial opinion" addresses a question and that existing precedent is "similarly unhelpful," certification is not just appropriate but prudent.

*Williams* may ultimately reach the correct result, and the opinion's practical approach to electronic communications has considerable appeal. But the decision to resolve these questions without Louisiana Supreme Court guidance represents a missed opportunity for cooperative federalism. The "well" of certification exists to be used when appropriate—and this case presented exactly such an occasion.
