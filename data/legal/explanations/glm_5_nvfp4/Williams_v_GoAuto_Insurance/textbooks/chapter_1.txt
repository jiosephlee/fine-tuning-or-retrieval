Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Context

## The Parties: Williams, Wright, Jenkins, GoAuto, and APAC

This case involves a dispute between former insurance policyholders and their insurance company over the validity of policy cancellations. The plaintiffs are Kimberly Williams, Felita Wright, and Nicholas Jenkins. Williams and Wright were insureds of GoAuto Insurance Company. Jenkins was not himself a GoAuto insured but was involved in an accident with a third GoAuto insured (who is not a party to this lawsuit). The defendant on appeal is GoAuto Insurance Company, the insurer that issued the policies at issue. A critical additional entity in this case is American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"), a premium finance company that financed the plaintiffs' insurance policies. APAC was also a defendant in the lawsuit, but the appeal focuses on the claims against GoAuto.

## Premium Financing Agreements and APAC's Power of Attorney

Understanding this case requires understanding how premium financing works. When policyholders cannot afford to pay their entire insurance premium upfront, they may enter into a financing arrangement with a premium finance company. Here, Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins's accident each financed their GoAuto policies through APAC. Under this arrangement, APAC paid GoAuto the full premium in advance, and the insureds agreed to repay APAC in monthly installments.

A key feature of these financing agreements is the grant of a power of attorney to the finance company. Each financing agreement granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds failed to make their payments. This mechanism is governed by Louisiana Revised Statutes section 9:3550, which establishes specific procedures that premium finance companies must follow when exercising this power of attorney. The statute requires strict adherence to its procedures; any defect in the cancellation process results in an ineffective cancellation, leaving the policy in force.

## The Underlying Accident and Policy Cancellations for Nonpayment

The plaintiffs' policies were cancelled due to nonpayment. When the insureds failed to make timely payments, APAC exercised its power of attorney and requested that GoAuto cancel the policies. The specific cancellation procedures are central to this dispute: APAC would send a notice to the insured warning of cancellation if payment was not made within ten days. If no payment came by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sent GoAuto an email with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. GoAuto's computer system would then cancel the policy. Later that morning, APAC employees would complete paperwork related to the cancellation, including affixing a signature stamp to printed documents.

The case arose because the plaintiffs contested whether these cancellations were effective under Louisiana law. Nicholas Jenkins had been involved in an accident with a third GoAuto insured, and the validity of the insurance coverage potentially depended on whether that driver's policy had been properly cancelled.

## Procedural History: State Court Filing, Removal, and Cross-Motions for Summary Judgment

Williams and Jenkins initially filed this case as a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court, alleging that GoAuto had not effectively cancelled their policies and had failed to act in good faith. Wright became an additional plaintiff when an amended complaint was filed. The complaint alleged misrepresentations and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing.

GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Removal is the process by which a defendant transfers a case from state court to federal court when federal jurisdiction exists—here, likely based on diversity of citizenship under 28 U.S.C. § 1332, since the parties were citizens of different states and the amount in controversy exceeded $75,000.

The parties filed cross-motions for summary judgment focused specifically on whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana law. Summary judgment is a procedural mechanism that allows a court to resolve a case without a trial when there is no genuine dispute as to material facts and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Here, the parties agreed on the facts of how cancellations were processed; their disagreement was purely legal—whether those procedures met the statutory requirements.

## The District Court's Ruling and Dismissal with Prejudice

The district court granted GoAuto's motion for summary judgment, holding that GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law. The court also denied the plaintiffs' motion for partial summary judgment. The district court reasoned that APAC's delivery of cancellation notices through an APAC employee who physically delivered documents to a shared filing cabinet satisfied the statutory requirement of sending notice via a "private carrier." The court found this definition of "private carrier" using an analogy to a Fifth Circuit railroad case.

Having determined that the cancellations were statutorily valid, the district court concluded that the plaintiffs' related claims—alleging misrepresentations and breach of good faith—necessarily failed. The court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. A dismissal "with prejudice" means the dismissal is final and bars the plaintiffs from bringing the same claims again in any court. The plaintiffs appealed this dismissal to the Fifth Circuit, raising three contentions: that the statutory procedures for delivery of the notice of cancellation were not followed, that GoAuto never properly received the cancellation requests, and that their own motion for partial summary judgment should have been granted.
