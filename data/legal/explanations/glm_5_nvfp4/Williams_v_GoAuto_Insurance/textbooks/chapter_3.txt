Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 3: # The Certification Issue: Whether a Signature is Required

## Plaintiffs' Argument for a Signature Requirement Based on the Term 'Certifying'

A central dispute in this appeal was whether Louisiana's premium finance statute requires a signature on the cancellation notice. The plaintiffs' argument hinged on statutory interpretation: they contended that the statutory term "certifying" necessarily implies a signature requirement. Their logic proceeded from the premise that certification, in common legal understanding, typically involves some form of authentication—often a signature attesting to the truth of the matters certified. Because APAC's cancellation requests were not signed until after the policy had already been canceled automatically by GoAuto's computer system, the plaintiffs argued the cancellation was statutorily defective.

This argument reflects a broader litigation strategy of attacking procedural deficiencies. The plaintiffs recognized that Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures. If they could establish that a signature was one such required procedure, the absence of a signature at the moment of cancellation would render the cancellation ineffective. This would mean the policies remained in force at the time of the accidents, potentially establishing insurer liability.

## Absence of Explicit Signature Requirement in the Statutory Text

The court's analysis began with the most fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: the text itself. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) sets forth what a premium finance company must include when requesting cancellation. The statute requires that the finance company send to the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated items: the valid power of attorney, the uncured default, that notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to other interested parties.

Notably, the statute does not explicitly require a signature. The court observed that "the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature." If a signature were required, the court explained, the word "certifying" would have to create that requirement by implication. This placed a significant burden on the plaintiffs: they needed to demonstrate that certification, as used in this statutory context, necessarily entails a signature. The court's approach here illustrates a standard interpretive principle—courts will not read requirements into a statute that the legislature did not include, even when interpreting a statute that demands strict adherence.

## Analysis of KMJ Services, Hodges, and Other Louisiana Precedent

The court surveyed existing Louisiana case law to determine whether any precedent supported the plaintiffs' position. This examination revealed no clear authority requiring a signature. In *KMJ Services, Inc. v. Hood*, a Louisiana appellate court had paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. This phrasing focused on the content of the statement rather than its form of authentication. Similarly, in *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd's Insurance*, the court described the statutory process as allowing cancellation "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." Again, the emphasis was on receipt of the statement, not its signature.

Other cases, such as *Lewis v. Julien*, were similarly unhelpful to the plaintiffs on this specific question. The absence of any Louisiana judicial opinion explicitly addressing the signature requirement left the court without binding precedent directly on point. This gap in the case law meant the court would have to resolve the issue through its own analysis of the statutory text and available precedent.

## Distinguishing Benitez v. Elsayed: Signature Discussed but Not Held Dispositive

The plaintiffs found their strongest support in *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a Louisiana Court of Appeal opinion that mentioned the lack of a signature among the deficiencies in a cancellation notice. In *Benitez*, the court noted that the "'notice of cancellation' does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein."

The Fifth Circuit carefully parsed this language and concluded that *Benitez* did not hold that the absence of a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation. The court emphasized that *Benitez* identified multiple problems with the notice in that case—it lacked employee identification, lacked a signature, and critically, did not "certify" the required information. The *Benitez* court did not isolate the signature requirement as independently dispositive.

More importantly, the present case differed from *Benitez* in a crucial respect: unlike the notice in *Benitez*, APAC's cancellation requests did "certify" the relevant information. The request included the four certifications required by statute. The court concluded that "without clearer Louisiana authority, we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified."

## Court's Holding That the Finance Company Itself Certifies, Not a Named Employee

The court's ultimate resolution of this issue turned on its identification of the proper actor under the statute. The statute specifies that "the insurance premium finance company" effects cancellation by "certifying" certain information. The court read this language as indicating that certification is an act of the corporate entity itself, not of an individual employee. If the statute contemplates the company as the certifying party, then authentication by a named individual becomes unnecessary.

This interpretation aligns with modern business practices where corporations routinely act through automated systems and stamped signatures. The court noted that requiring a named employee's signature would be adding a procedural requirement that the legislature did not include. The principle of strict adherence, the court explained, "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

The court thus held that "Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer." This holding allows the automated cancellation procedures used by GoAuto and APAC to satisfy statutory requirements, even though no human being signed the certification until after the cancellation had already taken effect. The court's analysis demonstrates how textual interpretation can defeat arguments based on implied requirements, even in contexts where courts apply strict adherence standards.
