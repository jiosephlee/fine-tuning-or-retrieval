Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 2: # Louisiana's Statutory Framework for Policy Cancellation

## Text and Structure of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)

Louisiana law governs the cancellation of insurance policies when premiums are financed through a third-party company. The statute at issue in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* is Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3), which establishes the procedures a premium finance company must follow to cancel a policy for nonpayment. This statute reflects a balance between protecting insureds from abrupt loss of coverage and allowing finance companies to manage their risk when borrowers default.

The statute's structure is straightforward. Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), a premium finance company may request cancellation "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail... a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four specific elements. The statute then enumerates these elements in subsections (i) through (iv). Separately, § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) addresses what happens next: "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested... and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract."

As the Fifth Circuit noted, the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature requirement. The court's analysis centered on whether the statutory language itself created such a requirement and what "receipt" means in the context of modern electronic communications. The statute's silence on signatures became a key point of interpretation.

## The Four Certification Requirements Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)

The statute requires the premium finance company to send the insurer "a statement certifying" four distinct facts. These certifications serve as the finance company's sworn attestation that all prerequisites for cancellation have been satisfied.

First, the finance company must certify that "the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as provided in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection." This power of attorney is what authorizes the finance company to act on the insurer's behalf in cancelling the policy. Without this authorization, the finance company would have no legal authority to initiate cancellation.

Second, the company must certify that "the premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured." This certification ensures that cancellation occurs only after the insured has actually defaulted and has been given an opportunity to fix the problem.

Third, the company must certify that "upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as provided in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured." This element guarantees that the insured received advance warning of the impending cancellation.

Fourth, the company must certify that "copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, holders of a security interest in the insured property, or third parties." This protects lienholders and other interested parties who might be affected by a lapse in coverage.

In *Williams*, GoAuto's cancellation procedures included a certification statement from APAC that tracked these four requirements almost verbatim. The Fifth Circuit found this significant: unlike the deficient notice in *Benitez v. Elsayed*, which did not "purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein," APAC's notices explicitly "certified" the relevant information.

## The 'Receipt' Requirement Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)

A separate provision addresses the insurer's role. Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) provides that "upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested... and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract."

The plaintiffs in *Williams* argued that "receipt" required GoAuto to take physical possession of the cancellation request before the policy could be cancelled. They pointed out that before 2017, the shared filing cabinet belonged to GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. After 2017, an APAC employee—not a GoAuto employee—stamped the documents as "received."

The Fifth Circuit turned to dictionary definitions to interpret this undefined statutory term. Citing Black's Law Dictionary, the court defined "receipt" as "the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source."

The critical question became whether electronic receipt satisfied the statute. The court concluded that it did. Once APAC's computer system sent an email to GoAuto's computer system, and GoAuto's system processed it, receipt had occurred. The court explained that a web service "is like a listener. It's always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it's called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity." The court characterized this as receipt by GoAuto: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did."

## Strict Adherence Standard from Louisiana Case Law

Louisiana courts apply a "strict adherence" standard to statutory cancellation procedures. The Fifth Circuit quoted three Louisiana appellate decisions that establish this standard.

In *Delatte v. Lemotte*, the court stated that "Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy." Similarly, in *Eaglin v. Champion Insurance Co.*, the court held that "any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy."

This standard might suggest that any deviation from the statute invalidates a cancellation. However, the Fifth Circuit drew an important distinction in *Williams*. The court explained that "strict adherence" to the statutory procedures "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." In other words, while finance companies must follow every requirement the statute specifies, courts should not invent new requirements that the statute does not contain.

This distinction proved decisive for the signature issue. The plaintiffs argued that strict adherence required a signature on the certification. The court examined *Benitez*, the only Louisiana case mentioning the absence of a signature. There, the court noted that the notice "does not identify any particular employee... is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." The Fifth Circuit read *Benitez* as distinguishing notices that fail to certify from those lacking signatures. The absence of a signature "would [not] by itself disqualify the cancellation if other elements were present." Because the statute itself does not mention a signature, and because the notices in *Williams* did contain the required certifications, the court held that no signature was required.

## Policy Purposes: Fixing Time of Cancellation and Providing Cure Period

The strict adherence standard serves practical purposes. In *Britten v. Reavis*, the Louisiana appellate court explained that strict adherence "serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured."

The first function—fixing the time of cancellation—matters because it determines when coverage ends. This affects whether a claim is covered and when the insurer's obligations terminate. Ambiguity about the cancellation date could lead to disputes about coverage for accidents occurring near the alleged cancellation date.

The second function—providing a cure period—protects insureds from losing coverage without warning. The statutory scheme ensures that insureds receive notice and have an opportunity to pay overdue premiums before cancellation takes effect. In *Williams*, for example, APAC's procedure included sending notice that the policy would be cancelled if payment was not submitted within ten days.

These policy purposes help explain why Louisiana courts require strict adherence. If courts allowed informal compliance, the time of cancellation might become uncertain, and insureds might lose the guaranteed cure period. Yet as the Fifth Circuit recognized, these purposes do not justify adding requirements beyond those the legislature specified. The statute's certification and receipt requirements, properly followed, accomplish both purposes without judicial embellishment.
