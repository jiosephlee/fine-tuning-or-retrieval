Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 4: # The Receipt Requirement and Modern Technology

## Plaintiffs' Argument That 'Receipt' Requires Physical Possession Before Cancellation

The plaintiffs in this case mounted a careful challenge to GoAuto's cancellation procedures, focusing on the statutory requirement that the insurer "receive" the cancellation request before acting. Their argument drew a sharp distinction between the timing of electronic cancellation and the subsequent physical handling of documents. They observed that prior to 2017, the shared filing room where printed cancellation requests were stored belonged not to GoAuto itself, but to a related entity, GoAuto Management Services. Since 2017, they contended, the problem persists because the employee who affixes the "received" stamp is actually an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee. 

The plaintiffs' position was straightforward: the Louisiana legislature used the word "receipt" rather than simply requiring that the request be "sent." This choice of language, they argued, necessarily implies that the insurer must take possession of the cancellation request before the policy can be cancelled. Because GoAuto's computer system cancelled policies automatically in the early morning hours—before any human being physically handled or stamped any document—the plaintiffs maintained that the statutory receipt requirement had not been satisfied. The cancellation occurred too early, in their view, before GoAuto had actually "received" anything.

## Statutory Distinction Between 'Sending' and 'Receipt'

The Louisiana statute at issue, Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3), draws a clear distinction between the finance company's obligation to "send" notice and the insurer's obligation upon "receipt." Subsection (G)(3)(a) describes how the premium finance company may request cancellation: "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail," a copy of the notice along with the required certification. The statute thus explicitly contemplates electronic transmission as a valid method of sending. 

Subsection (G)(3)(b)(i) then addresses the insurer's obligations: "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." The statute thus creates a sequence: the finance company sends the request, the insurer receives it, and then the insurer may cancel the policy. The Fifth Circuit had to determine what "receipt" means in this context and whether GoAuto's automated electronic processes satisfy that requirement.

## Dictionary Definitions of 'Receipt' and 'Receive'

The Louisiana Supreme Court had not defined "receipt" as used in this statute, and the statute itself provides no definition. When a state supreme court has not interpreted a statutory term, a federal court sitting in diversity must predict how that court would rule. The Fifth Circuit turned to a standard legal reference: Black's Law Dictionary. 

The court quoted two definitions. "Receipt" is defined as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession." "Receive" is defined as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." The court accepted these definitions and distilled from them a workable standard: GoAuto may cancel a policy once it "takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation." The dictionary definitions focus on the act of taking something in, of coming into possession, rather than on any particular physical form that possession must take. This focus on functional possession rather than physical form would prove important to the court's analysis.

## GoAuto's Computer System and the Web Service 'Listener' Mechanism

Understanding the court's holding requires understanding how GoAuto's cancellation system actually worked. The opinion provides a revealing glimpse into the automated process through deposition testimony. When an insured failed to make a timely payment, APAC's computer system would automatically send GoAuto an email containing the cancellation request and certification. GoAuto's computer system would then cancel the policy automatically upon receiving this email. 

A GoAuto deponent explained the technical operation using an analogy. The GoAuto web service functions as a "listener"—it is "always listening, waiting to be called." When the APAC process "makes a call" to the GoAuto web service, "it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity." No human being was involved in this exchange. The cancellation occurred in the early morning hours, entirely through computer-to-computer communication. Only later in the morning, after the policy had already been cancelled, would an APAC employee apply a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents and deliver them to a GoAuto employee for stamping and filing.

## Court's Holding That Electronic Receipt by Computer System Satisfies the Statute

The Fifth Circuit concluded that GoAuto's electronic receipt procedures satisfied the statutory requirement. The court's reasoning rested on two key observations. First, the dictionary definitions of "receipt" focus on coming into possession or taking something in—functions that an electronic system can perform. When GoAuto's web service "listens" and receives the email transmission from APAC, it has taken possession of the request. 

Second, the court recognized the reality of modern commercial practices. The statute, the court noted, "is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court did not require human involvement where the statute did not explicitly demand it. Electronic receipt by GoAuto's computer system was sufficient to constitute "receipt" under the statute. The court therefore held that "GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

This holding illustrates an important principle of statutory interpretation in a technological age: courts will not read statutes to require outdated physical processes when the statutory language can accommodate modern electronic methods. The statute required receipt; the computer system accomplished receipt; the statutory requirement was satisfied. The court declined to add requirements—such as human handling or physical possession of paper documents—that the legislature had not included.
