Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Case Background and Procedural Context

## The Parties: Williams, Wright, Jenkins, GoAuto, and APAC

This case involves a dispute between former insurance policyholders and their insurance company over the validity of policy cancellations. The plaintiffs are Kimberly Williams, Felita Wright, and Nicholas Jenkins. Williams and Wright were insureds of GoAuto Insurance Company. Jenkins was not himself a GoAuto insured but was involved in an accident with a third GoAuto insured (who is not a party to this lawsuit). The defendant on appeal is GoAuto Insurance Company, the insurer that issued the policies at issue. A critical additional entity in this case is American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"), a premium finance company that financed the plaintiffs' insurance policies. APAC was also a defendant in the lawsuit, but the appeal focuses on the claims against GoAuto.

## Premium Financing Agreements and APAC's Power of Attorney

Understanding this case requires understanding how premium financing works. When policyholders cannot afford to pay their entire insurance premium upfront, they may enter into a financing arrangement with a premium finance company. Here, Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins's accident each financed their GoAuto policies through APAC. Under this arrangement, APAC paid GoAuto the full premium in advance, and the insureds agreed to repay APAC in monthly installments.

A key feature of these financing agreements is the grant of a power of attorney to the finance company. Each financing agreement granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds failed to make their payments. This mechanism is governed by Louisiana Revised Statutes section 9:3550, which establishes specific procedures that premium finance companies must follow when exercising this power of attorney. The statute requires strict adherence to its procedures; any defect in the cancellation process results in an ineffective cancellation, leaving the policy in force.

## The Underlying Accident and Policy Cancellations for Nonpayment

The plaintiffs' policies were cancelled due to nonpayment. When the insureds failed to make timely payments, APAC exercised its power of attorney and requested that GoAuto cancel the policies. The specific cancellation procedures are central to this dispute: APAC would send a notice to the insured warning of cancellation if payment was not made within ten days. If no payment came by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sent GoAuto an email with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. GoAuto's computer system would then cancel the policy. Later that morning, APAC employees would complete paperwork related to the cancellation, including affixing a signature stamp to printed documents.

The case arose because the plaintiffs contested whether these cancellations were effective under Louisiana law. Nicholas Jenkins had been involved in an accident with a third GoAuto insured, and the validity of the insurance coverage potentially depended on whether that driver's policy had been properly cancelled.

## Procedural History: State Court Filing, Removal, and Cross-Motions for Summary Judgment

Williams and Jenkins initially filed this case as a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court, alleging that GoAuto had not effectively cancelled their policies and had failed to act in good faith. Wright became an additional plaintiff when an amended complaint was filed. The complaint alleged misrepresentations and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing.

GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Removal is the process by which a defendant transfers a case from state court to federal court when federal jurisdiction exists—here, likely based on diversity of citizenship under 28 U.S.C. § 1332, since the parties were citizens of different states and the amount in controversy exceeded $75,000.

The parties filed cross-motions for summary judgment focused specifically on whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana law. Summary judgment is a procedural mechanism that allows a court to resolve a case without a trial when there is no genuine dispute as to material facts and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Here, the parties agreed on the facts of how cancellations were processed; their disagreement was purely legal—whether those procedures met the statutory requirements.

## The District Court's Ruling and Dismissal with Prejudice

The district court granted GoAuto's motion for summary judgment, holding that GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law. The court also denied the plaintiffs' motion for partial summary judgment. The district court reasoned that APAC's delivery of cancellation notices through an APAC employee who physically delivered documents to a shared filing cabinet satisfied the statutory requirement of sending notice via a "private carrier." The court found this definition of "private carrier" using an analogy to a Fifth Circuit railroad case.

Having determined that the cancellations were statutorily valid, the district court concluded that the plaintiffs' related claims—alleging misrepresentations and breach of good faith—necessarily failed. The court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. A dismissal "with prejudice" means the dismissal is final and bars the plaintiffs from bringing the same claims again in any court. The plaintiffs appealed this dismissal to the Fifth Circuit, raising three contentions: that the statutory procedures for delivery of the notice of cancellation were not followed, that GoAuto never properly received the cancellation requests, and that their own motion for partial summary judgment should have been granted.

# Louisiana's Statutory Framework for Policy Cancellation

## Text and Structure of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)

Louisiana law governs the cancellation of insurance policies when premiums are financed through a third-party company. The statute at issue in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* is Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3), which establishes the procedures a premium finance company must follow to cancel a policy for nonpayment. This statute reflects a balance between protecting insureds from abrupt loss of coverage and allowing finance companies to manage their risk when borrowers default.

The statute's structure is straightforward. Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), a premium finance company may request cancellation "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail... a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four specific elements. The statute then enumerates these elements in subsections (i) through (iv). Separately, § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) addresses what happens next: "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested... and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract."

As the Fifth Circuit noted, the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature requirement. The court's analysis centered on whether the statutory language itself created such a requirement and what "receipt" means in the context of modern electronic communications. The statute's silence on signatures became a key point of interpretation.

## The Four Certification Requirements Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)

The statute requires the premium finance company to send the insurer "a statement certifying" four distinct facts. These certifications serve as the finance company's sworn attestation that all prerequisites for cancellation have been satisfied.

First, the finance company must certify that "the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as provided in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection." This power of attorney is what authorizes the finance company to act on the insurer's behalf in cancelling the policy. Without this authorization, the finance company would have no legal authority to initiate cancellation.

Second, the company must certify that "the premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured." This certification ensures that cancellation occurs only after the insured has actually defaulted and has been given an opportunity to fix the problem.

Third, the company must certify that "upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as provided in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured." This element guarantees that the insured received advance warning of the impending cancellation.

Fourth, the company must certify that "copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, holders of a security interest in the insured property, or third parties." This protects lienholders and other interested parties who might be affected by a lapse in coverage.

In *Williams*, GoAuto's cancellation procedures included a certification statement from APAC that tracked these four requirements almost verbatim. The Fifth Circuit found this significant: unlike the deficient notice in *Benitez v. Elsayed*, which did not "purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein," APAC's notices explicitly "certified" the relevant information.

## The 'Receipt' Requirement Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)

A separate provision addresses the insurer's role. Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) provides that "upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested... and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract."

The plaintiffs in *Williams* argued that "receipt" required GoAuto to take physical possession of the cancellation request before the policy could be cancelled. They pointed out that before 2017, the shared filing cabinet belonged to GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. After 2017, an APAC employee—not a GoAuto employee—stamped the documents as "received."

The Fifth Circuit turned to dictionary definitions to interpret this undefined statutory term. Citing Black's Law Dictionary, the court defined "receipt" as "the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source."

The critical question became whether electronic receipt satisfied the statute. The court concluded that it did. Once APAC's computer system sent an email to GoAuto's computer system, and GoAuto's system processed it, receipt had occurred. The court explained that a web service "is like a listener. It's always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it's called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity." The court characterized this as receipt by GoAuto: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did."

## Strict Adherence Standard from Louisiana Case Law

Louisiana courts apply a "strict adherence" standard to statutory cancellation procedures. The Fifth Circuit quoted three Louisiana appellate decisions that establish this standard.

In *Delatte v. Lemotte*, the court stated that "Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy." Similarly, in *Eaglin v. Champion Insurance Co.*, the court held that "any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy."

This standard might suggest that any deviation from the statute invalidates a cancellation. However, the Fifth Circuit drew an important distinction in *Williams*. The court explained that "strict adherence" to the statutory procedures "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." In other words, while finance companies must follow every requirement the statute specifies, courts should not invent new requirements that the statute does not contain.

This distinction proved decisive for the signature issue. The plaintiffs argued that strict adherence required a signature on the certification. The court examined *Benitez*, the only Louisiana case mentioning the absence of a signature. There, the court noted that the notice "does not identify any particular employee... is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." The Fifth Circuit read *Benitez* as distinguishing notices that fail to certify from those lacking signatures. The absence of a signature "would [not] by itself disqualify the cancellation if other elements were present." Because the statute itself does not mention a signature, and because the notices in *Williams* did contain the required certifications, the court held that no signature was required.

## Policy Purposes: Fixing Time of Cancellation and Providing Cure Period

The strict adherence standard serves practical purposes. In *Britten v. Reavis*, the Louisiana appellate court explained that strict adherence "serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured."

The first function—fixing the time of cancellation—matters because it determines when coverage ends. This affects whether a claim is covered and when the insurer's obligations terminate. Ambiguity about the cancellation date could lead to disputes about coverage for accidents occurring near the alleged cancellation date.

The second function—providing a cure period—protects insureds from losing coverage without warning. The statutory scheme ensures that insureds receive notice and have an opportunity to pay overdue premiums before cancellation takes effect. In *Williams*, for example, APAC's procedure included sending notice that the policy would be cancelled if payment was not submitted within ten days.

These policy purposes help explain why Louisiana courts require strict adherence. If courts allowed informal compliance, the time of cancellation might become uncertain, and insureds might lose the guaranteed cure period. Yet as the Fifth Circuit recognized, these purposes do not justify adding requirements beyond those the legislature specified. The statute's certification and receipt requirements, properly followed, accomplish both purposes without judicial embellishment.

# The Certification Issue: Whether a Signature is Required

## Plaintiffs' Argument for a Signature Requirement Based on the Term 'Certifying'

A central dispute in this appeal was whether Louisiana's premium finance statute requires a signature on the cancellation notice. The plaintiffs' argument hinged on statutory interpretation: they contended that the statutory term "certifying" necessarily implies a signature requirement. Their logic proceeded from the premise that certification, in common legal understanding, typically involves some form of authentication—often a signature attesting to the truth of the matters certified. Because APAC's cancellation requests were not signed until after the policy had already been canceled automatically by GoAuto's computer system, the plaintiffs argued the cancellation was statutorily defective.

This argument reflects a broader litigation strategy of attacking procedural deficiencies. The plaintiffs recognized that Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures. If they could establish that a signature was one such required procedure, the absence of a signature at the moment of cancellation would render the cancellation ineffective. This would mean the policies remained in force at the time of the accidents, potentially establishing insurer liability.

## Absence of Explicit Signature Requirement in the Statutory Text

The court's analysis began with the most fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: the text itself. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) sets forth what a premium finance company must include when requesting cancellation. The statute requires that the finance company send to the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated items: the valid power of attorney, the uncured default, that notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to other interested parties.

Notably, the statute does not explicitly require a signature. The court observed that "the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature." If a signature were required, the court explained, the word "certifying" would have to create that requirement by implication. This placed a significant burden on the plaintiffs: they needed to demonstrate that certification, as used in this statutory context, necessarily entails a signature. The court's approach here illustrates a standard interpretive principle—courts will not read requirements into a statute that the legislature did not include, even when interpreting a statute that demands strict adherence.

## Analysis of KMJ Services, Hodges, and Other Louisiana Precedent

The court surveyed existing Louisiana case law to determine whether any precedent supported the plaintiffs' position. This examination revealed no clear authority requiring a signature. In *KMJ Services, Inc. v. Hood*, a Louisiana appellate court had paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. This phrasing focused on the content of the statement rather than its form of authentication. Similarly, in *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd's Insurance*, the court described the statutory process as allowing cancellation "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." Again, the emphasis was on receipt of the statement, not its signature.

Other cases, such as *Lewis v. Julien*, were similarly unhelpful to the plaintiffs on this specific question. The absence of any Louisiana judicial opinion explicitly addressing the signature requirement left the court without binding precedent directly on point. This gap in the case law meant the court would have to resolve the issue through its own analysis of the statutory text and available precedent.

## Distinguishing Benitez v. Elsayed: Signature Discussed but Not Held Dispositive

The plaintiffs found their strongest support in *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a Louisiana Court of Appeal opinion that mentioned the lack of a signature among the deficiencies in a cancellation notice. In *Benitez*, the court noted that the "'notice of cancellation' does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein."

The Fifth Circuit carefully parsed this language and concluded that *Benitez* did not hold that the absence of a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation. The court emphasized that *Benitez* identified multiple problems with the notice in that case—it lacked employee identification, lacked a signature, and critically, did not "certify" the required information. The *Benitez* court did not isolate the signature requirement as independently dispositive.

More importantly, the present case differed from *Benitez* in a crucial respect: unlike the notice in *Benitez*, APAC's cancellation requests did "certify" the relevant information. The request included the four certifications required by statute. The court concluded that "without clearer Louisiana authority, we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified."

## Court's Holding That the Finance Company Itself Certifies, Not a Named Employee

The court's ultimate resolution of this issue turned on its identification of the proper actor under the statute. The statute specifies that "the insurance premium finance company" effects cancellation by "certifying" certain information. The court read this language as indicating that certification is an act of the corporate entity itself, not of an individual employee. If the statute contemplates the company as the certifying party, then authentication by a named individual becomes unnecessary.

This interpretation aligns with modern business practices where corporations routinely act through automated systems and stamped signatures. The court noted that requiring a named employee's signature would be adding a procedural requirement that the legislature did not include. The principle of strict adherence, the court explained, "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

The court thus held that "Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer." This holding allows the automated cancellation procedures used by GoAuto and APAC to satisfy statutory requirements, even though no human being signed the certification until after the cancellation had already taken effect. The court's analysis demonstrates how textual interpretation can defeat arguments based on implied requirements, even in contexts where courts apply strict adherence standards.

# The Receipt Requirement and Modern Technology

## Plaintiffs' Argument That 'Receipt' Requires Physical Possession Before Cancellation

The plaintiffs in this case mounted a careful challenge to GoAuto's cancellation procedures, focusing on the statutory requirement that the insurer "receive" the cancellation request before acting. Their argument drew a sharp distinction between the timing of electronic cancellation and the subsequent physical handling of documents. They observed that prior to 2017, the shared filing room where printed cancellation requests were stored belonged not to GoAuto itself, but to a related entity, GoAuto Management Services. Since 2017, they contended, the problem persists because the employee who affixes the "received" stamp is actually an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee. 

The plaintiffs' position was straightforward: the Louisiana legislature used the word "receipt" rather than simply requiring that the request be "sent." This choice of language, they argued, necessarily implies that the insurer must take possession of the cancellation request before the policy can be cancelled. Because GoAuto's computer system cancelled policies automatically in the early morning hours—before any human being physically handled or stamped any document—the plaintiffs maintained that the statutory receipt requirement had not been satisfied. The cancellation occurred too early, in their view, before GoAuto had actually "received" anything.

## Statutory Distinction Between 'Sending' and 'Receipt'

The Louisiana statute at issue, Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3), draws a clear distinction between the finance company's obligation to "send" notice and the insurer's obligation upon "receipt." Subsection (G)(3)(a) describes how the premium finance company may request cancellation: "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail," a copy of the notice along with the required certification. The statute thus explicitly contemplates electronic transmission as a valid method of sending. 

Subsection (G)(3)(b)(i) then addresses the insurer's obligations: "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." The statute thus creates a sequence: the finance company sends the request, the insurer receives it, and then the insurer may cancel the policy. The Fifth Circuit had to determine what "receipt" means in this context and whether GoAuto's automated electronic processes satisfy that requirement.

## Dictionary Definitions of 'Receipt' and 'Receive'

The Louisiana Supreme Court had not defined "receipt" as used in this statute, and the statute itself provides no definition. When a state supreme court has not interpreted a statutory term, a federal court sitting in diversity must predict how that court would rule. The Fifth Circuit turned to a standard legal reference: Black's Law Dictionary. 

The court quoted two definitions. "Receipt" is defined as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession." "Receive" is defined as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." The court accepted these definitions and distilled from them a workable standard: GoAuto may cancel a policy once it "takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation." The dictionary definitions focus on the act of taking something in, of coming into possession, rather than on any particular physical form that possession must take. This focus on functional possession rather than physical form would prove important to the court's analysis.

## GoAuto's Computer System and the Web Service 'Listener' Mechanism

Understanding the court's holding requires understanding how GoAuto's cancellation system actually worked. The opinion provides a revealing glimpse into the automated process through deposition testimony. When an insured failed to make a timely payment, APAC's computer system would automatically send GoAuto an email containing the cancellation request and certification. GoAuto's computer system would then cancel the policy automatically upon receiving this email. 

A GoAuto deponent explained the technical operation using an analogy. The GoAuto web service functions as a "listener"—it is "always listening, waiting to be called." When the APAC process "makes a call" to the GoAuto web service, "it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity." No human being was involved in this exchange. The cancellation occurred in the early morning hours, entirely through computer-to-computer communication. Only later in the morning, after the policy had already been cancelled, would an APAC employee apply a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents and deliver them to a GoAuto employee for stamping and filing.

## Court's Holding That Electronic Receipt by Computer System Satisfies the Statute

The Fifth Circuit concluded that GoAuto's electronic receipt procedures satisfied the statutory requirement. The court's reasoning rested on two key observations. First, the dictionary definitions of "receipt" focus on coming into possession or taking something in—functions that an electronic system can perform. When GoAuto's web service "listens" and receives the email transmission from APAC, it has taken possession of the request. 

Second, the court recognized the reality of modern commercial practices. The statute, the court noted, "is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court did not require human involvement where the statute did not explicitly demand it. Electronic receipt by GoAuto's computer system was sufficient to constitute "receipt" under the statute. The court therefore held that "GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

This holding illustrates an important principle of statutory interpretation in a technological age: courts will not read statutes to require outdated physical processes when the statutory language can accommodate modern electronic methods. The statute required receipt; the computer system accomplished receipt; the statutory requirement was satisfied. The court declined to add requirements—such as human handling or physical possession of paper documents—that the legislature had not included.

# Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court and Final Disposition

## Plaintiffs' Certification Request and Arguments for Importance to Louisiana Insurance Industry

After losing on both of their principal statutory interpretation arguments, the plaintiffs made a final request: that the Fifth Circuit certify the unresolved questions of Louisiana law to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Certification is a procedural mechanism by which a federal court may refer unsettled questions of state law to the highest court of that state for authoritative resolution. The plaintiffs argued that the proper interpretation of the statutory terms at issue—specifically, whether "certifying" requires a signature and what constitutes "receipt" under Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550—was sufficiently important to warrant certification. They emphasized that these questions were significant to Louisiana's insurance industry and to the broader statutory scheme governing premium finance arrangements. The plaintiffs' theory was that because insurance cancellation procedures affect countless policyholders and insurers across the state, the Louisiana Supreme Court should be the one to resolve any ambiguity.

## Fifth Circuit's Standard for Certification (Jefferson v. Lead Industry Ass'n)

The Fifth Circuit rejected the plaintiffs' certification request by applying its established standard for when certification is appropriate. In *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Ass'n*, 106 F.3d 1245 (5th Cir. 1997), the court articulated a restrained approach to certification. The court described itself as "chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so." This language signals that certification is the exception rather than the rule. The court explained that certification is "such an important resource" that the court will not "risk its continued availability by going to that well too often." This institutional concern reflects the reality that federal courts must exercise restraint when asking state supreme courts to answer questions; overuse of certification could strain comity and make federal courts appear unwilling to do their interpretive work. Significantly, the *Jefferson* court held that the mere fact "that a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court is not sufficient to warrant certification." This principle directly addressed the plaintiffs' situation: they were essentially asking for certification because the Louisiana Supreme Court had not yet ruled on the signature or receipt questions.

## Court's Rationale for Declining Certification

Applying *Jefferson*, the Fifth Circuit concluded that this case presented no compelling reason to certify. While the plaintiffs were correct that the Louisiana Supreme Court had not previously defined the relevant statutory terms, that fact alone was insufficient under *Jefferson*. The court had already undertaken the necessary statutory interpretation itself, examining the statutory text, relevant Louisiana appellate decisions, and standard dictionary definitions. The court's analysis demonstrated that the questions were answerable through ordinary tools of statutory construction without requiring definitive guidance from the state supreme court. The court's reference to certification as a limited resource that must be preserved reflects a pragmatic understanding of federal-state judicial relations. By declining certification, the court implicitly signaled its confidence that its interpretation of Louisiana law was sound and that no extraordinary circumstances justified invoking the certification procedure.

## Summary of the Court's Conclusions on Both Disputed Issues

Before reaching its final conclusion, the court had resolved both statutory questions the plaintiffs raised. First, on the signature requirement, the court held that "Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer." The court reasoned that the statute spoke of "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that certifies, and the word "certifying" did not imply a signature requirement. The court distinguished *Benitez v. Elsayed*, noting that while that case mentioned the lack of a signature, it did not hold that a signature was independently required. Second, on the receipt requirement, the court held that electronic receipt satisfied the statute. Drawing on Black's Law Dictionary definitions, the court concluded that "GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation." The court recognized that modern automated systems can effect receipt without human involvement, and GoAuto's computer system—acting as a "listener" that received and processed APAC's electronic requests—satisfied the statutory command.

## Final Disposition: Affirmance of Summary Judgment for GoAuto

With both statutory issues resolved in GoAuto's favor, the court's conclusion followed directly. The court held that "GoAuto's procedures comply with the statute," meaning the district court had correctly granted GoAuto's motion for summary judgment on the question of statutory compliance. Because the cancellation procedures were valid, the underlying claims for misrepresentation and breach of good faith could not survive. The court therefore affirmed the district court's dismissal of all claims with prejudice. The disposition—AFFIRMED—signified that the Fifth Circuit found no error in the district court's legal conclusions and that the plaintiffs' case against GoAuto was at an end. This final disposition illustrates how statutory interpretation questions can be dispositive: once the court determined that GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law, the plaintiffs' entire case collapsed because their claims were premised on an allegedly invalid cancellation.
