Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Crowded Fields and the Sixth DuPont Factor: Federal Circuit Expands the Universe of Third‑Party Uses in Finance

## Why this case matters
Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. matters because it clarifies that, once the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) has found two parties’ services to be highly similar (or even partially legally identical), the Board cannot cabin its sixth DuPont factor analysis to a narrower subset of services. The Federal Circuit reversed the Board for doing exactly that — excluding Aspire‑formative marks used in banking and financing contexts simply because CC Serve’s registration expressly identified “credit card services.” The decision reinforces a broader, more consistent approach to evaluating third‑party uses when assessing a mark’s commercial strength, and it will push litigants and examiners to collect cross‑category marketplace evidence in cases involving adjacent financial services.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
The Government’s central task under Section 2(d) is the DuPont multi‑factor likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry. The Board had concluded that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK mark and CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE registration were highly similar in services — the Board found significant overlap between “credit card services” and the banking and financing services Apex identified. Yet, when it came to the sixth DuPont factor (“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”), the Board restricted its universe to only those third‑party uses expressly tied to credit card services and ignored Aspire uses in other banking and finance contexts.

The Federal Circuit held that was legal error. The court relied on established precedent — including Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, and others — that the sixth factor requires consideration of similar (not necessarily identical) goods or services. When the Board has already found the parties’ services highly similar under DuPont factor two, it cannot then narrow the lens for factor six so tightly as to exclude evidence reasonably related to that same sphere of commerce. Because the Board’s inconsistent scope could materially affect the commercial‑strength analysis and the marks’ overall commercial impression, the court vacated the sixth‑factor finding and also remanded the first‑factor (marks’ similarity/overall impression) analysis for reconsideration in light of a corrected sixth‑factor inquiry.

## Doctrinal basis and precedents
Apex reiterates two stable propositions in trademark law. First, similarity of goods and services is a matter of degree; identity is not required for many DuPont inquiries. Second, evidence of third‑party registrations and marketplace uses is relevant to show a mark’s relative weakness — a crowded field may narrow the senior user’s scope of protection.

The Federal Circuit invoked Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation to emphasize that the sixth factor contemplates similar goods, not exact matches, and cited Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation for the practical proposition that courts sometimes look for a substantial volume of third‑party uses before finding a mark weak. The panel did not disturb those precedents; rather, it applied them to fault the TTAB’s internally inconsistent treatment of similarity across factors. That consistency principle — the Board should use the same factual scope once it has described the relevant marketplace — is the opinion’s practical doctrinal thrust.

## Practical implications for litigants and the PTO
The ruling has immediate, concrete effects for both trademark practitioners and the PTO:

- Collect the broader marketplace record. When litigating (or opposing) marks in the financial services realm, parties should assemble third‑party use and registration evidence across the entire banking and financing ecosystem — not just the exact subcategory named in the registrations. That means capturing Aspire (and Aspire‑formative) uses attached to issuing, processing, servicing, and underwriting financial products, even if the senior registration is narrowly worded as “credit card services.”

- Anticipate a broader relevance standard on remand and in future TTAB disputes. The Board will need to justify any decision to exclude related uses that the Board has already deemed similar in factor two. Parties should craft record narratives showing why a broader set of third‑party uses is probative of consumer familiarity and marketplace crowdedness.

- Consider prosecution strategy. Trademark applicants and practitioners should be mindful that a registration’s identification of services can be a double‑edged sword: a narrowly worded identification does not necessarily limit the Board’s inquiry into relative strength if adjacent services are found similar elsewhere in the record.

## What counts as a “considerable” volume of third‑party use?
The Federal Circuit remands rather than prescribing a bright‑line threshold. Prior decisions have described “considerable” or “voluminous” evidence in concrete terms — for example, the court in Juice Generation referred to roughly two dozen third‑party marks as significant, while Jack Wolfskin highlighted a smaller set of repeated design usages. Those prior numbers serve as guideposts but not fixed rules. Apex highlights two takeaways:

- Quantity matters, but so does quality. A handful of third‑party uses may be persuasive if they are prominent, geographically widespread, or tied to major national providers. Conversely, a larger number of obscure or narrowly local uses may carry less weight.

- Relevance to the defined marketplace is crucial. The Sixth‑factor inquiry asks whether the field is crowded in a way that educates consumers to distinguish similar marks. Evidence that demonstrates consumer exposure across the relevant services — advertising, co‑branding by banks and processors, national registrations, and usages in closely adjacent financial products — will have outsized importance.

Expect appellants to marshal both breadth and depth: enumerating many third‑party marks and documenting their commercial visibility (web presence, advertising, consumer‑facing products) to show that consumers can and do distinguish among similar names.

## Possible downstream effects and litigation strategy
On remand, a broader sixth‑factor inquiry could lead the Board to conclude CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark is relatively weaker and thus entitled to a narrower scope of protection. That in turn could tilt the first‑factor analysis (commercial impression) and the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion balance toward Apex. But the decision does not guarantee Apex victory — the second factor (services similarity) was affirmed and remains a strong datum for CC Serve. The outcome will turn on how crowded the record proves the Aspire field to be, and whether the Board concludes that consumers have been conditioned to distinguish among Aspire‑formative marks across banking and financing offerings.

For litigators, the message is straightforward: if you represent a senior user, you cannot rely on a narrow identification of services to immunize your mark from challenges about crowdedness. If you represent an applicant, build a robust record of third‑party registrations and marketplace uses across the entire finance space, and be prepared to show consumer exposure and the commercial prominence of those third‑party users.

## Bottom line
Apex Bank v. CC Serve sharpens a commonsense rule: once the Board identifies the relevant marketplace and finds services similar, it must use that same marketplace lens when evaluating third‑party uses. The decision does not change the governing precedents about similarity or commercial strength, but it requires the TTAB to apply them consistently. For practitioners, the case elevates cross‑category evidence gathering in crowded fields like finance — and signals that the breadth of the marketplace record can be decisive in the commercial‑strength calculus.
