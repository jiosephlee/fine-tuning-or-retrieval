# Credit Cards, Banks, and Program Managers: Defining ‘Credit Card Services’ in a Post–ID Manual World

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a timely reminder that the way trademarks are described matters — especially in the shifting landscape of banking, fintech, and program-manager arrangements. The opinion affirms that “credit card services” can overlap substantially with more general “banking and financing” services, while also policing how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) analyzes the competitive field when the Trademark ID Manual no longer supplies a tidy definition. For fintech program managers, issuing banks, and traditional banks launching online sub-brands, this case affects both how to draft identifications of services and how to evaluate risk of opposition from prior marks.

At bottom, the Federal Circuit did three things: (1) affirmed the Board’s factual finding that Apex’s intended “banking and financing services” are highly similar to CC Serve’s longstanding “credit card services” registration (the second DuPont factor); (2) vacated the Board’s treatment of third-party “Aspire” marks (the sixth DuPont factor) because the Board narrowed the relevant universe of third-party uses too much after it had already found high similarity between the parties’ services; and (3) vacated and remanded the Board’s first-factor (marks’ similarity/commercial impression) analysis for reconsideration in light of a corrected sixth-factor inquiry. The practical upshot: when services are found to overlap, the Board must consider third-party uses across that entire scope — and that, in turn, may alter the perceived strength of a registered mark and the overall likelihood-of-confusion calculus.

## How the Board tried to fill the ID Manual gap — and why the Circuit pushed back

A procedural wrinkle drove much of the dispute: “credit card services” had once been an entry in the Trademark ID Manual, but that entry was deleted after CC Serve’s registration issued. The Board therefore reconstructed the meaning of “credit card services” from evidence: it read the term to cover issuing cards, processing transactions, account management, monitoring and alerts, authorization services, and counseling regarding card debt. With that reconstructed definition in hand, the Board concluded the parties’ services were highly similar — in part legally identical — because banking and finance services encompass the issuance of credit and related account services. The Federal Circuit affirmed that conclusion as supported by substantial evidence.

So far, this is a straightforward application of the DuPont test: when goods/services overlap in nature, channels, or consumer perception, the “similarity of the services” factor can cut heavily toward a finding of likelihood of confusion. The wrinkle — and the reversible error identified by the court — came next. Having found high similarity, the Board limited its duPont-six analysis (third-party uses) to only those third-party marks explicitly tied to “credit card services,” excluding third-party “Aspire” marks used across other banking and finance services. The Board reasoned that marks for other services were “essentially irrelevant.” The Federal Circuit rejected that approach: once the Board itself has found the services highly similar (or partially legally identical), it must treat the universe of potentially relevant third-party uses consistently. The sixth factor does not demand identical goods; it demands consideration of marks used on similar goods. Narrowing the scope at that stage undercuts the purpose of the sixth factor — to assess commercial strength or crowding across the relevant marketplace.

Because the number and nature of third‑party “Aspire” uses could materially affect the commercial strength attributed to CC Serve’s mark, the court vacated and remanded the sixth‑factor finding and, prudently, remanded the first‑factor analysis as well (marks’ overall commercial impression), since that inquiry is influenced by the mark’s perceived strength.

## What this means for fintech program managers and bank–fintech partnerships

Modern credit-card programs are often split arrangements: a fintech may run the user-facing product and program management, while a bank issues the card and appears as the regulated party on the account. Apex Bank highlights that, for likelihood-of-confusion analysis, courts and the Board will look beyond corporate labels to the services as described and perceived. If an applicant’s identification of services encompasses issuing credit or managing accounts — even if the applicant itself is not the regulated issuing bank today — such services can be sufficiently similar to classical “credit card services” to trigger conflict with an earlier mark in that space.

For fintech program managers, the decision has two practical implications. First, offering program‑management functions that substantially overlap with aspects of card issuance, authorization, account management, or billing can place you in the same “service universe” as traditional credit-card providers for trademark purposes. That increases the risk that a prior word mark used in connection with credit-card services will block your registration. Second, the outcome reinforces that submitting specimens and identifications that accurately and narrowly describe your role is critical. Where possible, identify your services in a way that differentiates program‑management or software‑as‑a‑service functions (e.g., “program management services for third‑party banks in connection with credit card programs” or “software for managing credit card rewards programs”) from the regulated act of issuing credit or maintaining customer accounts.

Issuing banks and fintech partners should also be mindful in contract drafting and branding. If a fintech’s mark appears on the card and in customer communications, courts may view the services as emanating from a common source (even when the bank is the legal issuer). Joint branding, co‑marks, and prominent placement of the fintech’s trademark on the card or portal can increase the prospect of confusion with an earlier mark.

## What traditional banks and sub‑brands should take away

Traditional banks launching sub‑brands or internet banking offerings (like Apex’s planned ASPIRE BANK mark) should understand that a generic-seeming term (e.g., Aspire) used in a bank sub‑brand may collide with existing credit‑card marks, even those owned by program managers or fintechs that operate through issuing-bank partnerships. The Federal Circuit’s affirmation that “banking and financing services” can be highly similar to “credit card services” means the two categories are not siloed for comparison.

If your sub‑brand will involve account issuance, card issuance, or services that customers reasonably associate with credit, search and clearance should include not just bank marks but also marks used by program managers and fintechs in the payments ecosystem. And if a prior mark is identified, be prepared for the Board or a court to consider third‑party uses across a wide slice of the financial-services marketplace in assessing whether the prior mark is conceptually weak.

## Drafting guidance for modern identifications — practical tips

1. Be precise about the actor and function. If you are a program manager or SaaS provider, make that explicit: “program management services for credit card programs provided by issuing banks” or “software for processing credit card transactions for third‑party banks.” Explicit role descriptions help draw a line between your services and the act of issuing credit.

2. Limit the recitation of services to what you actually offer. Avoid broad omnibus terms like “banking and financing services” unless you truly mean them. Broad terms invite apples‑to‑oranges comparisons (and, as here, apples may be treated like oranges when evidence shows overlap).

3. Include channels and classes of customers when appropriate. If your services are limited (e.g., “provided online” or “to small‑business merchants”), including that restriction can narrow the scope of similarity in practical perception.

4. Use specimens that reflect the services named. The TTAB and courts rely on specimens to understand how the mark is actually used. If your specimens show a clear distinction (e.g., a platform for banks rather than consumer‑facing card accounts), that can support a narrower perception of goods/services.

5. Consider coexistence and consent agreements. If you operate in a crowded field, negotiating coexistence or consent agreements with prior users can be an efficient way to clear registration risk, but beware of terms that concede scope more broadly than intended.

6. Perform clearance searches across the ecosystem. Include program managers, issuing banks, processors, and fintechs in search queries. Prior marks may live under different corporate names and still be relevant.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex Bank confirms two doctrinal points that should guide practitioners. First, the ID Manual’s evolving contents do not insulate parties from ordinary evidentiary work: when the Manual no longer clarifies a term, the TTAB must reconstruct meaning from record evidence — and that reconstruction is entitled to substantial‑evidence review. Second, and more consequentially for practitioners, once the Board has found that services are highly similar, it cannot then cabin the sixth DuPont inquiry to a narrower slice of third‑party uses. Doing so undermines the very purpose of the sixth factor: measuring the commercial field and the relative weakness or strength of a mark across similar services. The Federal Circuit’s remand requires the Board to take a more holistic view when services overlap, which could lead to a finding that a seemingly strong mark is, in fact, conceptually or commercially weaker because of multiple third‑party uses across the relevant marketplace.

For case law and practice, expect more robust examination by the TTAB of third‑party uses across adjacent financial services once a similarity finding is made. That will benefit applicants who can show that a mark-formative term is crowded across banking and payments uses, but it will increase the evidentiary burden on owners of prior marks to establish commercial strength within the full universe of similar services.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve underscores that in the post‑ID Manual world, careful drafting and honest, precise evidence about the nature of services matter. Fintechs, program managers, and banks can’t rely on corporate form or labels to immunize a mark from comparison to legacy credit‑card registrations. Likewise, trademark opponents and registrants should be ready to litigate the scope of the relevant marketplace — and the Board must consider third‑party uses across that full scope when services are found to overlap. For brand owners in the payments ecosystem, the safest course is clarity: specify who does what, show how customers perceive the service, and search broadly across the modern financial‑services landscape.



## Strength First, Similarity Second? Why the Federal Circuit Vacated the First DuPont Factor

Why this case matters: The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a reminder that the DuPont likelihood-of-confusion analysis is an integrated exercise. In a seemingly narrow interlocutory move—vacating the Board’s findings on the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks) and the sixth (third‑party use)—the court underscored that a mark’s commercial strength or weakness can materially alter the “appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression” analysis. The opinion is important not because it upends DuPont, but because it clarifies the sequencing and interdependence of DuPont factors: when the Board’s own findings (here, that the parties’ services are highly similar) bear directly on what third‑party evidence is “similar enough” to dilute a mark’s strength, the Board cannot compartmentalize its review. This decision should push trademark practitioners and the TTAB toward a more holistic treatment of commercial strength before finalizing conclusions about mark similarity.

What the court did: The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK mark and CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE registration applied to highly similar services (the second DuPont factor). But the court vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor—number and nature of third‑party marks—because the Board, after concluding the services were highly similar, proceeded to consider only third‑party uses that were limited to “credit card services,” excluding many third‑party uses of “Aspire” in other banking and financing contexts. The Federal Circuit held that this narrowing was legally erroneous: similarity under factor six need not be limited to identical goods or services, and where the Board already found high similarity between services, it should adopt a matching scope when evaluating third‑party uses bearing on commercial strength. Because commercial strength (or weakness) informs the overall commercial impression central to the first DuPont factor, the court remanded the first factor as well for reconsideration in light of any revised assessment of third‑party use. In short: first reassess the field, then reassess how consumers experience the marks.

The court’s core reasoning and its limits: Two points drive the court’s approach. First, the DuPont factors are fact‑intensive and interrelated; a factual finding under one factor (here, service similarity) constrains the scope of relevant evidence under another (six). Second, evidence that a mark is “crowded”—numerous third‑party uses of similar marks on related services—can lessen a mark’s commercial strength and thus narrow its scope of protection. The Board’s mistake was methodological: it accepted that the services overlapped substantially for purposes of the second factor but then tightened its lens when considering third‑party evidence. The Federal Circuit did not announce a new rule that the first factor must always follow a sixth‑factor analysis; rather, it required that the Board apply consistent definitions of similarity across factors so that the Board’s own findings are not self‑contradictory.

A practical roadmap for integrating commercial‑strength evidence into the first‑factor analysis: The opinion invites a more structured approach on remand—one that practitioners should bear in mind.

- Define the relevant universe of services and consumers at the outset. If the Board finds (as it did here) that services are “highly similar” or partially legally identical, it should adopt that scope when determining what third‑party registrations and uses are “on similar goods or services” under factor six. Narrowly limiting the universe at factor six after finding broad similarity at factor two creates analytical tension.

- Assess third‑party evidence with parity. When third‑party uses of the same (or similar) term appear across the broader field of related financial services, the Board should consider whether such use is widespread enough to suggest that consumers have been conditioned to distinguish among ASPIRE‑formative marks. That assessment should focus on both the number of such uses and their nature—market visibility, geographic scope, and whether the third parties operate in channels likely to be encountered by the same consumers.

- Translate third‑party use into commercial strength findings. If factor six shows a crowded field, the Board should explicitly find that the prior mark is conceptually or commercially weak—or at least entitled to a narrower scope of protection. Those findings should explain how consumer expectations and marketplace realities reduce the likelihood that consumers would be confused by similar marks.

- Revisit “appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression” in light of strength. The first DuPont factor asks how the marks will be perceived; that perception is not developed in a vacuum. If consumers routinely encounter many ASPIRE variants in related financial contexts, the Board should factor that conditioning into the first‑factor analysis—asking, for example, whether ASPIRE has distinctive connotations in this field or functions more as a descriptive or suggestive element. Where a mark’s strength is diminished, the Board should be explicit about how that diminishment reduces the weight of similarities in appearance or sound.

- Explain how strength shifts the likelihood‑of‑confusion calculus overall. The Board’s ultimate likelihood finding should tie the threads together: show how service similarity, the number and nature of third‑party uses, and the resulting commercial impression of the marks converge (or diverge) to make confusion more or less likely.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects: Apex Bank reiterates long‑standing principles—third‑party use can erode a mark’s scope and the DuPont factors are interdependent—but it also signals that the Federal Circuit will police methodological consistency. Boards that treat DuPont factors as siloed checklist items risk reversal if earlier factual findings are inconsistent with narrower evidentiary choices later in the analysis. Practically, litigants should press for a clear sequence at the TTAB: define the relevant marketplace and consumers; consider third‑party uses across that market; make explicit strength findings; and then use those findings when analyzing mark similarity. For trademark owners, this decision is a warning: an older registration’s priority date helps, but commercial ubiquity of similar marks across related services can blunt protection. For applicants, the case underscores the utility of marshaling broad third‑party evidence to show dilution of distinctiveness—and pressing the Board to fold that evidence into its first‑factor analysis.

Concluding perspective: The Federal Circuit’s remand in Apex Bank is not a technicality; it is an invitation to the TTAB to treat commercial strength as more than a passing consideration. The proper sequence is not necessarily “strength then similarity” as matter of fixed order, but it is insistence on coherence: factual findings about the marketplace and service overlap must inform the ambit of third‑party evidence and, through that, the commercial impression consumers will take from the marks. On remand, the Board has a relatively straightforward task—expand its lens to include third‑party uses across related banking and financing services, decide whether ASPIRE is crowded in that broader field, and then reconsider how, if at all, that crowding changes what consumers will perceive when they see ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK. That integrated approach will produce a more defensible likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis and clearer guidance for practitioners.
