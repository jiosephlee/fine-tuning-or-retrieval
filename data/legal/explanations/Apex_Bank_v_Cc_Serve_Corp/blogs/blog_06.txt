# Third-Party Registrations vs. Marketplace Use: Two Edges of the Same DuPont Sword

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful reminder that the DuPont likelihood-of-confusion analysis is not a set of silos. Third-party evidence can be deployed in multiple, sometimes inconsistent-seeming ways: to show that different services are “of a type that may emanate from a single source” (supporting similarity under DuPont factor two), and to show that a term or mark is commercially weak because many others use it (DuPont factor six). The Court affirmed the Board’s conclusion that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks and CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark cover highly similar services. But it vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth and, accordingly, the first DuPont factors because the Board artificially narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it considered when assessing commercial weakness. The panel’s ruling reinforces a core evidentiary principle: consistency in defining the relevant scope of goods and consumers matters across DuPont factors.

Practitioners should take note: the Federal Circuit did not change the law about what counts as “similar” services or what third‑party evidence can do; it simply insisted that the Board apply the same reasonable scope to both queries. That has immediate practical consequences for how parties should assemble and present third‑party registration and marketplace‑use evidence.

## What the court said, in plain terms

Two parts of the Board’s analysis drove the appeal. First, the Board concluded (and the Federal Circuit affirmed) that CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s “banking and financing services” are highly similar and, in part, legally identical. That finding was grounded in definitions, industry practice, and third‑party registrations showing that entities commonly offer both kinds of services under the same mark.

Second, when the Board turned to DuPont factor six — the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” — it significantly narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it would consider. Although Apex had offered dozens of third‑party uses of “Aspire” across the broader financial services space, the Board limited its inquiry to marks expressly tied to “credit card services” and excluded marks used for related banking and financing services. On that truncated record, the Board found only nine relevant third‑party uses and concluded that CC Serve’s mark was not shown to be weak. The Federal Circuit held that limitation was legal error. Because the Board had already (and correctly) found the services highly similar under factor two, it was inconsistent and impermissible to require a stricter notion of similarity for purposes of factor six. The court vacated the Board’s factor six finding and — because commercial strength feeds into the marks’ overall commercial impression — remanded factor one as well.

## Why the court’s approach is correct (and useful)

Two principles animate the opinion.

First, a DuPont analysis rests on interlocking factual findings. If the Board establishes that the relevant consumers and services encompass a broad range of banking and credit‑related offerings, it cannot then adopt a narrower evidentiary lens when assessing how many other parties use similar marks in that same space. The same universe of goods/services and consumers should inform both similarity and weakness analyses, absent a persuasive reason to differentiate.

Second, the opinion underscores how third‑party registrations and marketplace use play different but complementary roles. Registrations (and Trademark ID Manual entries) are useful to show how services are classified and how the examining public or USPTO historically treats particular descriptions — helpful when deciding whether services are related. Marketplace use evidence (real‑world branding, actual product names, consumer‑facing materials) is the classic way to show dilution of commercial strength. The Board’s error was not relying on registrations per se; it was cherry‑picking which third‑party evidence applied to which factor.

This is doctrinally modest but practically important: the Federal Circuit is policing consistency, not elevating one kind of evidence above another. That fit is what makes the decision a good reminder for how to build a record.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

The opinion will likely influence both how the Board evaluates DuPont proofs and how practitioners litigate oppositions and cancellations:

- Consistency across factors. Boards and courts will be more attentive to whether the same factual scope—who the consumers are, what services are encompassed—applies across related DuPont factors. Parties cannot win by arguing a broad scope for similarity and a narrow scope for third‑party weakness (or vice versa) without justification.

- Broader use of marketplace evidence. Because the Federal Circuit required the Board to consider third‑party marks across the same range of services it had found similar under factor two, applicants and opposers should expect more comprehensive consideration of marketplace uses. That increases the value of well‑documented real‑world evidence (screenshots, trade literature, declarations).

- Registration evidence still matters, but only in context. Third‑party registrations and the Trademark ID Manual remain important for establishing how services are routinely classified and related, but registrations alone may be insufficient to prove commercial weakness. Conversely, strong marketplace use across the broader class can tend to narrow a mark’s scope of protection.

- Tactical consequences in prosecution and oppositions. Applicants should resist overly broad descriptions that invite comparisons. Opposers should be prepared to counter registrations with convincing marketplace evidence that a mark enjoys commercial strength despite other registrations. Conversely, applicants or third parties seeking to prove weakness should collect evidence across the whole field the Board treats as relevant.

- Remands and re-litigation. The opinion suggests more remands where the Board’s scope choices are inconsistent. Expect additional development of the record on re‑examination or remand, not immediate finality.

## A practical checklist for developing third‑party evidence (for applicants and opposers)

Below is a practical approach to building the two complementary sides of the DuPont third‑party story. The goal is to avoid the kind of scope inconsistency that doomed the Board’s factor six analysis in this case.

1. Define the scope up front and be consistent
   - From the outset, define the relevant goods/services and the relevant consumer class and channels of trade, and use that definition consistently across DuPont factors. If you need to argue a narrower scope for a persuasive reason, articulate and support that reason with evidence.

2. Use registrations to show classification and common sourcing
   - Collect third‑party registrations that pair the contested services (e.g., “credit card services”) with broader categories (e.g., “banking and financing services”) to support relatedness under factor two.
   - Include serial numbers, registration dates, and specimens where available. If an ID Manual entry was deleted (as happened here), explain its historical meaning and any resulting ambiguity.

3. Collect marketplace use for commercial strength/weakness
   - Gather real‑world evidence of third‑party use of the formative term across the same service universe: screenshots, advertisements, product pages, service agreements, press releases, and app listings.
   - Time‑stamp and archive materials (Wayback Machine, affidavits) to show the duration and geographic extent of use. Demonstrate prominence (e.g., national rollouts, partnerships).

4. Quantify and contextualize
   - Don’t just produce lists. Explain how many third‑party uses are national vs. local; whether usage is incidental or core to the business; overlaps in channels of trade and consumers.
   - If you claim a crowded field, show whether competitors are sizable players or obscure niche offerings—both matter for whether consumers are “educated” to distinguish marks.

5. Present declarations from industry witnesses
   - Use declarations from industry professionals, distributors, or knowledgeable consumers to explain market practices: whether banks routinely offer credit cards under the same brand used for everyday banking, whether consumers expect unified branding, and how consumers encounter these services.

6. Tie evidence to the relevant DuPont factors explicitly
   - Explain how each piece of evidence bears on factor two (relatedness), factor six (third‑party presence and weakness), and factor one (commercial impression), rather than dumping material into the record and leaving the Board to make inferences.

7. Anticipate counterarguments about quality vs. quantity
   - If an opponent relies on a handful of registrations, be prepared to show active marketplace use or, conversely, distinguish permutations as not genuinely competitive (different channels, different consumers, limited duration).

8. Track priority and timing
   - Be mindful of priority dates (registration/first use) and, when relevant, the timing of third‑party uses relative to the parties’ marks. Use of a term after a registrant’s priority date can still bear on weakness, but the Board will examine probative value carefully.

9. Consider supplemental evidence on remand
   - If a court remands on factor six (as here), be ready to supplement the record. A remand is an opportunity to present broader evidence consistent with the Board’s findings on relatedness.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is not a doctrinal revolution. It is, instead, a corrective: the Federal Circuit insisted that the Board not wear two different lenses when examining relatedness and third‑party weakness. For litigators and prosecution counsel, the lesson is practical and immediate—be comprehensive and consistent. Third‑party registrations and marketplace use are different edges of the DuPont sword; know when to use each, but don’t let them point in different directions.
