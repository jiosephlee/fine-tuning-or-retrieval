# Standard of Review in TTAB Appeals: Substantial Evidence vs. Legal Error in Apex v. CC Serve

## Why Apex v. CC Serve matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) is a useful road‑map for practitioners litigating trademark oppositions at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and on appeal. It is a paradigmatic mixed‑disposition: the court affirms the Board’s factual finding (that the parties’ services are highly similar) under the substantial‑evidence standard, but vacates and remands parts of the Board’s DuPont analysis as legal error—most notably the Board’s overly narrow treatment of third‑party “Aspire” marks under the sixth DuPont factor and, as a consequence, its first‑factor analysis of overall commercial impression.

The case sharpens the boundary between deferential appellate review of fact findings and plenary review of legal rulings about the correct scope or standard to be applied. That boundary is where many TTAB appeals live. Apex confirms two practical points: (1) when the Board’s conclusions rest on record facts supported by substantial evidence, the Federal Circuit will not disturb them; but (2) when the Board applies an incorrect legal rule—here, by narrowing the universe of third‑party marks considered under factor six inconsistent with its own finding on factor two—the court will vacate and remand for reconsideration.

## What the Federal Circuit did and why

Two points drove the outcome.

First, the court affirmed the Board’s finding under the second DuPont factor that credit‑card services (CC Serve’s identified services) and “banking and financing services” (Apex’s identification) are highly similar and, in part, legally identical. The Board did the standard work: define the identified services, consult dictionary definitions and entries in the Trademark ID Manual, and consider third‑party registrations showing overlap. On appeal the Federal Circuit applied the familiar test—legal questions de novo, fact findings for substantial evidence—and concluded the Board’s factual findings were amply supported. That meant the second DuPont factor could stand as decided.

Second, and crucially, the court identified a legal mistake in the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis. The sixth factor looks to the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods and can show commercial weakness (and thus a narrower scope of protection) if the field is crowded. The Board had, however, confined its examination to third‑party marks identified only for “credit card services,” excluding many third‑party uses of “Aspire” across the broader banking/finance landscape. That narrower universe contradicted the Board’s own earlier finding that the parties’ services are highly similar (indeed, partially legally identical). The Federal Circuit held that, because DuPont factor six examines uses on similar—not identical—goods, the Board must use a similarly broad scope when counting and characterizing third‑party uses. Limiting consideration to only exact matches on “credit card services” was a legal error requiring vacatur and remand. Because commercial strength and third‑party crowding can materially affect the marks’ overall commercial impression, the court also remanded the first DuPont factor for reconsideration.

In short: affirm the fact finding on similarity (substantial evidence), vacate the sixth‑factor analysis as legal error, and remand both the sixth and the first factors for reconsideration consistent with the Board’s finding of high similarity.

## Analytical perspective: doctrine and coherence

Apex highlights an important doctrinal coherence principle: the Board’s treatment of a given DuPont factor should not be logically inconsistent with its treatment of another where the same underlying definitional choice governs both. Here the Board settled on a broad conceptual overlap between “credit card services” and “banking and financing services” for factor two, yet then narrowed the lens for factor six. The Federal Circuit’s remedy—insist the scope be consistent—reflects sound appellate housekeeping. It prevents the Board from having it both ways: relying on a broad relatedness rationale to find likely confusion while excluding relevant evidence that could undermine the strength of the cited mark.

The decision also reaffirms familiar but consequential points about TTAB appeals. The Board is entitled to deference on pure fact findings supported by substantial evidence, including assessments of services’ similarity when based on dictionary entries, registrations, and other record evidence. But when the Board employs an incorrect legal test—here, a too‑narrow conception of “similar” in the sixth factor—that is a legal error subject to correction. That division is predictable but practically important: many appeals turn on whether an argument can be reframed as a challenge to the sufficiency of evidence (hard to win on appeal) or as a dispute over the correct legal standard (a stronger appellate posture).

Finally, the opinion underscores the substantive interplay of DuPont factors: evidence that a field is crowded with similar marks can narrow the senior mark’s protection, which in turn can change the weight given to visual/sound/connotation similarities. The Board must perform that balancing transparently and consistently.

## Practical implications and downstream effects

For TTAB practitioners and appellants, Apex suggests several tactical takeaways.

First, keep the record broad when arguing relatedness. If you assert that services are “related” across a product line, preserve and develop evidence of third‑party use across that same breadth. Courts will expect consistency: a Board cannot find services closely related for one factor and then ignore relevant third‑party uses for another.

Second, when defending a registration, build a robust record on commercial strength or weakness. Third‑party registrations and evidence of marketplace use are crucial. The Federal Circuit relied on the small number of “Aspire” uses limited to credit‑card services to find that the Board had insufficient evidence to deem the mark weak—but it vacated because the Board artificially constricted the universe of comparable uses. Parties should therefore introduce third‑party evidence across the entire spectrum of services that the parties themselves claim are related. Include registrations, real‑world examples (web pages, trade publications), and, where possible, sales/use data to show whether a term is crowded or distinctive in the broader category.

Third, preserve legal arguments about scope and standard. Apex succeeded on appeal because it could point to a legal inconsistency—the Board’s adoption of incompatible scopes across DuPont factors. Appellate courts are receptive to arguments that ask them to enforce consistent legal standards rather than second‑guess factual inferences. If you want the Federal Circuit to revisit a Board ruling, frame at least one of your main contentions as a pure legal question (e.g., whether the Board applied an incorrect formulation of “similarity” for factor six, or whether the Board’s definition of the relevant public was legally erroneous).

Fourth, think strategically about framing evidence problems as legal errors. Not every misstep is a persuasive legal issue on appeal; arguing that the Board “erred in weighing” evidence without pointing to a legal rule is a weaker position. Better to show that the Board applied a wrong legal test or an internally inconsistent standard (as the Federal Circuit found here). The line between “insufficient evidence” and “incorrect application of law” is sometimes narrow—specifically articulate where the Board’s approach clashes with precedent (e.g., DuPont, Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, Olde Tyme Foods).

Finally, remember that remand is a real remedy. The Federal Circuit did not substitute its own balancing for the Board’s; it sent the case back to ensure the Board applies the correct scope. That outcome often gives the prevailing party at the TTAB a second chance if the record can be augmented or the Board’s reassessment favors them on reconsidered factors.

## How to preserve issues and build the record in TTAB proceedings

A few concrete practice points follow from Apex:

- When alleging relatedness of services, explicitly identify the boundaries of the “relevant public” and the relatedness rationale you expect the Board to apply. If you argue relatedness broadly (banking/finance), tie any third‑party‑use evidence to that same broad category.

- Proactively collect third‑party evidence across the full range of allied services: registrations, screenshots, pricing pages, product/service descriptions, and any industry commentary that shows the terms of art (for example, whether “Aspire” is used for both issuing credit cards and other banking products). Authentication and clear topic tagging in the record make this evidence easier to weigh on appeal.

- Offer, and if necessary litigate for, discovery aimed at showing commercial strength or weakness: marketplaces, number of users, co‑existence agreements, licensing, and consumer surveys (where feasible). Surveys can be expensive, but when the question is crowdedness and consumer recognition, they can move the needle.

- Preserve legal objections to scope at the TTAB hearing and in post‑hearing briefing. Ask the Board to state its definitional choices explicitly so those choices are reviewable on appeal.

- If you expect to appeal, preserve transcripts and carefully identify contested factual findings and legal questions. The Federal Circuit will affirm factual findings supported by substantial evidence; reversible error is likelier where the Board’s rule or scope is inconsistent with precedent or internally contradictory.

## Conclusion

Apex v. CC Serve is a tidy lesson in appellate posture for trademark litigants: substantial‑evidence review is a high bar for upsetting factual findings, but appellate correction is readily available when the Board applies an incorrect legal standard or an inconsistent scope across related DuPont factors. Practitioners should aim both to build a deep evidentiary record and to frame a clean legal question for appeal. That dual approach—robust facts, preserved legal issues—is the best way to ensure a TTAB case survives the gauntlet of mixed standard review.
