Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Who Is the “Relevant Public” for Financial Services Marks?

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. matters because it revisits a recurring tension in trademark law: how to define the “relevant public” when assessing the weight of third‑party uses under the sixth DuPont factor. That tension is particularly acute in the financial services space, where services (banking, lending, payment processing, card issuing, account servicing) often overlap functionally even when they are offered through different business models and partners. The Board’s original decision narrowed the universe of third‑party marks to consumers of “credit card services” alone; the Federal Circuit rejected that narrowing and remanded, holding that the Board must treat the scope of third‑party use consistently with its earlier factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. The ruling sharpens an important lesson: once a tribunal has found that services are similar (or partially legally identical) under DuPont factor two, it cannot cabin the sixth‑factor inquiry to a narrower consumer subset without explaining why a different scope is appropriate.

Why practitioners should care: factor six evidence is often decisive in gauging the commercial strength of a mark and therefore the breadth of protection it enjoys. Courts’ willingness (or not) to construe the “relevant public” narrowly can change the outcome of a likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry, particularly in crowded or descriptive fields. Apex clarifies that the Board must remain faithful to its own service‑similarity findings when defining which third‑party uses matter.

## What the Federal Circuit did

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion that Apex’s “ASPIRE BANK” marks and CC Serve’s prior ASPIRE registration concern highly similar services (DuPont factor two). But it found legal error in the Board’s treatment of the sixth factor. The Board, having concluded the services overlapped considerably, nevertheless limited the sixth‑factor inquiry to third‑party Aspire marks used specifically for “credit card services,” excluding Aspire uses in other banking and financing contexts. The court held that was inconsistent: the sixth factor asks about the number and nature of similar marks “on similar goods,” and similar—not identical—goods are sufficient for the Board to consider. Because the Board had already accepted that the parties’ services were highly similar (indeed partly legally identical), restricting the sixth‑factor universe to a narrower subgroup of consumers was unsound. The Federal Circuit therefore vacated the sixth‑factor finding and also vacated the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks) for reconsideration, since a revised assessment of third‑party use could alter the marks’ commercial impression and relative strength.

## The doctrinal point: consistency across DuPont factors

At bottom, Apex reinforces a straightforward doctrinal principle: the scope of the “relevant public” for purposes of weighing third‑party uses must be tethered to the Board’s (or court’s) findings about the relatedness or similarity of the parties’ goods or services. DuPont factor two is about product/service similarity; factor six is about the marketplace density of similar marks that might weaken a proprietary mark’s reach. If you find two services sufficiently similar to warrant comparison, you cannot then adopt a stricter, narrower definition of the relevant consumer group for factor six without explaining why the narrower scope is analytically justified.

Two commonly recurring errors this opinion pushes back on:

1) Cherry‑picking the consumer subset most likely to help the result. The Board in Apex effectively narrowed the consumer definition only for factor six—despite finding broader service overlap under factor two. The Federal Circuit barred that move.

2) Treating the “buyer of the product” as the only relevant referent for evaluating third‑party weakness when the product categories themselves were found broader. As the court said, similarity for factor six does not require identity; so third‑party marks in closely related banking/finance channels are relevant even if they are not labeled “credit card services.”

## How this fits with prior case law

Apex sits comfortably with prior Federal Circuit instructions that the DuPont factors must be applied coherently. The court has long held that the sixth factor looks to similar—not identical—goods or services (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation), and that findings under one DuPont factor inform the others (see Bose, M2 Software). Apex simply enforces that cross‑factor logic: if your factor‑two finding sweeps broadly, your factor‑six canvas must too.

The decision also aligns with cases that treat third‑party evidence as probative of a mark’s commercial weakness where such evidence demonstrates widespread, similar use in the relevant market (Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, Juice Generation). But it underscores that the question of what counts as the “relevant market” is not free‑floating; it depends on the Board’s factual conclusions about similarity. Apex does not lower the bar for showing weakness—indeed, the Federal Circuit left untouched the Board’s ultimate conclusion that CC Serve had not shown the ASPIRE mark to be so widely used as to be weak—but it requires the Board to consider the correct universe of marks when making that judgment.

## Practical implications for litigants and the Board

For litigants in trademark oppositions and cancellations—especially in financial services and other crowded industries—the Federal Circuit’s ruling has immediate tactical and evidentiary consequences.

For plaintiffs (prior users/registrants seeking to block a later mark):
- Expect challengers to argue that third‑party uses should be limited to a narrow consumer group when that narrows the evidence of crowding. Apex warns that such a narrowing will be resisted if the defendant has already shown service overlap across a broader field.
- If you want the Board to use a narrower “relevant public” in assessing third‑party use, build the record at factor two to support a distinction: demonstrate distinct channels of trade, different end users, and materially different consumer sophistication or decisionmaking contexts between the specific subcategory and the broader field. Don’t rely on post hoc narrowing.

For applicants (those seeking registration of a mark):
- Assemble a broad record of third‑party uses not only in nominally identical categories but in all closely related financial services channels if you aim to show the mark is weak. Apex highlights that evidence of Aspire‑formative marks across banking, lending, and payments—not only card issuing—can be relevant when services overlap.
- When opposing a likelihood‑of‑confusion defense grounded in a prior mark, emphasize differences in channels of trade, consumer profiles, and marketing that justify a more focused relevant public. But be prepared for the Board to require more than labels—show the practical marketplace distinctions.

For the Board and lower tribunals:
- Be explicit about the consumer universe you adopt for factor six and anchor that choice to the findings made under factor two. If you decline to carry over factor‑two scope, explain why a different frame is warranted (for example, specific differences in channels of trade or consumer sophistication for the sixth‑factor inquiry).
- When considering third‑party registrations and uses, state the reasons for including or excluding related categories; a terse conclusion that some uses are “essentially irrelevant” without tying that decision to the factual record risks reversal.

## Downstream effects and unresolved questions

Apex will be cited in future TTAB and district court opinions as a caution against inconsistent DuPont treatment. It will matter most in industries where branding overlaps across adjacent services—financial services, healthcare, telecommunications, software-as-a-service—because these sectors routinely involve partnering and layered service delivery models (banks partnering with processors, fintechs white‑labeling cards, insurers offering loans, etc.).

Two issues remain fertile for dispute. First, how granular must a Board’s justification be when it chooses a narrower relevant public for factor six? The Federal Circuit did not prescribe a formula; it demanded coherence. So, tribunals will need to develop a more textured practice of explaining why consumers of a subcategory are the right referent even when service similarity has been found broadly. Second, the decision may increase the burden on litigants to develop a robust marketplace record early—demonstrating channels of trade, how products are marketed, and who buys them—to support a preferred scope of the “relevant public.”

## Conclusion

Apex makes a simple but consequential point: you cannot have your DuPont cake and eat it too. If the Board finds services to be highly similar (or even partially legally identical), it must consider third‑party uses across the same scope when evaluating the number and nature of similar marks under factor six. That holding should improve doctrinal coherence in crowded fields and push parties to develop records that align their arguments about consumer scope across factors. In practical terms, the opinion rewards disciplined, market‑focused factual development and penalizes snap legal shortcuts that narrow the relevant public in a way that conflicts with findings already made about product or service relatedness.
