Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 7: # Consistency Across Factors: Avoiding Scope Mismatches

Trademark likelihood-of-confusion analyses under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act are multi-factor inquiries. The DuPont framework supplies up to thirteen factors, but courts and tribunals consider only those that are relevant to the case. Although the factors are often analyzed discretely, they do not function in isolation. The critical methodological obligation for adjudicators and advocates is coherence: once the tribunal adopts a scope for the marketplace or a definition of similarity for goods or services, that scope should inform all interrelated DuPont factors unless the record supplies a clearly articulated reason for treating a later factor differently. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful and recent illustration of this principle. There, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar (indeed partially legally identical) under the second DuPont factor but then narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it considered under the sixth DuPont factor to a subset that fit a more limited view of similarity. The Federal Circuit held that narrowing was error and vacated the Board’s sixth- and first-factor findings for reconsideration. This chapter explains why that error matters doctrinally, how it arises in practice, and how practitioners and tribunals can structure analyses to avoid it.

## Principle of analytical consistency: same marketplace scope should inform interrelated factors

At its core, the DuPont inquiry asks whether consumers are likely to confuse the origin of goods or services offered under the competing marks. That question necessarily depends on a factual determination about the marketplace: what products or services are at issue; how they are marketed; who the buyers are; and where and how consumers encounter the marks. The Board’s factual determinations about these matters—particularly determinations that certain goods or services are legally identical or highly similar—are findings that shape the relevant universe for other DuPont factors. A hallmark rule of sound adjudication is that those factual determinations should be carried forward unless there is independent record support to the contrary.

Apex Bank makes this point succinctly. The Board decided that CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s “banking and financing services” were highly similar and even partly legally identical. That factual determination defined a broad universe of financial-services consumers who might encounter both marks in related commercial contexts. Despite that, the Board limited its sixth-factor analysis (evidence of third‑party use of similar marks) to marks specifically identifying credit-card services, excluding marks that used “Aspire” in connection with other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit held that this restriction was inconsistent with the Board’s earlier finding and therefore legally erroneous. Put differently, a tribunal cannot fairly claim a broad overlap of services for one factor and then adopt a narrower conception of the marketplace for another factor unless it explains, on the record, why the narrower conception is separately warranted.

This requirement for consistency serves several functions. It avoids internal contradictions in legal reasoning; it ensures that different DuPont factors—many of which depend on the same underlying marketplace facts—are evaluating the same commercial reality; and it produces predictable, administrable outcomes. When a tribunal treats the same connection between goods or services as broad for one factor and narrow for another, the result is an apples-to-oranges comparison that may distort the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion conclusion.

## How a narrow view under Factor 6 can conflict with a broad view under Factor 2

Factor 2, which examines the similarity of goods or services, is often the linchpin of the DuPont analysis. A finding that services are “related in some manner” or that the “circumstances surrounding their marketing” could cause confusion establishes the relevant market overlap. That same overlap determines the relevant class of third‑party marks to be considered under Factor 6, which asks whether a crowded field of similar marks indicates that a particular mark is weak (entitling it to narrower protection).

A narrow Factor 6 inquiry can distort the analytical picture when the tribunal has already adopted a broad Factor 2 finding. Suppose, as in Apex Bank, the Board concludes that credit card services and banking services are highly similar because banks often issue credit cards and the services are marketed to substantially overlapping consumers. If Factor 6 then considers only marks expressly registered for “credit card services,” it disregards similar uses of the same formative in other banking or financing services that are part of the same marketplace. By excluding those uses, the tribunal artificially reduces the apparent frequency of third‑party use, exaggerating the mark’s commercial strength and narrowing the factual basis for the crowded‑field defense. The Federal Circuit recognized this very dynamic and explained that Factor 6 requires consideration of similar marks on similar goods and services—not only identical ones—so a prior finding of substantial overlap in Factor 2 should ordinarily guide the universe considered under Factor 6.

Beyond doctrinal consistency, the practical consequence of such a mismatch is substantive: an improperly narrow Factor 6 analysis can skew the overall likelihood-of-confusion outcome by overstating the senior user’s exclusivity or the junior user’s potential for confusion. The first DuPont factor—similarity of the marks as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—also depends on the mark’s commercial strength. If the Board understates the extent of third‑party use (i.e., the mark’s weakness), it may mischaracterize the mark’s commercial impression and therefore reach an erroneous conclusion about how consumers will perceive the competing marks.

The error identified in Apex Bank is thus not an abstruse methodological quibble; it goes to the heart of how the DuPont factors interlock. Treating similarity as broad for purposes of identifying the relevant consumer group and narrow for collecting third‑party evidence undermines the integrity of the multi-factor test.

## Practical approach: set the scope once, then carry it through

The simplest and most reliable method to avoid scope inconsistencies is to set a clear marketplace scope at the outset of the analysis and apply that scope consistently across interrelated factors. That does not mean a tribunal may never refine its understanding later; it does mean that any refinement must be explained and supported by the record.

A pragmatic seven-step process will help triers of fact and advocates maintain consistency:

1. Define the goods/services precisely. Start by identifying the parties’ claimed classes of goods or services according to the registrations and applications. Use the language in the identification but also analyze what colloquial and industry usage those terms encompass.

2. Decide whether the goods/services are identical, legally identical, or similar. Consider statutory identifications, actual use, channels of trade, overlapping customers, and industry practice. If a tribunal finds “highly similar” or “legally identical” relationships, that finding should be treated as the baseline for related factors.

3. Fix the relevant consumer universe and commercial channels. Once similarity is determined, specify the relevant consumers (e.g., “consumers of banking and credit card services” rather than a narrower subset) and the places they encounter the marks (online portals, bank branches, co‑branded cards issued by partner banks, etc.).

4. Collect third‑party evidence consistent with that scope. For Factor 6, identify third‑party marks used in connection with all services that the tribunal has found to be similar. Include registrations, marketplace examples, and evidence of consumer recognition across the same channels.

5. Apply interrelated factors using the same scope. When evaluating the appearance, sound, and commercial impression of the marks (Factor 1), the fame/strength of the prior mark (Factor 9), channels of trade (Factor 3), conditions of purchase (Factor 11), and consumer sophistication (Factor 13), use the previously defined consumer universe and channels as the common frame.

6. If narrowing or broadening is necessary, explain why. If the record later reveals that a subset of services is meaningfully distinct (for example, different regulatory regimes or distinct customer classes with separate decision-making processes), the tribunal should articulate that evidence and explain why a narrower or broader scope is warranted for the particular factor.

7. Revisit totality-of-the-circumstances with awareness of interdependence. The final likelihood‑of‑confusion determination should reflect the integrated impact of factors analyzed within a consistent marketplace frame.

Advocates can employ the same approach offensively. A plaintiff seeking broad protection should develop the record to support a broad similarity finding at Factor 2 (market studies, overlap in distribution, typical co‑branding relationships). A defendant seeking to show weakness under Factor 6 should gather third‑party registrations and marketplace examples across the same service classes and channels identified in Factor 2.

## Impacts on other factors: channels of trade, consumer sophistication, conditions of purchase

A consistent scope decision will affect many DuPont factors, not just Factors 1, 2, and 6. Three frequently interrelated factors are channels of trade, consumer sophistication/conditions of purchase, and commercial strength/fame.

Channels of trade (often considered under DuPont Factor 3) are central to the likelihood-of-confusion calculus because overlapping channels increase the risk that consumers will encounter both marks. If the tribunal finds, under Factor 2, that banking and credit card services are highly similar and typically sold through the same channels (e.g., online account portals, bank branches, co-branding with retailers), that finding must inform the analysis of trade channels. A later finding that restricts the channels considered—say, by focusing only on the niche channels used exclusively for one party—creates an analytical inconsistency and may underestimate the true overlap in consumer exposure.

Conditions of purchase and consumer sophistication (typically analyzed under DuPont Factors 11 and 13) are likewise affected by scope. If the relevant consumer class is the general public acquiring everyday financial services, the tribunal might conclude that consumers are price- or brand-conscious and not highly sophisticated, which favors a likelihood of confusion. If a tribunal starts with that broad consumer group under Factor 2, it cannot later characterize the relevant purchasers as sophisticated specialists under another factor without record support. Conversely, where the record shows that particular services are purchased after careful consideration (for example, bespoke commercial lending for sophisticated corporate clients), a narrower finding about purchaser sophistication may be justified—but again, the tribunal must explain and ground that narrower view.

Commercial strength and fame (DuPont Factor 9) depend on both the extent of third‑party use and consumer recognition in the relevant market. In Apex Bank, the Board’s limited consideration of third‑party uses for Factor 6 led it to conclude that CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark was entitled to “normal scope” protection. If the Board had included additional third‑party examples in the broader banking-and-finance universe, it might have concluded the mark was conceptually or commercially weaker, changing the weight of Factor 1 and the ultimate outcome. Thus, the initial scope choice directly influences whether a mark is treated as strong or weak.

In short, scope choice is not a technicality; it is the lens through which many DuPont factors are viewed. An inconsistent lens produces inconsistently supported findings that can be—and, as Apex Bank shows, will be—reversed on appeal.

## Using Apex Bank to design a replicable, consistent factor analysis template

Apex Bank gives a clear remedial blueprint: where a tribunal has made a determinative factual finding about similarity of services, it must use that factual baseline when evaluating other interrelated factors, unless an evidentiary reason compels a different approach. Below is a replicable template—derived from Apex Bank and practical adjudicative practice—that practitioners and tribunals can use to frame and execute a consistent DuPont analysis.

1. Opening statement of market scope. Begin the opinion or brief with a short, explicit statement: “For purposes of this analysis, the relevant market comprises [precise list of goods/services], and the relevant consumers are [description].” If the record provides no precise evidence of consumer segmentation, state that the parties’ identifications control.

2. Factor 2 first. Analyze the similarity or dissimilarity of the goods/services thoroughly and explain why the court finds them identical, legally identical in part, highly similar, or dissimilar. Record the conclusion in a way that can be referenced—for example: “Finding: services are highly similar and marketed to the same consumer base.”

3. Carry the finding forward. Use that finding verbatim or reference it by name when analyzing Factors 1, 3, 6, 9, 11, and 13. For example: “Because the services are highly similar (see supra Factor 2), the relevant universe for assessing third‑party use under Factor 6 includes marks used in connection with all banking and financing services—not only credit-card services.”

4. Factor-specific tailoring with explanation. If a factor requires a finer-grained approach (for instance, evidence shows that one category is typically sold only to corporate clients while another is consumer-facing), explain the record basis for that narrower application before applying the narrower scope. Do not simply shift scope for convenience or because one factor might produce a preferred legal result.

5. Consistent evidentiary collection. Ensure that exhibits, third‑party registrations, survey instruments, and witness testimony introduced for one factor would also be considered for related factors unless rebutted. When admitting or citing third‑party evidence for Factor 6, make clear the criteria used to select that evidence (matching service descriptions, overlapping channels of trade, etc.).

6. Conclude with integrated reasoning. End with an integrated “totality of circumstances” discussion that explicitly ties together the findings under the interrelated factors and explains how the consistent scope led to the ultimate conclusion.

7. Preserve appellate record. On appeal, courts review factual findings for substantial evidence and legal conclusions de novo. A well-documented record that shows consistent application of scope reduces the risk of reversal by making clear that factual findings were neither arbitrary nor internally inconsistent.

Apex Bank shows the consequence of failing to follow such a template: the Board’s inconsistent scopes produced error requiring vacatur and remand. By contrast, a clear initial scope and a discipline to carry it through the analysis create defensible, durable decisions.

## When deviations are justified, explain and record them

The requirement of consistency is robust but not absolute. There are legitimate circumstances where different factors reasonably require different scopes. For example, a third‑party use in one narrow segment may inform the relative weakness of a mark in that segment even if the marks are not used in another, broader segment; or empirical evidence might show that certain channels are functionally separate markets with different buyer behavior. The key is transparency: any deviation from the baseline scope must be grounded in evidence and reasoned explanation.

If a tribunal concludes that a narrower scope is appropriate for Factor 6 despite a broader Factor 2 finding, it should state the grounds: perhaps consumer surveys show no cross‑recognition between the sub-segments, or industry witness testimony establishes that firm boundaries exist between the services at issue. Without such an explanation, the decision risks appellate reversal as in Apex Bank.

## Practical takeaways for litigants and examiners

- Litigants should build the record on marketplace overlap early. If you want a broad scope, collect evidence of co‑branding, joint ventures, shared distribution channels, consumer-facing materials showing overlap, and third‑party uses across the related services. If you seek a narrow scope, collect contrary evidence: segregated channels, distinct consumer classes, and purchasing practices showing little or no overlap.

- Examiners and TTAB judges should state their baseline marketplace finding at the outset of the opinion and reference it throughout. Doing so reduces the need for later justificatory gymnastics and helps appellate courts review for substantial evidence.

- When using third‑party registrations and marketplace evidence, collect and analyze examples consistent with the pre-stated scope. Avoid cherry-picking evidence that aligns with an inconsistent or shifting conception of similarity.

- Finally, litigants should anticipate that inconsistent applications of scope will be noticed and corrected on appeal; the appellate courts are particularly sensitive to internal mismatches among DuPont factor findings because those mismatches often signal underlying analytical error.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a practical reminder that DuPont factors are interrelated and that a tribunal’s factual determination about the similarity of goods or services creates an analytic frame that should inform the other, related factors. A consistent approach—define the relevant marketplace early, gather evidence across that marketplace, and apply that scope coherently across Factors 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 11, and 13—promotes reasoned decision-making and reduces reversible error. Where deviation from the baseline is appropriate, articulate the evidentiary basis clearly. Following these rules will lead to analyses that are both persuasive and durable.
