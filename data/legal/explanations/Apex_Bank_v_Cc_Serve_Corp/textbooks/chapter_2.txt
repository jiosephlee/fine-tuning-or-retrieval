Chapter 2: # Appeals from the TTAB and Standards of Review

This chapter explains how decisions of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB or Board) are reviewed on appeal, what appellate pathways are available to parties who lose at the TTAB, the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over TTAB appeals, and the standards the appellate court applies to the Board’s factual findings and legal conclusions. The discussion uses Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL ___ (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) (No. 2023‑2143), as the primary vehicle to illustrate these principles. Apex Bank is a useful recent example because the Federal Circuit applied the familiar division between de novo review of law and substantial‑evidence review of fact, explained the interplay between factual findings on related DuPont factors, and demonstrated the practical remedies—affirmance in part, vacation in part, and remand—when review reveals mixed errors.

## Appellate pathways: § 1071(a) appeal to the Federal Circuit versus § 1071(b) de novo civil action

When the TTAB issues an adverse final decision in an opposition or cancellation proceeding, the Lanham Act gives the losing party two mutually exclusive options. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1071(a), the party may appeal the Board’s decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. That appeal is confined to the administrative record developed before the Board. Alternatively, under 15 U.S.C. § 1071(b), the party may instead bring a civil action in a federal district court, where the case is tried de novo—with the ability to introduce new evidence, call live witnesses, and develop a fuller factual record. A plaintiff may not do both; the statute provides an election of remedies.

The practical consequence of this choice is fundamental. An appeal under § 1071(a) is an appellate proceeding: the reviewing court confines itself to the record before the Board and reviews the Board’s findings under the statutory judicial standards of appellate review. By contrast, a § 1071(b) suit in district court is an original trial that substitutes the federal district court’s findings for the Board’s and permits expansion of the evidentiary record. Because appellate reversal of factual findings is difficult under the substantial‑evidence standard (discussed below), parties who believe they need additional evidence to prevail may prefer the de novo district‑court option. But the district‑court path is often more expensive and time‑consuming; parties must weigh the likelihood of success on the existing administrative record versus the cost and delay of a new trial.

Apex Bank illustrates this strategic contrast. Apex chose the § 1071(a) route and appealed to the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit therefore reviewed only the TTAB record and remanded to the Board where the court found legal errors in how the Board treated the DuPont factors and the universe of third‑party marks. Had Apex sought a § 1071(b) de novo trial in district court, Apex could have introduced additional evidence about third‑party uses of “Aspire” and the marketplace to support its arguments that the registered mark was weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection.

## Federal Circuit jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)

The Federal Circuit’s power to hear appeals from the TTAB is statute‑based. Title 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B) gives the Federal Circuit exclusive appellate jurisdiction over “an appeal from a decision of the United States Patent and Trademark Office” with respect to certain trademark matters. When appellants take the § 1071(a) path, their appeals proceed under that statutory jurisdictional grant to the Federal Circuit. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. invoked § 1295(a)(4)(B) as its jurisdictional basis for review, a routine posture for TTAB appeals.

Because the Federal Circuit has nationwide jurisdiction over these appeals, it provides uniform federal law on trademark questions decided administratively by the PTO. That uniformity also underscores why the precise standards of review and the limits of the record are important: the Federal Circuit applies a developed body of administrative‑law and trademark precedent (for example, DuPont, M2 Software, Coach Services, Jack Wolfskin, Juice Generation) to review Board determinations.

## Standards of review: de novo for law; substantial evidence for fact

A foundational principle in judicial review of agency adjudications governs TTAB appeals: appellate courts review questions of law de novo and review the agency’s findings of fact for substantial evidence. The Federal Circuit stated this division explicitly in Apex Bank, citing M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006), and other precedents. That division organizes review of a TTAB decision and is particularly salient in trademark oppositions and cancellations where the ultimate issue—likelihood of confusion—is a legal determination informed by underlying factual findings (the DuPont factors).

How does this division operate in practice? The often‑cited articulation in DuPont v. R. P. H. Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973), remains the starting point: likelihood of confusion is a question of law based on factual findings relating to the applicable DuPont factors. Thus, when the Board applies legal standards—e.g., how to define the goods or services at issue, which DuPont factors are relevant, or whether a particular evidentiary showing suffices to establish weakness—the Federal Circuit reviews those legal conclusions de novo. When the Board makes factual findings—e.g., the definition of the ordinary purchaser, the nature of the goods or services, whether specific third‑party uses exist, or credibility determinations—the Federal Circuit defers so long as those factual conclusions are supported by substantial evidence in the record.

This structure explains many outcomes on appeal. For example, in Apex Bank the Board’s legal characterization of the parties’ services and the scope of the mark’s protection were reviewed with mixed results: the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services were highly similar was sustained under the substantial‑evidence standard, and the conclusion that the second DuPont factor weighed heavily in favor of confusion was affirmed. But the Board erred as a matter of law by narrowing the universe of third‑party marks it would consider under the sixth DuPont factor even though it had already found that the services were highly similar; that legal error required vacation and remand.

## Substantial evidence defined and applied

“Substantial evidence” is the statutory administrative standard of factual sufficiency. The Federal Circuit uses the classic definition adopted from the Supreme Court: substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion” (Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938)). Applied in the TTAB context, the substantial‑evidence inquiry asks whether the Board’s factual findings have enough evidentiary support in the administrative record that a reasonable factfinder could reach the same conclusion.

Substantial‑evidence review is deferential but not toothless. The court does not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for the Board’s on matters of credibility or competing inferences. But the court will reverse when factual findings are unsupported by any reasonable basis in the record or when the Board makes no factual finding on a material issue. The Federal Circuit’s decisions repeatedly emphasize that each DuPont factor presents questions of fact to be tested for substantial evidence when challenged on appeal. See Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002).

In Apex Bank the Federal Circuit applied this standard to affirm the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor. The Board defined “credit card services” and relied on dictionary definitions, evidence of how credit cards overlap with banking and finance, and third‑party registrations to support its conclusion that the services were “highly similar” and partly legally identical. The court concluded that substantial evidence supported that factual finding: a reasonable mind could accept the Board’s evidentiary basis and inferences.

But substantial evidence is also contextual. The court will examine whether the Board’s factfinding was internally consistent and whether legal errors infected the factual analysis. In Apex Bank the Board’s later restriction of the universe of third‑party marks considered under the sixth DuPont factor was inconsistent with its earlier factual determination that the services were highly similar; the Federal Circuit treated that inconsistency as a legal error that required vacatur and remand. In short, substantial‑evidence review requires both the presence of supporting evidence and an adequately reasoned connection between that evidence and the Board’s findings.

## Limits of the record on appeal

A key practical consequence of choosing the Federal Circuit appeal under § 1071(a) is that review is limited to the administrative record before the TTAB. The appellate court will not consider new evidence first introduced on appeal; parties cannot expand the evidentiary universe in a § 1071(a) appeal. The record includes the pleadings, exhibits, testimony (if any), stipulations, and arguments the parties presented to the Board. Motions filed with the Federal Circuit asking the court to consider extra‑record evidence generally fail unless the evidence relates to a purely procedural or clerical issue or the parties first seek to add it to the TTAB record through a stay and remand or a motion to reopen at the Board (and the Board accepts it).

This constraint matters for proof of weak marks (sixth DuPont), fame (fifth DuPont), the nature of the goods (second DuPont), or actual confusion (DuPont’s eighth factor). If a party believes the TTAB record lacks critical third‑party evidence or consumer surveys, the party must either have introduced that evidence before the Board or opt for a § 1071(b) civil action where the district court will hear new evidence de novo. As Apex Bank demonstrates, the Federal Circuit will base its review on the record as it stands. Apex submitted third‑party materials to the Board; the court analyzed those materials. Where weakness or crowded‑field arguments were inadequately supported in the TTAB record, the Federal Circuit’s remedial choice was remand so the Board could reconsider the issue with the proper definitional scope—rather than permitting the appellant to add materials for the first time on appeal.

There is a corollary administrative‑law principle: the party challenging a Board decision must show that the record either lacks substantial evidence for a particular factual conclusion or that the Board made a legal error in applying the law to the facts. Procedural objections to the TTAB’s process (for example, failure to consider a relevant factor or reliance on inadmissible evidence) can also form the basis for reversal when preserved in the record.

## Consequences of legal error versus evidentiary insufficiency: affirm, vacate, remand

The remedies an appellate court fashions after review depend on whether the error identified is legal, factual, or a combination.

When the reviewing court finds legal error, reversal that vacates the Board’s relevant holdings and remands for further proceedings is a common remedy. Legal errors include misinterpretation of governing precedent, incorrect legal standard, or misapplication of law to facts—particularly when the misapplication infected the Board’s factfinding or reasoning. Apex Bank provides a clear example: the Board adopted inconsistent scopes of similarity when it defined the parties’ services for the second DuPont factor but then limited the sixth‑factor analysis to a narrower universe of third‑party marks. Because that restriction was a misapplication of law—there was no principled reason to constrict the sixth factor after finding the services highly similar—the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis and remanded for reconsideration with the correct legal scope. The court also vacated the first‑factor analysis (similarity of marks) because the first factor depends on commercial impression, and the Board’s reassessment of the sixth factor on remand could alter conclusions about commercial strength and impression. Thus, legal error typically leads to remand for the agency to apply the correct legal standard to the existing record (or to take additional factfinding within the agency’s mandate).

When the reviewing court finds inadequate evidentiary support for the Board’s factual findings—i.e., the Board’s findings are not supported by substantial evidence—the court will set aside those findings and remand for further proceedings. Depending on the circumstances, the court may enter judgment for the appellant if the record compels a contrary factual finding or if the Board’s error was so fundamental that no proper factfinding could cure it without new evidence. More often, however, the court remands so the Board can reassess the facts under the correct legal framework or make additional factual findings. The Apex Bank decision shows both outcomes in combination: the court affirmed aspects of the Board’s factual findings (the similarity of services) because they were supported by substantial evidence, and it vacated and remanded other parts because of legal error in the scope of marks considered under the sixth factor and the ripple effects on the first factor.

Sometimes the appellate court will affirm in part and reverse or vacate in part. This disposition is common in DuPont analysis because multiple independent factors interact to determine the legal conclusion of likelihood of confusion. The Federal Circuit often leaves intact fact findings that are adequately supported while instructing the Board to revisit particular analyses or to articulate its reasoning more clearly. In Apex Bank the court affirmed the Board’s second‑factor finding but vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth and first factors, then remanded the case so the Board could apply the appropriate legal scope to the sixth factor and reexamine the first factor in light of any new conclusion about commercial strength.

For practitioners, this pattern has tactical consequences. If only the Board’s legal reasoning is deficient, the court’s remedy of vacatur and remand gives the Board another chance to apply the correct standard to the same evidence. But if the TTAB’s factfinding lacks support, the appellate court may be constrained: it cannot make new factual findings; it can only vacate and remand (or—rarely—render judgment if the record compels a different factual result). That reality reinforces the earlier practical point: parties dissatisfied with the TTAB should consider whether they need a de novo trial in district court to develop additional factual proof rather than rely on the appellate remedy.

## Practical implications and concluding observations

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. reinforces several practical and doctrinal lessons about TTAB appeals. First, the choice between a § 1071(a) appeal and a § 1071(b) district‑court action is critical. The former binds a party to the TTAB record and the substantial‑evidence standard; the latter permits new evidence but imposes the costs and uncertainties of a new trial. Second, the Federal Circuit’s division of labor—de novo review of legal conclusions and substantial‑evidence review of facts—governs outcomes and shapes litigation strategy. When the Board’s legal analysis is faulty, appellate courts will not hesitate to vacate and remand. When the Board’s factual findings hang on sufficient evidentiary support, they will be sustained even if another reasonable factfinder might have drawn different inferences. Third, the interdependence of DuPont factors means that an error on one factor can infect others; appellate courts will often remand for the Board to reconsider related factors together in light of corrected legal standards.

For law students and practitioners, the Apex Bank decision is an instructive model for how the Federal Circuit applies these principles. Read the opinion to see the familiar patterns: the court’s careful parsing of what is law and what is fact, its deference to well‑supported Board findings, and its willingness to correct legal missteps and return the matter to the agency for further proceedings. Understanding these appellate mechanics is essential to advising clients about forum selection, building an administrative record, and framing issues for appeal.
