Chapter 6: # Similarity of the Marks (DuPont Factor 1): Commercial Impression and Context

The first DuPont factor directs courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (the Board) to compare the marks "in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1361 (C.C.P.A. 1973). That principle is deceptively simple in words and complex in application. This chapter explains how courts apply the entireties rule without improper dissection, how certain elements (generic or descriptive matter, design elements, word order, and dominant components) are weighted, how standard-character registrations expand scope, and how the mark’s conceptual strength (including evidence of third-party uses) interlocks with the comparison under Factor 1. We ground the doctrinal points in the Federal Circuit’s Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. decision and related precedent, and we give practicable rules of decision that exam-takers and practitioners can apply.

## Evaluating marks in their entireties: no dissecting but recognizing dominant elements

The “entireties” principle means examiners and courts must strive to form the overall commercial impression a mark makes on an ordinary consumer, rather than engaging in a formal element-by-element dissection that treats each word or design in isolation. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). That said, the doctrine does not prohibit recognizing that one element of a compound mark is the source-identifying or dominant element and therefore merits greater weight in the comparison. The line between prohibited dissection and permissible identification of dominant features is practical and fact-driven.

Permissible focus on a dominant element: Courts often say that consumers typically shorten multiword marks in colloquial use to their most salient or distinctive word. Thus, when a mark is a compound (for example, ASPIRE BANK), the dominant word (ASPIRE) may drive the commercial impression, and courts will give that dominant element more weight in assessing similarity. Recognizing a dominant portion is not the same as comparing only that portion while ignoring context; rather, the court compares the whole mark but accordingly explains why one part is likely to catch a consumer’s attention and dominate recollection. The Federal Circuit made this clear in I.AM.Symbolic: the focus remains on the overall commercial impression, but attention may reasonably center on the portion of the mark that consumers are most likely to remember.

Impermissible dissection: What courts must avoid is artificially parsing marks into neat parts and weighing each as if they were independent words of equal source-identifying force, then mechanically comparing like-to-like. For example, breaking ASPIRE BANK into AS + PIRE + BANK and treating the syllables separately, or comparing only the shared letters without regard to the presence of the generic “BANK” or to any distinctive stylization, would be prohibited dissection. Likewise, isolating a design element from its context and comparing it alone—when the design is not the dominant impression—can produce an erroneous result.

Application in Apex: The Federal Circuit stressed the need to revisit the Board’s Factor 1 analysis because a revised assessment of the mark’s strength (Factor 6) could change the overall commercial impression. The court did not mandate that the Board ignore dominant elements; it required that any weighting of such elements occur within an overall, contextual assessment consistent with the entireties rule.

Practical guidance: On exam or in practice, articulate why a particular component is likely to dominate consumer perception—frequency of use, linguistic prominence, or a descriptive tail that consumers treat as generic. Then explain the whole-mark comparison, explicitly addressing appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression, and showing why the dominant element shifts the balance.

## Weighting descriptive or generic matter (e.g., ‘Bank’) in the overall impression

Generic and highly descriptive matter receives little or no source-indicating weight in a likelihood-of-confusion comparison. If part of the mark will be perceived by consumers as merely describing the goods or the class of provider (e.g., “BANK” for banking services), that matter should be accorded accordingly reduced importance in the Factor 1 analysis.

Legal baseline: Generic terms can never function as trademarks and thus contribute nothing to distinctiveness; descriptive terms are weaker and receive narrower protection. When a composite mark includes a generic or descriptive component, courts will generally discount that component in determining the commercial impression. The Board and courts also give less weight to matter that is commonly used in the trade. For example, “BANK” used in connection with banking and financial services will generally be treated as generic or at least descriptive and given minimal weight when the marks are compared.

How to apply the rule: The analyst should (1) determine whether the element is generic or descriptive, sometimes by reference to dictionary definitions or trade usage; (2) decide how consumers will perceive that element in context; and (3) treat that element as less important in the comparison, while still noting any color, stylization, or placement that might cause consumers to treat the descriptive word as part of a unitary source indicator.

Apex-specific considerations: In Apex Bank, the applicant’s filings claimed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services.” “BANK” is plainly descriptive/generic in that context. Thus "ASPIRE" is likely the dominant source-identifying element. The Board on remand should discount “BANK” and evaluate how consumers will remember and refer to the mark—most likely as “Aspire.” That discounting does not automatically produce a likelihood-of-confusion finding, but it focuses attention on the true source-identifying portion of the mark.

Caveat: Descriptive matter may be stylized or combined with design elements such that, in practice, it creates a distinctive composite. If the design or stylization is sufficiently distinctive and prominent, the descriptive portion may exert less effect on the consumer’s recollection than the design. Always analyze whether the visual or phonetic presentation elevates the descriptive element’s source-identifying potential.

## Standard character marks and scope across stylizations

A standard-character (or word) mark registration protects the mark’s literal word(s) regardless of font, style, size, or color. That breadth is important in Factor 1 comparisons because a registered standard-character mark can be shown in any stylized form in evidence and still be considered the same mark.

Legal effect: When a registrant owns a standard-character registration for, say, ASPIRE, their protection covers the word in any stylization, making it harder for an applicant to rely on differing fonts or design to distinguish its mark unless it can show that the design contributes a materially different overall commercial impression. Conversely, an applicant’s design claim (a word-plus-design mark) may be meaningful if the design is distinctive enough to alter the commercial impression beyond the protected scope of the registrant’s standard-character mark.

Application in Apex: CC Serve owned a standard-character registration for ASPIRE. Apex sought a registration for ASPIRE BANK as a word and design mark. Because CC Serve’s mark is standard-character, the Board must compare Apex’s proposed stylized composite to the registrant’s plain ASPIRE across any stylization. If the design elements of Apex’s mark are minimal or conventional, they will be insufficient to avoid confusion, because the standard-character ASPIRE covers identical word usage in any style. Thus the Board should evaluate whether the design features in Apex’s logo create a distinctive overall impression that would lead consumers to perceive ASPIRE BANK as a different source from ASPIRE.

Practical point: A party defending against a standard-character mark should show that the applicant’s stylization is sufficiently distinctive—through unusual design, color, or arrangement—to change the overall commercial impression. The applicant, conversely, should not assume that stylization alone will defeat a standard-character proprietor’s claim.

## Connotation and suggestiveness in financial services (e.g., aspirational terms like ‘Aspire’)

Connotation—what the mark suggests to an ordinary consumer—is central to the first-factor analysis. Names in the financial services context often operate as suggestive or laudatory terms (e.g., “Aspire,” “Prosper,” “Trust”) and therefore occupy varying positions on the distinctiveness spectrum.

Spectrum placement: Arbitrary or fanciful marks (e.g., made-up words) are the strongest for trademark purposes; suggestive marks (which require consumer imagination to connect to the goods/services) are next strongest; descriptive marks are weaker and generic marks are unprotectable. “Aspire” is typically suggestive or laudatory in the financial-services field: it connotes aspiration, upward mobility, or customer ambition. Because it evokes the character of the user’s goals rather than directly describing a specific financial product, it will often be treated as inherently distinctive (i.e., protectable without proof of acquired distinctiveness), but its conceptual distinctiveness is not absolute—it can be weakened by pervasive third-party use.

How connotation affects Factor 1: A mark with a strong, unique connotation commands broader protection; a suggestive but common term, used widely in the industry, may be less likely to cause confusion when used by different sources. Thus the meaning consumers attach to a word—how ordinary customers interpret “Aspire” in the context of financial services—shapes the entireties analysis.

Apex application: The Board must decide how consumers perceive “Aspire” in the financial-services universe. Do customers see it as a distinctive brand name or as a dictionary-like aspirational verb commonly used in marketing? The answer affects how much weight to place on the common element between CC Serve’s ASPIRE and Apex’s ASPIRE BANK. If “Aspire” has a strong conceptual meaning for consumer identity of source, an “ASPIRE BANK” mark may be more likely to cause confusion; if the term is weak because many banks and card programs use it, the Board should treat it as entitled to a narrow scope of protection.

## Interdependence with mark strength: weaker marks require greater similarity to confuse

The DuPont framework is interdependent: findings under one factor often inform others. Factor 6 (third-party use and registrations) is especially relevant to Factor 1 because evidence that a mark is commercially weak—common in the trade—narrows its scope of protection and means that only highly similar marks will create a likelihood of confusion. Put another way, when a senior mark is weak, an applicant’s mark must be more similar to cause confusion; when the senior mark is strong, even moderate similarity can be confusing.

Doctrinal basis: The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized this interdependence. Evidence of numerous third-party uses of a formative element tends to show that consumers have been “educated” to distinguish between similar marks and that the element is weak as a source identifier. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

How that affects analysis: On remand, the Board must not treat Factor 1 in a vacuum. If, after considering all relevant third-party uses (and adopting a scope of similar services consistent with its finding under Factor 2), the Board finds that many financial-services providers use “Aspire” or similar marks, it should construe the commercial impression of the registered ASPIRE as weaker and require a closer similarity for a finding of likelihood of confusion. Conversely, if ASPIRE is relatively unique across the relevant field, that will amplify the significance of a shared element (ASPIRE) in the Factor 1 comparison.

Apex implications: The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s Factor 6 analysis because the Board limited its universe of third-party marks too narrowly. Because Factor 6 bears directly on how much protection ASPIRE merits, the Factor 1 comparison must be recalibrated on remand in light of a proper evaluation of the third-party usage evidence.

## The I.AM.Symbolic principle: overall commercial impression drives the inquiry

I.AM.Symbolic makes explicit that the central question under Factor 1 is the overall commercial impression made by the marks as experienced in the marketplace. That decision teaches several points: first, the marks are compared as a whole; second, the comparison is contextual—what the consumer sees and expects in the marketplace matters; third, the analysis is not purely lexical but visual and conceptual.

Operationalizing I.AM.Symbolic: To determine the commercial impression courts consider: (1) how the mark looks on packaging, websites, or advertisements; (2) how it sounds when spoken (important for services marketed orally); (3) what the mark connotes to the relevant consumer; and (4) whether the arrangement of words and the presence of design elements create an impression distinct from the literal words. The court should ask: what impression is likely to persist in the mind of the ordinary purchaser who encounters the mark during a typical marketing channel?

Evidence that matters: Actual marketing excerpts, specimens, consumer surveys, trade usage, and even dictionary meanings can inform the commercial-impression inquiry. Where there is a colorable difference in stylization or a striking design element, a specimen showing how the mark is used in commerce will be especially valuable.

Apex guidance: The Federal Circuit recognized that the Board must revisit its Factor 1 assessment because the mark’s commercial impression depends upon the mark’s conceptual strength (Factor 6) and on how consumers will perceive the ASPIRE element across services. Thus, on remand the Board should apply I.AM.Symbolic’s holistic, contextual approach: give appropriate weight to the whole-next-to-whole comparison, but explain the role of particular elements (like “BANK”) in forming the overall impression.

## Apex Bank remand: revisiting Factor 1 after recalibrating mark strength

What precisely must happen on remand? The Federal Circuit’s opinion gives a roadmap. First, the Board must expand the universe of third-party marks it considers under Factor 6 to include marks used on services the Board has already found to be highly similar (not merely identical). Because the Board concluded the parties’ services are highly similar, the Board cannot limit third-party evidence to credit-card-only uses; it must consider all third-party uses of ASPIRE or Aspire-formative marks in banking and broader financial services. That broader review may reveal that ASPIRE is either weak (widely used) or relatively strong (few common uses).

Second, the Board must revisit Factor 1 in light of whatever it finds under Factor 6. If it finds the ASPIRE element commercially weak, the Board should explain why the residual differences—such as the presence of “BANK,” any design elements in Apex’s mark, or differences in trade channels—are sufficient to avoid confusion despite the shared term. If, instead, ASPIRE retains normal or strong protection, the Board should explain why the shared word ASPIRE (likely the dominant element in ASPIRE BANK) creates a likelihood of confusion even if “BANK” is generic.

Third, the Board must provide a reasoned explanation for how each aspect of the marks contributes to the overall commercial impression: appearance (shared word, typography, design), sound (how consumers say “Aspire” and “Aspire Bank”), connotation (aspirational meaning and the likely consumer reaction), and commercial impression (where consumers will encounter the marks—their sophistication, channels, and typical decision-making process).

Practical steps the Board should take on remand:
- Reassess the relevant consumer group and channels of trade consistent with the earlier Factor 2 finding of high similarity in services.
- Re-evaluate the scope and impact of third-party ASPIRE uses on financial services, including registrations, marketplace evidence, and the extent of use (national vs. limited).
- Determine whether “BANK” is generic/descriptive as to Apex’s identified services and reduce its weight accordingly.
- Compare the entire marks—ASPIRE (standard-character) versus ASPIRE BANK (word and design)—considering the standard-character breadth of the registrant’s rights.
- Explain whether and to what extent any design elements in Apex’s proposed mark materially change the commercial impression from ASPIRE alone.
- Tie the analysis to consumer sophistication and purchasing conditions that might mitigate or exacerbate confusion risk.

## Application heuristics and a checklist for Factor 1 analysis

For law students and practitioners, here is a concise checklist to use when preparing or evaluating a Factor 1 analysis:

1. Identify the marks and their registrations (standard-character vs. stylized, word-plus-design).
2. Determine the relevant consumer and channels of trade (use Factor 2 findings to inform this).
3. Ask whether any component is generic or descriptive (e.g., “BANK”) and give it reduced weight.
4. Evaluate appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression—explain which element(s) are likely dominant and why.
5. Consider the standard-character mark’s scope: does it cover the applicant’s stylization?
6. Review evidence of third-party use/registration (Factor 6) and assess how that evidence affects conceptual strength. Adjust the required degree of similarity accordingly: weaker senior marks require closer similarity.
7. Analyze whether the combined mark (word + design) creates a materially different commercial impression than the senior mark alone.
8. State a reasoned conclusion that integrates all aspects and explains the likely reaction of an ordinary purchaser.

Illustrative hypotheticals:
- “ASPIRATION BANK” vs. registered “ASPIRE”: If many banks use “aspire” or “aspiration” in marks, ASPIRE is weak—mere addition of “BANK” probably unlikely to cause confusion unless the stylization is identical or other factors (channel of trade, consumer sophistication) point to confusion.
- Stylized logo “Aspire” with unique swoosh vs. standard-character “ASPIRE”: if the logo is prominent and used consistently in advertising, it may tilt the commercial impression away from the plain ASPIRE; however, a standard-character registrant can still assert rights against highly similar stylizations because its registration covers the word across styles.

Conclusion

Factor 1 requires a holistic, contextual comparison that looks at how ordinary consumers perceive the marks in the marketplace. Courts must avoid impermissible dissection, but they may and should explain why certain elements dominate the commercial impression. Generic or descriptive elements like “BANK” receive less weight, and standard-character registrations carry broad scope across stylizations. Crucially, the mark-strength evidence gathered under Factor 6 is not an add-on but a determinant of how much similarity is needed to cause confusion; a weak mark narrows protection and demands closer similarity for confusion to exist. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. illustrates these interdependencies: because the Board limited the universe of third-party marks improperly, its Factor 6 finding could not stand, and the Factor 1 analysis—dependent on the mark’s strength—must be revisited. On remand, a careful, contextual explanation that follows the checklist above should produce a reasoned, reviewable decision.
