Chapter 10: # Comparative Jurisprudence: Key Federal Circuit and TTAB Precedents

This chapter situates Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL ___ (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025), within the Federal Circuit’s developing body of law on Section 2(d) likelihood-of-confusion disputes. The Apex Bank decision is a useful focal point because it both applies established DuPont principles and articulates a practical consistency principle: when the Board finds services to be highly similar (or legally identical) under the second DuPont factor, that factual finding should inform the scope of inquiry under related factors—especially the sixth (crowded field/third-party marks) and the first (marks compared in their entireties to determine commercial impression). To understand Apex’s holding and its implications, we must review the precedent that frames DuPont analysis, the proper use of third‑party evidence, and the standards governing appellate review. The following sections examine the principal cases and explain how each doctrine operated in Apex and how law students and practitioners should apply the principles to new fact patterns.

## Coach Services: relatedness of goods/services and third-party registrations

Coach Services v. Triumph Learning, 668 F.3d 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2012), is often cited for the principle that the goods or services of the parties need not be identical to support a finding of likelihood of confusion; instead, the relevant inquiry is whether the goods or services are “related in some manner” or marketed in such a way that consumers could believe they come from the same source. Coach emphasizes that evidence of relatedness may come from the nature of the goods and services, their channels of trade, typical purchasers, or third‑party registrations that cover both categories of goods and services.

Apex relied on the Coach principle when the Board examined whether CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s “banking and financing services” were related. The Board concluded—based on dictionary definitions, the parties’ descriptions, and third‑party registrations—that the services were highly similar and even partially legally identical. That determination is squarely within the scope of Coach’s guidance: the Board did not have to find that a bank and a credit card processor are the same business in all respects, but only that the services are related and marketed in ways that could cause consumer confusion. Coach thus informs factor‑two analysis: examiners and the Board should look beyond mere labels to the real-world nature, channels, and partnerships that connect two services.

For students: when analyzing relatedness under DuPont factor two, assemble multiple streams of evidence—statutory or dictionary definitions, specimen or application descriptions, third‑party registrations that cover both services, websites or promotional materials showing overlap in channels or target consumers, and evidence of commercial relationships (e.g., banks issuing cards serviced by third‑party processors). Each item complements the others and may together satisfy the “related in some manner” standard.

## Palm Bay: probative value of third-party registrations and weakness

Palm Bay Imports, Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2005), addresses the probative value of third‑party registrations and use evidence in showing a mark’s weakness. Palm Bay explains that third‑party registrations and evidence of use can be admitted to show that a descriptive or common element is weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection. The key point is that such evidence tends to show that the marketplace is saturated with marks using the same or similar formative elements, and thus consumers have become accustomed to distinguishing among such marks.

In Apex, the parties disputed whether CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark was commercially or conceptually weak because other market participants used “Aspire” or Aspire‑formative marks in the financial services space. The Board, citing Palm Bay, acknowledged third‑party uses but concluded the number of similar marks was not “considerable” or “ubiquitous” enough to demonstrate weakness. Palm Bay teaches that the strength or weakness inquiry is fact intensive: the Board must evaluate the quality, quantity, and nature of the third‑party evidence and explain how that evidence undermines (or does not undermine) a mark’s distinctiveness.

For students: third‑party registrations alone have limited probative value; the Board and courts prefer evidence of marketplace use—advertisements, screenshots, specimens—because such evidence shows actual consumer exposure. The weight assigned to that evidence depends on its volume relative to the field, its recency, and whether the marks are used on services that the relevant consumer would actually encounter when making purchasing decisions.

## Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin: crowded field evidence and its effect on scope

Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015), and Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2015), both illustrate how “crowded field” evidence affects the scope of protection afforded to a mark. Juice Generation found that 26 third‑party registrations and uses constituted “a considerable number” that was probative of weakness for a short, common word element; Jack Wolfskin stressed the importance of voluminous and representative examples showing that the public has been trained to distinguish among similar marks (the opinion pointed to 14 notable examples and many additional registrations and sales figures).

These decisions show two related points. First, the number of third‑party marks is important—what constitutes “considerable” depends on the marketplace and how often consumers are likely to see such marks. Second, courts expect context and explanation: raw lists of registrations are less persuasive than carefully documented, industry‑relevant examples showing consumer exposure, similarity of services, or even geographic breadth. Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin also emphasize that a crowded field can narrow a mark’s protection; where the field is crowded, the owner’s monopoly over a formative element should be limited to prevent unfairly blocking newcomers from using otherwise weak but descriptive elements.

Apex illustrates the interplay between factor two and factor six: the Board found the services highly similar but then limited its sixth‑factor inquiry to marks used only for “credit card services,” excluding Aspire marks used in other banking contexts. The Federal Circuit vacated that narrowing. Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin guide the remand: if, as the Board found, banking and credit‑card services are highly similar, then the Board must consider the broader universe of Aspire‑formative marks used across banking and finance when assessing whether the field is crowded and how broad CC Serve’s protection should be.

For students and practitioners: when assembling crowded‑field evidence, present both breadth (how many similar marks are in use) and depth (how similar those marks are in channels and consumers and how much exposure they have earned). Show not only registrations but specimens, market share or sales data, licensing, and consumer testimony where possible. If you have evidence of consumer sophistication or distinguishing house marks, include that too, because a crowded field interacts with purchaser sophistication in reducing likely confusion.

## Olde Tyme Foods: ‘similar’ does not mean ‘identical’ under the factors

Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200 (Fed. Cir. 1992), stands for the simple but important proposition that “similar” does not require “identical.” The Olde Tyme court clarified that DuPont’s factor concerning the number and nature of similar marks (and related inquiries) does not demand exact identity of goods or services; rather, it requires a finding that the goods are similar enough that consumers would be likely to associate them with a single source.

Apex draws on Olde Tyme to support the proposition that the sixth DuPont factor may—and often should—look beyond literally identical goods to those that are similar in the sense recognized under factor two. The Federal Circuit in Apex explicitly invoked Olde Tyme and related cases to explain why the Board erred in narrowing third‑party consideration to only credit card services after it had already found credit card and banking services to be highly similar. Olde Tyme therefore functions as a cross‑factor coherence rule: once the Board finds similarity for relatedness, it must not impose a stricter similarity requirement for examining the marketplace for similar marks.

For application: when litigating, resist attempts by the opposing party to compartmentalize evidence by calling it “not identical” on a narrow, literal basis. Show how the goods or services are used in the same channels, targeted to the same consumers, or otherwise overlap so that the court or Board may rely on Olde Tyme to broaden the inquiry appropriately.

## M2 Software and Bose: articulation of standards of review and factor-by-factor findings

M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2006), and Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2002), address appellate standards and the necessary factual rigor in DuPont analyses. M2 Software reiterates that legal questions are reviewed de novo while factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. Bose adds that each DuPont factor presents a factual question and that the Board’s analysis should make clear findings on which courts can perform a substantial‑evidence review.

The practical lesson from these cases appears in Apex’s opinion: the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factual finding on factor two under substantial‑evidence review, but it vacated and remanded factors one and six because the Board’s legal framing and selective narrowing of evidence were inconsistent with its earlier factual findings. The appellate court’s error correction reflects Bose and M2 Software: an appellate court can and will require the Board to explain its reasoning, apply consistent scopes, and ensure that legal framework and factual findings are aligned.

For students: when evaluating Board decisions, identify whether the Board has (1) made explicit factual findings for the DuPont factors it considered, (2) tied those findings to evidence in the record, and (3) applied a consistent legal standard across factors. If the Board changes scope or relies on a narrower standard in a later factor without explanation—especially after making a broad factual finding—the decision is vulnerable on appeal under the M2/Bose framework.

## I.AM.Symbolic: commercial impression and mark comparison in their entireties

In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315 (Fed. Cir. 2017), the Federal Circuit emphasized that the first DuPont factor focuses on the marks “in their entireties,” considering appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. I.AM.Symbolic reiterates that an analysis which cherry‑picks elements of a mark or mechanically compares components without considering the overall impression is insufficient. The court also reminded tribunals to consider how consumers encounter marks in real life—how they are displayed, pronounced, and perceived.

Apex’s remand of the first factor derives directly from I.AM.Symbolic’s teachings. Because the Board’s evaluation of commercial impression might change if the sixth factor is reweighed to include a broader set of Aspire uses (thereby possibly showing weakness of the formative element), the Board must reconsider whether the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks would create a similar commercial impression to CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark. I.AM.Symbolic requires the Board to perform that comparison holistically and to explain how any changes in perceived strength or narrowing of protection affect the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion calculus.

For classroom application: when comparing marks under the first DuPont factor, avoid analyzing only dominant or distinctive elements in isolation. Instead, simulate consumer perception—how is the mark used in context? Are there distinctive design elements or additional words that change the commercial impression? Is the sound prominent, or does the visual design dominate? Remember that differences in trade channels and marketing-focused matter in forming consumer impressions.

## Harmonizing these cases with Apex Bank’s consistency principle

Apex Bank contributes a clarifying principle of practical importance: factual determinations about similarity made in one DuPont factor should constrain (and inform) the scope of inquiry under other related factors. The court held that after the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar (and in part legally identical) under factor two, it erred by later constricting the sixth‑factor comparison to only marks used for “credit card services” while excluding marks used in the broader banking and financial services arena. The Federal Circuit’s ruling harmonizes earlier precedent in several ways.

First, it operationalizes Olde Tyme and Coach: similarity is not a binary, narrow concept; once a finding of high similarity is made, the Board should not adopt a stricter similarity standard for other factors without justification. Second, it follows Palm Bay, Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin by directing the Board to consider the broader marketplace when assessing field‑crowding and mark weakness—particularly those third‑party marks consumers would realistically encounter when deciding between services that the Board itself has found related. Third, consistent with M2 Software and Bose, Apex requires the Board to articulate and reconcile its factual findings across factors so that an appellate court can review the record meaningfully.

This “consistency principle” does not mean the Board must treat every related factor identically; rather, it must apply coherent boundaries of similarity. If factor two identifies the relevant goods or services as covering, for example, banking and credit‑card services broadly, then third‑party evidence under factor six should encompass the same or reasonably overlapping categories, and the Board must explain any deviation. Similarly, commercial impression analysis under factor one must consider whether market saturation or weakness of a formative element meaningfully alters how consumers perceive source—a determination that cannot be made in isolation from the sixth factor’s findings.

Practical implications for practitioners and examiners:

- When you obtain a favorable factual finding on relatedness (factor two), press the Board or tribunal to use that same scope when evaluating third‑party evidence and commercial impression. Conversely, if you seek to limit the scope under later factors, you must provide a reasoned basis for narrowing the relevant universe of marks or services.
- Assemble third‑party evidence that matches the scope you want the Board to acknowledge. If your theory depends on a broad marketplace, include registrations and use evidence across the relevant related services—not just identical labels.
- Anticipate the Board’s obligation, highlighted by M2/Bose, to explain how each DuPont factor was considered and to tie findings to record evidence. If the explanation is missing or inconsistent, the decision is vulnerable on appeal.

Conclusion

Apex Bank reaffirms and refines key strands of DuPont jurisprudence. It confirms that the Board’s findings of relatedness under factor two should inform the universe of comparison for factor six (crowded field/third‑party marks) and that changes in perceived mark strength feed back into the holistic commercial‑impression inquiry of factor one. Reading Apex alongside Coach, Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, Olde Tyme Foods, M2 Software, Bose, and I.AM.Symbolic yields a coherent framework: the Board must (1) evaluate relatedness with real‑world evidence, (2) treat third‑party use as contextually probative of weakness and scope, (3) compare marks in their entireties with attention to commercial impression, and (4) make clear, consistent factual findings that a court can review for substantial evidence. For law students and practitioners, the controlling strategy is to marshal evidence that aligns the scopes of these inquiries—so that once the Board accepts a broad or narrow definition of the marketplace, it must either apply that definition across relevant DuPont factors or explain why it does not.
