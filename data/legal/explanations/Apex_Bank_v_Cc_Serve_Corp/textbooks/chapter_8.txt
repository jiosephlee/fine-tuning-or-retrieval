Chapter 8: # Evidence in TTAB Proceedings: Building and Attacking the Record

Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) proceedings are in many respects contests of proof. Success or failure often turns on how well a party builds a record to support findings under the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion framework and how skillfully the opposing party undermines that same record. The Federal Circuit’s opinion in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 (Fed. Cir. No. 2023‑2143), provides a useful touchstone: the Board’s ultimate ruling turned as much on its evaluation of documentary evidence — what it considered and what it excluded — as on the legal tests. This chapter treats the types of evidence the TTAB will accept, the limits on their probative value, and the procedural tools practitioners should use (and not rely upon) while making and attacking the record. Practical steps and traps to avoid are emphasized throughout.

## Acceptable evidence: dictionary definitions, industry publications, websites, archived pages

The TTAB commonly relies on contemporaneous printed materials to define ordinary meanings and trade usage. Dictionary definitions, industry glossaries, and authoritative treatises are often the simplest way to establish the ordinary meaning or scope of a word in an identification of goods or services. In Apex, the Board consulted dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” when construing the parties’ respective identifications; that is a textbook example of how definitions help the Board determine whether services are similar for DuPont factor two.

Industry publications and trade press are particularly valuable when the dispute turns on how an industry uses a term or how services are marketed. Trade journals, banking or payments industry reports, and vendor materials can show that consumers or businesses treat two services as part of the same product ecosystem (for example, that banks and credit‑card program managers are commonly associated in the marketplace). Such evidence is often more persuasive than dictionaries because it shows usage in context rather than abstract meaning.

Websites and web pages are routinely used to show real‑world relationships among services, branding practice, and channels of trade. The TTAB accepts website printouts and online materials subject to proper authentication. Screenshots must be accompanied by either testimony or a declaration that establishes the page, the date it was viewed, and the source. The Wayback Machine and other internet archives are powerful tools for showing historical use because they preserve page content at a given date; courts and the TTAB have accepted Wayback captures when properly authenticated. Apex’s record included website evidence and third‑party material that the Board used to assess relatedness; the control and dating of webpages proved important.

Practical pointers:
- Whenever you rely on online material, capture and preserve the page with metadata (URL, capture date, and the archived image). File an affidavit or declaration of the person who captured the page describing when and how it was obtained and identifying the source.
- Use corroborating sources (e.g., press releases, trade publication coverage, or corporate filings) to confirm claims made on websites, which might be outdated or incomplete.
- Remember that stale or undated webpages are vulnerable to credibility challenges; always anchor web evidence to an authenticated date.

## Third‑party registrations and applications: probative value and limits

Third‑party registration evidence is one of the most frequently used tools to show conceptual or commercial weakness in a mark. The basic idea is that if many different parties are using or have registered variations of a term for similar goods or services, consumers are more likely to be adept at distinguishing among similar marks, and the claimant’s mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection. The Federal Circuit articulated these principles in Palm Bay and subsequent cases; the Board relied on third‑party registrations in Apex to support relatedness.

But there are three important limits to their probative value that practitioners must keep in mind:

1. Registrations do not prove marketplace use. A live federal registration establishes only that the registered party secured a federal registration; it does not prove actual use in commerce at relevant times. If the opponent wants to show that a third party was using a mark in commerce such that consumers were exposed to it, the registrant or other evidence of use is needed. Because of this, a pile of registrations without proof of use is less persuasive on commercial weakness than registrations coupled with specimens, advertising materials, or sales figures.

2. The relevance of a registration depends on the goods/services identified. Registrations are probative only insofar as their identifications overlap with the goods or services at issue. If a registration covers “financial consulting” and the case concerns “credit card services,” the registration is relevant only if the Board can reasonably deem those services similar or within the same channels of trade. The Apex decision underlines this point: the Board initially limited its review of third‑party marks to those explicitly identifying credit card services; the Federal Circuit found that limitation legally flawed because the Board had already found broader similarity between “banking/finance” and “credit card services.”

3. Quantity and quality matter. A handful of third‑party registrations for a conceptually similar mark is not the same as voluminous evidence of third‑party use and registration. Cases that find weak marks typically show many examples of active commercial use or registrations in the same field. The Board in Apex found only nine Aspire‑formative marks for credit card services and deemed that insufficient to show pervasive use; that quantitative judgment illustrates the distinction.

How to present third‑party registration evidence effectively:
- Use the USPTO’s TSDR records to show registration dates and current status; include screenshots of TSDR pages accompanied by a declaration authenticating the capture.
- Where possible, supplement registrations with evidence of commercial use—specimens, website captures showing the mark in use, sales or advertising materials, and press coverage.
- Organize registrations by timing and by the overlap of identifications with the parties’ services; show why each registration is relevant to the DuPont factor at issue.

## Proving marketplace use: screenshots, articles, consumer reviews, corporate filings

When the Board must evaluate commercial strength, channels of trade, or consumer exposure, proof of marketplace use is often decisive. Proof can take many forms, but each form has authentication and foundation requirements.

Screenshots and web captures. As noted above, screenshots must be authenticated. TTAB practice commonly uses:
- Declarations under oath by a custodian or by counsel’s client who retrieved the pages, stating the URL, the date retrieved, and how the pages were captured;
- Wayback or other archived captures showing the page content on specific dates, authenticated with a declaration from the person who captured the archival image; and
- Records of domain registration or hosting that corroborate the timing and ownership of the page.

Trade and news articles. Trade press and newspaper coverage can demonstrate the marketplace presence and consumer awareness of a brand. Such articles should be filed as printed publications under notice of reliance or introduced through testimony (so as to permit cross‑examination). The more prominent the source and the closer the date to the priority window, the stronger the evidence of consumer exposure.

Consumer reviews and social media. Reviews and social media posts can be persuasive on consumer recognition and reputation, but they are highly fact‑specific and readily challenged on authenticity and representativeness. Use them in combination with other evidence and authenticate through declarations describing the method of capture and the provenance of user posts.

Corporate filings and business registrations. SEC filings, state corporate filings, and official business registrations can show the identity of a mark owner, the dates of business operations, and the nature of goods or services sold. Financial statements, investor presentations, and sales reports (when available) provide especially persuasive proof of commercial activity and consumer reach. For private companies, affidavits from corporate officers with supporting documents can serve the same function.

Practice points on authenticity and admissibility:
- Use notice of reliance (37 C.F.R. §2.122(d)) for printed publications, third‑party registrations, and official records when permissible; when relying on documents through notice of reliance, include a concise explanation of the source and date.
- When possible, present critical marketplace evidence through witness testimony (declaration or deposition) to avoid hearsay challenges and to permit cross‑examination.
- Build redundancy: corroborate website claims with press coverage, industry reports, and third‑party citations to reduce vulnerability to credibility attacks.

## Letters of protest during examination: purpose and effect

Letters of protest are a tool available during ex parte examination; they allow third parties to bring evidence of confusing similarity, lack of use, or other issues to an examining attorney’s attention before publication or registration. The TTAB and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office often receive such letters, and they can be influential in prosecution. In Apex, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest during prosecution, but the examining attorney nevertheless allowed Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks to publish for opposition. That sequence illustrates several points about the role and limits of letters of protest.

Purpose. A letter of protest permits a third party to present documentary evidence to the examining attorney (for example, proof of priority, third‑party registrations, or evidence of public confusion) so that the examiner can consider the matter in the initial examination. Letters of protest can be cost‑effective to bring issues to the agency’s attention early in the process.

Limits. Letters of protest do not adjudicate rights between private parties. They are not a substitute for an opposition or cancellation action. An examining attorney may consider and act upon the evidence in the protest, but the PTO is under no obligation to grant the protest, and the letter does not create a record before the TTAB in an inter partes proceeding unless it is introduced there. Apex shows that an applicant can publish a mark despite a protest and still face a successful opposition later on.

Practical advice:
- Use letters of protest to preserve resources and to flag problems during prosecution, but be prepared to file an opposition if the application proceeds to publication.
- When drafting a protest, include concise, well‑documented, contemporaneous evidence (TSDR records, screenshots, press coverage) and explain why the evidence is relevant to registrability.
- If the protest produces no action and the application publishes, promptly evaluate the need for an opposition and consider preserving the protest materials for use in that later proceeding.

## Intent‑to‑use applications and their implications for scope and timing

Intent‑to‑use (ITU) applications under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act are common for businesses that want to secure priority for a mark they intend to use. ITU filings, however, carry special implications for TTAB proceedings that every practitioner must understand.

Priority and constructive use. An ITU filing alone does not equate to actual use in commerce; registration is not issued on an ITU application until the applicant files a verified statement of use (SOU) or obtains an extension and proves use. That means an ITU applicant does not automatically have the same rights as a registrant who has used the mark. In oppositions under Section 2(d), priority is typically established by either earlier use in commerce or an earlier registration. A registered prior user (like CC Serve in Apex, whose ASPIRE registration dated to 1998) holds established rights that an ITU applicant cannot defeat simply by having filed an ITU application later. Thus, ITU filings can be attacked on the ground that the applicant does not yet have use or priority.

Constructive nationwide priority upon registration. If the ITU applicant ultimately proves use and obtains registration, the applicant may enjoy constructive nationwide priority as of the registration date (or in certain circumstances the filing date). But this valuable protection only arises with successful prosecution to registration. During prosecution and pending opposition, the applicant’s rights remain limited.

Implications for evidence. When opposing an ITU application, the opponent should emphasize its earlier priority (registration or use) and present evidence that the applied‑for services are related to the opponent’s services under DuPont factor two. The ITU applicant must be prepared to show that it is working toward bona fide use and to marshal evidence of intent and subsequent use when required. In Apex, Apex Bank’s ITU application did not insulate it from opposition by CC Serve — the Board evaluated relatedness and likelihood of confusion on the merits while Apex still lacked registration.

Strategic considerations:
- If you represent an ITU applicant, start collecting use evidence early (specimens, website captures, advertising) so that you can establish use if needed and to rebut assertions of relatedness and likely confusion.
- Opponents should exploit the temporal gap between ITU filing and actual use to press the priority and relatedness arguments based on established registrations and marketplace presence.

## Trademark ID Manual: role in construing identifications; effect of entry deletions

The Trademark ID Manual (ID Manual) is the PTO’s list of pre‑approved identifications of goods and services that an examining attorney can accept without further parsing. The ID Manual streamlines prosecution because it offers standard language that the Office treats as acceptable. But when an entry is deleted from the ID Manual or when an examining attorney must construe an identification that is not a precise ID Manual entry, evidence becomes necessary to define the scope of the goods or services.

Role in TTAB analysis. When interpreting an application or registration, the Board gives weight to the ID wording. If the parties’ identifications align with ID Manual entries, the Board will often rely on the ordinary meaning articulated therein. Apex shows the flip side: the entry for “credit card services” had been deleted from the ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, and the Board needed to define what that phrase encompassed. The Board did so by consulting dictionaries and industry evidence, concluding that the phrase included issuing cards, account management, and related activities. That conclusion then shaped the DuPont analysis.

Effect of entry deletions. When the PTO deletes an entry, the absence does not leave the identifications undefined; it simply requires examiners and the TTAB to construe the terms from other evidence — dictionaries, industry sources, registrations, and marketplace materials. Practitioners should be proactive: when a deletion affects a contested identification, submit evidence and argument defining the ordinary understanding of the term and mapping how services overlap.

Practical rules:
- Don’t assume an ID Manual entry will be dispositive; be ready to defend or clarify the meaning of an identification with contemporary sources.
- If you sign a consent or file an amendment to the identification, be very careful to preserve the scope you intend; later litigation may hinge on fine differences in phraseology.

## Best practices: organizing evidence by factor and ensuring completeness of the TTAB record

The DuPont framework is the organizing principle for the TTAB’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis, and a successful evidentiary strategy maps evidence to the specific DuPont factors. Two themes run through best practice advice: (1) organize evidence so the Board can easily connect it to the factor it is intended to prove, and (2) eliminate weaknesses in your evidentiary foundation before they become substantive problems.

Organize evidence by DuPont factor. Create an evidentiary binder (electronic or printed) with tabs for each relevant DuPont factor. Under factor two (relatedness of goods/services), include third‑party registrations that show services offered under similar marks, website captures showing co‑marketing of services, and industry treatises. For factor six (third‑party use/number of similar marks), compile registrations and, crucially, proof of commercial use for each registered mark. For factor one (similarity of marks), include specimens showing how the mark appears in commerce, advertising, and trade dress information. Apex demonstrates why consistent scope across factors is important: the Board’s separate treatment of similarity for the sixth factor — applying a narrower conception than it used for factor two — was reversible error.

Authenticate aggressively. Anticipate authentication challenges to web evidence and archival captures. File affidavits or declarations that explain the capture process, identify custodians of records, and confirm dates. When you rely on third‑party registrations, provide TSDR screenshots showing current status and registration dates. When relying on business filings, use certified copies when possible.

Use testimony strategically. Where critical facts hinge on credibility or detailed explanation (e.g., the scope of an applicant’s marketing program, sales volumes, or the sophistication of purchasers), present witness testimony either through declaration or live/cross‑examinable deposition. Testimony permits cross‑examination, which the Board values.

Avoid surprises; build the record during prosecution or early in the opposition. Letters of protest can be useful but are no substitute for producing your evidence in a TTAB proceeding. Use discovery to obtain specimens of use, advertising spend data, and other materials that reveal the breadth of exposure.

Create a timeline and priority chart. For every opposition, produce a timeline that ties the filing and registration dates to marketplace evidence and shows which uses predated which filing dates. In Apex, the timing of CC Serve’s 1996 priority and Apex’s 2019 ITU filing is a central organizing fact; an opposite strategy would be useless without dates.

Checklist of items to include in an opposition or defense file:
- TSDR records for all cited registrations and applications; screenshots and printed copies with source metadata.
- Website captures (live and archived), with declarations authenticating capture dates and methods.
- Trade press coverage, industry reports, and dictionaries establishing meanings and use.
- Specimens of use, advertising samples, and sales/marketing data to show commercial strength, channels of trade, and consumer exposure.
- Declarations from corporate officers, marketing officers, or third‑party witnesses addressing use, channels of trade, and target customers.
- Consumer survey evidence when appropriate, methodologically sound and submitted with expert witness support.
- A clear index tying each piece of evidence to the specific DuPont factor(s) it is meant to support.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a reminder that the TTAB’s determinations often turn on the texture of documentary proof and the scope of the record. Practitioners who anticipate the Board’s need to define identifications, who authenticate and date web and archival materials, and who understand the probative limits of registrations and protests will be far better positioned to win. Build the record with DuPont in mind, authenticate aggressively, and use the PTO’s procedural tools (not as shortcuts but as complements) to secure the evidence you need for trial.
