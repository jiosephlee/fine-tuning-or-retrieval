Chapter 4: # Relatedness of Goods and Services (DuPont Factor 2): Broad Identifications and ‘Legal Identity in Part’

One of the most frequently dispositive inquiries in a Section 2(d) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis is whether the applicant’s goods or services are the same as, or related to, the registrant’s goods or services. That inquiry is embodied in the second DuPont factor: “the similarity of the goods or services as described in an application or registration.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and the Federal Circuit approach this question with a set of working rules that emphasize the text of the identifications in the pending application and the registration(s) at issue, not the idiosyncrasies of a party’s actual current business. The governing idea is practical: the public evaluates source using the goods and services listed on the face of the application or registration, and registration confers rights based on what is claimed, not on what is currently sold. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. provides a useful, recent illustration of these principles in action.

This chapter explains the doctrine that governs DuPont factor two, emphasizing (1) why identifications—rather than actual current use—control in TTAB cases; (2) how broadly worded identifications are interpreted; (3) the presumptions about channels of trade and classes of consumers that follow from unrestricted identifications; (4) the use of dictionary and industry evidence to construe service descriptions; (5) the probative role of third‑party registrations; (6) what courts mean by services being “legally identical, in part,” and how that conclusion affects the weight given to factor two; and (7) the particular application of these points in Apex Bank v. CC Serve.

## Why identifications, not actual current use, control in TTAB cases

In opposition and cancellation proceedings before the TTAB, the primary evidentiary focus is the identifications in the filed application and in the cited registration. The Board “looks to the goods and/or services as they are identified in the application and registration,” and applies a legal, not strictly factual, inquiry into whether those identifications describe the same or related goods/services. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1381–82 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (explaining that likelihood of confusion is a legal determination grounded in factual findings under the DuPont factors). The rule rests on two pragmatic premises. First, an applicant or registrant may claim rights in any goods or services encompassed by the words of the identification, so the Board must treat the listing as its outer bounds. Second, the scope of consumer perception and marketplace overlap is often determined by descriptive language rather than by a registrant’s particular sales or marketing history; registration gives constructive notice of the goods or services described.

Because of these premises, the Board routinely compares the plain language of the identifications. If the enrolled language is broad enough to encompass the same goods or services as the cited registration, the Board can find those goods/services “legally identical” even where the parties’ actual operations do not overlap. That legal identity often proves decisive: when identifications are identical, the relatedness inquiry is resolved in the registrant’s favor without need for extrinsic evidence that the goods/services are used together or marketed through the same channels. Courts and the TTAB have repeatedly upheld this rule: identical or encompassing identifications establish identity as a matter of law, and do so whether or not the parties themselves are contemporaneously competing in the marketplace.

Nonetheless, the Board is not blind to actual use. If a party submits evidence showing that the goods or services, as actually provided, are markedly different or occupy distinct channels/consumers, that evidence can rebut the presumption that broad identifications create overlap. But such evidence must be in the record; absent it, identifications control.

## Interpreting broadly worded identifications (e.g., “banking and financing services”)

Broad identifications are common in trademark applications. Words such as “banking,” “financial services,” “software,” or “consulting” may encompass wide swaths of commercial activity. Interpreting these phrases begins with their ordinary and industry meanings; the TTAB and the Federal Circuit construe them in light of dictionary definitions, industry practice, and the built‑in legal principle that a party owns rights in the full scope of the written identification.

A broad phrase can sweep in services that might, in a narrower factual sense, appear distinct. For example, “banking and financing services” can, depending on ordinary usage, include credit card issuance and account management, consumer loans, deposit accounts, payment processing, and similar services that fall within the ordinary ambit of a bank’s activity. If a registration identifies “banking and financing services” and an applicant identifies “credit card services,” the identifications may overlap because credit card services can be a species of financial services. The overlap need not be complete; it suffices that the identifications encompass some identical or closely related services.

Interpreting the phrase requires ordinary‑meaning tools. Courts often turn to dictionaries to define contested words; they also consider industry evidence—trade publications, websites, or statutory/regulatory definitions—when those materials provide specialized meanings. The central question is what an ordinary consumer or buyer (or, where relevant, a person in the trade) would understand the terms to include. This interpretive step is crucial because subsequent DuPont factors—channels of trade, classes of consumers, third‑party usage—depend on the scope established here.

## Channels‑of‑trade and classes‑of‑consumers presumptions for unrestricted identifications

When identifications are unrestricted—that is, they include no limitations as to trade channels, purchasers, or use—the Board presumes that the goods or services travel in all normal channels of trade for those services and will be purchased by all normal classes of consumers for those services. This presumption is powerful. It follows directly from the TTAB’s primary reliance on the identifications: if an identification covers all banking services, then the Board will assume the applicant’s and registrant’s services could be offered through the same channels and to the same consumers, absent evidence to the contrary.

The practical consequence: an applicant cannot defeat a finding of relatedness by asserting, without proof, that its services are offered only online or only to a niche class of sophisticated commercial buyers. To overcome the statutory presumption, the applicant must introduce evidence that the actual channels of trade or consumer classes are distinct—for example, contracts showing a registrant sells only to large institutions, customer lists, targeted advertising demonstrating nonoverlap, or testimony about product distribution. In TTAB practice, a litigant who wishes to avoid the presumption should narrow the identification at the application stage or be prepared to produce evidence that shows the intended narrower scope of use.

## Use of dictionary definitions and industry evidence to construe service descriptions

Because identifications are textual, the TTAB and reviewing courts often resort to extrinsic sources to construe them. Dictionaries supply readily accessible definitions for ordinary words (e.g., “banking,” “financial”), while industry sources illuminate specialized uses. The Apex Bank v. CC Serve opinion exemplifies this approach: the Board found that the phrase “credit card services” included activities such as issuing credit cards, processing payments, managing accounts, and providing credit monitoring and counseling. When the ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been deleted, the Board relied on dictionary definitions and other evidence to determine what the service term encompassed.

In practice, litigants should submit dictionaries, statutory definitions, regulatory materials, trade glossaries, and industry websites to anchor the plain‑meaning interpretation. Courts will credit authoritative industry materials showing common usage; conversely, where a term has a specialized meaning in a trade, the Board will treat that usage as probative. The parties’ own admissions and testimony about the services at issue are also relevant. Ultimately, the construal should be anchored in how a reasonable consumer or buyer would understand the language of the identification.

## Third‑party registrations as evidence that goods or services may emanate from a single source

Third‑party registrations and evidence of third‑party use play two related roles in the DuPont analysis. First, they supply evidence that different entities have used similar marks on the same or similar goods/services—showing that multiple producers can provide those services under similar branding. The Board treats this as evidence that consumers are accustomed to different sources offering similar services under similar marks, which tends to show the cited mark is relatively weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005).

Second, third‑party registrations support the relatedness analysis by demonstrating marketplace overlap: if many firms have registered the same mark for both credit card services and other banking services, that suggests the services are often provided together by a single source. The Board in Apex considered third‑party registrations covering both “credit card” and broader “banking and financing” services to support the conclusion that the two types of services may emanate from a single source.

Caveats are important. Third‑party registrations are not conclusive proof; they are evidence of market possibilities, not definitive proof of consumer perception. Courts look at both the number and quality of third‑party uses. A handful of isolated registrations may not be persuasive; a “considerable number” or “voluminous” showing—coupled with evidence of actual marketplace use—carries more weight. Cases like Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters., LLC, 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015), and Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2015), illustrate how courts consider the aggregate force of such evidence in assessing a mark’s relative weakness and the likelihood of confusion.

## The concept of “legally identical, in part” and its effect on weighting Factor 2

The phrases “identical,” “legally identical,” and related formulations have precise practical meaning in TTAB and Federal Circuit jurisprudence. Goods or services are “identical” when the identifications in the application and the registration literally cover the same terms. They are “legally identical” when, although not phrased identically, one identification encompasses the other; for example, “banking and financing services” may encompass “credit card services,” making some of the services legally identical even if other parts of the identification are not.

When the Board finds that services are legally identical, or legally identical in part, that finding tends to carry substantial weight in favor of confusion. The reason is straightforward: identical identifications eliminate the need for extensive proof of relatedness—consumers who encounter both marks associated with the same type of services can reasonably be confused about source. The Apex opinion used the language “legally identical, in part” to describe the relationship between CC Serve’s “credit card services” registration and Apex’s “banking and financing services” identification: issuing and managing credit cards fall within the ordinary scope of banking and financing.

But “legally identical in part” does not always dictate the outcome. The Board must still consider other DuPont factors—such as the similarity of the marks (factor one), the channels of trade and classes of consumers (factor four), fame or strength of the mark (factor three), and the presence of numerous similar marks in the market (factor six). An applicant can, by competent evidence, counter the implications of legal identity—for example, showing that in practice the registrant’s “credit card services” are provided only through co‑branding relationships with large institutions, or that the services target a different class of consumers—thus removing or attenuating the realistic risk of confusion.

In short: a finding that services are “legally identical, in part” gives factor two substantial force, but it is one piece of the multi‑factor DuPont mosaic. The Board must maintain internal consistency across factors: if it determines the services are broad and overlapping for factor two, it should use the same scope when evaluating third‑party evidence and the commercial strength of the marks in factor six.

## Application in Apex Bank v. CC Serve: credit card services within banking and financing

Apex Bank v. CC Serve illustrates the operation and limits of these rules. CC Serve owned a 1998 registration for ASPIRE in connection with “credit card services.” Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB concluded, and the Federal Circuit affirmed as to factor two, that the parties’ services were highly similar. How did the Board and court reach that result?

First, the Board construed the identifications. It determined—using dictionary definitions and an examination of the plain meaning of the terms—that “credit card services” reasonably encompassed issuing and managing credit cards, processing payments, account management, credit authorization, and related services. It also determined that “banking” and “finance” ordinarily include extending credit and providing funds, which implicates credit card issuance and account servicing. Because the wording of Apex’s identification could be read to include credit card services, the services overlapped; hence, the Board characterized them as “legally identical, in part,” and concluded that factor two weighed heavily in favor of confusion. The Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supported that conclusion and affirmed the Board’s factor‑two analysis.

Second, the TTAB relied on third‑party registrations that covered both credit card services and broader banking/financial services to support the proposition that the two types of services often emanate from a single source. The Board cited examples showing that other firms use Aspire‑formative marks on both types of services—evidence that undercut the inference that the services are distinct in the marketplace.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factor‑two finding, but it also identified a logical inconsistency in the Board’s subsequent analysis of factor six. After determining that the services were highly similar (and thus broadening the class of consumers and channels under consideration), the Board later narrowed its universe of third‑party Aspire marks for factor six to only those identified for “credit card services,” excluding those covering other banking/finance services. The court held that this inconsistency was legal error: the scope used to identify similar third‑party marks should be consistent with the earlier finding of service similarity. Accordingly, the Federal Circuit vacated and remanded the Board’s factor‑six analysis (and, because the strength of CC Serve’s mark and the overall commercial impression of the marks may be affected by factor six, it remanded factor one as well), while affirming factor two.

The lesson from Apex is twofold. First, construing identifications and deciding whether they encompass each other is a textual and evidentiary task the Board and courts will resolve based on the plain meaning of the terms, aided by dictionaries and industry materials; a finding that two identifications are legally identical in part is supported by evidence that a named service (like credit card issuance) falls within the normal ambit of a broader category (like banking). Second, once the Board determines the services overlap broadly, it must apply that same scope when it measures the number and nature of similar marks in use (factor six) and when it assesses the marks’ commercial strength and overall commercial impression (factor one). Inconsistency among those analyses will invite vacatur and remand.

## Practical framework for applying Factor 2 (and linked factors) to new problems

When you confront a new fact pattern involving alleged relatedness of goods or services, proceed in this order:

1. Identify the operative wording. Start with the application’s and registration’s identifications. If either is broad and unrestricted, the default presumptions about channels and consumers apply.

2. Construe the terms. Use dictionaries, statutory/regulatory definitions, trade resources, and the parties’ evidence to determine what the terms would reasonably encompass to an ordinary consumer or a person in the trade.

3. Determine overlap or legal identity. Ask whether one identification literally matches the other, and if not, whether one identification reasonably includes the other (legal identity), or whether the two cover related but distinct services.

4. Apply the presumption of shared channels and consumers if identifications are unrestricted. Seek evidence to rebut the presumption if you want to argue for distinct channels/consumers.

5. Produce third‑party evidence. Collect registrations and (preferably) evidence of third‑party use showing that similar marks are used on the same or related services. Evaluate whether the quantum and quality of third‑party use suggest the cited mark is relatively weak.

6. Maintain consistent scope across DuPont analyses. If you find broad similarity for factor two, use the same service scope when evaluating third‑party evidence under factor six and when assessing the marks’ commercial impressions under factor one.

7. Remember interaction with other factors. Factor two is important but not dispositive. Even when services are legally identical, the Board must weigh other factors—especially the similarity of the marks, the strength of the senior mark, channels and consumers, and any evidence of actual confusion or lack thereof—to reach the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion.

## Concluding observations

The TTAB’s emphasis on identifications in the DuPont analysis is a doctrinal and procedural reality. Applicants and counsel should draft identifications with care: broad wording invites expansive presumptions that can prove fatal in oppositions. Conversely, owners of earlier registrations should be mindful that even narrow service terms can be subsumed within broader descriptions, and that third‑party evidence may narrow the scope of protection. Apex Bank v. CC Serve demonstrates both the power of textual construction (the affirmation of factor two) and the necessity of analytical consistency across factors (the vacation of factor six and factor one where the Board tightened its universe of comparison after finding broad similarity). Mastery of these principles enables better fact development and clearer strategic choices in trademark litigation before the TTAB and on appeal.
