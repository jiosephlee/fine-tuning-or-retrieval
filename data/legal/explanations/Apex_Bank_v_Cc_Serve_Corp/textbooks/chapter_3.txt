Chapter 3: # The DuPont Framework: A Flexible, Factor-Based Test

The Supreme Court of the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals framed the now-familiar multi-factor inquiry that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and the courts apply to disputes over likelihood of confusion. That decision, commonly referred to as DuPont, did not create a checklist to be applied mechanically; rather it supplied a set of factual considerations that permit a holistic, context-sensitive judgment about whether consumers are likely to be confused as to source. The Board and the courts restate this principle constantly: likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion drawn from underlying factual findings—findings that must be supported by substantial evidence on appeal. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., decided in 2025, illustrates many of the practical and doctrinal contours of the DuPont framework: it confirms that not all factors are relevant in every case, that factual findings in one factor typically inform other factors, and that an inconsistent or artificially narrow treatment of related factors can require remand.

This chapter surveys the DuPont factors, explains their purposes, discusses how tribunals weigh and interrelate the factors, and gives practical guidance for litigating and deciding trademark opposition and cancellation proceedings, with particular attention to financial-services disputes. Apex Bank serves throughout as an illustrative vehicle because the opinion grapples with relatedness, third-party evidence of similar marks, commercial strength, and the proper scope of analysis when services overlap.

## Overview of the DuPont factors and their purposes

DuPont enumerated a suite of considerations—thirteen in the original articulation—that guide the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry. The list is descriptive rather than exhaustive; the Board need not mention every factor, and some will be irrelevant in a given dispute. The factors are best understood as different lenses through which to view the core question: would an ordinary consumer viewing these marks and the goods or services involved be likely to conclude they come from the same source?

A concise articulation of the DuPont factors includes: (1) the similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression; (2) the relatedness of the goods or services and the circumstances of their marketing; (3) the established, likely-to-continue nature of any trade channels; (4) the degree and conditions of buyer care; (5) the fame or strength of the prior mark; (6) the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods (third-party use); (7) the nature and extent of any actual confusion; (8) the length and exclusivity of concurrent use without evidence of confusion; (9) the variety of goods on which the mark is used (i.e., breadth of product line); (10) the market interface—whether the mark is used on goods that a single entity would commonly produce or provide; (11) the extent of potential bridging of product lines; (12) the defendant’s intent in adopting the mark; and (13) any other established probative facts (a catchall).

Each factor serves a purpose. The first factor probes whether the marks themselves create the risk of misattribution of source. The second factor, perhaps the most frequently dispositive, measures the marketplace proximity of the parties’ offerings—identical goods or services or those marketed through the same channels increase confusion risk. The fifth and sixth factors explore source-identifying strength: a famous or conceptually strong mark enjoys broader protection, whereas a crowded field of similar marks suggests that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks and that a mark is relatively weak. Actual confusion, when proved, offers strong contemporaneous evidence that consumers are confused in the marketplace. Intent and the conditions of purchasing (impulse vs. careful decision) likewise inform the likelihood that similarity will translate into confusion. Viewed together, these factors help a tribunal shape a calibrated conclusion about confusion risk.

It is critical to remember that DuPont is flexible: courts and the TTAB have consistently said that not every factor is relevant in every case, and a tribunal need only address those with evidentiary support. In the Apex Bank case, the Board focused on the relatedness of services (factor two), the number and nature of similar marks (factor six), and the similarity of the marks (factor one), among other considerations; the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s assessment of relatedness but remanded for a coherent treatment of factor six and, because of interdependence, factor one.

## Weighting and interdependence among factors

A central feature of the DuPont framework is that factors are neither numerically nor hierarchically fixed. No factor is inherently dispositive in every case. Instead, the weight assigned to any factor depends on the case’s context and the evidentiary record. Courts avoid a mechanical “point count” in favor of a qualitative balancing. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that some factors commonly carry more weight—relatedness of goods and similarities in channels of trade and consumers often prove determinative—but the tribunal’s role is to evaluate how the particular mix of evidence maps onto the statutory concern: source confusion.

Interdependence among factors is both inevitable and important. A factual finding under one factor will often shape the analytic frame for another. For example, a determination that the parties’ services are identical or highly similar (factor two) should inform the scope of the relevant consumer class, channels of trade, and even the universe of third‑party marks that are pertinent under factor six. Apex Bank v. CC Serve exemplifies this principle. There, the Board concluded in its second-factor analysis that the parties’ services—credit card services and banking and financing services—were highly similar and in part legally identical. Once that finding was made, the universe of “similar goods and services” for purposes of evaluating third-party marks (factor six) necessarily expanded to include related banking and financing services, not only marks connected specifically to credit card services. The Board’s later decision to limit factor-six consideration to marks for credit card services thereby created an analytical inconsistency; the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s factor-six finding and remanded.

This interdependence means that a tribunal’s sequencing and framing of findings matters. Early, clear findings about the scope of goods or services and about the relevant consumer group should shape the other inquiries. Further, the conceptual strength or commercial fame found under factor five will influence how the tribunal treats third‑party uses under factor six and how it interprets the marks’ overall commercial impressions under factor one. Likewise, a showing of actual confusion may dwarf weaker evidence elsewhere. Judges and practitioners must therefore resist the temptation to treat factors in isolation; instead the tribunal should articulate how specific factual findings interact and how those interactions inform the ultimate legal conclusion.

## What qualifies as a relevant factor on the record

DuPont explicitly allows the Board to consider only the factors that are both relevant and supported by the record. Relevance, in turn, depends on the nature of the parties’ marks and the available evidence. A factor that turns on an evidentiary showing—such as actual confusion, length of concurrent use, or consumer sophistication—will be irrelevant unless the party offers admissible evidence on the point. This limitation preserves the evidentiary underpinnings of administrative decision-making and avoids speculative findings.

Practical sources of admissible evidence include: the pleaded registrations and their identifications of goods or services; third‑party registrations; declarations or testimony about marketplace practices; third‑party advertisements and websites showing use in commerce; consumer surveys; evidence of actual confusion (emails, declarations, mistaken transactions, social-media posts); documents showing marketing and distribution channels; and proof of fame (sales figures, advertising expenditures, press coverage). The board may also draw rational inferences from the record—for example, treating statutory identifications as sufficient to deem goods identical—but courts will set aside inferences not supported by substantial evidence.

The Apex Bank opinion teaches a clear lesson: the Board must be careful that its evidentiary boundaries are consistent. After finding high similarity between services, it was not permissible to limit factor-six consideration to a narrower slate of third‑party marks without explaining why those other marks were irrelevant despite their links to banking and financing services. If an applicant wants the Board to treat a factor as irrelevant, it must show that no admissible evidence bears on that factor. Conversely, opponents should marshal evidence on factors likely to be dispositive—relatedness, channels of trade, third‑party uses, and consumer sophistication are often fruitful hunting grounds.

## Commonly dispositive factors in TTAB practice

Although DuPont rejects categorical rankings, TTAB practice exhibits recurring patterns. The two most frequently decisive factors are the similarity of the marks (factor one) and the relatedness of the goods or services (factor two). If the marks are nearly identical and the goods or services are the same or closely related, likelihood of confusion often follows. Other factors that frequently tip the balance include the channels of trade and classes of consumers (under factors two and four), the strength or fame of the prior mark (factor five), and evidence of actual confusion (factor seven).

Strength (factor five) often acts as a force multiplier: a strong, distinctive mark commands a broad scope of protection. Conversely, a registrant that can show numerous third‑party uses of similar marks in the same commercial space (factor six) can persuade the Board to narrow protection. Evidence of a crowded field tends to cut against a finding that an applicant’s mark should be barred. Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, and Palm Bay exemplify cases in which third‑party use and registrations influenced the scope of protection. In Apex Bank, the Board and the Federal Circuit turned squarely to factor six because a number of third‑party “Aspire” marks existed in the financial-services universe; the disagreement centered on whether the Board had properly defined the relevant universe of similar marks.

Actual confusion, while powerful, is often difficult to prove because the period between adoption and confusion tends to be short in many cases. Still, where evidence of actual confusion is reliable and contemporaneous, it can be decisive because it demonstrates what consumers are actually doing, not what legal inferences predict. Purchaser sophistication and purchasing conditions also frequently matter: expensive, considered purchases reduce the likelihood of confusion, whereas inexpensive or impulse purchases increase it. In the financial-services context, courts examine the degree of consumer care with attention to whether customers make deliberate choices (opening accounts, choosing financial-service providers) or rely on brand shortcuts.

## How courts articulate and balance factors in financial services disputes

Financial services present distinctive analytical challenges. Banks, credit-card processors, program managers, and other financial-service providers routinely collaborate, license, or co-brand. Those partnerships mean that services can be marketed under the name of one company yet managed by another, and that any one financial institution may offer a wide variety of services under different marks. This marketplace reality often blurs the line between bona fide competition and co-promotion or co-branding, making the relatedness inquiry (factor two) particularly salient.

In Apex Bank, CC Serve owned a federal registration for ASPIRE for “credit card services” and in practice partnered with banks to issue ASPIRE-branded cards; Apex Bank sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking services. The Board—correctly, in the Federal Circuit’s view—recognized that customary dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations could show that “banking” and “credit card services” overlap. Even when a party operates as a program manager (a back‑office provider) rather than a depository bank, its services may still be related to “banking and financing services” as a practical matter because issuing and servicing credit cards is one way of extending consumer credit. Thus, identified services and real-world relationships between entities are both relevant.

Courts in financial services disputes also pay close attention to third‑party registrations and uses because the market is often crowded with similar marks. If many entities in the financial-services space use the same or similar formative terms—Aspire, Premier, Trust, etc.—that evidence can show the public distinguishes among such marks and that a given word may be conceptually weak in that sector. In turn, a finding that a mark is commercially weak will narrow its scope of protection and lessen confusion risk. Apex Bank underscores that when the Board finds the parties’ services are highly similar, the Board must treat third‑party marks across the related spectrum as relevant for factor-six analysis; narrowing the consideration to a subset (e.g., only credit-card-marketing marks) is inconsistent if the broader services are deemed similar.

Finally, financial services often involve sophisticated purchasers—business customers choosing payment processors or consumers opening bank accounts after researching rates and fees. But sophistication is not automatic; the Board must consider exactly which consumers are likely to encounter the marks. Apex demonstrated that the Board must coherently define the relevant consumer, typically shaped by the services’ identifications, marketing channels, and prices. If credit-card products are marketed broadly to average consumers, the Board cannot assume a high degree of care simply because “banking” can involve complex products. Thus, careful factual findings about purchaser sophistication are essential.

## Practical sequencing of factor analysis

Because of interdependence among DuPont factors, an effective structure for analysis benefits both decisionmakers and advocates. A recommended, practice‑oriented sequence proceeds from the most framing issues to the more derivative ones:

Begin by defining the marks themselves and the identifications of goods or services in the registrations or applications. This exercise frames the rest of the inquiry. The Board frequently relies on the statutory identification to determine goods/services identity or relatedness; where the identification is broad, the Board will treat the services as encompassing all ordinary channels of trade unless the record shows otherwise.

Next, resolve relatedness of goods or services (factor two) and define the relevant consumer and channels of trade. These findings are foundational: they set the scale for what “similar” means elsewhere. If the services are identical or overlap, then third‑party marks used on any of those services become relevant for factor six; if services are unrelated, the universe of third‑party evidence is narrower.

With knowledge of the relevant consumer and trade channels, assess the similarity of the marks in their entirety (factor one). Consider appearance, sound, connotation and the commercial impression the marks create for the defined consumer. Because commercial impression is shaped by how consumers encounter the marks, this analysis should incorporate the relatedness and trade-channel determinations.

Having framed the marks, evaluate mark strength and third‑party use (factors five and six). Determine whether the prior mark is conceptually or commercially strong; analyze the quantity and nature of third‑party uses in the identified goods or services. Make sure the scope of third‑party considerations aligns with the services the tribunal has already found similar—Apex Bank demonstrates the error of narrowing the factor-six universe after determining service overlap.

Then examine consumer care (factor four) and any evidence of actual confusion, length of use, and defendants’ intent (factors seven, eight, and twelve). These factors often fine-tune the result; a showing of actual confusion is weighty, but lack of confusion during a lengthy period of concurrent use can cut the other way.

Finally, synthesize: explain how the factual findings cohere and why they lead to a legal conclusion about likelihood of confusion. Avoid rote counting of factors; instead articulate which factual findings are decisive and how lesser findings support the ultimate legal judgment.

A concrete illustration: in a remand following Apex Bank, the Board must first explain precisely why “banking and financing services” and “credit card services” are highly similar—e.g., by citing dictionary definitions, the parties’ own practices, and third‑party registrations. It must then identify the relevant consumer and trade channels (are these services marketed to retail consumers, to businesses, or both?). With those boundaries set, the Board should reexamine third‑party uses of “Aspire” across banking and financial-service offerings broadly, not only those limited to credit cards. That broader review will inform whether ASPIRE is commercially weak and thus entitled only to a narrow scope of protection; that re-assessment in turn affects the marks’ commercial impressions under factor one. The Board should articulate these connections in a manner that allows meaningful appellate review and that respects the Federal Circuit’s instruction to maintain consistent scope across factors.

## Closing guidance for litigators and adjudicators

Two practical points follow from the DuPont regime and from Apex Bank. First, litigants must shape their evidentiary strategy to the likely dispositive factors. When services are similar, the battle often revolves around strength and third‑party use; parties should therefore marshal persuasive market evidence—third‑party use exhibits, competitor registrations, advertising spend, and consumer surveys. When goods are dissimilar, focus on channels of trade, purchaser sophistication, and the marks’ visual and aural differences. Evidence of actual confusion is valuable but needs to be carefully authenticated and contemporaneous to be persuasive.

Second, adjudicators should structure their opinions to reveal the factual scaffolding that supports legal conclusions. Because DuPont invites context-dependent balancing, decisionmakers must explain not only what they concluded on each factor but why the findings cohere. Apex Bank teaches that when a tribunal finds strong similarity between goods or services, it must not retreat into a narrower conception when reviewing other factors. Coherence and transparency in sequencing, scope, and evidentiary reasoning are essential to both sound decisionmaking and intelligible appellate review.

The DuPont framework is not formulaic. It is a tool for translating factual patterns into reasoned legal conclusions about consumer perception. Apex Bank highlights the flexibility and the constraints of that tool: factors can and should be weighted according to context, but factual findings must be internally consistent across the analysis. Mastery of DuPont requires both careful evidentiary preparation and disciplined, holistic reasoning.
