Chapter 1: # Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: Foundations of Likelihood of Confusion

Section 2(d) is the statutory axis around which denial of federal trademark registrations turns when a proposed mark so resembles a previously used or registered mark that confusion among consumers is likely. The administrative process for testing registrability—the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) proceedings—borrows much of its analytic architecture from infringement doctrine, but its purpose, remedies, and evidentiary rules differ in important respects from a civil suit for infringement. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., No. 2023-2143 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025), supplies a useful vehicle for exploring how Section 2(d) operates in practice, how the Board applies the DuPont factors, and where administrative and judicial inquiries diverge.

## Text and purpose of 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d)

Section 2(d) provides, in relevant part:
“No trademark by which the goods of the applicant may be distinguished from the goods of others shall be refused registration on account of its nature unless it—(d) Consists of or comprises a mark which so resembles a mark registered in the Patent and Trademark Office, or a mark or trade name previously used in the United States by another and not abandoned, as to be likely, when used on or in connection with the goods of the applicant, to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive.” 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d).

The statutory command is straightforward: deny registration where the applicant’s mark and a prior mark are so similar that consumers are likely to be confused as to source. The provision serves a tripartite set of public and private interests. First, it protects consumers by reducing the risk that purchasers will be misled about the source, sponsorship, or affiliation of goods or services. Second, it protects senior users’ ability to exclude confusingly similar junior marks from the federal register, preserving the commercial value of their marks. Third, it preserves the integrity and informational function of the federal register by preventing the issuance of registrations that would be misleading in the marketplace.

Although the statute speaks in terms of marks that “so resemble” prior marks that confusion is likely, courts and the TTAB have constructed a multi-factor test—the DuPont analysis—to translate that general standard into adjudicative practice. The DuPont framework asks decisionmakers to examine the parties’ marks, the relatedness of their goods and services, the strength of the prior mark, the presence of third‑party uses, channels of trade, consumer classes, evidence of actual confusion (if any), and a host of other considerations. Apex Bank illustrates how those discrete factual inquiries cohere into a decision under Section 2(d): the Board found the parties’ services highly similar and sustained the opposition, but the Federal Circuit vacated parts of the Board’s analysis where the Board treated related DuPont inquiries inconsistently.

## Registration versus use‑based infringement: why the standard is similar but the forum and remedies differ

The analysis for refusing a registration under Section 2(d) and for deciding a civil trademark infringement claim in federal court are closely related. Courts and the TTAB rely on largely the same evidentiary considerations—typically the DuPont factors in the TTAB and the Polaroid or similar multi-factor tests in district courts. Both inquiries center on the likelihood that consumers will be confused about the source or sponsorship of goods or services.

Despite the shared doctrinal DNA, important distinctions separate registrability proceedings and infringement litigation. The TTAB’s inquiry is forward-looking and confined to the question whether registration would create a likelihood of confusion; it does not adjudicate rights in the marketplace nor grant remedies for past harm. A TTAB decision can refuse registration or cancel a registration, thereby depriving a party of some procedural advantages that flow from federal registration. But the TTAB cannot award injunctions against use, damages, or an order requiring that an already used mark be abandoned in commerce; those remedies are available only through a district court action.

A district court adjudication of infringement assesses actual use on the market, may award injunctive relief, monetary damages or statutory relief, and can determine priority of use as between actual users. Section 2(d) proceedings, by contrast, resolve the narrower issue whether confusion is likely in the abstract if both marks were used as identified in their applications or registrations. Apex Bank demonstrates this distinction practically: CC Serve, a prior registered user of ASPIRE for credit card services, opposed Apex’s attempt to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board’s decision controlled only the registrability of Apex’s mark; it did not directly alter the parties’ ability to use their respective marks in commerce or determine remedies that would be available in a civil suit.

## The role of the TTAB in opposition proceedings

The TTAB is an administrative adjudicative body within the United States Patent and Trademark Office charged with deciding inter partes disputes about the registrability of marks. One common device is an opposition: after the PTO examiner approves a mark for publication in the Official Gazette, any party believing it will be damaged may file an opposition within a statutory period. The TTAB hears the parties’ factual and legal arguments, admits evidence into the administrative record, applies the DuPont factors to decide whether confusion is likely, and issues a written decision.

The TTAB’s mission is to police the register. It decides whether a mark should enter the federal register given existing registrations and the facts of record. Because the TTAB’s focus is registrability rather than on‑the‑ground commercial disputes, the evidentiary universe it considers is shaped by the identifications of goods and services in the registrations and applications, and by what the administrative record contains. The Board does not consider the parties’ conduct outside the record and typically does not evaluate use or marketplace facts beyond what the parties introduce administratively. Apex Bank’s procedural posture—an opposition to an intent-to-use application—follows this pattern. The TTAB evaluated the likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) based on the parties’ identifications of services, the nature of third‑party registrations and uses in the record, and other admissible evidence; the Federal Circuit reviewed that administrative decision for substantial evidence.

The TTAB’s decisions are binding on the registrar and have consequences limited to registration: a sustained opposition prevents an applicant from obtaining federal registration for the challenged mark. Where the TTAB cancels a registration in a cancellation proceeding, it removes the mark from the federal register, but courts, when asked, may reach different results about infringement, priority, and remedies. Parties dissatisfied with the TTAB’s decision can appeal to the Federal Circuit, as Apex Bank did; alternatively, they can initiate a civil suit in federal district court asserting infringement and registrability defenses.

## Burden of proof: petitioner’s burden to show likelihood of confusion; applicant’s rebuttal

In TTAB oppositions under Section 2(d), the opposer (the petitioner) bears the burden of proving that registration should be refused because of likelihood of confusion. The burden is one of persuasion, and the Board assesses whether the opposer has proved likelihood of confusion by a preponderance of the evidence. The opposer typically relies on its priority—earlier use or registration—and marshals evidence under the DuPont factors: the similarity of the marks, relatedness of goods and services, strength of the prior mark, channels of trade, classes of consumers, and any evidence of actual confusion or of consumers’ sophistication.

The applicant (the party seeking registration) may rebut by producing contrary evidence under the same factors. Common defensive showings include evidence that the marks are sufficiently different in sight, sound, and meaning to avoid confusion; evidence that the prior mark is weak or diluted (often through third‑party registrations and uses); evidence that the goods or services travel in distinct channels of trade or target different consumers; declarations, surveys, or expert testimony demonstrating an absence of actual confusion; and proof of concurrent long-standing use without confusion. The applicant can also argue legal points—for example, that the relevant scope of similarity should be narrower or broader for certain factors, or that the opposer has failed to introduce evidence on a material point.

Apex Bank illustrates both sides of this dynamic. CC Serve carried the burden of establishing likelihood of confusion between its ASPIRE registration (priority 1996) and Apex’s intent-to-use ASPIRE BANK filings (2019). The Board accepted CC Serve’s showing on several DuPont factors—most importantly, on relatedness of services—but Apex countered with third‑party evidence of other “Aspire”-formative marks and arguments that CC Serve’s services (as a credit-card program manager) differ from Apex’s banking services. The Board weighed the competing evidence and found for CC Serve; on appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on service relatedness but remanded portions of the analysis where the Board had applied inconsistent scopes to related factors.

## The record rule: evidence confined to the administrative record

Administrative adjudication before the TTAB is governed by a strict record rule: appellate courts review only the evidence that was before the TTAB. A party cannot cure a deficiency in the administrative record by introducing new evidence for the first time on appeal. The record rule serves several purposes: it preserves the dominant role of the factfinder (the Board), prevents piecemeal litigation, and ensures that appellate review is based on the evidence the Board actually considered.

Appellate review in TTAB appeals follows the familiar mix: legal questions are reviewed de novo, while factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. In Apex Bank, the Federal Circuit emphasized this standard when it affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar—declining to substitute its own judgment where substantial evidence supported the Board’s factfinding. At the same time, the Federal Circuit vacated and remanded parts of the Board’s analysis where legal error (not a mere factual dispute) had infected the Board’s approach—specifically, the Board’s inconsistent definition of the universe of third‑party marks considered under DuPont factor six. That aspect demonstrates the combination of deference on facts together with correction of legal error under the record rule framework.

Practically, parties before the TTAB must present their full case in the administrative record, with declarations, documentary exhibits, and expert evidence if appropriate, because appellate courts will not normally consider new affidavits or studies. If new evidence is essential, a party may seek to reopen the record in the Board, or litigate the matter in district court where the evidentiary scope differs.

## Comparing marketplace realities with identification‑of‑services control

A recurring tension in Section 2(d) litigation is between two modes of inquiry: the “identification‑of‑goods/services” approach and consideration of marketplace realities. The identification approach—used frequently by the TTAB and sustained by courts—starts from the literal wording in the registrations and applications. Where the identifications are broad, the Board will often treat the questions of relatedness and channels of trade broadly, on the theory that registration gives rights precisely for the identified goods or services, regardless of whether the applicant presently uses the mark in every conceivable subcategory.

By contrast, marketplace realities focus on what the parties actually do: actual consumers, real channels of trade, advertising strategies, and empirical evidence of confusion (or lack of it). District courts, because they adjudicate real‑world disputes and award remedial relief, are often more receptive to evidence of actual marketplace distinctions. The TTAB, however, is not precluded from considering marketplace evidence; it may weigh actual use, targeted consumers, and survey evidence if that evidence is in the record. But the identification of services remains a potent tool in administrative proceedings because it sets presumptive bounds for the services at issue.

Apex Bank highlights the interdependence of these two modes. The Board began its DuPont analysis by determining the meaning of “credit card services” and the scope of Apex’s “banking and financing services.” Guided by dictionary definitions and third‑party registration evidence, the Board concluded that the services overlapped significantly—even treating them as “legally identical, in part.” That identification-oriented finding carried implications for other DuPont factors. When the Board later analyzed third‑party “Aspire” marks under factor six, it unreasonably narrowed the universe of comparable marks to those limited to credit card services alone, excluding marks used in broader banking or financing contexts. The Federal Circuit corrected that error: once the Board has found the services highly similar for purposes of factor two, it must keep that same scope in play when assessing relatedness or weakness under other factors. In other words, identification-of-services control can be decisive, but it must be applied consistently across the analysis and reconciled with any real-world evidence in the record.

For students, the takeaway is twofold. First, the language used to identify goods and services in applications and registrations matters tremendously in Section 2(d) disputes: broad identifications magnify the risk of overlap and a finding of relatedness. Second, administrative factfinders should—and courts will—harmonize identification-based inferences with record evidence about how services are marketed and perceived in the real world. Apex demonstrates a situation in which an identification-based finding (high similarity) required the Board to widen, not narrow, the universe of third-party uses considered under factor six.

## Relationship between Section 2(d) proceedings and civil infringement actions

Section 2(d) proceedings and civil infringement suits are complementary but distinct instruments for resolving trademark conflicts. They overlap doctrinally because both examine factors that bear on likelihood of confusion, and because a TTAB decision may be persuasive in a later district court case. But the two forums offer different remedies and reach distinct types of finality.

A TTAB victory grants registration control: the opposer can prevent the applicant from registering the mark (or obtain cancellation of an existing registration). Registration carries procedural benefits—prima facie evidence of validity, nationwide constructive notice, and a presumption of ownership—but it is not a substitute for actual use rights. Conversely, a district court can enjoin use, award damages, recover profits, and shape equitable remedies tailored to marketplace realities.

The relationship between TTAB and district court adjudications is nuanced. A party who loses at the TTAB may litigate a parallel or subsequent infringement suit in district court; the district court is not mechanically bound by the TTAB’s factual or legal conclusions, though collateral estoppel (issue preclusion) may apply if the TTAB’s determinations meet the usual preclusion criteria (identical issues, actually litigated and decided, with a full and fair opportunity to litigate, and necessary to the judgment). Apex Bank’s decision underscores the appellate path for TTAB litigants: a TTAB ruling is appealable to the Federal Circuit, which reviews legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. That review can affirm, vacate, or remand. If the TTAB’s findings are vacated in part, as in Apex, the registrability dispute returns to the administrative forum for further proceedings consistent with the appellate court’s guidance.

Practically, strategic considerations shape a party’s choice of forum. An opposer with a strong registration may choose the TTAB route to block a confusing junior registration and preserve its position on the register; a party seeking immediate relief from ongoing infringing use is more likely to file a district court suit seeking an injunction and monetary relief. Parties sometimes pursue both tracks in parallel, mindful of the different burdens, evidence, and outcomes each forum produces.

Conclusion: integrating doctrine and practice

Section 2(d) sits at the crossroad of statutory text, administrative procedure, and common law likelihood-of-confusion doctrine. The DuPont framework furnishes the analytic architecture within which the TTAB and courts weigh competing evidence about marks, goods and services, consumer perception, and market conditions. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an instructive decision because it shows how a Board’s factfinding about the similarity of services can govern the appropriate universe of evidence when assessing the weakness of a prior mark and the overall commercial impression of the marks. That case also reminds practitioners and students that administrative decisions must apply legal standards consistently across related DuPont factors, that appellate courts will correct legal error while deferring to factual findings supported by substantial evidence, and that registrability decisions, however consequential, are not substitutes for the broader remedies and fact‑intensive inquiries available in civil infringement litigation.
