Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 3: # Commercial Strength, Crowded Fields, and Third-Party Use (DuPont Factor 6)

The sixth DuPont factor—“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”—is the primary vehicle for proving that a senior mark should receive a narrowed scope of protection because the relevant marketplace is “crowded.” Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., decided by the Federal Circuit in 2025, illustrates both the substantive role of that factor and a procedural point of immediate practical importance: the tribunal must define the relevant goods or services consistently across DuPont factors. This chapter explains how courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) treat the interplay between conceptual distinctiveness and commercial strength, how third‑party evidence is used to measure market crowding, what counts as “similar” goods or services for factor six, and how litigants should build and present a persuasive factor‑six record.

DuPont and the crowded‑field intuition. Under the DuPont framework, factor six asks whether there are numerous similar marks used on similar goods or services. The theory is simple: when many parties use similar marks in a sector, consumers become accustomed to distinguishing among them; the senior mark is therefore less commercially strong and entitled to a narrower scope of protection. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly applied that intuition: voluminous third‑party use supports narrowing (Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC), while only a handful of examples may be insufficient (Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.). But those decisions do not announce a numeric threshold; the inquiry is qualitative and contextual.

Conceptual versus commercial strength

Two different ideas are important when courts talk about “strength”: conceptual strength and commercial strength.

- Conceptual strength (inherent distinctiveness) is about the mark’s source‑indicating capacity in the abstract. Marks are classified as generic, descriptive, suggestive, arbitrary, or fanciful; a suggestive, arbitrary, or fanciful mark is “inherently distinctive” and presumptively strong in concept.

- Commercial strength (market strength) is the mark’s recognition and distinctiveness in the marketplace, based on consumer exposure, advertising, sales, and importantly for DuPont factor six, the extent to which others use similar marks on similar goods or services.

These two dimensions operate independently. A mark can be conceptually strong (e.g., ASPIRE is not generic for many financial services and can be an inherently distinctive standard character mark) but commercially weak in a particular field if many competitors use or have registered ASPIRE‑formative marks for similar services. Apex underscores this distinction: the Federal Circuit accepted the Board’s conclusion that CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration did not have an unusual degree of commercial strength but found the Board had not properly measured commercial weakness under factor six because it limited the universe of third‑party marks too narrowly.

The role of third‑party use and registrations

Third‑party evidence comes in two principal forms: evidence of actual marketplace use (web pages, advertising, product packaging, screenshots, press releases, business filings) and registrations (USPTO certificates). Both matter, but they carry different weight.

- Actual use is the most persuasive evidence. Use shows that consumers encounter the mark on the relevant goods or services and that coexistence has been realized in the marketplace. Courts favor contemporaneous, corroborated examples showing sales, marketing, channels of trade, and duration of use.

- Registrations alone are weaker evidence, because a registration by itself does not prove use or marketplace recognition. Registrations, however, can be probative when corroborated by proof of use, or where they show a pattern of attempts to claim exclusive rights in a term across related services.

When assembling factor‑six proof, therefore, prioritize demonstrable use. Screenshots with dates, advertising spend, sales figures, citations to industry directories, and evidence that marks reach the same consumer class are all helpful. The Apex opinion reflects the TTAB’s use of both registrations and use evidence, but the Federal Circuit emphasized that the scope of marks the Board considered should reflect the similarity finding made under factor two.

Crowded‑field rationale and consumer education

The crowded‑field doctrine rests on an empirical claim about consumer behavior: exposure to many similar marks teaches consumers to discriminate among them, reducing the likelihood that one more similar mark will cause confusion. Courts consider not just raw counts of similar marks but whether those marks are sufficiently visible and widespread to have educated consumers. The existence of many obscure registrations filed but never used is much less persuasive than a smaller number of prominent, widely used marks.

Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin illustrate the inquiry. Juice Generation found “a considerable number” of third‑party marks (26) and treated that evidence as a basis for narrowing; Jack Wolfskin relied on a catalog of design and word uses (about 14 examples) to show the field was crowded. But those numbers are suggestive, not controlling facts. Apex makes this point explicit: the Federal Circuit reversed the Board for treating a set of nine uses limited to “credit card services” as insufficiently numerous—because the Board had earlier concluded that banking and credit card services were highly similar, it could not artificially narrow factor six to exclude marks used on closely related financial services.

No bright‑line numbers

It is tempting to search for a numerical recipe for when factor six “tips” toward weakness. The appellate decisions caution against that approach. Courts evaluate the quality, prominence, and nature of the marks as much as quantity. A small number of major, well‑known third‑party uses may be more persuasive than a larger number of obscure registrations. Conversely, a long list of mere registrations filed for narrow or unrelated services may be entitled to little weight. Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin should therefore be read as context: they show that courts may accept mid‑teens to mid‑twenties of examples as persuasive in some contexts, but they do not convert those counts into universal thresholds.

Defining “similar goods or services” under factor 6: consistency with factor 2

A critical procedural point from Apex is that the Board must be consistent in how it defines the relevant goods or services across factors. The second DuPont factor—similarity of goods or services—is commonly the core of the confusion analysis. When the Board finds, as it did in Apex, that services are “highly similar” or even partially “legally identical,” it must use that same conception of similarity when assembling and evaluating third‑party evidence under factor six.

Apex’s error illustrates the inverse problem: the Board found Apex and CC Serve’s services highly similar (banking/financing versus credit card services) for factor two but then confined its factor‑six inquiry to marks expressly used on “credit card services” only, excluding other banking and financial services uses. The Federal Circuit held that inconsistency was legally flawed. If the services are close enough that a DuPont factor two finding supports likely confusion, then third‑party marks on related banking and financing services are plainly relevant to whether the field is crowded. Put simply, you cannot broaden the goods for purposes of establishing similarity and then narrow them for purposes of proving crowding.

Relevance of broader financial services evidence when services are highly similar or partially identical

In financial‑services contexts—credit cards, lending, account servicing, payment processing, and general banking functions—consumers often experience services through overlapping channels (banks, online platforms, card co‑brands, servicing companies). When the record shows close functional overlap between credit card services and broader banking/financing services, the Board should consider third‑party marks used anywhere in that cluster. Third‑party bank brands, fintech services, card issuer programs, and payment‑processing brands that use a common formative (like ASPIRE) are probative of consumer exposure and market crowding. The Federal Circuit’s remand instructs the Board to reevaluate factor six with that expanded lens.

Building a persuasive factor‑six record

Practical litigation and prosecution advice centers on how to assemble third‑party evidence that is both admissible and compelling.

1. Start with a properly scoped goods‑or‑services definition. Tie the scope to the likely consumers and trade channels (e.g., consumers of “banking and financial services” or “card issuance and account servicing”). If factor two is contested, develop evidence that supports your chosen scope because inconsistency will be fatal.

2. Prioritize actual use evidence. Screenshots of active web pages, archived pages (with dates), press releases announcing programs, product brochures, and screenshots of online account menus show real marketplace exposure. Dates matter—contemporaneous evidence near the time of the senior mark’s use or the applicant’s filings is particularly probative.

3. Corroborate registrations with use proof. Registrations can fill gaps but are not a substitute for use. If you cite a registration, attempt to show how the registrant uses the mark in commerce.

4. Demonstrate geographic and channel overlap. Evidence that third‑party marks operate in the same geographic markets and through the same channels of trade (online banking portals, co‑brand credit card programs, bank branch signage, national advertising) increases probative value.

5. Show continuity and prominence. Longstanding uses, substantial advertising, or large user bases are more persuasive than ephemeral or localized uses. Market share or advertising spend figures, when available, can materially strengthen a crowded‑field claim.

6. Provide context for consumer exposure. Consumer surveys, industry analyses, press coverage, and third‑party consumer reviews can show that consumers actually perceive multiple ASPIRE‑formative brands as distinct competitors.

7. Address distinctiveness of the third‑party marks. If many third‑party marks are descriptive or low in distinctiveness, the fact that they exist still informs crowding—but courts will consider whether consumers are likely to confuse those third‑party marks among themselves.

8. Be precise and organized. A single compilation exhibit with each example annotated for date, geographic reach, channels, and whether the evidence shows actual use or mere registration will help the tribunal evaluate the qualitative aspects of the field.

Implications of a weak field term like ASPIRE

When factor six shows a crowded field for a formative like ASPIRE in the financial services arena, several practical outcomes follow.

- Narrowed scope of protection. The senior user may be entitled only to protection in a narrow range of goods or services, or against marks that create the same commercial impression and operate in the identical channels of trade. Unlike a famous mark, it will not block all uses of the formative across the field.

- Greater tolerance for coexistence. Courts and the TTAB may be more inclined to permit the applicant to register or use the contested mark for distinct services or even for overlapping services if the two marks can coexist without likely confusion in practice (for example, via disclaimers, careful use of house marks, or differences in stylization and channels).

- Different remedies and doctrines. A crowded field reduces the likelihood of an injunction sweeping broadly; relief—if any—may be limited to specific goods/services or geographic channels. The crowded field also affects the weight of other DuPont factors such as fame and the senior mark’s commercial impression under factor one.

Practical checklist for factor six litigation and prosecution

- Define the relevant consumer class and channels of trade, consistent with your factor‑two theory.

- Collect contemporaneous evidence of third‑party use (screenshots with timestamps, press releases, brochures, app store pages).

- Gather registrations only as supporting evidence, and where possible, corroborate registrations with use evidence.

- Show the nature and prominence of third‑party uses: duration, advertising, market reach, and any evidence of consumer recognition.

- Organize examples by similarity of services and by commercial prominence; explain why each example is relevant to the consumer class you identified.

- Anticipate and rebut arguments that third‑party examples are geographically limited, merely descriptive, or not in the same channels.

- If you are the senior user, be prepared to show why your mark is commercially distinct despite other uses (via sales, advertising, and goodwill evidence).

Conclusion

Factor six is a fact‑intensive, context‑sensitive inquiry. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. teaches two enduring lessons. First, crowding is about market reality as much as word counts: tribunals evaluate the quality, nature, and marketplace prominence of third‑party uses, not just the number of registrations. Second, and critically for practice, the tribunal must assess similarity of goods or services consistently across DuPont factors: a finding that services are highly similar under factor two cannot be the basis for an artificially narrow universe when testing whether similar marks appear in the same marketplace under factor six. Attorneys should therefore build a factor‑six record that (1) mirrors the goods/services scope used elsewhere in the DuPont analysis, (2) emphasizes demonstrable marketplace use across those services, and (3) explains how consumer exposure to multiple similar marks has educated the relevant public. When done well, that record can deprive a senior owner of expansive rights in a common formative; when done poorly, it risks a remand or reversal like the one in Apex.
