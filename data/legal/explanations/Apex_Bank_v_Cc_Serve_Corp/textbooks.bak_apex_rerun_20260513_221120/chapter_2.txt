Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 2: # Relatedness of Services (DuPont Factor 2) in the Financial Sector

The second DuPont factor asks whether the goods or services identified in the parties’ registrations or applications are related in such a way that consumers are likely to believe they come from the same source. In the financial sector—where banks, card issuers, program managers, servicers, and other intermediaries share channels, customers, and marks—this inquiry is frequently decisive. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. 2025) provides a compact illustration of the principles courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) apply: tribunals rely on the words of the identifications, will find relatedness even where entities play different roles in a program (issuer v. servicer), and treat legacy or deleted identification terms by reference to ordinary meaning and dictionary sources. This chapter explains the legal test, shows how to read identifications, unpacks “legally identical, in part,” and offers practice guidance for branding and litigation in the banking and credit‑card space.

## The legal test: relatedness does not require identity or direct competition

The governing principle is simple but broad: the services need not be identical or directly competitive to be related. The Board and courts ask whether “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012). Thus, the inquiry is both (1) a product/service‑to‑product/service comparison and (2) an assessment of the marketplace context and channels of trade. Evidence relevant to this factor includes: the identifications themselves, dictionary definitions, evidence of overlap in channels of trade and consumers, testimony or publications showing that services are commonly offered by the same companies, and third‑party registrations demonstrating that different services are often provided under a single mark.

In Apex, the Board concluded—and the Federal Circuit affirmed—that credit card services (CC Serve) and banking/financing services (Apex) are “highly similar.” The Board relied on the plain meanings of the identifications and on third‑party registrations indicating that banking and credit card services commonly emanate from a single source. The upshot: where the descriptive sweep of one party’s identification encompasses activities that are a routine part of another party’s offering (e.g., extending credit through cards), DuPont factor two will often favor finding relatedness.

## Reading identifications: words control; marketplace reality is secondary

A foundational rule in trademark examination and oppositions is that the scope of protection depends primarily on the express words of the application/registration identification. That means tribunals first determine what the listing covers, based on its plain language, not on the actual business model or the registrant’s particular way of conducting commerce. If the identification literally encompasses overlapping services, the services may be treated as related for DuPont factor two even if, in practice, one party never performs part of the covered activity.

Two consequences follow:

- When drafting identifications applicants should remember that broad, generic wording (for example, “banking and financing services”) will be read in its ordinary sense and can sweep in activities that create overlap with other marks. A broad identification therefore increases vulnerability to opposition from owners of related marks.
- Conversely, an applicant facing an opposition can sometimes limit the scope of its identification (by amendment, restriction, or affidavit of use showing narrow channels) to avoid overlap. But absent such narrowing, the Board and courts are bound to the plain wording.

Apex shows the doctrine in action. Apex filed to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” CC Serve’s registration recited “credit card services.” The Board examined the plain meanings of those terms and concluded they overlap: dictionaries show that “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” can include extending credit through cards; thus the identifications overlap irrespective of how Apex intended to operate (internet bank) or how CC Serve structures its business (partnering with banks).

## Defining legacy terms: interpreting “credit card services” after removal from the ID Manual

Trademark Office practice and the Board often cite the Trademark ID Manual for standard identifications; when a term has been removed from the ID Manual, tribunals fall back on ordinary meaning, dictionary definitions, and record evidence to ascertain scope. Apex illustrates that approach. The ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been deleted after CC Serve’s registration, so the Board turned to dictionaries and to an analysis of the usual functions associated with credit cards: issuing cards, processing transactions and authorizations, account management, monitoring/alerts, and counseling on credit.

Practitioners should therefore expect that:

- When an ID Manual entry is absent or has been revised, tribunals will construe the term according to its plain and ordinary meaning, possibly supplemented by dictionary definitions, industry sources, and the parties’ evidentiary submissions.
- Evidence showing common consumer understanding or the typical bundle of services associated with a labeled offering (e.g., what “credit card services” normally include) can be decisive in showing overlap.

In Apex the Board’s dictionary-based construction supported a finding of partial legal identity between the parties’ services, because the ordinary meaning of banking and financing includes providing credit and related account services.

## “Legally identical, in part”: what partial identity means and why it matters

Courts use the term “legally identical” to describe situations where the identifications are the same for at least some goods/services. “Legally identical, in part” means that one or more services in one identification literally include services identified in the other party’s application/registration. This partial identity is significant because a finding of legal identity on any subset of the listed services often weighs heavily in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

Why partial identity matters:

- If a claim covers identical goods/services (even only in part), there is no need to probe marketplace realities for those identical services—the overlap is established by the identifications themselves.
- Partial identity can “tip” factor two strongly toward a finding of relatedness because it eliminates the need to show channels, customers, or typical marketing—identical scope in the identification is dispositive for that overlapping slice.

In Apex the Board found that, to the extent “credit card services” are encompassed by the ordinary meaning of “banking and financing,” the services are legally identical in part. That conclusion supported a finding that factor two weighed heavily in favor of confusion because the overlap was not merely speculative; it flowed from the identifications’ plain meanings.

## Third‑party registrations as evidence that banking, financing, and credit‑card services emanate from a single source

Third‑party registrations are admissible evidence that certain goods or services are of a kind that may emanate from a single source under one mark. While these registrations do not establish marketplace use of the marks, they are probative of how the PTO has allowed goods/services to be combined under single registrations—useful circumstantial evidence that consumers encounter such combinations.

Key points about third‑party registrations:

- They are most persuasive when the registrations show a pattern: many registrations combining credit card services with banking or financing services suggest that the relevant trade commonly uses a single mark across those services.
- They do not, on their own, prove consumer perception or actual marketplace co‑branding, but they strengthen the inference that consumers expect various financial services to come from the same source.
- Their probative value depends on their relevance to the services at issue. If the Board has already concluded the services are highly similar, the universe of relevant third‑party registrations should be at least as broad as the overlap found.

Apex demonstrates both the power and the limits of this evidence. The Board relied on third‑party registrations showing combined financial and card services to support relatedness under factor two. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed factor two but vacated the Board’s narrower handling of third‑party marks under DuPont factor six—holding that once the Board finds high similarity, it must consider all relevant third‑party uses and registrations of marks used on similar services, not a truncated subset. That interplay between factors 2 and 6 is important for litigators: evidence marshalled to show relatedness can also bear on commercial strength and market crowdedness.

## Coach Services and related authority: overlap without direct competition

Apex’s result aligns squarely with Coach Services v. Triumph Learning, where the Federal Circuit confirmed that relatedness may be found when goods or services are marketed in a way that could create a source‑association, regardless of direct competition. Other precedents—Olde Tyme Foods (relatedness without identity), Juice Generation (crowded field effect), and Palm Bay (weakness via third‑party use)—illustrate the tools courts use. Collectively these cases show:

- Courts do not require the parties to sell identical items in the same store or to be direct competitors to find relatedness.
- The existence of common marketing channels, overlapping consumers, or routine co‑branding by others in the industry suffices.
- The Board and courts give weight to industry practice: if banks commonly put their marks on both deposit accounts and card products, consumers are likely to associate marks across those services.

Thus a servicer that never issues cards can still be related to an issuing bank for DuPont factor two if the marketing and consumer interaction create linkage.

## Program managers and bank partnerships: why role distinctions seldom defeat relatedness

Modern credit card programs often involve distinct players: a sponsoring/issuing bank, a program manager or servicer, and a brand or marketing partner. Trademark disputes sometimes rest on whether a non‑issuing party’s use should be treated as separate for likelihood of confusion purposes. Apex instructs that such structural distinctions do not automatically negate relatedness.

Why role distinctions are often irrelevant for factor two:

- Consumers may never know or care which entity performs back‑office issuance or servicing; they relate the customer experience (statements, rewards, customer service) to the same brand.
- Service descriptions in identifications are controlling. If an applicant’s identification covers “banking and financing services” and an opponent’s registration covers “credit card services,” the legal overlap exists regardless of an entity’s internal role division.
- Evidence that the public encounters a unified brand across issuing and servicing (co‑branded cards, card materials, marketing) strengthens the conclusion that consumers could believe services emanate from the same source.

In Apex CC Serve did not itself issue cards; it partners with banks that issue ASPIRE‑branded cards while CC Serve services accounts. That model did not defeat the Board’s finding of relatedness because the ordinary consumer’s perception of card services and bank services can cohere under one mark.

## Practice pointers: drafting and defending identifications in financial branding

For counsel advising banks, fintechs, or servicers, strategic identification drafting and evidentiary planning can materially affect DuPont factor two outcomes. The following practical guidance flows from the law illustrated by Apex.

For applicants seeking to maximize protection:
- Consider broad identifications if broad protection is desired, but recognize that broad wording invites opposition from owners of related marks.
- File for multiple, tiered identifications (e.g., separate classes or narrower wording) when possible to hedge risk.

For applicants seeking to minimize conflict risk:
- Narrow the identification to the specific services actually provided (for example, “online personal deposit accounts” rather than “banking services”) and be prepared to submit clarifying declarations or specimen evidence.
- If a mark will be used only in conjunction with a partner bank (e.g., as a white‑label servicer), consider disclaimers or statements of limitation and make the record clear as to the narrow scope of use.

For defendants/opponents:
- Emphasize the plain meaning of the opponent’s identification and use dictionaries and industry publications to show overlap.
- Produce third‑party registrations and evidence of co‑branding to show that consumers commonly encounter the services under a single mark.
- Show how marketing channels and consumers overlap (e.g., shared online portals, joint statements, co‑branded materials).

For litigation and appeals:
- When the PTO/Board construes an identification too narrowly or inconsistently across DuPont factors, preserve the issue: courts will scrutinize inconsistencies (Apex vacated the Board’s factor‑six analysis because it imposed a narrower scope than adopted under factor two).
- Gather marketplace evidence (specimens, screenshots, marketing agreements) to show how the public encounters the services and brands.
- Use surveys selectively; courts accept well‑designed consumer surveys showing source association, but the absence of a survey is not fatal where identifications overlap.

Sample drafting alternatives (to reduce overlap risk):
- Instead of “banking and financing services,” consider “online deposit account services; accepting deposits in connection with online banking” or “consumer checking and savings accounts; automated teller machine services.”
- For card program managers: “credit card program administration and servicing for issuers” (if accurate), rather than a generic “credit card services” identification that could be read to mean issuance.

## Litigation angle: what to raise on appeal

When the Board’s finding on factor two rests on the identification and is supported by record evidence, the Federal Circuit applies the substantial‑evidence standard to fact findings and reviews legal conclusions de novo. Appellate success typically requires showing either that the Board misread the identifications or that the factual record cannot support the determination of relatedness. Apex demonstrates that a solid factual and documentary record—plain‑meaning definitions, third‑party registrations, and examples of co‑branding—will survive appellate scrutiny. Conversely, where the Board’s analysis of relatedness is inconsistent with its treatment of other DuPont factors (as the Federal Circuit identified with factor six here), appellate courts will demand coherence and may vacate and remand for reconsideration.

## Conclusion

In the financial services context, DuPont factor two resolves frequently against applicants with broad identifications because customers encounter a wide range of related services under common brands. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. illustrates the controlling procedures: start with the identification’s plain meaning (using dictionaries and the ID Manual when appropriate), treat partial legal identity seriously, and use third‑party registrations and marketplace evidence to show that banking, financing, and credit‑card services commonly emanate from a single source. Practitioners should draft identifications with care, gather and present market evidence that clarifies how consumers experience services, and be prepared to challenge inconsistent DuPont treatment on appeal.
