Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 5: # Strength of the Prior Mark and the ‘Crowded Field’ Doctrine (DuPont Factors 5 and 6)

This chapter examines how courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) evaluate the strength of a prior mark and the role of third‑party marks in narrowing a senior registrant’s scope of protection. The analysis is driven by two DuPont factors that frequently determine the outcome of Section 2(d) disputes: (1) conceptual and commercial strength of the prior mark (often treated under the umbrella of the “fifth” DuPont factor or as an aspect of the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis) and (2) the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services (the sixth DuPont factor). Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., decided by the Federal Circuit in 2025, provides a useful vehicle for discussing these issues. Apex demonstrates both the utility and the limits of third‑party evidence: third‑party registrations and marketplace uses can—and often should—inform the scope of protection accorded to a prior mark, but they must be evaluated against a properly defined universe of similar goods or services and a consistently applied definition of the relevant consumer class.

## Conceptual strength on the Abercrombie spectrum versus commercial strength from marketplace recognition

Trademarks possess two distinct types of strength that courts weigh when determining the likelihood of confusion: conceptual strength and commercial strength. Conceptual strength is a function of the inherent distinctiveness of a mark independent of marketplace use. The classic way to analyze conceptual strength is the Abercrombie spectrum coined in Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc.: generic (no protection) → descriptive (protection only with acquired distinctiveness or limited to narrow scope) → suggestive → arbitrary or fanciful (highest inherent strength). A mark that is fanciful or arbitrary (e.g., "Kodak") is conceptually strong and presumptively entitled to a broad scope of protection; a descriptive mark that has not acquired distinctiveness is conceptually weak.

Commercial strength, by contrast, derives from marketplace recognition—consumer awareness, sales, advertising, length of use, and evidence of fame. A conceptually weak mark can acquire commercial strength through extensive use and advertising, and fame can increase a mark’s remedial reach. Courts look for metrics: long and continuous use, large sales volumes, intensive advertising campaigns, high market share, and consumer surveys showing recognition. Survey evidence is especially persuasive because it directly measures the likelihood of consumer confusion and the public’s association of the mark with a single source.

The two dimensions interact. A conceptually strong mark with substantial commercial recognition will enjoy a very broad protection. A conceptually strong but obscure mark enjoys the benefit of inherent distinctiveness but may be less likely to generate confusion in practice if consumers aren’t familiar with it. Conversely, a descriptive mark that has become famous through long use can be afforded broad protection despite its descriptive origins. In practice, courts and the Board often conflate these inquiries because both feed into the overall “commercial impression” inquiry under the first DuPont factor.

Apex Bank illustrates this interplay. CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark is not an invented term, and its conceptual strength is not categorically at the top of the Abercrombie scale; “aspire” is an English verb with ordinary meaning and thus not fanciful. The Board nevertheless concluded that CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark is entitled to a “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks” because Apex had not established that the mark was conceptually or commercially weak. That conclusion folded together questions of conceptual distinctiveness and marketplace strength, and it highlights the point that a mark’s placement on the Abercrombie spectrum is only part of the inquiry—the Board must also consider actual marketplace recognition if third‑party evidence suggests the mark is relatively weak in practice.

## What third‑party registrations can and cannot prove

Third‑party trademark registrations (and applications) are commonly introduced in DuPont litigation. They can be useful, but they also have important evidentiary limits.

What registrations can prove:
- Conceptual weakness of a term: A significant number of third‑party registrations incorporating a particular word or element for similar goods/services suggests that the word is in common commercial use and unlikely to serve as a strong source identifier on its own. Courts have treated third‑party registrations as evidence that a word is weak or commonly used in a field, and therefore deserving of a narrower scope of protection (see Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772).
- The existence of a crowded field: Registrations across a range of parties for similar goods can support a conclusion that the marketplace is populated with similar marks.
- The acceptability of coexistence in theory: The fact that the PTO issued registrations for similar marks can be cited to show that similar marks have legally coexisted (but this is only persuasive, not dispositive).

What registrations cannot prove (or prove only weakly):
- Actual marketplace use or consumer recognition: A registration by itself does not demonstrate that consumers actually encountered the mark in the marketplace. The PTO registers marks that may not be in use (or whose use is uncontested), and the registration does not quantify market penetration, advertising, or sales. Relying solely on registrations risks mistaking bureaucratic outcome for market realities.
- Temporal or geographic relevance: Registrations may be abandoned, may postdate priority, or may have limited territorial impact. Registrations filed after the priority date of the senior user cannot show preexisting consumer habituation.
- Legal conclusions: Registrations do not establish that confusion will not occur; the PTO’s allowance of a registration is not binding on courts or the Board in an opposition or cancellation.

Because of these limitations, courts often give stronger weight to registrations that are accompanied by evidence of actual use (screenshots, specimens, sales figures) and to registrations for identical or truly similar goods/services. In short, registrations are helpful but are most persuasive when paired with marketplace evidence.

## What third‑party marketplace use can and cannot prove

Actual marketplace use—advertising, websites, sales, customer testimonials, trade press coverage, distribution networks—is generally the most persuasive evidence of the real commercial environment. Third‑party use can show that consumers routinely encounter multiple similar marks in a field, which supports the “crowded field” argument.

What marketplace use can prove:
- Consumer habituation and ease of distinction: Extensive marketplace use of similar marks teaches consumers to distinguish among them. Courts treating crowded‑field evidence rely on the assumption that repeated exposure trains consumers to pick up on differences and reduces the likelihood of source confusion (see Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC and Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.).
- Relative weakness of the senior mark in practice: If many others use the same or similar term in commerce for related goods, that tends to show that the term is a common industry term rather than a strong source indicator.
- Channels and consumers: Marketplace use helps define who the relevant consumers are (trade purchasers, sophisticated business customers, mass public) and the typical channels through which they encounter marks.

What marketplace use cannot prove (or proves only weakly):
- Consumer confusion outcomes: Evidence of third‑party marketplace use does not by itself show that consumers will or will not be confused by the particular pair of marks at issue. It is circumstantial and must be tied to the other DuPont factors.
- Comparability without context: A showing that a third party uses an “Aspire” mark for an unrelated line of goods (e.g., educational services) is less relevant than showing use in the same class of services. The similarity of the goods/services is essential to the probative value of marketplace use evidence.
- Indifference to timing and scope: Marketplace use after the critical priority date does not demonstrate preexisting consumer habituation. Also, localized use may not inform a nationwide assessment unless the relevant market is localized.

The practical implication is that litigants should pair registration evidence with contemporaneous specimens of use, advertising metrics, sales data, and consumer-facing materials that demonstrate how often consumers actually see similar marks and in what contexts.

## The crowded field rationale: consumer habituation to similar marks reduces confusion risk

The core policy behind crowdsourcing third‑party evidence is intuitive: when consumers routinely encounter many similar marks in a field, they become accustomed to distinguishing sources and are less likely to be confused by another similar mark. Courts describe this as a reduction in a senior mark’s commercial strength and a narrowing of its scope of protection.

Several Federal Circuit decisions illustrate this point. In Juice Generation, the court considered over two dozen third‑party uses of “Juice” and related terms in the beverage field and concluded that the crowded field reduced the senior mark’s claim to an expansive territorial or product scope. In Jack Wolfskin the court relied on voluminous evidence of paw‑print design elements used in the outerwear market to conclude that the design element's conceptual and commercial strength were attenuated by common use. These cases show that the presence of many similar marks can be powerful evidence that consumers will make distinctions rather than assume a common source.

But the crowded field rationale is not an automatic defense to a senior user’s claim. Courts still ask whether the third‑party marks are sufficiently similar and used on sufficiently similar goods or services to teach consumers to distinguish among them. The strength of a crowded field argument depends on the number of similar marks, their visibility, the closeness of the goods or services, and the sophistication and buying patterns of the relevant consumers.

## Thresholds and labels: ‘considerable’ or ‘ubiquitous’ evidence—no magic numbers

Judges and practitioners often use adjectives—“considerable,” “ubiquitous,” “voluminous”—to describe the quantity of third‑party evidence necessary to show a crowded field. The phrasing in Federal Circuit decisions left some readers hoping for a numeric rule: how many third‑party uses suffice? The short answer is that there is no fixed numerical threshold; context matters.

A few guiding principles emerge from the case law:
- Relative, not absolute: Courts assess the evidence in relation to the size and character of the relevant market. Twenty similar marks in a small, niche industry may be significant; the same number in a vast national market may be less persuasive.
- Quality beats quantity: A smaller number of highly visible competitors (national brands with prominent advertising and retail presence) can be more damaging to a senior mark’s claim than numerous obscure or marginal uses. A single national player with significant market share may reinforce the senior mark’s strength, not weaken it.
- Overlap of goods/services matters: Third‑party uses that are closely aligned with the senior user’s goods or services are far more probative than tangential uses. Courts discount evidence of similar words used in distinct service contexts.
- Consumer experience and sophistication: Where consumers are sophisticated or make careful purchasing decisions (e.g., B2B financial services), smaller amounts of third‑party use may suffice for habituation effects.
- Timing and continuity are important: Evidence must be contemporaneous with the dispute’s relevant priority timeframe to show habituation of consumers, and consistent use over time strengthens the crowded‑field argument.

Practically, litigants and adjudicators should avoid obsession with counts. The question is whether the cumulative record—quantity, quality, visibility, and relevancy of third‑party uses—shows that the marketplace has trained consumers to distinguish among similar marks, thereby narrowing the senior mark’s practical scope.

## Financial services examples: relatedness across credit cards, banking, lending, and fintech

The financial services sector illustrates the subtleties in applying Factor 6. Companies often provide overlapping services under different arrangements, and brands appear across traditional banking, card processing, lending platforms, and fintech apps. This intermixing complicates the assessment of “similar goods/services” and the probative value of third‑party marks.

Key observations for financial services:
- Functional overlap: Credit card programs, payment processing, account servicing, and lending are often integrated by partnerships. A bank may issue a co‑branded card while a fintech processes transactions. Even if different entities perform functions, the consumer often perceives the services as functionally related (e.g., both involve credit, repayment, account management).
- White‑label arrangements: In the credit card world, issuers and program managers commonly white‑label services for banks. A consumer’s card may bear a bank’s brand while the processing and account servicing are performed by a separate company. This blurs the line between the provider of the service and brand identification.
- Distribution channels matter: Some financial services are heavily digital (fintech apps), while others operate through branch banking. Third‑party marks used across different channels—e.g., “Aspire” used both by a credit card program and a mobile banking app—can be persuasive evidence of a crowded field if consumers encounter both offerings in similar contexts (mobile enrollment, app stores).
- Consumer expectations: Financial products often involve considered decisions (reading terms, comparing rates), but many consumers purchase financial services with limited brand attention (e.g., acceptance of a merchant‑provided card). Sophistication varies across products and consumer segments.

In Apex Bank, the Board recognized a high degree of similarity between CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s “banking and financing services.” Given such factual overlap, evidence of third‑party “Aspire” marks used across banking and card products was especially relevant. The case shows that in finance, labels that appear across product lines and delivery channels are apt to be probative for Factor 6—provided the Board treats them consistently with its earlier finding about relatedness (a point to which we return).

## What third‑party evidence must show: alignment with the relevant consumer and market scope

Two related concepts govern whether a piece of third‑party evidence will be given weight under Factor 6: (1) the relevant consumer class, and (2) the proper scope of goods/services to consider. Both are fact questions informed by the second DuPont factor (similarity of goods/services) and the evidence of marketplace channels.

Define the relevant public: The probative value of third‑party marks depends on which consumers are likely to encounter the marks. If the product is a retail consumer product sold in supermarkets, the relevant public is the general purchasing public; if it’s a specialized service sold to sophisticated purchasers, the relevant public is narrower and more knowledgeable.

Align goods/services across factors: If the Board or court has already found that the parties’ goods or services are highly similar (as happened in Apex with credit card and banking/finance services), then it is inconsistent to narrow the third‑party universe for Factor 6 to a subset (e.g., limiting to only “credit card services”) without explanation. The similarity finding in Factor 2 sets the scope for what counts as “similar goods/services” for purposes of measuring how crowded the field is. Apex Bank stands for the proposition that the analysis must be coherent across factors: you cannot use a wide definition of relatedness to find similarity in Factor 2 and then contract that definition for Factor 6 to exclude many relevant third‑party marks.

Temporal relevance: Third‑party uses must be shown to have existed at a time that could have affected consumer habituation before or at the senior user’s priority date (unless the dispute concerns subsequent issues). Evidence of third‑party uses that postdate the relevant timeframe is less helpful to demonstrate preexisting crowding.

Geographic relevance: Where services are local or regional, third‑party uses confined to distant markets may be less probative.

## Apex Bank’s core lesson: scope of ‘similar goods/services’ under Factor 6 must align with Factor 2 findings

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. provides the clearest recent articulation of a principle that follows logically from DuPont: when a tribunal has already found the parties’ goods or services to be highly similar (or partially legally identical) under the second DuPont factor, it must apply that same definition of similarity when evaluating the field of third‑party marks under the sixth factor. The Board in Apex found that “credit card services” and “banking and financing services” were highly similar; nevertheless, when assessing Factor 6, the Board restricted itself to third‑party marks that explicitly referenced credit card services and excluded many marks using “Aspire” or “Aspire‑formative” elements in broader banking and finance contexts. The Federal Circuit held this to be error—once the Board has accepted a broad scope of similarity, it cannot then narrow the scope for assessing crowding without explanation.

The practical takeaways:
- Coextensive analysis: The universe of third‑party marks relevant under Factor 6 should be at least as broad as the universe of similar goods/services the tribunal found under Factor 2. Otherwise the Board risks undercounting or mischaracterizing evidence of crowding.
- Explain deviations: If the Board narrows the universe for Factor 6 despite a broader Factor 2 finding, it must provide a reasoned explanation—e.g., showing that particular third‑party uses, while arguably related, still differ in channels or consumer base enough to be irrelevant.
- Integrative reasoning: DuPont factors are not siloed. Findings under one factor inform the application of others. The appellate reversal in Apex demonstrates that inconsistent treatment can be reversible error because it distorts the aggregate likelihood‑of‑confusion assessment.

## Practical guidance for practitioners and tribunals

For litigators:
- Collect both registrations and contemporaneous marketplace evidence (screenshots, sales summaries, advertising, app‑store listings, press coverage) showing that third‑party uses were visible to consumers within the relevant timeframe and market.
- Define the relevant public carefully and use evidence of distribution channels and advertising to demonstrate which consumers are likely to encounter competing marks.
- If arguing crowded field, emphasize both number and prominence: a few national players may suffice; many obscure uses may not.
- If the senior user’s mark has been used across related financial services, compile evidence of cross‑use (co‑branding, white‑labeling, partnership disclosures) to show functional relatedness.

For the Board and courts:
- Apply an internally consistent definition of “similar goods/services” across DuPont factors. If Factor 2 finds high similarity, Factor 6 must consider third‑party marks across that same range, unless the tribunal explains why certain uses should be excluded.
- Assess registrations and marketplace use together; registrations without use are weaker evidence of consumer habituation.
- Avoid numeric fixation; instead, explain why the quantity, quality, and visibility of third‑party marks amount to (or fall short of) a “crowded field” given the market at issue.

## Checklist for applying Factor 6 in light of Apex Bank

1. Start from your Factor 2 finding: define the goods/services that you have already found to be similar.
2. Define the relevant consumer class and channels of trade that those consumers use.
3. Collect third‑party evidence that matches the scope from step 1 and the consumer/channel definition from step 2.
4. Evaluate quantity, prominence, timing, and continuity of third‑party uses—give weight to national, longstanding, or heavily advertised uses.
5. Pair registrations with specimens of actual use; evaluate registrations that postdate the critical timeframe with caution.
6. Explain reasoning: if you exclude classes of third‑party marks, provide a fact‑based justification consistent with your Factor 2 analysis.
7. Remember the holistic DuPont inquiry: results on Factor 6 will inform, and be informed by, conclusions on conceptual/commercial strength and the overall commercial impression.

Conclusion

Third‑party registrations and marketplace uses are indispensable tools in modern DuPont analysis, but they are not a panacea. The evidence must be relevant to the proper universe of goods/services and consumers. Apex Bank reinforces that DuPont factors must be applied coherently: findings about product relatedness dictate the breadth of the field the tribunal should examine when assessing crowding and the prior mark’s practical strength. When courts or tribunals keep the analytical frame consistent and evaluate registrations and use together, the crowded‑field doctrine can be applied in a principled way to ensure that the scope of trademark protection reflects both legal distinctiveness and marketplace realities.
