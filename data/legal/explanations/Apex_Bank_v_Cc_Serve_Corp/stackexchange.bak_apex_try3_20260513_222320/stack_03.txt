Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the court vacate and remand instead of reweighing the DuPont factors itself?
Question:
The panel found legal error in the Board’s sixth-factor analysis and vacated the first factor because commercial impression could change if strength changes. Given that likelihood of confusion is a question of law, why didn’t the Federal Circuit reweigh the factors and decide the § 2(d) issue? Is this because factual findings under each factor are for the Board and reviewed for substantial evidence (and/or because of Chenery-type limits on appellate factfinding for agencies)?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the panel corrected a legal error in the Board’s analysis that requires the Board — not the court — to reapply the correct legal standard to the factual record and make any necessary factual findings. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo but reviews the underlying DuPont fact-findings for substantial evidence; it will not usurp the Board by making new fact-findings or reweighing evidence in the first instance.

Why that follows from the opinion
- The court framed the doctrine up front: “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors,” and “[e]ach of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence.” (Opinion §II (citing M2 Software; Bose)). That establishes the appellate role: correct legal error; review factual findings for substantial evidence; do not ordinarily make new factual findings.
- On the sixth factor the Board made a legal error in defining the universe of third‑party marks it could consider (it limited the universe to “credit card services” even though the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar). The court said that was a legal mistake and therefore “we vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks.” (Opinion §III.B). Because the Board applied the wrong legal standard, the court could not simply accept the Board’s factual conclusion as properly framed.
- The court also vacated the first factor because commercial impression “informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor,” and a corrected sixth‑factor analysis “may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” (Opinion §III.C). That shows the court recognized interdependence between fact‑intensive DuPont factors and that a remand is necessary where a legal error in one factor could change factual conclusions under another.

Relation to Chenery / limits on appellate factfinding
- The opinion follows the same separation-of-functions principle reflected in Chenery: an agency must apply the correct legal standard and make the necessary factual findings; a reviewing court should not supply missing factual determinations or reweigh the evidence in the first instance. The Federal Circuit therefore corrected the legal error and remanded so the Board can reapply the correct legal standard to the record and make any attendant factual findings (subject to appellate substantial‑evidence review).

Practical takeaway
- Where the Board misstates the legal standard that determines what evidence is relevant (here, the scope of third‑party marks), the Federal Circuit will vacate and remand rather than reweigh the DuPont factors itself, because the remaining determinations are factual and properly belong to the Board to make under the corrected legal standard.
