Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the court vacate the first DuPont factor based on potential changes in 'commercial impression' stemming from the sixth factor?
Question:
The court remanded factor one because a different determination of the mark’s strength/weakness under factor six could alter the 'overall commercial impression.' How does the perceived strength of a mark feed into the similarity-of-marks analysis? Is this cross-factor dependency common in Federal Circuit cases evaluating likelihood of confusion?
Answer:
Short answer
The court vacated factor one because the Board’s erroneous narrowing of factor six could change its assessment of CC Serve’s mark strength, and mark strength is part of the “commercial impression” the first DuPont factor asks you to evaluate. Put plainly: evidence under factor six (how many similar third‑party marks are used) bears directly on how distinctive or weak ASPIRE is, and that distinctiveness affects how consumers will perceive the mark in the marketplace — which is the core of the first factor. Because the Board’s defective sixth‑factor analysis might lead to a different conclusion about the mark’s strength, the court remanded factor one so the Board can reassess the marks’ overall commercial impression with the corrected sixth‑factor record.

Explanation and authorities from the opinion
- What factor six does: The opinion explains that the sixth DuPont factor “considers ‘[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,’” and that third‑party use is “relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” J.A. 6–7 (quoting DuPont and Palm Bay Imps.). The court cites Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin for the proposition that a crowded field can educate consumers to distinguish among similar marks and thereby reduce a mark’s commercial strength. J.A. 6–7.
- How strength/weakness feeds into commercial impression: The first DuPont factor looks to “the ‘entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression’” and focuses on whether the “overall commercial impression” would lead to source confusion. J.A. 7 (quoting DuPont and I.AM.Symbolic). The opinion ties these together: because commercial impression “informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor,” a different conclusion about commercial strength under factor six could alter the first‑factor outcome. J.A. 7.
- Why the Board’s error required vacatur of factor one: The Board limited its sixth‑factor inquiry to marks only for “credit card services” despite having already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (indeed partially legally identical) in its second‑factor analysis. The court held that restricting the sixth‑factor universe was legally flawed and remanded factor six for reconsideration (J.A. 6–7). Because that reconsideration “may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression,” the court remanded factor one as well. J.A. 7.

Is cross‑factor dependency common?
Yes. The opinion shows the routine interdependence of DuPont factors: factual findings on one factor (here, the scope/similarity of goods under factor two) set the scope of analysis for other factors (here, which third‑party marks are “similar” under factor six). Likewise, conclusions about mark strength under factor six naturally inform the “commercial impression” assessment under factor one. The court explicitly says the Board “should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors” once it found the services highly similar (J.A. 6), and it remanded factor one because its commercial‑impression analysis could change depending on the corrected sixth‑factor finding (J.A. 7). That reflects the Federal Circuit’s practice of treating the DuPont factors as related factual inquiries, not hermetically sealed boxes.

Practical takeaway for applying DuPont in future cases
- Define the relevant marketplace consistently: a finding on goods/services similarity (factor two) fixes the universe of “similar goods” for factor six. J.A. 6.
- Treat third‑party use evidence (factor six) as evidence of commercial weakness; be explicit about how that weakness alters the consumer’s perception of the mark for purposes of the first factor. J.A. 6–7.
- If one factor’s factual error could plausibly alter the commercial impression, expect the court to remand both the erroneous factor and any factors that depend on it. J.A. 7.

In short: factor six can change a mark’s assessed strength; mark strength is an element of the “commercial impression” that factor one requires you to evaluate; the Board’s flawed narrowing of factor six could therefore change the factor‑one analysis, so the court vacated factor one and remanded both factors for reconsideration.
