Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Could Section 2(d)’s likelihood-of-confusion refusal raise First Amendment concerns post-Tam and Brunetti?
Question:
The opinion doesn’t raise constitutional issues, but after Matal v. Tam and Iancu v. Brunetti, are there any First Amendment constraints on refusing registration under § 2(d) for likely confusion? Is § 2(d) treated as a permissible regulation of source-identifiers to prevent consumer deception, and how have courts analyzed any content-based aspects of a confusion-based refusal?
Answer:
Short answer
The opinion does not raise any First Amendment issue; it treats § 2(d) as a straightforward, fact‑driven rule for preventing consumer deception. Under the court’s reasoning, a § 2(d) refusal is tied to the source‑identifying function of marks and to the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry (appearance, sound, connotation, services, third‑party uses, etc.), not to suppressing ideas or viewpoints. As the opinion frames the dispute, the Board’s § 2(d) analysis is a permissible regulation of source‑identifiers to prevent consumer confusion, because it looks to the marks’ commercial impression and marketplace conditions rather than to the applicant’s message.

Why — key doctrinal points drawn from the opinion
- The opinion treats § 2(d) as a likelihood‑of‑confusion rule, not a content‑ban. It repeatedly frames the inquiry as whether consumer confusion “is likely because of concurrent use of the marks” and resolves that question through the DuPont factors (see opinion §II and opening paragraph of §III). That is a consumer‑protection, source‑identification inquiry.
- The factors the Board and court apply are market‑ and impression‑based: similarity of goods/services (second DuPont factor), the marks’ “appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression” (first DuPont factor), and the number and nature of similar marks in use (sixth DuPont factor). The opinion repeatedly ties the inquiry to the marks’ commercial impression and the relevant consuming public (see Sections III.A–C). Those are paradigmatically commercial, not communicative, considerations.
- The court vacated and remanded only because the Board applied inconsistent scopes of similarity across factors (it treated services as highly similar under factor two but then narrowed the universe of third‑party marks under factor six). The remedy ordered was factual reconsideration of the DuPont factors, not any constitutional analysis. The court’s remedy reinforces that § 2(d) disputes are resolved by fact‑intensive DuPont balancing (see last two paragraphs of §III(C)).

How that maps onto First Amendment concerns (practical takeaways)
- The opinion implies that § 2(d) regulates the marketplace role of marks (who consumers will think is the source), which is the classic zone of trademark law. When the PTO/TTAB and courts refuse registration under § 2(d), they are doing so to prevent deception about source, a regulatory objective squarely tied to labels’ commercial function.
- Because the court here evaluated the DuPont factors (commercial impression, relatedness of services, third‑party uses) and remanded for further factual work, the opinion indicates courts will treat confusion refusals as content‑neutral, market‑oriented adjudications rather than viewpoint‑based suppression. The focal questions are: would ordinary consumers be confused; how strong/weak is the prior mark; and what commercial context surrounds the marks?
- That matters because constitutional challenges (e.g., on viewpoint/discrimination grounds) typically target statutes that deny registration because of the communicative content or viewpoint of the mark. This opinion shows the Board’s § 2(d) work is geared to source‑identification and likelihood of confusion, not to content‑based selection among messages.

A conservative framing for litigation or briefing
- If you want to raise a First Amendment argument in a § 2(d) case, you should show that the refusal turns on the expressive content or viewpoint of the applicant’s mark rather than on its capacity to function as a source identifier. Absent that showing, the court’s approach in this opinion suggests § 2(d) will be resolved through the DuPont factual framework and treated as a permissible regulation of source‑identifiers to prevent consumer deception.
- In this appeal the court did not entertain any free‑speech claim and remanded for further DuPont analysis; that procedural posture underscores that § 2(d) issues will typically be decided on the trademark factors before any constitutional question is reached.

Conclusion
Under this opinion, § 2(d) is applied as a marketplace, source‑identification rule (DuPont factors) aimed at preventing consumer confusion. The court’s treatment signals that, so long as a refusal is based on ordinary likelihood‑of‑confusion reasoning (appearance, commercial impression, relatedness of services, third‑party use), it will be characterized as a permissible regulation of source‑identifiers rather than a First Amendment‑forbidden content or viewpoint ban.
