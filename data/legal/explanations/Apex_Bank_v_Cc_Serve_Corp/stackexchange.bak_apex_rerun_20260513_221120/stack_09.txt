Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the court vacate the first DuPont factor based on potential re-evaluation of commercial strength under factor six?
Question:
The panel says commercial impression under factor one is informed by the mark’s commercial strength/weakness, so it vacates factor one because factor six might change on remand. Is the court treating conceptual/commercial strength as a necessary input to commercial impression? In practice, how interdependent are these factors—can the Board ever decide factor one purely on appearance/sound/connotation without knowing strength, or is that always an abuse of discretion/legal error?
Answer:
Short answer
The court vacated the Board’s DuPont factor one finding because the Board’s flawed factor six analysis could change its assessment of the mark’s commercial strength, and commercial strength is a relevant input to the “commercial impression” inquiry. The panel did not hold that every factor‑one determination must always await a full strength inquiry; rather, when strength (factor six) is both contested and potentially outcome‑determinative of how the public will perceive the mark, it is proper — and sometimes required — to reassess factor one after resolving factor six.

Why the court did this (doctrine and the opinion’s reasoning)
- DuPont factor one asks about similarity “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” The phrase “commercial impression” imports not only raw appearance/sound/meaning but also how consumers will perceive the mark in marketplace context.
- Factor six (third‑party use) is a principal means of showing a mark’s commercial weakness or strength. As the panel explains, evidence of third‑party uses can show a field is “crowded” and that consumers are “educated to distinguish between marks,” which reduces a mark’s scope of protection. (Opinion §III.B, citing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin.)
- The panel found the Board erred by narrowing the universe of third‑party marks considered under factor six even though it had already found the parties’ services to be “highly similar.” Because a different, broader consideration of third‑party uses might lead to a different conclusion about CC Serve’s commercial strength, the court concluded that the Board’s factor‑one “commercial impression” assessment could be affected and therefore vacated factor one for reconsideration. (Opinion §III.B–C, J.A. 47, 51, 7.)

How interdependent are the factors in practice?
- They are related but analytically distinct. Factor one is essentially a side‑by‑side comparison of the marks’ appearance, sound, connotation and overall commercial impression; factor six (third‑party use) is evidence used to gauge how strong or weak the senior mark is in the marketplace.
- In many cases you can evaluate factor one on the bases of appearance/sound/meaning alone (e.g., where strength is undisputed, irrelevant, or trivial). The Board and courts often do so. But commercial strength is a factual circumstance that can—and sometimes does—alter the net “commercial impression.” For example:
  - A very strong, unique mark may be perceived by consumers as a single source so that even modest similarities in appearance/meaning produce confusion.
  - A weak or crowded mark may be perceived by consumers as describing a class of goods or as one of many similar marks, making even moderately similar marks less likely to cause confusion.
- Thus, when strength is meaningfully contested and the record evidence on factor six could realistically change how consumers are expected to perceive the mark, resolving factor six first (or at least reassessing factor one after resolving factor six) is not only permissible but required to avoid an incomplete analysis. That is exactly what the panel did here: because the Board limited the third‑party universe improperly, the court could not be sure the Board’s commercial‑impression finding was correct absent a proper factor‑six analysis. (Opinion §III.B–C.)

Practical rule of thumb for Board decisions and appeals
- If third‑party use/strength evidence is weak, undisputed, or plainly irrelevant to how consumers will understand the mark, the Board can decide factor one on appearance/sound/connotation alone.
- If third‑party use/strength is disputed and could materially change whether consumers view the mark as strong/unique or weak/crowded, the Board should fully analyze factor six and then (re)assess factor one in light of that strength determination. Failure to do so risks vacatur for an incomplete likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis (as the panel remedied here). (See Opinion §§III.B–C.)

Bottom line
The court treated commercial strength as a potentially necessary input to the commercial‑impression inquiry only because factor six could materially change the marketplace perception of the ASPIRE mark. It did not announce a per se rule that factor one always requires prior resolution of factor six; rather, it required the Board to correct its defective factor‑six analysis and then reassess factor one because strength was contested and relevant to commercial impression in this case.
