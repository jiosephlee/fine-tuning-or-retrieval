Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# One Scope to Rule Them All: Federal Circuit Demands Consistency Across DuPont Factors

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an important reminder that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board cannot define the relevant marketplace or class of consumers for one DuPont factor and then change the rules for another without explanation. In a short, pragmatic opinion, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services were highly similar (the second DuPont factor), but vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor — and, as a consequence, the first factor — because the Board improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party “Aspire” uses it would consider. The court’s instruction is straightforward: once the Board fixes the scope of similarity for goods and services, it must carry that scope through its evaluation of other DuPont factors that rely on the same underlying marketplace concept.

That instruction matters in practice because many DuPont inquiries depend on how the Board characterizes the relevant consumers and the relatedness of the parties’ goods or services. A defendant (or applicant) who wins a narrow characterization at step two should not be permitted to escape a crowded‑field analysis at step six by artful narrowing; nor should an applicant be able to stretch one finding across factors without record support. Apex clarifies the correct mechanics: similarity under factor two informs which third‑party marks are relevant under factor six, and that link must be respected.

## What the court did and why

The Board found that CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration (for credit card services) and Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK mark (for banking and financing services) were highly similar — even “legally identical, in part” — and therefore treated the second DuPont factor as weighing heavily in favor of confusion. But when the Board turned to the sixth factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods), it limited its inquiry to third‑party uses of Aspire in the credit‑card context and excluded Aspire uses in other banking and financing contexts. The Federal Circuit held that bifurcating the analytical scope in that way was erroneous: the sixth factor only requires similarity, not identity, of goods and services, and where the Board has already found high similarity (or partial legal identity), it must use that same breadth when evaluating the sixth factor.

The court relied on settled precedent — Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, and related decisions — which long ago made clear that the sixth factor looks to similar, not necessarily identical, goods and services. Because the Board’s factor‑two finding encompassed banking and financing as sufficiently like credit‑card services, excluding third‑party uses tied to banking or other financing services from the factor‑six inquiry was inconsistent with that earlier finding. The court therefore vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and remanded for reconsideration. The court also vacated and remanded the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks) because the commercial impression analysis can shift depending on how crowded or weak a mark is found to be under factor six.

## Analytical perspective: coherence, not uniformity

The Federal Circuit’s holding is doctrinally modest but practically significant. Modest, because the court did not announce a new test or rework DuPont; it applied existing precedents about relatedness and crowded fields. Significant, because it enforces a coherence principle that the Board must already have been following in theory but sometimes neglected in practice: the factual findings that drive one DuPont factor (particularly the characterization of the relevant marketplace and consumers) should inform the scope of evidence considered under other factors.

This coherence principle does not mean that the Board is mechanically bound to replicate every finding across all factors. DuPont is flexible: the Board may, with reasoned explanation and evidentiary support, adopt narrower or broader characterizations for particular inquiries (for example, focusing on a particular subset of consumers when evaluating actual confusion). But Apex underscores that any such narrowing must be justified on the record. The Board cannot, after finding the services “highly similar,” suddenly constrict the third‑party mark universe to a subset that produces a different result without reconciling that choice with its prior findings.

The court’s vacatur of factor one is also analytically sound. The similarity‑of‑marks analysis is not performed in isolation; the mark’s commercial strength — which the sixth factor informs — affects whether consumers are likely to confuse two marks that look or sound alike. Thus a remand on factor six can and often will change the factual backdrop for factor one.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Practically, Apex will change how counsel litigate oppositions and dispositions before the TTAB in several predictable ways. First, litigants will be more likely to submit broad third‑party evidence across all related banking and financial services once a Board finding narrows or broadens the relevant marketplace. Applicants should prepare to defend a wide universe of third‑party uses if the Board has already found goods or services similar. Conversely, registrants who secure a finding of similarity for one factor should be prepared to defend the same scope when the opponent offers crowded‑field evidence.

Second, the opinion invites the Board to be clearer and more explicit in its findings about the relevant consumer class and why it chooses any narrower scope for later DuPont inquiries. The Board can still focus on the most relevant subset of consumers, but it must tie that narrowing to record evidence (e.g., distinct channels of trade, separate target consumers, or limiting nature of the goods) rather than invoke different scopes for different factors as a matter of convenience.

Third, expect more remands in cases where the Board’s DuPont analysis shows internal inconsistencies between findings. That will likely increase the evidentiary burden on both sides to assemble comprehensive third‑party use proofs early, and to develop argumentation about why a narrower or broader universe of marks is appropriate for particular DuPont inquiries.

Finally, Apex reinforces a salutary limit on cherry‑picking. Trademark adjudication turns on rival considerations: mark similarity, goods’ relatedness, marketplace reality, and the overlay of third‑party use. The Federal Circuit’s direction here nudges the Board toward a more holistic, internally consistent analysis that better tracks consumers’ real‑world decisionmaking.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a useful corrective: it tells the TTAB (and litigants) to be consistent in how they define the relevant marketplace across DuPont factors. Similarity in one factor should generally mean similar scope in others unless the Board supplies a reasoned, evidence‑based justification for a different view. That principle preserves the integrity of the multifactor DuPont inquiry and reduces the opportunity for strategic narrowing that distorts the crowded‑field analysis. Practitioners should take this ruling as a prompt to litigate and document the universe of related goods and services comprehensively from the outset — and to press the Board for reasoned explanations whenever it deviates from its own prior factual framework.
