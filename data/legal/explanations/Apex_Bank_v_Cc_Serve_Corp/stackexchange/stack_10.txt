Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### How can a mark get the 'normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks' despite no showing of commercial strength?
Question:
The Board found CC Serve hadn’t shown particular commercial strength but still gave ASPIRE the normal scope of protection for inherently distinctive marks. How do courts reconcile conceptual (inherent) strength with limited commercial strength when setting the scope of protection? Under what circumstances would a mark’s commercial weakness narrow its protection even if it is inherently distinctive?
Answer:
Short answer
Courts treat two different kinds of strength separately: conceptual (inherent) strength and commercial (market) strength. A mark that is inherently distinctive is entitled to the “normal” scope of protection that flows from that conceptual distinctiveness unless evidence shows it is commercially weak (e.g., the field is crowded, consumers are educated to distinguish similar marks, or third‑party uses are widespread). In this opinion the Board found no particular commercial strength but also found no evidence of substantial commercial weakness, so it gave ASPIRE the usual protection for an inherently distinctive mark. See J.A.51. The Federal Circuit then vacated the Board’s limited sixth‑factor analysis because the Board had improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party uses it should consider (contradicting its finding that the services are highly similar), and remanded so the Board can reassess commercial strength and, if necessary, adjust the scope of protection under the first and sixth DuPont factors. See J.A.27–28, 47, 51–52.

Legal doctrine and how the two strengths interact
- Conceptual (inherent) strength: if a mark is inherently distinctive (arbitrary, fanciful, or suggestive), it is presumptively entitled to the “normal” scope of protection against confusingly similar marks. The Board relied on this principle when it said CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark “is entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A.51.
- Commercial (market) strength: evidence that many competitors use similar marks for similar goods/services can show that the mark is relatively weak in the marketplace and therefore deserves a narrower scope of protection. The sixth DuPont factor is the vehicle for that inquiry: third‑party registrations/uses on similar goods are relevant to show weakness. See J.A.40–47; Palm Bay Imps. v. Veuve Clicquot, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (cited at J.A.47); Juice Generation, 794 F.3d 1334 (cited at J.A.47).
- Interaction: courts do not conflate the two. Conceptual distinctiveness establishes baseline protection; commercial evidence can reduce (narrow) that protection if it shows the mark is weak in the market for the relevant goods or services. The Board here said CC Serve had not shown “particular commercial strength” but equally had not shown commercial or conceptual weakness sufficient to narrow protection (J.A.51). The Federal Circuit rejected the Board’s limited approach to the sixth factor because the Board had already found the services highly similar and therefore should have considered third‑party uses across the broader (but similar) set of banking/finance services, not just narrow “credit card” entries (J.A.27–28, 47–52). That error required remand so the Board can determine whether broader third‑party use evidence shows commercial weakness that would narrow ASPIRE’s protectable scope.

When commercial weakness will narrow protection (practical guide)
A mark’s commercial weakness will narrow its protection when the record shows, in the relevant marketplace, that consumers have been educated to distinguish many similar marks or that many competitors use substantially similar marks on similar goods/services. Typical indicators:
- Numerous third‑party registrations and actual marketplace use of the same or very similar term on the same or closely related goods/services (the opinion cites examples and compares “nine” credit‑card uses to prior cases finding “considerable” or “voluminous” use). See J.A.40–47, J.A.47 (discussing nine Aspire‑formative marks and comparing to cases with 14, 26 examples).
- Evidence that the field is “crowded” such that purchasers routinely distinguish among similar marks (consumer education/sophistication evidence). See Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin discussion cited at J.A.47.
- Relevant marketplace is defined broadly enough to capture those third‑party uses: as the court explained, once the Board found the parties’ services “highly similar,” it should not artificially limit the universe of third‑party marks (J.A.27–28, 47–52). If the Board, on remand, finds widespread use across that broader scope, it may conclude ASPIRE is commercially weak and narrow its protection.

Takeaway for this case
- The Board gave ASPIRE the normal protection of an inherently distinctive mark because it found no meaningful evidence of commercial weakness in the limited set of third‑party uses it considered (J.A.51).
- The Federal Circuit ordered reconsideration because the Board limited the sixth‑factor comparison to credit‑card‑only uses even though it had already found the parties’ services highly similar (partially legally identical). On remand the Board must consider a properly scoped set of third‑party uses; if those uses show the mark is commercially weak, the Board should narrow ASPIRE’s protective scope and that will affect the first DuPont factor (commercial impression). See J.A.27–28, 47–52.

In short: conceptual distinctiveness gives a baseline, but commercial weakness—proved by substantial third‑party uses in the relevant market—can narrow that baseline. The opinion turns on whether the Board examines the correct universe of similar uses when deciding whether ASPIRE is commercially weak.
