### On remand from the Federal Circuit, can the TTAB reopen the record to accept additional third-party evidence?
Question:
The court vacated and remanded for reconsideration of factors six and one. Is the TTAB limited to the existing administrative record on remand, or does it have discretion to allow supplementation (e.g., additional third-party uses/registrations)? What rules or cases govern record reopening after a judicial remand in TTAB proceedings?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The Federal Circuit’s decision vacated the Board’s findings on DuPont factors six and one and remanded for reconsideration “in light of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” That mandate requires the Board to consider the broader universe of third‑party “Aspire” uses and the marks’ commercial strength. If the existing administrative record does not contain sufficient third‑party evidence to perform that reconsideration, the Board should allow supplementation on remand; if it refuses, the Board risks an inadequate record and a further remand.

Why (what the opinion actually says)
- The court expressly vacated the Board’s DuPont‑six finding and remanded “for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Opinion, pp. 6–7.)
- The court then vacated the DuPont‑one analysis and remanded “for the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods and the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks in light of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Opinion, pp. 7–8.)
- The court tied its remand to a factual determination (the services are highly similar) and directed the Board to reconsider the DuPont factors “in light of” that finding. That instruction is substantive: the Board must evaluate third‑party evidence covering the broader class of financial/banking services—not the narrower subset of only “credit card services” that it previously considered.

What that means for reopening the record
- The opinion does not specify procedures for remand or say the Board must accept particular new exhibits. But the remand necessarily contemplates that the Board will consider third‑party uses beyond those in the present record (because the court held the Board’s prior restriction of the universe of marks was error and remanded to correct it). If the existing record lacks evidence necessary to perform the mandated analysis, the Board must give the parties a meaningful opportunity to present that evidence on remand.
- Practically, that means the Board has discretion over the mechanics (e.g., whether to reopen the record, what form submissions must take, briefing schedule), but it cannot confine itself to the Board’s prior, legally flawed scope of evidence when deciding factors one and six. A remand that prevents the parties from introducing plainly responsive third‑party evidence would be inconsistent with the court’s instruction to reconsider those factors “in light of” the services‑similarity finding.

What to do in practice (for counsel)
- On remand, move to supplement the record (or file a written proffer and request leave) with any additional third‑party registrations/uses that show Aspire/Aspire‑formative marks used in banking/financial services beyond “credit card services.”
- Ask the Board to adopt a briefing schedule and to allow limited new evidence focused on the broader category the Federal Circuit identified.
- If the Board refuses to permit supplementation and then limits its reconsideration to the pre‑remand record in the same legally erroneous way, preserve the issue for appeal by renewing the request and creating a record of the denial.

Bottom line
The Federal Circuit’s mandate requires the Board to consider a broader universe of third‑party marks when it reanalyzes DuPont factors six and one. The opinion does not micromanage procedure, so the TTAB has procedural discretion, but it must permit sufficient evidence on remand to comply with the appellate court’s substantive directions.
