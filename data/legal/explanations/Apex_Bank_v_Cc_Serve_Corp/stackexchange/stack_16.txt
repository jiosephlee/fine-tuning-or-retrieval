### What is the significance of CC Serve’s 1996 priority date here, and why didn’t priority factor into the analysis?
Question:
The opinion notes CC Serve’s effective priority date (1996) but doesn’t discuss priority further. In a § 2(d) opposition, when is priority dispositive, and when is it effectively assumed (e.g., when the opposer owns a prior registration)? Would anything have changed if Apex had earlier common-law rights for different services?
Answer:
Short answer: CC Serve’s 1996 effective priority date meant it was a prior user/registrant with respect to Apex’s 2019 intent‑to‑use application, so priority wasn’t a live dispute in the Board’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. In a § 2(d) opposition, priority is dispositive only when it is disputed (i.e., when the applicant can show earlier rights than the opposer for the relevant goods/services). Where the opposer owns a valid earlier federal registration (or otherwise establishes earlier use for the same goods/services), the Board treats priority as established and proceeds to the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. If Apex had earlier common‑law rights, the outcome would depend on whether those rights predated CC Serve’s priority and whether they covered the same or sufficiently similar services.

Explanation tied to the opinion

- CC Serve’s registration and priority. The opinion records that CC Serve “is the owner of Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE” and that the registration “has an effective priority date of October 17, 1996.” (J.A. 6927–28; J.A. 6930.) Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications in August 2019 (J.A. 7027). Those facts establish that CC Serve had earlier federal rights (via its registration) long before Apex’s filing.

- § 2(d) framework and priority. The court repeats the governing principle that a § 2(d) opposition requires refusal “when ‘confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods,’” quoting DuPont. (Opinion § II, citing DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1360.) That language shows why priority is threshold: the statutory refusal turns on confusion with a prior user. Once a party shows it is the prior registrant/user for the relevant goods/services, priority is not a separate issue to litigate further unless the applicant challenges it.

- Why the Board didn’t discuss priority further here. Because CC Serve’s registration dated to 1998 with an effective 1996 priority date and Apex’s applications were filed in 2019, priority was effectively established in CC Serve’s favor and therefore not litigated further. The Board proceeded directly to the DuPont factors (see generally Parts III.A–C) and the court reviewed those factors. The Board also sustained the opposition (J.A. 63), reflecting that it treated CC Serve as the prior user/registrant and focused on likelihood of confusion.

- What would change if Apex had earlier common‑law rights? Priority is goods/services specific. If Apex could demonstrate earlier common‑law priority that predates CC Serve’s 1996 date and that covers the same (or legally highly similar) services at issue, that would put priority in play and could defeat a § 2(d) opposition or at least narrow the scope of the dispute. But two points follow from the opinion:
  1. The Board found the parties’ services to be “highly similar” (even “partially legally identical”) (J.A. 27–28), so an applicant’s earlier rights would need to cover those same services or sufficiently similar services to override CC Serve’s earlier federal priority.
  2. If the applicant’s earlier common‑law use were limited to different services that are not similar to CC Serve’s credit‑card services, those earlier rights would not likely displace CC Serve’s priority for the services in dispute — because priority is compared with respect to the goods/services that actually overlap. The court’s discussion of service similarity (Part III.A) underscores that the relevant comparison is whether the uses are for the same or related services.

Bottom line: The opinion shows why the Board and court treated priority as a nonissue here—CC Serve’s 1996 effective date (via its federal registration) predated Apex’s 2019 filings, so the dispute moved straight to the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. Priority would only have mattered if Apex had credible earlier rights (in time and scope) that covered the same or sufficiently similar services to displace CC Serve’s earlier priority.
