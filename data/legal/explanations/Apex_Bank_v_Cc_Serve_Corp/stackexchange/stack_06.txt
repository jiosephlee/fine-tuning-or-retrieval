Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### On remand, can the TTAB reopen the record to accept new evidence on third-party use and commercial strength?
Question:
The Federal Circuit vacated the TTAB’s findings on DuPont factors six and one and remanded for reconsideration. Is the Board limited to the evidentiary record as it stood, or may it accept additional evidence (e.g., more third-party uses or marketplace context) on remand in an inter partes opposition? What rules govern reopening the record after a Federal Circuit remand in a Section 2(d) case, and does the nature of the identified legal error (scope of similarity) affect whether new evidence is permitted or required?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The Federal Circuit’s remand in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. requires the Board to reexamine DuPont factors six and one using the correct scope of similarity, and that reconsideration can (and, if the existing record is inadequate, should) include additional third‑party and marketplace evidence. The opinion itself makes clear the Board misapplied the scope of “similar” marks and remanded for reconsideration “in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar,” so the Board must consider a broader universe of third‑party uses and the effect that consideration has on the mark’s commercial strength and overall commercial impression.

Why — legal standard and what the opinion says
- DuPont factor six looks to “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” to show relative weakness or narrow scope of protection. Apex relies on that principle (citing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, etc.).
- The Federal Circuit held that the Board “restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and excluded marks related to other banking and financing services,” and that was error because the Board had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (Part III.B). The court therefore vacated factor six and remanded “for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”
- The court also vacated factor one because “reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression,” so the Board must reassess the marks’ appearance/sound/connotation/ commercial impression in light of any revised analysis of weakness (Part III.C).

What that means for the record on remand
- The remand’s substance (vacatur and instruction to reconsider with a broader scope) necessarily contemplates that the Board consider third‑party uses beyond the narrow credit‑card‑only subset it relied on. If marks or uses already in the original record (for example, Apex’s asserted 42 Aspire‑formative uses) were excluded by the Board’s erroneous scope, the Board must now consider them.
- If the existing administrative record does not contain adequate evidence to apply the correct scope or to assess commercial strength and consumer perception for that broader universe, the Board can and should allow supplementation. Reconsideration under the correct legal standard may require fact‑finding (e.g., additional third‑party use examples, evidence of marketplace ubiquity, consumer surveys, sales/advertising figures) to reach a supported factual conclusion on factor six and any knock‑on effect on factor one.
- Conversely, if the record already contains sufficient, probative evidence for the broader scope, the Board need only reweigh and explain its conclusions under the correct standard.

Does the type of legal error affect whether new evidence is allowed or required?
- Yes. Where the error is purely legal and the record already contains adequate evidence for the correct legal standard, the agency may simply reanalyze the existing record (no new evidence required). The court’s affirmance of factor two illustrates that the Board’s factual findings can stand even when other factors must be reassessed under the correct law.
- Where the error involves an improperly narrowed scope that excluded material categories of evidence (as here), remand ordinarily requires the Board to consider that broader category — and if the record is deficient for that broader inquiry, the Board should accept new evidence. The Federal Circuit’s instruction that the Board consider “the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration” and its linkage of factor six to factor one makes clear that the Board must address whatever evidence is necessary to perform that broadened analysis.

Practical points for the parties and the Board
- Parties should re‑submit or identify any third‑party registrations/uses related to banking and financing (not just credit card services) and provide marketplace context or evidence of commercial strength/weakness they want the Board to consider on remand.
- If the Board believes the current record is sufficient, it should explain why; if it believes further evidence is needed, it should reopen the record (or request supplemental briefing/evidence) and permit the parties to submit it so that its factual findings on factors six and one rest on substantial evidence under the correct standard set out on remand.

Bottom line: Apex’s remand was based on a legal error in defining the universe of “similar” marks. The Board must reconsider factors six and one using the broader scope dictated by its finding that services are highly similar. That reconsideration may — and where the record is incomplete for the broader inquiry likely will — include new third‑party and marketplace evidence.
