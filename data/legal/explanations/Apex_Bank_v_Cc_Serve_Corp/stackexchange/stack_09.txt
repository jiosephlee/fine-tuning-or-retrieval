Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### How should the Board determine the scope of legacy identifications like 'credit card services' after deletion from the ID Manual?
Question:
Because 'credit card services' was deleted from the ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board used dictionary definitions to define its scope. Is that the correct interpretive approach? Does the deletion have any retroactive narrowing effect on earlier registrations? What sources (ID Manual at time of registration, dictionaries, common usage, prosecution history) are appropriate for construing legacy identifications?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes—the Board may rely on dictionary definitions (and other extrinsic evidence) to construe a legacy identification after the ID Manual entry for that term has been deleted. The Federal Circuit accepted the Board’s use of dictionary meanings here as part of its factual construction of the identified services. See J.A. 25–28 (Board’s dictionary-based definition) and the court’s affirmation of the second DuPont factor.
- No—the deletion of an ID Manual entry after registration does not automatically narrow the scope of earlier registrations. What controls is how the identification would have been understood—i.e., the registration’s own wording construed in light of evidence of meaning and trade usage (including the ID Manual as it existed at the time, if probative), not a later administrative deletion that retroactively alters prior registrations.
- Sources that are appropriate to construe a legacy identification include: the ID Manual as it existed at the time of registration (where relevant), dictionary definitions and other neutral extrinsic definitions, evidence of common usage in the trade (specimens, websites, advertisements), prosecution history and applicant/registrant statements, and third‑party registrations/uses — all evaluated as factual evidence of how the public would understand the identification. The Board’s reliance on dictionaries here was one permissible way to determine scope; the court treated that as part of the Board’s factual finding supported by substantial evidence. See J.A. 25–28, 33–34; Fed. Cir. review standard, J.A. 2–3.

Explanation and authorities from the opinion
- Why dictionaries were used here: the Board faced an identification—“credit card services”—that had been removed from the ID Manual after CC Serve’s 1998 registration, so the Board “first determined the meaning of the identified services” and concluded that “credit card services” encompassed a range of functions (issuing cards, processing transactions, managing accounts, alerts, etc.) based on dictionary definitions. The Board expressly relied on dictionary meanings when construing the phrase. J.A. 24–25.
- How the court treated that approach: the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding under the second DuPont factor that the parties’ services are “highly similar,” saying the Board “carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services, and substantial evidence supports” the Board’s finding. That endorsement shows the court viewed the Board’s dictionary-based construction as a permissible factual determination supported by evidence. J.A. 27–28 (affirming second factor); see also standard of review (legal conclusions de novo; fact findings for substantial evidence) J.A. 2–3.
- Why deletion is not a retroactive narrowing device: the opinion treats the task as one of construing what the identification means for purposes of comparing services. The Board’s job is to determine the scope of the registered identification as a matter of fact; it may rely on the best available evidence of meaning (dictionaries, trade usage, etc.). Nothing in the opinion suggests that a later PTO deletion of an ID Manual entry automatically rewrites earlier registrations’ scope. Rather, the Board construed “credit card services” on the record, and the court reviewed that factual construction. J.A. 24–28, J.A. 2–3.
- Appropriate sources listed by the opinion: the Board used dictionary definitions and also considered third‑party registrations showing overlap between credit-card and banking/finance services (J.A. 25, 33–34). The court criticized a different aspect of the Board’s analysis (limiting third‑party marks considered under DuPont 6) but did not fault the Board for using dictionaries or third‑party evidence to define the services. J.A. 33–35, 47. That demonstrates that (1) dictionaries and (2) third‑party registrations/uses are appropriate factual evidence to establish scope; by extension, prosecution history, specimens, and common commercial usage are also appropriate evidence to the extent they show how consumers and traders understand the term.

Practical takeaway for practitioners and the Board
- When construing a legacy identification whose ID Manual entry has been deleted, gather and present: the ID Manual as it existed at the relevant time (if available and probative), contemporary dictionary and industry‑specific definitions, specimens and marketing material, prosecution statements, and relevant third‑party registrations/uses. The record should show what the ordinary consumer or relevant trade understands the identification to cover.
- Don’t assume a deletion of an ID Manual entry will automatically narrow prior registrations—prove narrowing (or breadth) through evidence of meaning and commercial practice.

In short: the Board’s use of dictionaries here was a permissible means of factual construction; deletion of an ID Manual entry does not by itself retroactively narrow earlier registrations; and multiple evidence sources (ID Manual at the relevant time, dictionaries, trade usage, prosecution history, and third‑party registrations/uses) are appropriate for construing legacy identifications. The Federal Circuit treated the dictionary-based construction as substantial evidence supporting the Board’s finding on the services’ scope (J.A. 24–28, affirmed as to DuPont 2).
