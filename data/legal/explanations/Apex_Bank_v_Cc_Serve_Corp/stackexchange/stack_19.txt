### Does substantial-evidence review require deference to the Board’s assessment that 'only nine' third-party uses were insufficient?
Question:
The Board characterized nine third-party uses as not 'considerable' based on other cases. The Federal Circuit did not endorse that assessment but vacated for using too narrow a set of goods/services. If the Board had used the correct scope, would the Federal Circuit still defer to its qualitative assessment of whether the (now-expanded) third-party showing demonstrates weakness, or is there a threshold where the question becomes legal rather than factual?
Answer:
Short answer: Substantial-evidence review will usually require deference to the Board’s qualitative judgment about whether an expanded set of third‑party uses makes a mark “weak,” but only so long as the Board applies the correct legal standard and its determination is supported by enough evidence that a reasonable mind could accept it. If the Board misstates the law (or adopts an impermissibly categorical numerical rule), the court reviews the legal error de novo; and if no reasonable factfinder could accept the evidence, the court will set the finding aside as unsupported.

Why, and how that follows from the opinion

- The DuPont factors are principally factual. The opinion reiterates the settled rule that the Board’s findings on the DuPont factors are findings of fact reviewed for substantial evidence (citing M2 Software, Bose, and DuPont). See pp. 3–4 (collecting authority). The sixth DuPont factor — “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” — is the kind of factual finding the Board makes to assess commercial strength/weakness. The Federal Circuit treated the Board’s choice of which third‑party uses to consider as a legal error (too narrow a scope), and therefore vacated and remanded that part of the analysis. See pp. 6–8.

- Deference follows when the Board applies the law correctly and weighs evidence. Because the court characterized the sixth factor as one where the Board must consider similar (not identical) goods and must be consistent with the scope already found under factor two, the proper next step is for the Board to reconsider the third‑party evidence under the correct scope. Once the Board does so, its qualitative judgment about whether the (now‑expanded) third‑party showing is “considerable” or otherwise shows weakness is a factual determination to be tested on appeal by the substantial‑evidence standard. That is why the court vacated and remanded instead of resolving the sufficiency question itself — it directed the Board to take the factual step the Board had skipped. See pp. 6–8.

- The limits of deference. The Federal Circuit’s opinion also points to the limits of substantial‑evidence deference:
  - If the Board misapplies legal standards — for example, by limiting “similar marks” to only identical goods when it previously found the goods highly similar — the appellate court reviews and corrects that legal error (that is what the court did here). See pp. 6–8.
  - If the Board adopts an impermissible bright‑line rule (e.g., “nine uses can never be ‘considerable’”), that would be a legal error subject to de novo review. The opinion criticized the Board for narrowing scope, not for stating a numeric immutability, but the principle applies: numerical counts are evidence, not a dispositive legal formula. See discussion of Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation on pp. 6–7 (the Board relied on cases that treated “voluminous” or “26” examples as notable; those cases are examples of evidence, not legal thresholds).
  - Finally, even where the issue is factual, a finding cannot stand if “no reasonable mind might accept it” (the familiar substantial‑evidence baseline quoted at p. 3).

Practical guidance for the remand

- On remand the Board should (and the court expects it to) consider the full universe of third‑party uses that are similar under the Board’s own factor‑two finding (pp. 6–8).
- The Board should weigh qualitative indicators (number of uses, geographic reach, duration of use, strength of each use, consumer recognition, whether the third‑party uses are bona fide commercial uses vs. mere registrations) — exactly the types of considerations the court described as relevant in the sixth‑factor context (pp. 5–7, with citations to Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin).
- If, after that careful factfinding, the Board concludes that the expanded evidence is or is not enough to show weakness, that conclusion will be reviewed for substantial evidence; if the Board misapplies the law or treats a raw number as determinative, the court will review that part de novo.

Bottom line: The Federal Circuit’s opinion leaves the qualitative question to the Board on remand and will defer to the Board’s factual assessment so long as the Board uses the correct legal scope and presents evidentiary support that a reasonable mind could accept.
