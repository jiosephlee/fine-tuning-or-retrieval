### Did the Chenery principle drive the decision to vacate and remand instead of reweighing factors six and one?
Question:
The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s sixth-factor analysis as legally flawed and then also vacated the first factor because commercial impression could change. Is this an application of SEC v. Chenery’s bar on affirming an agency decision on grounds the agency didn’t adopt? How does Chenery limit the Federal Circuit’s ability to reweigh DuPont factors or find harmless error?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The court’s choice to vacate and remand (rather than simply reweigh factors one and six itself or declare the errors harmless) reflects the Chenery principle — the appellate court will not affirm or substitute a new basis for an agency decision on grounds the agency did not itself adopt — combined with the limits of appellate fact review under the DuPont framework.

Why (legal doctrine and how it applies here)

- Chenery principle. SEC v. Chenery Corp. (1947) stands for the proposition that a reviewing court cannot affirm an agency action on grounds that the agency did not rely on or consider. The Federal Circuit’s opinion tracks that logic: it identifies a legal error in the Board’s sixth-factor analysis (the Board improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party “Aspire” marks to only credit-card‑specific uses), and then refuses to supply a substitute analysis or to reweigh the factors itself. Instead the court vacated the sixth‑factor finding and remanded for the Board to consider the broader set of similar marks consistent with its prior finding that the services are “highly similar.” See Op. at 6–8 (vacating the sixth factor because the Board “restricted the universe of marks … That was an error. … We thus vacate … and remand”).

- Limits on reweighing DuPont factors. DuPont factors are fact‑intensive. The court treats each DuPont factor as a factual finding subject to substantial‑evidence review. Because the Board’s legal error as to factor six could change the factual picture (how many similar marks exist; the mark’s commercial strength), the court correctly declined to resolve those factual issues itself. The opinion explains that the Board had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (second factor), and that when reconsidering factor six the Board must use the same scope for similarity; because that reconsideration could affect the mark’s commercial strength and thus the “commercial impression” under factor one, the court remanded factor one as well. See Op. at 6–8 (vacating factor six and remanding factor one because “reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression”).

- Harmless‑error doctrine and why it wasn’t applied. An appellate court may deem an agency error harmless if the record permits the court to conclude the error could not have affected the outcome. But under Chenery, the court cannot invent or rely on a different agency rationale; and under DuPont, the outcome depends on factual findings (number/nature of third‑party marks, commercial strength) that the Board has to make in the first instance. Here the panel concluded the Board’s error could meaningfully alter the factual determinations (and thus the likelihood‑of‑confusion balancing), so the court could not properly find the error harmless on the existing record. That is precisely why the court remanded rather than reweighing or affirming: the possible change in the sixth‑factor analysis could change factor one (commercial impression), and those are matters for the Board to address. See Op. at 7–8.

Takeaway for reweighing/harmless‑error questions

- The Federal Circuit will not reweigh DuPont factors when doing so would require resolving factual issues or adopting a rationale the Board did not articulate — Chenery forbids supplying a new basis for decision on review.  
- The court will find an error harmless only when the record permits the court to conclude the outcome would be the same despite the error. If the agency’s error could plausibly change key factual findings (here, the scope of similar marks and the related commercial strength/commercial impression), the court will vacate and remand so the agency can reconsider with the proper legal standard.

So: Chenery didn’t need a separate citation in the opinion to justify remand — the court’s reasoning and remedy are exactly what Chenery requires: don’t affirm on a ground the Board didn’t adopt and don’t substitute your own factfinding where the agency must address the issues in the first instance.
