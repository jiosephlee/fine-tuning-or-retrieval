### Why did the Board define the relevant public for factor six as 'consumers of credit card services,' and why did the Federal Circuit reject that framing?
Question:
The Board limited the sixth factor to credit card consumers and excluded banking/financing uses, calling them 'essentially irrelevant.' The Federal Circuit said this was too narrow once the Board had already found the services highly similar. What is the correct way to define the relevant consuming public when the identifications include broader 'banking and financing services,' and how does that definition influence which third-party marketplace evidence counts?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Board defined the relevant consuming public as “consumers of credit card services” because it narrowed the sixth DuPont inquiry to the subset of third‑party uses that expressly involved credit card services. See J.A.47–48 (Board focused “solely on the marks identified for credit card services”).
- The Federal Circuit rejected that framing because the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (indeed, partly legally identical) in its second‑factor analysis (J.A.27–28), so it may not then impose a stricter, narrower similarity requirement when deciding factor six. The court held that once services are found highly similar, the relevant consuming public and the universe of third‑party marks to consider must reflect that broader scope (see Parts III.B and the court’s remand instruction).

What the opinion requires you to do on remand (the correct approach)
1. Use the same scope for the relevant consuming public that the Board used (or should have used) in its second‑factor finding of similarity. Because the Board found credit card services and banking/financing services to be highly similar (and in part legally identical) (J.A.27–28), the appropriate relevant consuming public for factor six is not limited to only consumers who purchase credit card services but includes consumers of banking and financing services more broadly (i.e., the consumers of the services the Board already found similar).
2. Consider third‑party marketplace evidence across that full scope. The sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of third‑party uses of similar marks on similar goods/services—not strictly identical goods. The Federal Circuit cites Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation for that principle (see discussion at J.A.7). Thus on remand the Board must include third‑party uses of “Aspire” (and Aspire‑formative marks) in connection with banking and financing services generally, not only those tied expressly to credit card programs.
3. Evaluate what that broader evidence means for commercial strength. Evidence of many third‑party uses of “Aspire” across banking/financing services tends to show relative weakness (narrower scope of protection) because consumers familiar with the crowded field are more likely able to distinguish similar marks (see J.A.6–7; citing Palm Bay and Juice Generation). Conversely, if third‑party uses remain sparse even across the broader field, the mark retains normal protection.

How this influences which third‑party evidence counts
- Include third‑party marks used in the broader category the Board already found similar: credit card services plus other banking and financing services encompassed by Apex’s identifications (J.A.25–28, 33–34). The Board may not ignore third‑party uses solely because they are not labeled “credit card services” if those uses fall within the broader banking/finance marketplace the Board found to overlap.
- Exclude only third‑party uses that are outside the scope of services the Board found similar. If a third‑party use is for services that do not reasonably overlap with the parties’ services as found under factor two, it remains less relevant.
- Assess both quantity and nature of third‑party uses across the full scope: number of uses, prominence/ubiquity, and whether consumers for these services are likely the same or overlapping groups—because those facts determine whether the market is “crowded” and whether consumers have been educated to distinguish similar marks (J.A.6–7; Jack Wolfskin discussion cited by the Board at J.A.47).

Bottom line
The Board erred by narrowing factor six to “consumers of credit card services” after it had already found the parties’ services (credit card vs. banking/financing) highly similar. The Federal Circuit instructs that the relevant consuming public and the universe of comparable third‑party marks for factor six should mirror the scope of services the Board found similar under factor two, and that third‑party marketplace evidence should be evaluated across that broader set when deciding commercial strength and the mark’s scope of protection (remand directed: J.A.7–8).
