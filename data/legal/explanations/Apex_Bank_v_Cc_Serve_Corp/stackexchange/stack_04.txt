Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the Federal Circuit deem it legal error to narrow 'similar goods' under DuPont factor six compared to factor two?
Question:
The Board found the parties’ services 'highly similar' and 'legally identical, in part' for factor two but then limited the relevant marketplace to only credit card services for factor six. The Federal Circuit called this 'legally flawed.' Is there a rule that the scope of similarity adopted for one DuPont factor must be consistently applied across other factors, or is this consistency required only when the Board has already found a high degree of similarity?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no free‑standing, across‑the‑board rule that every DuPont factor must use an identical scope of goods/services. But where the Board has already made a factual finding of a high degree of similarity (or partial legal identity) under factor two, the Federal Circuit held it is legal error for the Board to adopt a narrower conception of “similar goods/services” when applying factor six. In other words: consistency is required in that circumstance.

Why the Court said the Board erred
- Factor two: the Board found the parties’ services “highly similar” and even “legally identical, in part.” See J.A. 27–28 (Board’s analysis of banking/credit card overlap) and the court’s affirmance of that factual finding (III.A).
- Factor six: the Board then limited the universe of third‑party “Aspire” uses it considered to only those used for credit‑card services, excluding uses on other banking/financial services. See J.A. 47 (Board limited relevant public to “consumers of ‘credit card services’” and excluded marks “related to other banking and financing services”).
- Federal Circuit’s reasoning: factor six requires consideration of similar (not necessarily identical) goods/services (citing Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation). Because the Board had already found the services highly similar under factor two, it was inconsistent and legally flawed to constrict the scope for factor six. The court: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar … the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.” Op. at J.A. 7 (pp. 6–7 of the slip opinion). The court therefore vacated the factor‑six finding and remanded for reconsideration with a broader universe of third‑party uses.

Legal doctrine behind the decision
- DuPont is a multi‑factor likelihood‑of‑confusion test; factors are distinct but interrelated. Findings of fact under one factor (e.g., similarity of services) will inform what’s probative under another factor (e.g., the number/nature of similar marks in the marketplace).
- Factor six’s purpose is to show commercial weakness (crowding) by evidence of third‑party use on “similar goods” — the standard is similarity, not strict identity. The court relied on precedent that factor six allows consideration of third‑party uses on similar goods/services (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation).
- Where the Board has made a specific factual finding that the services are highly similar (or partially legally identical), it must apply that factual scope consistently when evaluating factor six; otherwise the Board imposes an impermissibly stricter standard on factor six than the Board applied to factor two.

Practical takeaway for the Board (and practitioners)
- If the Board finds a high degree of similarity (or legal identity in part) under factor two, it should use the same scope of goods/services when gathering and assessing third‑party evidence under factor six.
- If the Board intends to narrow the universe for factor six, it should either (a) explain why the narrower scope is appropriate despite the factor‑two finding, or (b) reexamine its factor‑two finding—because the two findings must be reconcilable and supported by substantial evidence.

This is why the court vacated factor six (and remanded factor one as affected by factor six): the Board’s narrowing of the relevant market for factor six was inconsistent with its earlier factual finding under factor two. See pages cited above (III.A and III.B, J.A. 27–28, 47, and the court’s discussion at J.A. 6–7).
