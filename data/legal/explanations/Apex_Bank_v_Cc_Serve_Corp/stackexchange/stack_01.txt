Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why was the Board’s narrowing of DuPont factor six treated as a legal error rather than a factual one?
Question:
The opinion says legal conclusions are reviewed de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. Likelihood of confusion is a legal question with factual underpinnings. Here, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factor-two factual finding (services are highly similar) but vacated factor six because the Board considered only third-party marks for credit card services and excluded other banking/financing uses. Is the mistake characterized as legal because it applied the wrong legal standard for 'similar goods' under factor six, even though the underlying evidence was factual? How do courts decide when an error in the DuPont framework is legal (scope/standard) versus factual (weight of evidence)?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the Board didn’t just weigh the third‑party evidence differently — it applied the wrong legal scope for “similar” goods under DuPont factor six. The Federal Circuit treated that as a legal error (reviewed de novo) because the Board imposed a narrower legal standard on factor six than it had applied under factor two and than governing precedent allows.

Why: the governing review rules and the opinion
- As the opinion restates, likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion based on underlying factual findings under the DuPont factors. Legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382; Consol. Edison, 305 U.S. at 229 (J.A. 12–13).
- Each DuPont factor is usually a factual inquiry, but an appellate court will treat as legal any error that consists of mis-defining or mis-applying the legal standard that governs how those facts are to be considered. See the court’s statement that the Board’s narrowing “was an error” because the sixth factor “does not require identical goods—only similar ones.” (Opinion at J.A. 6–8).

How that applies here (what the Board did and why that was legal error)
- The Board found, as a factual matter under DuPont factor two, that Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” are highly similar (indeed “legally identical, in part”). (J.A. 27–28).
- Despite that factual finding, when analyzing factor six the Board limited the universe of third‑party marks it would consider to marks used on “credit card services” only and excluded marks used in other banking/finance contexts. (J.A. 47).
- The Federal Circuit held this was not merely a disagreement over weight or sufficiency of third‑party evidence but a legal mistake: the Board adopted a more stringent legal definition of “similar” for factor six than it applied under factor two and than precedent permits. Because the alleged error was a mis‑application of the legal standard (scope of marks to be considered), the Court vacated factor six and remanded. (J.A. 6–8).

How to decide legal vs factual errors in DuPont contexts (rule of thumb from the opinion)
- If the challenger asserts the Board used the wrong legal standard, applied inconsistent standards across factors, or misinterpreted controlling precedent about what the factor requires — that is a legal issue for de novo review. That is what happened here: an improperly narrowed legal definition of “similar” marks under factor six.
- If the challenger attacks whether the record contains enough third‑party uses, whether particular exhibits were persuasive, or whether a particular factual finding is supported by the evidence — those are factual issues for substantial‑evidence review.
- Put differently: ask whether the purported error is an issue of law (how to define/apply the factor) or an issue of fact (did the evidence support the Board’s application of the factor under the correct legal standard). The opinion treats the Board’s narrowing of the factor as the former, not the latter. (See discussion at J.A. 33–34; J.A. 47–51).

Bottom line: The Court vacated factor six because the Board legally narrowed the universe of “similar” marks it would consider in a way that was inconsistent with its own factual finding of highly similar services and with precedent holding factor six requires only similarity, not identity. That is a legal error (wrong standard/scope), not merely a dispute over the weight of the third‑party evidence.
