### What does it mean that the parties’ services are 'legally identical, in part,' and what presumptions follow?
Question:
The Board said 'credit card services' and 'banking and financing services' are 'legally identical, in part.' In TTAB practice, when services are identical (or overlap) as identified, does that trigger presumptions about overlapping channels of trade, classes of consumers, and the degree of purchaser care? How did that play into the heavy weight assigned to factor two here?
Answer:
Short answer
“Legally identical, in part” means the parties’ identified service descriptions overlap for at least some of the listed activities (here, both parties’ descriptions cover issuing and servicing credit-card accounts). Once the Board found that overlap, it treated the services as highly similar and (as the Board actually did) limited the relevant public to consumers of credit‑card services — a factual/legal consequence that supported giving the second DuPont factor heavy weight. The Federal Circuit affirmed that approach as to factor two (because substantial evidence supports the finding of high similarity), but it warned that the Board may not then narrow other analyses (e.g., what third‑party “Aspire” uses to consider under factor six) to a smaller universe than the scope it adopted for factor two.

What the opinion says, in more detail
- What “legally identical, in part” meant in this case: the Board interpreted the parties’ identifications of services and concluded that the phrase “credit card services” (as used in CC Serve’s registration) includes issuing cards, processing transactions, managing card accounts, providing credit monitoring, etc., and that the dictionary definitions of “banking” and “finance” encompass issuing credit and cards. See J.A. 25, 27–28. Because those activities appear in both parties’ descriptions, the Board called the services “legally identical, in part.” (Opinion § III.A, J.A. 24–28.)
- Immediate presumptions/consequences the Board drew: the Board treated the overlap as establishing a high degree of similarity between the parties’ services, concluded the relevant consumer group was “consumers of ‘credit card services,’” and thus gave heavy weight to DuPont factor two. See J.A. 33–35, 47. The court affirmed that factual/legal conclusion as to factor two: “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Op. § III.A.)
- Limits the court placed on using that finding elsewhere: because the Board had already found the services highly similar (indeed, partially identical), the court explained the Board should keep that same scope when it evaluates other DuPont factors. The court faulted the Board for narrowing the universe of third‑party marks under factor six to only those tied strictly to “credit card services” while excluding third‑party uses for other banking/finance services — even though the Board had itself found overlap between those categories. The court vacated factor six (and remanded factor one) so the Board will reconsider those factors using the same scope of similarity it adopted for factor two. See Op. § III.B–C.

What follows, practically
- If a Board finds two goods/services identical or overlapping as identified, that normally anchors other parts of the DuPont inquiry:
  - The relevant channels of trade and purchasers are likely to overlap (the Board here narrowed the relevant public to credit‑card consumers because of the overlap — J.A. 47).
  - That overlap tends to make the second DuPont factor carry more weight (the Board gave it “heavy” weight here and the court affirmed as supported by substantial evidence — J.A. 35; Op. § III.A).
- But you cannot adopt an overlap finding for factor two and then on factor six apply a narrower definition of similarity that is inconsistent with that finding; the same scope should govern related factual inquiries (the court remanded to correct that inconsistency — Op. § III.B).

Note on purchaser care: the opinion does not elaborate a separate holding about degree of purchaser care in this case; it focuses on (1) the services’ overlap and the resulting relevance of the credit‑card consumer public, and (2) the correct scope for considering third‑party uses. The practical effect, however, is that a finding of high similarity makes confusion more likely without needing separate proof of overlapping channels or customers, and so strengthens the weight of factor two (as happened here).
