Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the Board treat Apex’s broad 'banking and financing services' as encompassing credit card issuance even though Apex doesn’t currently issue cards?
Question:
Apex argued the parties’ services aren’t directly competitive, emphasizing it doesn’t offer credit cards. The Board still found the services 'legally identical, in part' based on dictionary definitions of 'banking' and 'finance' that include extending credit via credit cards. In Section 2(d) oppositions, is the similarity of services assessed strictly by the identifications in the application/registration rather than actual marketplace activities? How do tribunals handle broad service identifications that logically subsume more specific services the applicant does not (yet) offer?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in Section 2(d) oppositions tribunals begin with and often give dispositive weight to the identifications of services in the application/registration. If a claimant’s identification is broad enough that its plain meaning (as shown by definitions, the Trademark ID Manual, or other record evidence) encompasses a registrant’s narrower services, the Board may treat those services as legally identical even if the applicant does not yet offer the narrower service in the marketplace. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an example: the Board (and the Federal Circuit, as to the second DuPont factor) treated “banking and financing services” as encompassing credit‑card issuance because dictionary definitions showed that “banking” and “finance” include extending credit by cards. J.A.25, 27–28; opinion § III.A.

Why and how tribunals do this (doctrinal explanation tied to the opinion)

- Identification-driven inquiry: The second DuPont factor asks whether the parties’ goods/services are similar or related. As the court explained, the services need not be literally identical — the question is whether they are related or marketed in such a way that consumers could think they come from the same source. Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC is cited for that principle and the opinion applies it to the parties’ identified services. Op. § III.A (citing Coach Servs.); see also M2 Software/M2 Communications standard for review. J.A.24–28.

- Plain‑meaning/record evidence controls when given: Where the record shows the plain meaning of the identifications (here via dictionary definitions and the Trademark ID Manual entries), the Board relied on those definitions to conclude that “banking and financing services” encompass credit‑card services (issuing, processing, managing cards). J.A.25, 27–28. The Federal Circuit affirmed that factual finding as supported by substantial evidence, and therefore affirmed the Board’s conclusion on the second DuPont factor. Op. § III.A.

- Marketplace non‑use is not necessarily dispositive: Apex argued it doesn’t issue cards and thus the services aren’t directly competitive. The court rejected that argument because the Board’s analysis focused on the scope and plain meaning of the identified services and on third‑party registrations showing overlap; substantial evidence supported the Board’s conclusion despite Apex’s current non‑use. Op. § III.A (Apex’s argument “unavailing”).

- Third‑party evidence and scope: Tribunals will also consider third‑party registrations/uses to show that particular services are commonly provided together (and to assess commercial strength under the sixth DuPont factor). The Board used third‑party registrations as corroboration that credit‑card services and broader banking/finance services can emanate from the same source. J.A.33–34. The Federal Circuit did not fault that step; it only faulted the Board for then narrowing the universe of third‑party marks it considered under the sixth factor (the court held the Board should have included marks for similar—not necessarily identical—services once it had found the services highly similar). Op. §§ III.B, III.C.

Practical takeaway from the opinion (what tribunals will do)

- When an applicant uses a broad goods/services identification, the Board will interpret that identification according to its ordinary meaning as shown in the record (dictionaries, ID Manual, registrations, evidence of market practice). If that ordinary meaning subsumes the opponent’s narrower services, the services can be treated as legally identical for DuPont factor two even if the applicant is not yet offering the narrower service. See J.A.25, 27–28; Op. § III.A.

- The Board may and should then use a consistent scope when considering other DuPont factors (e.g., third‑party registrations under factor six and commercial impression under factor one). The Federal Circuit remanded here because the Board limited its sixth‑factor analysis to credit‑card marks only, despite its finding that the parties’ services were highly similar; the court required a broader universe of comparable marks consistent with that finding. Op. §§ III.B–C.

That explains why the Board treated Apex’s “banking and financing services” as encompassing credit‑card issuance even though Apex didn’t currently issue cards: the legal analysis turns on the identifications’ plain meaning and the record evidence that those terms cover card issuance, not solely on the applicant’s present marketplace offerings. The Federal Circuit affirmed that aspect of the Board’s DuPont‑two finding (but vacated and remanded parts of the analysis tied to factors one and six because those were applied inconsistently with the Board’s finding of high similarity). Op. §§ III.A–C.
