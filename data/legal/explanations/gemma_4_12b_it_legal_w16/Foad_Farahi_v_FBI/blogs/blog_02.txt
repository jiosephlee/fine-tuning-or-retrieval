Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Information, Not Words: The High Bar for Segregability in National Security Files

The tension between the public’s right to know and the government’s need to conduct sensitive investigations is a cornerstone of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) jurisprudence. When the FBI seeks to withhold records, the law requires it to perform a balancing act: it must protect the integrity of ongoing proceedings while releasing any "reasonably segregable" nonexempt information. However, as the District Court of Columbia Circuit recently clarified in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the definition of what constitutes "segregable" information can lean heavily in favor of the government when dealing with complex, high-volume investigative files.

In *Farahi*, the court dealt with a massive repository of information—over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs—related to an Iranian national's ties to terrorist organizations. The FBI withheld the bulk of these records under Exemption 7(A), arguing that disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. While the passage of time typically complicates the government's ability to prove that an investigation is still "ongoing," the court found the FBI’s evidence sufficient. The court looked past a somewhat "terse" update from a section chief, choosing instead to view it as a continuation of more detailed, ex parte declarations provided earlier. This reinforces a long-standing principle of deference to the executive branch in matters of national security and counterterrorism.

However, the most significant takeaway from *Farahi* lies in the court’s treatment of "segregability." The government is mandated to take "reasonable steps" to redact exempt material and release the rest. Farahi argued that in a file of such vast proportions, there must surely be some releasable information. The court’s response, however, underscored a high bar for plaintiffs: the distinction between "meaningful information" and "individual words." The court noted that if nonexempt material is "interspersed line-by-line" throughout a document, it is not "reasonably segregable." Because the focus of FOIA is on the substance of the information rather than the mere existence of unclassified words, the court allowed the FBI to withhold the records in their entirety.

This reasoning creates a powerful "all-or-nothing" mechanism for the government. By shifting the focus from the *existence* of nonexempt text to the *utility* of that text, the court provides the FBI with a robust defense for withholding massive volumes of records. If an investigative narrative is woven such that a single sentence of harmless information is embedded within a paragraph of sensitive intelligence, the agency can argue that the information is "inextricably intertwined" with the protected material. In practice, this means that for complex files where sensitive data is the primary "thread" of the narrative, the "reasonable steps" requirement may be satisfied by doing very little.

The doctrinal implications of *Farahi* are significant for the future of FOIA litigation. By emphasizing that FOIA is about "information, not simply words," the court has narrowed the scope of what a plaintiff can successfully demand in a high-volume disclosure case. For agencies, this provides a convenient standard: if a file is sufficiently dense with sensitive data, the "interspersed" defense becomes a default shield against granular disclosure. For transparency advocates, the ruling suggests that the sheer volume of modern surveillance and investigative files may effectively insulate them from public scrutiny, as the more complex the file, the easier it becomes for the government to claim that the public-facing pieces are not "meaningfully" segregable.
