Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Foundations of FOIA and Exemption 7(A)

## Overview of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the General Agency Duty to Disclose

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a foundational principle of transparency in government: federal agencies are generally required to release records to the public upon request. As the court notes in *Farahi*, this duty is the default state of the statute. However, this transparency is not absolute. The law provides a specific list of statutory exemptions that allow agencies to withhold certain types of information. When a requester like Farahi sues for records, the burden lies with the government to prove that the withheld information falls under one of these specific exemptions. If the government cannot meet this burden, it must disclose the records.

## The Specific Language of Exemption 7(A): 'Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes'

One of the most common hurdles for FOIA requesters is Exemption 7(A), which protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes." To successfully invoke this exemption, the government must show that the records in question were created as part of an actual law enforcement activity. In *Farahi*, the court emphasizes that because the FBI is a specialized law enforcement agency, its determination on whether a record was compiled for such purposes is entitled to a "fair measure of deference." 

However, this deference is not a blank check. The court requires that the government establish a "rational nexus" between the investigation and the agency’s law enforcement duties. This means the records must connect a specific individual or incident to a possible security risk or a violation of federal law. In this case, the FBI met this burden by demonstrating that Farahi’s file was compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism, specifically linking him to known terrorist organizations and individuals.

## The 'Interference' Test: Determining if Disclosure Could Reasonably Be Expected to Interfere with Proceedings

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." This is a forward-looking test; the government must argue that releasing the information would harm the integrity or success of a law enforcement action. 

In *Farahi*, the court accepted the FBI’s argument that releasing the records could "tip off" the targets about the scope of the ongoing investigation. This is a widely recognized justification in counterterrorism cases. The court looks for a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding"—meaning the government must show that the investigation is not merely theoretical but is a real, active effort to gather evidence or identify threats.

## Defining 'Proceedings': Distinguishing Between Pending and Reasonably Anticipated Actions

A critical point of contention in *Farahi* was whether the "proceedings" were still active. Under FOIA, "proceedings" are defined as those that are either "pending" (currently in progress) or "reasonably anticipated" (likely to happen in the near future). The court clarifies that an ongoing criminal investigation counts as a "proceeding" for the purposes of Exemption 7(A). 

Even if a formal court case has not yet been filed, if the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future case and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of evidence, the exemption applies. Farahi argued that the passage of time suggested the investigation might have stalled. The court rejected this, looking at the breadth of the investigation—which involved high-level terrorist links—and concluding that as long as the FBI maintains that the investigation is "ongoing," the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard is satisfied.

## The Government's Duty to Segregate Non-Exempt Information

Finally, the court addresses the agency's duty to "segregate" information. Even when a record falls under an exemption, the government is not permitted to withhold the entire document if only portions of it are exempt. Agencies must take "reasonable steps" to delete the exempt portions and release the rest. 

In *Farahi*, the court explained the limits of this duty through the concept of "inextricably intertwined" material. If exempt information is so woven into the document that it cannot be separated without destroying the meaning of the record, the government may withhold the entire file. The court clarifies a vital distinction for students: FOIA is about "information," not "words." While a plaintiff might argue that individual words or phrases are not secret, the government can withhold a document if the *meaningful information* it conveys is protected. Because the FBI demonstrated that the exempt material was not reasonably segregable, the court upheld the government's right to withhold the records.
