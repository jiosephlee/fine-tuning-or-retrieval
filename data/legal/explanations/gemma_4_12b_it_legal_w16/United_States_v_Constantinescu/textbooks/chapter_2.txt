Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Procedural Framework: Reviewing the Sufficiency of an Indictment

## The 'de novo' standard of review for indictments

When a district court dismisses an indictment and the government appeals that dismissal, the appellate court must determine the appropriate standard of review. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies that it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*. In legal procedure, a *de novo* review means the appellate court does not give any deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. Instead, the appellate court looks at the issue with "fresh eyes," performing its own independent analysis of whether the indictment meets the legal requirements to proceed.

## The 'take as true' rule: evaluating allegations without weighing evidence

A critical component of reviewing an indictment’s sufficiency is the "take as true" rule. Because an indictment is merely a charging document—not a final judgment on the facts—the appellate court is not tasked with deciding whether the defendants actually committed the crimes. Rather, the court must accept every allegation in the indictment as factually true for the purpose of the analysis.

Under this rule, the court's only question is whether the facts, as alleged in the indictment, satisfy the legal elements of the crime. If the government alleges a set of facts that, if true, would constitute a violation of the law, the indictment is sufficient. The court does not weigh evidence, consider the credibility of witnesses, or determine the likelihood of conviction; it only determines if the government has stated a valid offense.

## The three primary requirements for a sufficient indictment

To ensure that a criminal prosecution is fair and legally sound, the court identifies three primary requirements that an indictment must satisfy to be considered sufficient. First, the indictment must enumerate each *prima facie* element of the charged offense. This means the document must explicitly state the basic facts required to prove the crime.

Second, the indictment must "fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him." This ensures the defendant knows exactly what conduct the government is accusing them of so they can prepare a defense. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a "double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." This ensures that the government cannot repeatedly charge a defendant with the same conduct under different labels, protecting the defendant from being prosecuted twice for the same act.

## The requirement to allege each material element of the charged offense

The court summarizes these requirements into a core principle: to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each "material element" of the offense. A material element is a fundamental component of the crime's legal definition.

In the context of securities fraud, this means the indictment cannot simply allege a general "bad act." It must specifically allege the components that make up the fraud, such as a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud." If the indictment fails to describe the facts necessary to meet even one of these material elements, it is legally insufficient and must be dismissed. In *Constantinescu*, the focus of the review was specifically whether the indictment alleged the material elements of "scheme" and "intent" as required by the securities fraud statute.
