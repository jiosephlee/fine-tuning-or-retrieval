Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What does 'sufficiency of an indictment' mean during a de novo review?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural standard here. The court says it reviews the sufficiency of the indictment 'de novo' and is required to 'take the allegations of the indictment as true.' Does this mean the court is not allowed to question whether the defendants actually did anything wrong, but only whether the written charges are worded specifically enough to meet the legal elements of the crime?
Answer:
In short: **Yes, your interpretation is correct.** 

When a court reviews the "sufficiency of an indictment" *de novo*, it is not performing a final determination of guilt. Instead, it is performing a procedural check to ensure that the government has properly "stated" the crime in the charging document.

### The Legal Standard
The term ***de novo*** means the appellate court reviews the issue "anew," giving no deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. In the context of an indictment's sufficiency, the court follows a specific procedural rule: it must **"take the allegations of the indictment as true"** and determine whether those allegations, if true, constitute a crime.

As the court notes in *United States v. Constantinescu*, an indictment is sufficient if it:
1. Enumerates each *prima facie* element of the charged offense;
2. Fairly informs the defendant of the charges; and
3. Provides a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.

### Application to the Opinion
To understand how this works in practice, look at how the court handled the defendants' arguments in *Constantinescu*:

*   **The Lower Court's Error:** The district court dismissed the indictment because it believed the allegations didn't describe a traditional property theft. 
*   **The Appellate Court's Review:** The Fifth Circuit did not look for evidence of whether the defendants actually stole money or whether they were "bad people." Instead, it looked only at the **written allegations** in the indictment. 
*   **The "True" Test:** The court took the indictment's claims as facts. It looked at the text and determined that the indictment *did* allege a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud" by "fraudulently inducing" followers to "part with their money or property." 

Because the indictment contained these specific "material elements," the court reversed the dismissal. It didn't matter if the government could *prove* the defendants did these things yet; it only mattered that the government *alleged* them in a way that met the legal requirements of the statute.

### Summary for your notes
Think of the sufficiency review as a **"blueprint check."** The court is not checking if the house is actually built (the evidence); it is checking if the blueprint (the indictment) includes all the necessary rooms required by law (the elements of the crime). If the blueprint is complete, the indictment is "sufficient," and the case proceeds to trial where the evidence will be weighed.

### What is the legal distinction between the 'right-to-control' theory and the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory?
Question:
The court spends a lot of time distinguishing this case from Ciminelli. In Ciminelli, the government lost because they tried to prove fraud based on the 'right-to-control' theory (depriving someone of info). In this case, the court says the government is using 'fraudulent-inducement.' Can someone explain the practical difference? If a defendant lies to an investor about a stock price, isn't that depriving them of information? Why does the theory they use to describe the lie change whether the charge is valid?
Answer:
The distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory hinges on what specific "property" the government claims was stolen. To understand the difference, you have to look at what the Supreme Court considers a "traditional property interest" under federal fraud statutes.

### The Right-to-Control Theory (*Ciminelli*)
In *Ciminelli*, the government attempted to argue that fraud occurs when a defendant deprives a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" necessary to make an informed decision. Under this theory, the "property" being protected is the victim’s **right to control** their own assets. The logic is: if I lie to you about a fact, I have deprived you of the ability to make a rational choice about your money.

The Supreme Court rejected this in *Ciminelli* because the "right to information" (or the right to make a discretionary economic decision) is not a "traditional property interest." Because the statute only protects tangible things like money or land, a scheme that only "takes" information—even if that information is worth millions—does not technically constitute fraud under these specific federal laws.

### The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (*Constantinescu*)
The "fraudulent-inducement" theory, which the court applies in *Constantinescu*, shifts the focus from the *information* to the *transaction*. 

Under this theory, the property being protected is the victim's **actual money or property**. The lie (the misrepresentation of the stock price) is not the "property" being stolen; rather, the lie is the *tool* used to trick the victim into a contract or transaction where they voluntarily hand over their money. 

As the court notes in *Constantinescu*, this theory "protects money and property." In the "pump and dump" scheme, the government isn't just alleging that the defendants took away the followers' right to know the truth; they are alleging that the defendants used lies to induce the followers to **purchase securities**—an act that requires the followers to "part with their money or property."

### Summary of the Distinction
The difference is a matter of what the government identifies as the "object" of the fraud:

*   **Right-to-Control:** The "property" is the **decision-making process**. The crime is depriving the victim of the ability to make an informed choice. (Rejected by *Ciminelli*).
*   **Fraudulent-Inducement:** The "property" is the **victim's cash/assets**. The crime is using a lie to trick the victim into a transaction where they surrender those assets. (Accepted in *Constantinescu*).

To answer your specific question—*"If a defendant lies to an investor about a stock price, isn't that depriving them of information?"*—the answer is yes, but the law doesn't care about the "theft" of information. It only cares if that lie resulted in the victim handing over a traditional property interest (money). By framing the charge as "fraudulent inducement," the government moves the focus away from the "stolen" information and onto the "stolen" money, which satisfies the legal requirements of the securities fraud statute.

### How can there be a fraud conviction if the victim didn't suffer a 'net pecuniary loss'?
Question:
I'm struggling with the court's application of Kousisis. The opinion says that a defendant can be convicted even if they don't leave the victim economically worse off, as long as they 'obtain' the victim's money or property. In this 'pump and dump' scheme, the followers are buying securities. If the securities are of 'market value,' and the followers still own those securities, how did the defendants 'obtain' anything from the followers? It feels like the followers just traded cash for a stock, but it doesn't seem like the followers were actually 'deprived' of their property.
Answer:
The confusion you are experiencing arises from the distinction between the "right-to-control" theory of fraud and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. To understand how a conviction can occur without a net pecuniary loss, you have to look at what the law defines as the "object" of the fraud.

### The Right-to-Control Theory vs. Fraudulent Inducement
As the court explains in *Constantinescu*, the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli* rejected a theory of fraud where the only "harm" is the deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information." Under that theory, the property interest being protected is the victim’s right to make an informed decision about their own assets. Because "information" is not a traditional property interest, that type of fraud is not actionable.

However, the "fraudulent-inducement theory" (upheld in *Kousisis* and applied here) protects different interests. It focuses on the actual transfer of money or property. Under this theory, fraud occurs when a defendant uses a material misrepresentation to "trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." 

### The "Deprivation" of Property
The crux of your question is: *If the victim gets a stock of equal value, was there a deprivation?*

The *Constantinescu* court, citing *Kousisis*, answers this by shifting the focus from the victim's final balance sheet to the **act of obtaining** the property. The court notes that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when they "scheme to 'obtain' the victim’s 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

In the "pump and dump" scheme described in the opinion:
1.  **The Property:** The followers' money/cash.
2.  **The Act of Obtaining:** The followers were induced by false social media posts to purchase securities (i.e., to hand over their cash).
3.  **The Irrelevance of Value:** The court explicitly states that a defendant can be liable even if they "provide something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return." 

### Conclusion
From a legal standpoint, the "fraud" is the deception used to trigger the transfer of the followers' cash. Once the defendants successfully induced the followers to part with their money based on lies, the fraud was "complete." 

The fact that the followers received securities of "market value" does not undo the fact that the defendants successfully used fraudulent pretenses to "obtain" the followers' money. The law does not require a "mirror image" requirement where the victim’s loss must equal the defendant’s gain; it only requires that the defendant obtained property via a fraudulent scheme.

### Why is the court 'borrowing' concepts from mail and wire fraud for a securities fraud statute?
Question:
The opinion notes there is 'scant caselaw' for 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) and explains that the court is relying on mail and wire fraud precedents instead. Does this mean that the definition of 'securities fraud' is essentially just 'mail/wire fraud' with a different victim? Are there any specific elements of securities fraud that are being ignored or simplified by using these other statutes as a proxy?
Answer:
The court’s decision to "borrow" concepts from mail and wire fraud for the interpretation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) is a pragmatic judicial response to a lack of specific precedent. While it may appear to simplify the statute, the court is essentially applying a unified federal framework for what constitutes "fraud" to a specific type of transaction.

**Why the court is borrowing these concepts**
The court explicitly notes that there is "scant caselaw" specifically construing the securities fraud statute within its circuit. In such instances, courts often look to closely related statutes—specifically mail and wire fraud—to establish a consistent baseline for criminal liability. By doing so, the court ensures that the definition of "fraud" remains uniform across different types of white-collar crimes, preventing a situation where a defendant could commit a "securities fraud" that doesn't meet the standard definition of "fraud" found in other statutes.

**Is securities fraud just mail/wire fraud with a different victim?**
Not exactly. While the *mechanics* of the fraud (the "scheme" and "intent") are borrowed from mail and wire fraud, the *scope* remains distinct. The primary difference is the "nexus": securities fraud specifically requires the scheme to be conducted "in connection with... any security." 

The "victim" is not necessarily different, but the **protected interest** is. The court clarifies this by distinguishing between two theories of fraud found in mail and wire fraud jurisprudence:
1.  **The Right-to-Control Theory (Rejected):** This theory holds that fraud occurs when a defendant deprives a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" necessary to make decisions. The court, citing *Ciminelli*, rejects this because information is not a "traditional property interest."
2.  **The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Accepted):** This theory holds that fraud occurs when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over "money or property." 

By borrowing from mail and wire fraud, the court is adopting the **fraudulent-inducement theory**. In this view, the "securities" aspect of the statute defines *what* the transaction is, while the "mail and wire fraud" concepts define *how* the crime is committed (i.e., by tricking someone into parting with property).

**Are specific elements being ignored or simplified?**
The court is not ignoring elements so much as it is refining what "harm" means. By using the mail and wire fraud proxy, the court clarifies that the government does not need to prove "net pecuniary loss" (i.e., that the victim ended up with less money than they started with). 

Under the borrowed framework, the crime is complete the moment the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under false pretenses. Therefore, the court simplifies the *proof* required (by removing the need to show actual economic loss) but maintains the *requirement* that the defendant must target a traditional property interest rather than just depriving someone of information.

### At what point in a 'pump and dump' scheme is the 'object' of the fraud actually obtained?
Question:
The court says a fraud is complete when the defendant 'induced the deprivation of money or property.' In a pump and dump, the defendants lie to 'pump' the price (inducing followers to buy) and then they 'dump' (sell) their own shares. Does the 'object' of the fraud (the money/property) count as being 'obtained' at the moment the followers buy the stock, or is the 'object' the profit the defendants make when they sell their shares? I'm trying to understand which specific act in the timeline the court is identifying as the 'fraudulent-inducement.'
Answer:
Based on the court's opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the "object" of the fraud is obtained at the moment the followers are induced to purchase the securities.

The court distinguishes between two different theories of fraud to clarify exactly when the crime is "complete" and what the "object" is:

### 1. The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory
The court relies on the "fraudulent-inducement theory" (as established in *Kousisis v. United States*) to evaluate the defendants' actions. Under this theory, a fraud is committed when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." 

In the timeline of a pump and dump:
*   **The "Pump" (The Deception):** This is the act of making false and misleading statements on social media.
*   **The Purchase (The Completion):** This is the point of "fraudulent inducement." When the followers, relying on those lies, "part with their money or property" to buy the securities, the fraud is complete. The "object" is the money the followers handed over.

### 2. Distinction from the "Right-to-Control" Theory
The court specifically rejects the idea that the fraud is about the followers being deprived of "valuable economic information." That is the "right-to-control theory," which the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli*. Under that rejected theory, the "object" would be the information the followers lost. 

However, *Constantinescu* clarifies that the defendants here are not just depriving followers of information; they are "fraudulently inducing" them to "purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property."

### 3. The Defendant's Profit vs. The Victim's Deprivation
You asked if the "object" is the profit the defendants make when they sell. The court clarifies that while the defendants' profit is the *motive* for the scheme, it is not the point of completion for the "fraudulent inducement." 

The court explicitly states: 
> "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

This means the law does not require the victim to end up "broke" or the defendant to have finished their "dump" for the fraud to be complete. The crime is fully realized the moment the victim is tricked into the transaction of handing over their money. The defendants' subsequent profit ("the dump") is a result of the fraud, but the "fraudulent-inducement" occurs the moment the followers are tricked into buying.
