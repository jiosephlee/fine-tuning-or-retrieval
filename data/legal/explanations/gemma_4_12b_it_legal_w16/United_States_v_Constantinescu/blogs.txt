Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Distinguishing 'Right-to-Control' from 'Fraudulent Inducement' in the Wake of *Ciminelli*

The landscape of federal fraud prosecutions has undergone a significant shift in the last year. With the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*, the judiciary has signaled a move toward a more formalistic, property-centric interpretation of the mail, wire, and securities fraud statutes. By narrowing the scope of what constitutes "fraud," the Court has effectively killed the "right-to-control" theory—the idea that a defendant commits fraud simply by depriving a victim of the information necessary to make a discretionary economic decision. However, as the Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* demonstrates, there remains a robust, albeit more narrowly defined, path for the government to prosecute sophisticated schemes like "pump and dump" social media frauds.

## The Death of the Right-to-Control Theory

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, one must first look at the boundaries established in *Ciminelli*. In that case, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. When the government attempts to argue that a defendant's lie caused a victim to make a poor choice with their own assets, the Court has demanded more: a traditional property interest must be at stake. Under the rejected "right-to-control" theory, the victim’s "property" was their right to control their assets based on accurate information. The Supreme Court rejected this, insisting that the federal fraud statutes are not designed to police every instance of bad information, but rather to protect against the theft or misappropriation of tangible property.

The challenge for federal prosecutors in the wake of *Ciminelli* is ensuring that indictments do not fall into this trap. If an indictment merely alleges that a defendant lied to a victim, and that lie caused the victim to make a "bad" investment, the case may be dismissed for failing to state an offense. To survive, the government must now be much more precise in describing the mechanics of the deception, moving away from general "economic harm" and toward specific "property transfers."

## Fraudulent Inducement as the Solution

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* navigated this minefield by distinguishing between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In the *Constantinescu* case, the defendants were accused of a classic social media "pump and dump": they allegedly used their large followings to post false information, inducing followers to purchase securities. The district court dismissed the indictment, arguing it only alleged a deprivation of economic information. 

The Fifth Circuit disagreed, noting that the indictment didn't just allege the defendants lied to the followers; it alleged they tricked the followers into *purchasing* the securities. This is the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. As the Court highlighted via *Kousisis*, fraudulent inducement occurs when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. Unlike the right-to-control theory, this theory is squarely rooted in property. It doesn't matter if the victim's "choice" was bad; what matters is that the victim was tricked into the act of parting with their funds.

## The Irrelevance of "Net" Economic Loss

A secondary but equally important distinction made by the Fifth Circuit involves the concept of intent and harm. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that they didn't intend to "harm" anyone; they only intended to profit. They suggested that because the victims received securities in exchange for their money, they hadn't suffered a net pecuniary loss. 

The Fifth Circuit, leaning on *Kousisis*, dismissed this as a distinction without a difference. The Court clarified that the fraud statutes do not require the government to prove that a defendant sought to leave the victim "economically worse off." If a defendant induces a victim to part with property under false pretenses, the fraud is complete the moment that property is transferred. Even if the victim receives something of "equal value" in return, the fraudulent nature of the transaction remains. This is a critical nuance for high-frequency trading and social media schemes where the "loss" might be obscured by market fluctuations or the immediate acquisition of other assets.

## Doctrinal Implications and the Drafting Burden

The *Constantinescu* decision provides a roadmap for federal prosecutors who are still adjusting to the post-*Ciminelli* era. The takeaway is clear: the government must draft indictments that explicitly describe the "hand-off." It is no longer sufficient to allege a scheme to "deceive" or "mislead" in a general sense. Instead, the indictment must allege a specific transaction—a purchase, a sale, a contract—where the victim is induced to part with a traditional property interest.

This creates a higher hurdle for the government but provides a clearer rule of law. By forcing prosecutors to focus on the "fraudulent inducement" of property rather than the "deprivation of information," the courts are narrowing the scope of federal fraud to its traditional roots. For the securities markets, this means that while "pump and dump" schemes will still be prosecuted vigorously, the legal theory will lean less on the "bad advice" received by the public and more on the specific, fraudulent transactions that moved money from the followers' pockets into the hands of the fraudsters.



# The 'Net Pecuniary Loss' Fallacy: How *Kousisis* Limits the Defense of Intent

The era of the "finfluencer"—social media personalities who command massive audiences and offer investment advice—has created a unique challenge for securities regulators. While "pump and dump" schemes are as old as the markets themselves, the modern iteration involves sophisticated digital manipulation where the line between "bad advice" and "criminal fraud" can seem blurry. A recent decision by the Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies this boundary, signaling a significant victory for the government’s ability to prosecute these schemes by narrowing the "intent" defense.

At the heart of the *Constantinescu* dispute is a question of whether a defendant can be convicted of securities fraud if their victims aren't "worse off" in a net economic sense. The defendants, who allegedly manipulated stock prices through social media, argued that the government failed to allege they deprived their followers of any "traditional property interest." They suggested that at most, the followers were deprived of "valuable economic information"—a distinction that, according to the defendants, placed their conduct outside the reach of federal fraud statutes.

The District Court agreed with the defendants, dismissing the indictment. However, the Fifth Circuit reversed, providing a nuanced reading of two recent Supreme Court decisions: *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*. The court’s analysis highlights a critical distinction in federal fraud law: the difference between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory.

## Information vs. Property

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, one must first look at *Ciminelli*. There, the Supreme Court held that the government cannot prosecute someone for fraud simply by depriving a victim of "valuable economic information" necessary to make a decision. Under the "right-to-control" theory, the victim's property is their right to make informed choices. The Supreme Court rejected this, stating that the right to information is not a "traditional property interest."

The defendants in *Constantinescu* tried to lean on *Ciminelli* to shield themselves. They argued that because they only "misrepresented" trading positions, they were merely depriving followers of information. The Fifth Circuit, however, saw a different architecture in the indictment. The government wasn't alleging a right-to-control violation; it was alleging "fraudulent inducement." In this model, the defendant uses a lie to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over actual money or property—in this case, by purchasing securities. Because the followers were induced to part with their cash, the "traditional property interest" was compromised, regardless of the information they lost along the way.

## The Death of the "Net Pecuniary Loss" Defense

Even if the government successfully establishes that property was transferred, a second defense often arises: the "net pecuniary loss" argument. Defendants often contend that if the victim still holds an asset of market value (even if that asset was obtained through a lie), no "harm" occurred, and thus no "intent to defraud" existed. 

The Fifth Circuit dismantled this argument by citing *Kousisis*. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do not require the government to prove that a defendant sought to cause a "net pecuniary loss" to the victim. If a defendant tricks a victim into a contract—even if the defendant provides something of "equal value" in return—the fraud is complete the moment the victim is induced to hand over their property under false pretenses.

The Fifth Circuit’s characterization of this as a "distinction without a difference" is the pivotal takeaway for securities litigation. The court clarified that the "intent to defraud" does not require an intent to leave the victim's bank account empty; it only requires an intent to deceive the victim into a transfer of property. In the context of a pump-and-dump, the fact that a follower might still hold a security worth $100 at the moment of the "dump" does not negate the fact that they were fraudulently induced to part with their money to acquire it.

## Doctrinal Implications

The *Constantinescu* decision reinforces a broad and potent application of the mail, wire, and securities fraud statutes. By decoupling "intent to defraud" from "net economic harm," the court has ensured that the government does not have to perform a complex accounting of a victim's net worth to secure a conviction. 

The downstream effect is significant for the prosecution of digital-age financial crimes. It allows the government to pursue "finfluencers" who may engage in schemes where the victims are technically left with assets, but were nonetheless defrauded into the transaction. As long as the government can show a scheme to obtain money through material misrepresentations, the defense of "no net loss" is effectively dead. This marks a shift toward a "transactional" view of fraud: once the lie causes the movement of property, the crime is complete.



# Prosecuting the 'Finfluencer': Modernizing Fraud Theory for Social Media Schemes

The era of the "finfluencer"—social media personalities who command massive audiences and wield significant power over retail investment trends—has created a new frontier for securities fraud. As these influencers move from mere commentary to active, coordinated "pump and dump" schemes, the legal system faces a novel challenge: how to prosecute fraud when the "victim" may not suffer a traditional, immediate loss of property, but is instead induced to part with money based on a web of digital lies. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addressed this head-on, providing a critical roadmap for how federal fraud statutes apply to the decentralized, high-speed world of social media manipulation.

The case at hand involved a group of defendants with large social media followings who allegedly orchestrated a scheme to artificially inflate the price of securities. By posting false and misleading information to "pump" the price and then secretly selling their holdings to "dump" them, the defendants allegedly pocketed $114 million. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, however, on a technical but significant point of law. It argued that the government failed to allege that the defendants deprived their followers of a "traditional property interest." Instead, the court suggested the defendants were merely depriving followers of "potentially valuable economic information," a theory the district court found insufficient to sustain a fraud conviction.

In reversing that dismissal, the Fifth Circuit navigated the narrow corridor between two recent Supreme Court precedents: *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*. The district court had been influenced by *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court held that the "right-to-control" theory—the idea that fraud occurs simply by depriving someone of the information needed to make an economic decision—does not support a conviction because information is not property. The Fifth Circuit correctly identified that the government’s case in *Constantinescu* was not built on a "right-to-control" theory, but rather on a "fraudulent-inducement" theory.

The distinction is vital. Under fraudulent inducement, the fraud occurs when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires handing over money or property. In this case, the followers were induced to purchase securities—an act that requires them to part with their own money. The Fifth Circuit clarified that the law protects the money and property itself, not just the information used to decide how to spend it. By focusing on the transfer of assets rather than the mere withholding of data, the court salvaged the indictment and realigned the prosecution with the government’s actual allegations.

Perhaps the most significant takeaway from the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning is its treatment of "net pecuniary loss." The defendants argued that because their intent was to profit, not to harm their followers, the indictment was insufficient. The court, leaning heavily on *Kousisis*, rejected this, noting that fraud statutes do not require a victim to be left "economically worse off" at the end of the transaction. Because the fraud is complete the moment the defendant induces the deprivation of money under false pretenses, it does not matter if the victim receives a security of some market value in return. The fraud lies in the deception used to facilitate the transaction, not the ultimate balance sheet of the victim.

This decision has profound downstream implications for the prosecution of digital-first securities fraud. By anchoring the prosecution in the "fraudulent-inducement" theory, the Fifth Circuit has provided a robust framework that prevents defendants from using the complexity of modern markets as a shield. In a "pump and dump" scheme, an influencer might argue that they are "giving" followers a stock, even if that stock was purchased through a fraudulent price hike. The Fifth Circuit’s holding ensures that such a defense will fail; the focus remains on the illicit mechanism of the trade rather than the final possession of the security. This provides the Department of Justice with a clear path to hold "finfluencers" accountable, ensuring that the rapid evolution of social media does not outpace the reach of federal fraud laws.
