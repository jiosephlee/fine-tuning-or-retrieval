Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why was the police report excluded as extrinsic evidence for impeachment even though it contained the witness's own statements?
Question:
The defense wanted to use a police report to impeach C.F. because she gave a different story than what was in the report. The court blocked this because the report wasn't 'authored' by her. If the report is just a record of what she said to the officer, why does it fail the authentication requirement for extrinsic evidence? Why can't the defense just read the part she said?
Answer:
The court’s ruling rests on the distinction between the right to **inquire** about a prior statement and the right to **introduce** a physical document as extrinsic evidence. 

Even though the police report contained C.F.’s own words, the document itself is a piece of evidence written by a police officer, not a transcript authored by the witness. Under the rules of evidence, when a party seeks to use extrinsic evidence (such as a document) to impeach a witness for a prior inconsistent statement, that evidence must still be **authenticated**.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court clarified this requirement by stating:
> "Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." (quoting *United States v. Cumbie*).

The court's logic can be broken down into two parts:

**1. The Requirement for Foundation**
Because C.F. did not author the report, the defense could not simply read portions of it into the record without first laying a foundation to prove the report's authenticity. The defense needed to establish that the report was a reliable and accurate record of the specific conversation between C.F. and the investigator. Without that "proper foundation," the report remained unauthenticated evidence.

**2. Questioning vs. Reading**
It is important to note that the court did not block the defense from exploring the inconsistency. The court actually **allowed** the defense to cross-examine C.F. regarding what she had told the police. However, while the defense has a right to ask the witness about her past statements, they do not have an automatic right to introduce a third-party document into the record to "prove" those statements unless they can first authenticate that document.

In short, the report failed the authentication requirement because the court viewed the report as a piece of evidence created by the officer, and the defense failed to provide the necessary legal basis to prove that the document was a valid, authentic record of C.F.'s testimony.
