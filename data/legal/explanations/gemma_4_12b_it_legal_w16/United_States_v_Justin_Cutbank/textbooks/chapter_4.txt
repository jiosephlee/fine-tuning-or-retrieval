Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 4: # Standards of Review and Sentencing Reasonableness

## Standard of Review: Procedural Correctness versus Substantive Reasonableness

When an appellate court reviews a criminal sentence, it does not simply ask if the sentence feels "fair." Instead, the court follows a structured two-step inquiry. The first step is a procedural check: the court must ensure the district court committed no significant procedural errors, such as failing to calculate the Sentencing Guidelines correctly or improperly calculating the Guidelines range. As established in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, this procedural correctness is a prerequisite before the court can move to the next step.

Once the court determines that the Guidelines were calculated correctly, it moves to the second step: evaluating the "substantive reasonableness" of the sentence. At this stage, the court examines whether the sentence imposed by the district court is appropriate under the broader statutory requirements of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit first confirmed that the district court properly adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and correctly applied the necessary sentencing enhancements to arrive at the advisory guidelines range.

## Presumption of Reasonableness: Sentences Falling Within the Advisory Guidelines Range

A key principle in federal sentencing is the "presumption of reasonableness." Because the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines are advisory rather than mandatory, the district court has discretion to deviate from them. However, if a district court imposes a sentence that falls within the advisory guidelines range, the appellate court generally presumes that the sentence is reasonable. 

In the case of Cutbank, the district court determined an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months based on his extensive criminal history and the specific nature of his offense. Because the district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months—the bottom of that range—the Eighth Circuit applied this presumption. The court noted that because the sentence was within the range, it is presumed reasonable on appeal unless the defendant can show a significant error in the underlying calculation or a total lack of consideration for the necessary sentencing factors.

## Evaluating § 3553(a) Factors: The Court's Duty to Consider vs. Mechanically Recite Factors

Even when a sentence falls within the guidelines range, the district court is still required to consider the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). These factors include the nature and circumstances of the offense, the history and characteristics of the defendant, and the need to avoid unwarranted sentence disparities. However, the law does not require the district court to provide a "mechanical recitation" of every single factor in its written order.

The Eighth Circuit clarifies that to uphold a sentence, it must simply be clear from the record that the district court actually *considered* these factors. In *Cutbank*, the court observed that the district court had reviewed the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, and was aware of Cutbank’s specific arguments for a lower sentence. By acknowledging these arguments and still selecting a sentence within the guidelines range, the district court demonstrated that it had weighed the § 3553(a) factors and reached a reasoned conclusion.

## Substantial Sentencing Discretion: The Court's Role in Balancing the Defendant's History and the Nature of the Offense

The ultimate goal of the sentencing process is to balance the defendant's individual circumstances against the gravity of their crimes. The court acknowledges that district courts possess "substantial sentencing discretion" to make this balance. In reviewing Cutbank's sentence, the Eighth Circuit looked at the severity of his actions: he was involved in an incident of domestic abuse, kidnapping, and assault in a home with children, and he injured a police officer while resisting arrest.

The court also weighed his history, noting that Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many of which involved violence and firearms. By looking at both the brutal nature of the offense and the defendant's extensive history of violence, the appellate court concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion. The sentence of 292 months was seen as a proportionate response to the facts of the case, even if it sat at the lower end of the calculated guidelines range.

## Final Disposition

Because the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range, the sentence fell within that range (triggering the presumption of reasonableness), and the record showed the court considered the necessary § 3553(a) factors, the Eighth Circuit found no reason to interfere. The court affirmed the judgment of the district court, upholding Cutbank's 292-month sentence.
