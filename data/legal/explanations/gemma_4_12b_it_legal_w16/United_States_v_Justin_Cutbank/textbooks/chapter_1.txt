Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## The November 2020 incident: Domestic violence and firearm possession

The facts of *United States v. Justin Cutbank* center on a violent domestic incident that occurred during the night of November 19-20, 2020. According to the government’s evidence, Cutbank—who had been consuming alcohol and methamphetamine—subjected his girlfriend, D.F., to a harrowing ordeal. Cutbank allegedly pointed a firearm at D.F.’s face, accused her of being monitored by "listening devices," and forced her into a closet. He held her there for several hours at gunpoint, during which time he threatened her life and struck her in the face with the weapon. 

The violence extended to other occupants of the home. Cutbank ordered D.F. to wake up her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. After this interaction, Cutbank fled the residence, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. This sequence of events established the primary basis for the criminal charges, highlighting a pattern of domestic abuse and the immediate presence of a firearm during the commission of these acts.

## Police intervention and the recovery of the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle

Following Cutbank's flight from D.F.’s home, police were alerted to a man barricaded in a garage at a residence approximately half a mile away. During the subsequent standoff, Cutbank behaved erratically, claimed to possess a weapon, and resisted officers' attempts to detain him by biting an officer in the face and neck. 

During the police investigation, officers searched the area between the two residences. They recovered a butcher-style knife, D.F.’s cell phone, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. Forensic evidence, specifically DNA on the rifle, linked the weapon to Cutbank. Furthermore, both D.F. and C.F. identified the sawed-off rifle as a weapon they had observed in Cutbank’s possession on previous occasions. These findings provided the physical and testimonial evidence necessary to support a charge of firearm possession.

## Criminal history and the specific charges under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)

The government charged Cutbank with being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Because Cutbank had a significant criminal history, the government also sought an enhanced penalty under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). This specific statute applies to defendants who are convicted of a crime of possession of a firearm and have been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies. 

The interplay between Cutbank’s prior record and his current conduct is central to the sentencing issues in this case. The prosecution’s goal was to move beyond a standard possession charge to trigger the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), which imposes significant mandatory minimums and sentencing enhancements based on a defendant's history of violent and/or firearm-related offenses.

## The jury's verdict and the district court's sentencing decision

A jury ultimately convicted Cutbank of the charged felon in possession offense and found that he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby triggering the enhanced penalties of § 924(e). Following the conviction, the district court calculated the advisory guidelines for sentencing. 

The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended a high offense level (35) due to several factors: the sawed-off nature of the firearm, Cutbank’s prior convictions, the obliterated serial number on the gun, and the fact that he recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police. Combined with his high Criminal History Category (VI), the court arrived at an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, a term that fell within the calculated advisory range.

## The grounds for appeal: Evidentiary errors and sentencing enhancements

Cutbank appealed both his conviction and his sentence, raising two primary categories of error. First, he argued that the district court committed multiple evidentiary and procedural errors during the trial. Specifically, he challenged the admission of testimony regarding his prior possession of the rifle (arguing it was improper propensity evidence), the handling of hearsay and Confrontation Clause issues regarding D.F.'s statements to police, and the admission of testimony regarding a family member's death. 

Second, Cutbank challenged the sentencing phase, specifically the application of the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. This enhancement adds four levels to the offense level when a firearm is used or possessed in connection with another felony. Cutbank contended that applying this enhancement resulted in a sentence that violated the principles of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which requires that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." These arguments form the basis of the appellate review.
