Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Sentencing Guidelines and Statutory Enhancements

To understand the sentencing of a federal defendant, one must look beyond the crime itself and examine the mathematical and statutory framework of the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG). In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court’s analysis of the defendant's sentence serves as a masterclass in how specific statutory triggers and guideline enhancements interact to produce a final advisory range.

## The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and its Application to Repeat Violent Offenders

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) is a federal statute that imposes significantly harsher penalties on individuals who commit new firearm offenses after having been convicted of certain "prior violent felonies." In Cutbank’s case, the jury found that he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies. This finding triggered the enhanced penalty provisions of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e).

When the ACCA is triggered, it dictates a specific sentencing guideline. For Cutbank, the ACCA provided a guideline that would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The ACCA serves as a "floor" or a baseline for repeat offenders, ensuring that those with extensive criminal histories receive sentences that reflect the gravity of their recidivism. However, as the court demonstrates, the ACCA guideline is not always the final word on the offense level; it is one component of a larger calculation.

## USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): The 'Connection to Another Felony' Enhancement

While the ACCA looks at the defendant's past, other sentencing enhancements look at the specific circumstances of the current crime. One such enhancement is USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies if a defendant used or possessed a firearm in connection with another felony offense. 

This specific enhancement is powerful because of its broad scope. The court notes that the enhancement applies regardless of whether a criminal charge was actually brought or a conviction was obtained for that underlying felony. Furthermore, the "connection" can be to a federal, state, or local felony offense. In Cutbank's case, the district court adopted the factual findings in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) showing that he possessed the sawed-off rifle in connection with another felony. This triggered a four-level increase to his offense level.

## Calculating the Guidelines Range: Offense Levels, Criminal History, and Specific Adjustments

Federal sentencing begins with the calculation of the "Offense Level," which is then combined with a "Criminal History Category" to determine an advisory range. The PSR for Cutbank provided a step-by-step accounting of these levels:

1.  **Base Offense Level:** The starting point was 26, based on the possession of a sawed-off firearm and the defendant's prior violent crimes.
2.  **Specific Adjustments:** The court added a three-level increase for an obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing, and the four-level increase for the connection to another felony.
3.  **Total Offense Level:** These adjustments resulted in a total offense level of 35.
4.  **Criminal History Category:** Based on Cutbank's extensive record, he was placed in Category VI.

By plugging an offense level of 35 and a Category VI into the Guidelines table, the court arrived at an advisory range of 292 to 365 months.

## USSG § 4B1.4(b): Determining Which Offense Level Applies When Multiple Guidelines are Triggered

A critical procedural point in *Cutbank* involves how a court handles situations where two different guidelines yield two different offense levels. In this case, the ACCA produced an offense level of 34, while the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement resulted in an offense level of 35.

The court resolved this conflict by applying USSG § 4B1.4(b). This rule stipulates that when multiple guidelines apply, the court must use the greater offense level. Therefore, the court correctly moved from the ACCA-based level of 34 to the higher level of 35. This illustrates a key principle of federal sentencing: specific enhancements for the underlying conduct of the current crime can "stack" or supersede broader statutory categories like the ACCA if they result in a higher offense level.

## The Distinction Between Federal, State, and Local Felony Connections

Finally, it is important to note the jurisdictional breadth of the "connection to another felony" enhancement. Cutbank’s defense did not successfully argue that the court was limited only to federal felonies. Because USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) explicitly includes state and local felonies, the government can successfully argue for the four-level enhancement even if the underlying felony was a state-level crime that never reached a federal court. This broad definition ensures that the Guidelines account for a defendant's total conduct, regardless of which jurisdiction’s laws were violated during the commission of the underlying felony.
