Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the court's ability to take 'judicial notice' of copyright registrations affect the standard for a Rule 12(b)(6) motion?
Question:
I'm confused about the procedural aspect of the ruling. The court mentions it can take 'judicial notice' of copyright registrations while reviewing the complaint for a 12(b)(6) motion. Usually, I thought a 12(b)(6) motion is strictly limited to the facts alleged in the complaint. Does this mean the court can look at external government records to decide if a claim is legally sufficient to proceed?
Answer:
In the context of a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the court's ability to take judicial notice of copyright registrations expands the scope of "facts" the court may consider when determining whether a complaint states a valid claim, without technically violating the "four corners" of the complaint rule.

Typically, a Rule 12(b)(6) motion is decided based on the facts alleged in the complaint, any documents attached as exhibits, and any documents incorporated by reference. However, *Santos v. Kimmel* clarifies that for copyright actions, this evidentiary base is further supplemented by judicial notice.

**The Doctrine of Judicial Notice**
Judicial notice is a procedural device that allows a court to accept certain facts as true without requiring the parties to provide evidence for them. This is generally reserved for facts that are "indisputable" or part of the public record. In *Santos*, the court explicitly notes that while it considers the pleadings and attached documents, it "may also take judicial notice of 'copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.'"

**Effect on the 12(b)(6) Standard**
The ability to take judicial notice does not fundamentally change the standard for a Rule 12(b)(6) motion—the court is still evaluating whether the plaintiff has pleaded sufficient facts to state a claim—but it does broaden the *source* of those facts. 

In this opinion, the court used these external government records to establish specific facts relevant to the fair use defense, such as the date the videos were published relative to their creation. By allowing judicial notice of these registrations, the court is able to move beyond the mere allegations of the plaintiff to verify objective, public data. Consequently, a plaintiff cannot avoid a motion to dismiss simply by failing to allege a specific detail if that detail is a matter of public record (like a registration date) that the court is empowered to "notice" on its own.

In summary, while the 12(b)(6) motion remains a pleading standard, judicial notice allows the court to treat official government records as part of the "facts" of the case for the purposes of determining legal sufficiency.

### How does the court distinguish between 'subjective intent' and 'objective purpose' in determining transformative use?
Question:
In the fair use analysis, Santos argued that his intent in making the videos was the same as Kimmel's (to get money for absurd things). However, the court rejected this, citing the Warhol case. Can someone explain the legal distinction here? If the creator of the original work intended something specific, why does the 'reasonable observer's' interpretation trump the creator's own subjective intent in a fair use analysis?
Answer:
In the fair use analysis under *Santos v. Kimmel*, the court distinguishes between **subjective intent** and **objective purpose** by shifting the focus from what the parties *claim* they intended to what a **reasonable observer** actually perceives.

### The Legal Doctrine: The "Reasonable Observer" Standard
The court relies on the Supreme Court’s holding in *Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith* to establish that the "purpose of the use" is not determined by the internal state of mind of the creator or the secondary user. Instead, the court looks at the objective effect of the secondary use. 

As the court notes, the "subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use." To determine if a work is transformative, the court asks whether the new work adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, as perceived by the public.

### Application to *Santos v. Kimmel*
The distinction is clearly illustrated by the conflicting claims made by the parties:

1.  **Subjective Intent:** Santos argued that his original purpose for making the videos was to "get money for absurd things." He contended that because Kimmel’s use of the videos also centered on the fact that Santos was doing absurd things for money, the purpose remained unchanged. This is a subjective claim regarding his own motivations for the original work.
2.  **Objective Purpose:** The court rejected this by looking at the actual content of the videos as seen by an audience. The court pointed out that, based on Santos's own allegations, a "reasonable observer" would interpret the original videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." 

### Why Objective Purpose Trumps Subjective Intent
The reason the "reasonable observer's" interpretation trumps the creator's intent is that fair use is a "flexible concept" intended to protect creativity and commentary. 

If the law allowed subjective intent to dictate the analysis, a copyright holder could potentially block any use by claiming they "intended" for the work to be used in a specific way, or a secondary user could claim transformation simply by stating a high-minded goal regardless of the actual result. By using an objective standard, the court ensures that the "purpose and character of the use" is based on the actual function the work serves in the marketplace and its actual impact on the audience, rather than the private thoughts of the individuals involved.

In short, the court does not care what Santos *said* he wanted the videos to do; it cares what the videos *actually did* (or appeared to do) to the viewer. Because the videos objectively conveyed "positivity" while Kimmel used them for "sarcastic criticism," the use was deemed transformative.

### Why does a parody or mocking segment not count as a 'competing substitute' under the fourth fair use factor?
Question:
The court states that it doesn't matter if the second work 'devalues' the first through parody, but only if it 'usurps the market.' If Kimmel’s segment makes Santos’s videos look ridiculous, isn't that a form of market harm? I'm struggling to understand why the court thinks a critical review or lampoon is automatically excluded from the 'potential licensing market' for the original work.
Answer:
The distinction you are struggling with lies in the difference between **reputational devaluation** and **market substitution**. 

In fair use analysis, the fourth factor—the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work—does not ask whether the new work makes the original work less "cool," "respectable," or "valuable" in a subjective sense. Instead, it asks whether the new work serves as a functional replacement for the original in the marketplace.

Here is the breakdown of why a parody/mocking segment is excluded from that "substitution" analysis based on the *Santos* opinion:

### 1. Devaluation vs. Substitution
The court explicitly clarifies that "we ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." 

When Kimmel’s segment makes Santos look "ridiculous," he is causing **devaluation**. He is changing the public perception of Santos’s persona. However, a consumer who wants a video providing "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, [and] encouragement" (the purpose of Santos's original works) is not going to find that satisfaction by watching a mocking late-night comedy segment. Because the two works serve different purposes and cater to different audience needs, the mocking segment is not a "substitute."

### 2. The Scope of the "Potential Licensing Market"
The court relies on *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* to explain why criticism and lampoons are excluded from the fourth factor. The legal reasoning is that a copyright holder does not have a "potential licensing market" for people to mock them. 

If the law were to treat a parody as a market substitute, it would mean that every comedian, critic, or satirist would need a license from the original creator before they could make a joke. The court notes that the "unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." 

### Summary for your notes:
*   **Market Substitution (The Test):** Does the new work satisfy the same demand as the original? If a user would buy the new work *instead* of the original because it serves the same purpose, it is a substitute.
*   **Devaluation (Not the Test):** Does the new work make the original look bad? If the new work makes the original look ridiculous but people still want the original for its original purpose (e.g., inspiration), there is no market substitution.

In *Santos*, because Kimmel’s purpose was "sarcastic criticism" and Santos’s purpose was "positivity," the two works occupy different market spaces. Therefore, mocking the original does not constitute a "competing substitute."

### What is the legal threshold for a non-party to be considered a 'third-party beneficiary' of a Terms of Service agreement?
Question:
The court dismissed Santos’s breach of contract claim because he wasn't a party to the Cameo Terms of Service. It mentions a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties' and says there must be an 'express declaration.' Does this mean that unless a contract specifically names a class of people (like 'users' or 'creators') as beneficiaries, it is almost impossible for a third party to sue over a breach of that contract?
Answer:
To be considered a third-party beneficiary of a contract (such as a Terms of Service agreement), a non-party must overcome a "strong presumption" that the contract does not confer benefits upon them.

The legal threshold established in *Santos v. Kimmel* is that the implication of third-party rights must be so clear that it is "practically an express declaration." As the court noted, because there is a default presumption against conferring benefits on noncontracting parties, a court will not assume a third party has rights unless the contract explicitly provides for them.

In the context of your question regarding whether a contract must specifically name a class of people, the opinion provides a significant clue. The court observed that other provisions within the Cameo Terms of Service *did* contain specific third-party beneficiary language. The court used this to highlight that when the parties to the agreement intended to allow third parties to enforce certain provisions, they did so "expressly." 

By contrasting the sections that had third-party language with the sections that did not, the court signaled that the absence of such language in the relevant sections is fatal to a third-party claim. Therefore, while a contract might not necessarily need to list every individual, it must contain a clear, explicit indication that non-contracting parties are intended to be beneficiaries. Without an "express declaration" or a nearly identical level of clarity, a non-party like Santos cannot successfully claim a breach of contract against the contracting parties.

### What constitutes 'essential terms' of a contract when pleading an implied-in-fact contract?
Question:
The court dismissed the implied contract claim because Santos failed to allege the 'essential terms.' Since an implied contract isn't written down, how is a plaintiff supposed to know what the 'essential terms' are for the purpose of a complaint? Is there a standard list of elements (like price, duration, etc.) that must be pleaded to survive a 12(b)(6) motion in New York?
Answer:
When pleading an implied-in-fact contract, the "essential terms" are not defined by a standardized laundry list of elements (such as a fixed requirement for price or duration), but rather by the specific material obligations that the parties were understood to have agreed upon based on their conduct.

Under New York law, as applied in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the court determines the existence and extent of an implied-in-fact contract by looking at "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances." Because an implied contract is inferred from the behavior of the parties rather than an express written agreement, the "essential terms" are the specific promises or obligations that the parties' actions manifested.

To survive a 12(b)(6) motion, a plaintiff must move beyond generalities and plead these terms in "nonconclusory language." Specifically, the complaint must identify the "specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." In the context of *Santos*, the court dismissed the claim because the plaintiff failed to articulate the specific terms of the "putative implied contract" that the defendants allegedly breached. 

For a law student drafting a complaint, this means that while there is no "standard list," you must clearly allege:
1.  The specific conduct that demonstrates a "meeting of the minds" (the agreement); and
2.  The specific material obligations (the "essential terms") that the defendant failed to perform. 

If a plaintiff alleges that a contract exists but fails to state what that contract actually *required* the defendant to do, the complaint fails to state a claim because the court cannot determine what specific breach occurred.

### Does the 'out-of-pocket loss' requirement for fraudulent inducement bar claims based purely on reputational harm?
Question:
The court affirmed the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim because Santos didn't allege an 'out-of-pocket loss.' Under New York law, if a defendant tricks a plaintiff into doing something that ruins their reputation or causes emotional distress, but doesn't cost them actual cash, is there no way to bring a fraud claim? I'm trying to understand the scope of 'actual' loss in this context.
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the "out-of-pocket loss" requirement effectively bars a fraudulent inducement claim if the plaintiff only alleges non-monetary harms, such as reputational damage or emotional distress.

### The "Out-of-Pocket Loss" Standard
Under New York law, as cited by the court, a claim for fraudulent inducement requires the plaintiff to allege that they suffered an "actual out-of-pocket loss" as a result of the fraud. The court specifically referenced *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* to support the conclusion that this is a necessary element of the claim.

### Application to Reputational Harm
In *Santos*, the court affirmed the dismissal of the plaintiff's fraudulent inducement claim because his complaint failed to allege any such out-of-pocket loss. While the facts of the case—involving a "mocking series of segments" that would naturally impact a public figure's reputation—suggested a significant non-monetary injury, the court held that these types of harms are insufficient to sustain a fraudulent inducement claim under the prevailing state law standard.

### Conclusion
To bring a successful fraudulent inducement claim under the standard applied in this opinion, a plaintiff must move beyond alleging intangible harm. Because the court dismissed Santos's claim specifically for the absence of an "actual out-of-pocket loss," it clarifies that reputational harm or emotional distress, in the absence of a corresponding out-of-pocket financial expenditure, does not satisfy the scope of "actual loss" required by New York law for this cause of action.
