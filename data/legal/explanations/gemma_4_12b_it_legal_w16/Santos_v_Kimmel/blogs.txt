Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The 'Reasonable Observer' Standard: Navigating Transformative Use in the Wake of *Warhol*

In the evolving landscape of digital content and late-night satire, the line between protected speech and copyright infringement often blurs. The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* provides a clear roadmap for how courts will navigate this boundary, particularly when public figures attempt to use copyright law to shield their content from mockery. By affirming the dismissal of George Santos’s claims against Jimmy Kimmel, the court reinforced a crucial principle: the "transformative" nature of a work is determined by the perspective of a reasonable observer, not the subjective intentions of the creator.

The facts of the case are tailor-made for a modern era of "personal" content. Santos, a former Congressman, fulfilled several requests for personalized videos on the Cameo platform—videos he characterized as intended to convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." Kimmel then curated these videos into a mocking series on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* titled "Will Santos Say It?" Santos sued for copyright infringement, arguing that Kimmel’s use of his videos was not transformative because it misappropriated the "purpose" of his original creations.

The Second Circuit’s analysis centers on the first factor of the fair use doctrine: the purpose and character of the use. Following the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the court looked for whether the secondary use "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." Santos’s argument rested on a distinction of intent. He contended that because his intent was positivity and Kimmel’s intent was mockery, the use was not transformative. The court, however, rejected this "subjective intent" argument entirely.

The court’s reasoning highlights a vital check on copyright ownership. It held that whether a secondary use is transformative depends on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the internal motives of the parties involved. To a reasonable observer, Kimmel’s segment was clearly a commentary on Santos’s willingness to say "absurd things for money." By prioritizing the audience's perception over the creator's stated purpose, the court ensures that copyright cannot be used as a "gag order" to prevent public figures from being satirized. If a creator could successfully argue that their work was "intended" for a different purpose than the one used in a parody, the fair use doctrine would be hollowed out by the subjective whims of the copyright holder.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of the fourth fair use factor—the effect on the potential market—underscores the protective shell the law provides to satire. The court noted that a parody or critical review does not "usurp" the market for the original work because creators typically do not license the right to be mocked. This reinforces the idea that there is no "licensing market" for criticism, a standard that provides significant breathing room for late-night television and political commentary alike.

The doctrinal implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are particularly relevant for public figures. For politicians and influencers, the ruling confirms that once content is released into the public sphere, it is effectively fair game for transformative commentary. Because the "reasonable observer" standard is objective, public figures cannot shield themselves from criticism by claiming that the "true meaning" of their content is being misrepresented. Instead, they must accept that the transformative use of their likeness and words is a byproduct of a free and open marketplace of ideas—one where the court prefers to protect the satirist’s right to comment over the creator’s right to control the narrative.



# Market Substitution vs. Satirical Commentary: Defining the Boundaries of Fair Use

In the high-stakes arena of late-night television and political commentary, the line between "content" and "criticism" is often where the most consequential legal battles are fought. The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a vital waypoint in this evolution, clarifying the boundaries of the fair use doctrine when public figures attempt to exert copyright control over the mockery of their own personas. By dismissing former Congressman George Santos’s copyright claims against Jimmy Kimmel, the court reinforced a foundational principle of American media: that the law protects the right to critique, but does not grant creators a monopoly over how their works are used as fodder for public discourse.

The heart of the dispute lies in the Fourth Factor of the fair use analysis: the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of his personalized "Cameo" videos—produced under false pretenses—infringed upon his copyright. However, the Second Circuit adopted a nuanced distinction that is central to the preservation of satire. The court clarified that for a use to be infringing under this factor, it must "usurp" the market for the original work by offering a "competing substitute." In other words, the question is not whether the new work makes the original work less appealing or damages the creator's reputation; it is whether the new work functions as a replacement for the original in the marketplace.

The court’s reasoning relies heavily on the distinction between "devaluation" and "usurpation." When Kimmel aired the "Will Santos Say It?" segments, he was not attempting to sell a service that competed with Santos’s Cameo videos. Rather, he was using those videos as the raw material for a comedic critique. As the court noted, citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the fact that a creator might not want to license a lampoon of their own work does not mean a licensing market exists for such a use. By framing the use as a "parody or criticism," the court insulated late-night media from the threat of "reputation-based" copyright litigation, where a plaintiff might seek to use copyright law as a shield against unfavorable publicity.

From an analytical perspective, the court’s focus on the "reasonable observer" test for transformativeness—rather than the subjective intent of the parties—is a crucial procedural safeguard. Santos argued that his original purpose for the videos was to provide "positivity," while Kimmel’s purpose was to mock his willingness to say "absurd things for money." The court correctly identified that the law does not care about the internal motivations of the creator; it cares about how the public perceives the new work. Because a reasonable viewer would see the *JKL* segments as a commentary on Santos's character, the use was transformative. This prevents copyright holders from "vetoing" the meaning of their work by claiming that a critic's interpretation is a violation of their creative intent.

The doctrinal implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are significant for the future of personality-driven content. It sends a clear signal that public figures cannot use copyright law to litigate the "vibe" of a satire. If a creator produces content that is then repurposed by a third party to mock that creator, the creator's primary recourse is likely in the realm of defamation or "right of publicity" (where applicable), not copyright. By narrowing the scope of "market harm" to exclude the devaluation of a person’s reputation through critique, the Second Circuit has fortified a safe harbor for political satire and late-night comedy, ensuring that the "marketplace of ideas" remains robust against the chilling effects of copyright-based censorship.



# The Contractual Fortress: Standing and Third-Party Beneficiaries in Platform Terms of Service

In the modern "creator economy," the legal boundaries of content creation are often defined not by broad statutes, but by the fine print of platform Terms of Service (ToS). The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* highlights a burgeoning legal reality: while platforms like Cameo provide the infrastructure for the gig economy, their contracts may simultaneously construct a "contractual fortress" that shields the platform from liability to the very individuals who are the subjects of the content being produced.

The case arose from a series of mocking segments on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, in which Kimmel used fictitious identities to solicit personalized videos from former Congressman George Santos on the Cameo platform. Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state law claims, including breach of contract. While the court ultimately dismissed the copyright claims as fair use—a standard move in the context of parody—the more significant doctrinal development lies in the court’s treatment of Santos’s state law contract claims.

The District Court dismissed Santos’s claims for breach of contract and breach of implied contract, and the Second Circuit affirmed. The court’s reasoning centered on a fundamental principle of contract law: the "strong presumption against conferring benefits to non-contracting third parties." Because Santos was not a party to the agreement between Kimmel and Cameo, he could not claim a breach of the platform’s ToS unless the contract contained an "express declaration" that such provisions applied to him. The court noted that Cameo’s ToS actually included specific third-party beneficiary language in other sections, suggesting that when the platform intended to extend rights to non-signatories, it did so explicitly. Santos, however, failed to point to any such provision.

This ruling reinforces a significant hurdle for individuals who are "featured" in content but are not "users" of the underlying platform. By upholding the "contractual fortress," the court effectively insulated the platform from being held to its own rules regarding the behavior of its users. In this framework, the platform’s ToS function as a private agreement between the service provider and the creator (Kimmel), leaving the subject (Santos) in a legal vacuum. Even if a platform promises "respectful" conduct or specific protections in its user agreement, those promises are legally hollow if they do not explicitly bind the subjects of the content to the platform’s ecosystem.

From an analytical perspective, this creates a bifurcated legal reality for the creator economy. On one hand, platforms have the freedom to set high standards of conduct for their users; on the other hand, those standards provide no "privity of contract" to the people actually impacted by the content. This creates a situation where a platform's rules are essentially voluntary for the creator and unenforceable for the subject. For a platform like Cameo, this is a massive liability shield. It allows the platform to claim it "prohibits" certain behaviors in its ToS while remaining immune from lawsuits by the victims of those behaviors, because the victim is not a party to the contract.

The downstream implications of this "fortress" are profound for digital rights and personality rights. As more of our public lives are mediated through third-party platforms—where content is created, sold, and distributed—the lack of standing for the "subject" becomes a systemic issue. If the only mechanism to regulate the interaction between a creator and a subject is a platform's private contract, then the subject is perpetually excluded from the remedy. 

Furthermore, by dismissing the claims for failure to state a fact—specifically the failure to allege "out-of-pocket loss" for the fraud claim—the court also signaled a high bar for non-monetary grievances in these contexts. For the public, the message is clear: in the eyes of the law, the "terms" of the digital world are often strictly private. Unless a platform explicitly invites the public into its contract as a beneficiary, the subjects of the internet’s content may find themselves standing outside the walls of the law, unable to enforce the rules of the platforms that host their likenesses.



# Pleading Fraud in the Digital Age: The 'Out-of-Pocket' Hurdle

The collision between former Congressman George Santos and late-night host Jimmy Kimmel provides a masterclass in the gap between public spectacle and private litigation. While the public saw a bizarre "prank" involving mocked Cameo videos and "Will Santos Say It?" segments, the legal reality of the dispute was governed by the rigid requirements of New York’s fraud pleading standards. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit’s dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim serves as a stark reminder: in the digital age of "prank" culture and attention-seeking stunts, a deceptive interaction does not automatically equate to a compensable legal wrong.

The crux of the issue lies in New York’s specific requirement for "out-of-pocket" losses in fraudulent inducement claims. To sustain such a claim under New York law, a plaintiff must allege more than a deceptive promise or a dishonest interaction; they must demonstrate a tangible, pecuniary loss. In this case, the District Court—affirmed by the Second Circuit—dismissed Santos’s claim because he failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from Kimmel’s alleged deception. While Santos may have felt victimized by a series of orchestrated mockings, the law does not currently provide a general "fraud" remedy for the loss of dignity, reputation, or the "waste" of one's time in a high-profile media feud.

The court’s reasoning highlights a significant barrier for plaintiffs seeking to litigate "pranks" or social media "bait-and-switches." Because the court looks to *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* to establish the necessity of a tangible monetary loss, the threshold for fraud becomes almost insurmountable when the "deception" is aimed at generating content rather than extracting money. For Santos, the "injury" was largely performative—a public humiliation captured on camera. Because he did not allege that Kimmel’s deceptive tactics caused him to part with funds or specific assets, the claim was DOA (dead on arrival) at the pleading stage.

This creates a complex doctrinal landscape for the burgeoning "attention economy." As influencers and media personalities increasingly use deceptive tactics to elicit reactions from public figures or private citizens, the law remains stubbornly tethered to traditional commercial fraud. If a platform's "Terms of Service" or a "prank" results in a humiliating viral moment but no literal check is written, the legal system offers little recourse under a fraud theory. This reflects a judicial reluctance to expand the scope of fraud to cover "moral" injuries or the broader societal costs of misinformation and public mockery.

For practitioners, *Santos* underscores the necessity of identifying a "hook" beyond the bad faith of the defendant. In high-profile media disputes, a defendant's behavior may be ethically reprehensible or socially damaging, but without a clear path to quantifying a monetary loss, a fraud claim will likely fail the Rule 12(b)(6) test. The "out-of-pocket" hurdle ensures that while the public may judge the ethics of a prank, the courts will only intervene when the deception reaches the plaintiff's wallet.
