Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The Contractual Fortress: Standing and Third-Party Beneficiaries in Platform Terms of Service

In the modern "creator economy," the legal boundaries of content creation are often defined not by broad statutes, but by the fine print of platform Terms of Service (ToS). The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* highlights a burgeoning legal reality: while platforms like Cameo provide the infrastructure for the gig economy, their contracts may simultaneously construct a "contractual fortress" that shields the platform from liability to the very individuals who are the subjects of the content being produced.

The case arose from a series of mocking segments on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, in which Kimmel used fictitious identities to solicit personalized videos from former Congressman George Santos on the Cameo platform. Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state law claims, including breach of contract. While the court ultimately dismissed the copyright claims as fair use—a standard move in the context of parody—the more significant doctrinal development lies in the court’s treatment of Santos’s state law contract claims.

The District Court dismissed Santos’s claims for breach of contract and breach of implied contract, and the Second Circuit affirmed. The court’s reasoning centered on a fundamental principle of contract law: the "strong presumption against conferring benefits to non-contracting third parties." Because Santos was not a party to the agreement between Kimmel and Cameo, he could not claim a breach of the platform’s ToS unless the contract contained an "express declaration" that such provisions applied to him. The court noted that Cameo’s ToS actually included specific third-party beneficiary language in other sections, suggesting that when the platform intended to extend rights to non-signatories, it did so explicitly. Santos, however, failed to point to any such provision.

This ruling reinforces a significant hurdle for individuals who are "featured" in content but are not "users" of the underlying platform. By upholding the "contractual fortress," the court effectively insulated the platform from being held to its own rules regarding the behavior of its users. In this framework, the platform’s ToS function as a private agreement between the service provider and the creator (Kimmel), leaving the subject (Santos) in a legal vacuum. Even if a platform promises "respectful" conduct or specific protections in its user agreement, those promises are legally hollow if they do not explicitly bind the subjects of the content to the platform’s ecosystem.

From an analytical perspective, this creates a bifurcated legal reality for the creator economy. On one hand, platforms have the freedom to set high standards of conduct for their users; on the other hand, those standards provide no "privity of contract" to the people actually impacted by the content. This creates a situation where a platform's rules are essentially voluntary for the creator and unenforceable for the subject. For a platform like Cameo, this is a massive liability shield. It allows the platform to claim it "prohibits" certain behaviors in its ToS while remaining immune from lawsuits by the victims of those behaviors, because the victim is not a party to the contract.

The downstream implications of this "fortress" are profound for digital rights and personality rights. As more of our public lives are mediated through third-party platforms—where content is created, sold, and distributed—the lack of standing for the "subject" becomes a systemic issue. If the only mechanism to regulate the interaction between a creator and a subject is a platform's private contract, then the subject is perpetually excluded from the remedy. 

Furthermore, by dismissing the claims for failure to state a fact—specifically the failure to allege "out-of-pocket loss" for the fraud claim—the court also signaled a high bar for non-monetary grievances in these contexts. For the public, the message is clear: in the eyes of the law, the "terms" of the digital world are often strictly private. Unless a platform explicitly invites the public into its contract as a beneficiary, the subjects of the internet’s content may find themselves standing outside the walls of the law, unable to enforce the rules of the platforms that host their likenesses.
