Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Pleading Fraud in the Digital Age: The 'Out-of-Pocket' Hurdle

The collision between former Congressman George Santos and late-night host Jimmy Kimmel provides a masterclass in the gap between public spectacle and private litigation. While the public saw a bizarre "prank" involving mocked Cameo videos and "Will Santos Say It?" segments, the legal reality of the dispute was governed by the rigid requirements of New York’s fraud pleading standards. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit’s dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim serves as a stark reminder: in the digital age of "prank" culture and attention-seeking stunts, a deceptive interaction does not automatically equate to a compensable legal wrong.

The crux of the issue lies in New York’s specific requirement for "out-of-pocket" losses in fraudulent inducement claims. To sustain such a claim under New York law, a plaintiff must allege more than a deceptive promise or a dishonest interaction; they must demonstrate a tangible, pecuniary loss. In this case, the District Court—affirmed by the Second Circuit—dismissed Santos’s claim because he failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from Kimmel’s alleged deception. While Santos may have felt victimized by a series of orchestrated mockings, the law does not currently provide a general "fraud" remedy for the loss of dignity, reputation, or the "waste" of one's time in a high-profile media feud.

The court’s reasoning highlights a significant barrier for plaintiffs seeking to litigate "pranks" or social media "bait-and-switches." Because the court looks to *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* to establish the necessity of a tangible monetary loss, the threshold for fraud becomes almost insurmountable when the "deception" is aimed at generating content rather than extracting money. For Santos, the "injury" was largely performative—a public humiliation captured on camera. Because he did not allege that Kimmel’s deceptive tactics caused him to part with funds or specific assets, the claim was DOA (dead on arrival) at the pleading stage.

This creates a complex doctrinal landscape for the burgeoning "attention economy." As influencers and media personalities increasingly use deceptive tactics to elicit reactions from public figures or private citizens, the law remains stubbornly tethered to traditional commercial fraud. If a platform's "Terms of Service" or a "prank" results in a humiliating viral moment but no literal check is written, the legal system offers little recourse under a fraud theory. This reflects a judicial reluctance to expand the scope of fraud to cover "moral" injuries or the broader societal costs of misinformation and public mockery.

For practitioners, *Santos* underscores the necessity of identifying a "hook" beyond the bad faith of the defendant. In high-profile media disputes, a defendant's behavior may be ethically reprehensible or socially damaging, but without a clear path to quantifying a monetary loss, a fraud claim will likely fail the Rule 12(b)(6) test. The "out-of-pocket" hurdle ensures that while the public may judge the ethics of a prank, the courts will only intervene when the deception reaches the plaintiff's wallet.
