Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # Analysis of the Informational Standing Theory

## Informational Standing and Statutory Entitlements

To bring a case in federal court, a plaintiff must satisfy the requirements of Article III standing. This requires demonstrating a "concrete" injury caused by the defendant that is "likely to be redressed" by a favorable court decision. When a plaintiff’s claim is based on a lack of information—often called "informational standing"—the court looks for a specific type of concrete injury. According to the court in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury only if they are denied information that a "statute entitled him to receive."

In this case, the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) argued that it suffered a concrete injury because the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) refused to conduct an investigation into the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) FOIA policies. AFL’s theory was that by refusing to investigate, the OSC was depriving them of the "fruits" of that investigation—specifically, the notices and information that typically accompany such proceedings. However, the court emphasized that simply being denied information is not enough to establish standing; the plaintiff must prove they have a clear statutory right to that specific information.

## Distinguishing Section 1214 and Section 1216 Requirements

A central point of the court’s analysis involves the distinction between two different sections of the Civil Service Reform Act. Section 1216 requires the OSC to conduct investigations into the "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information" prohibited by the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). AFL argued that because Section 1216 mandates an investigation, they were entitled to the procedural protections and information flow inherent in such a process.

AFL attempted to link these requirements to Section 1214, which governs "prohibited personnel practices." Section 1214 contains specific provisions for notifying a complaining party about the progress of an investigation. AFL contended that the OSC should be required to provide these notices during a Section 1216 investigation. The court, however, noted that Section 1216 does not explicitly create an entitlement to receive the specific notices found in Section 1214. Because the statute does not explicitly state that a complainant is entitled to these notices in a FOIA-related investigation, AFL failed to show a "statutory entitlement" to the information.

## OSC's Discretionary Authority over Procedural Requirements

Even if the court were to consider the procedural requirements of Section 1214, the court highlighted a critical principle regarding administrative discretion. AFL argued that the OSC should be compelled to "engraft" the notice requirements of Section 1214 onto its Section 1216 investigations. The court rejected this, noting that the OSC maintains discretionary authority over whether to follow the specific procedural requirements of Section 1214 when handling a Section 1216 matter.

This distinction is vital for understanding the limits of judicial review over administrative agencies. The court pointed out that a judicial order mandating an investigation would not be "reasonably likely to redress" AFL’s injury because the court cannot generally compel an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion. Because the OSC has the choice to provide or withhold certain procedural notices in these types of cases, and because there is no statutory mandate requiring them to do so in a Section 1216 investigation, AFL cannot claim a "concrete" injury from the OSC's refusal to do so.

## Final Disposition and Vacatur of the District Court Judgment

The court’s analysis of standing leads directly to the final disposition of the case. Because AFL could not demonstrate a concrete injury, a causal connection, or redressability regarding the "fruits" of the OSC investigation, the court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing. Standing is a prerequisite for subject-matter jurisdiction; without it, a federal court lacks the authority to hear the merits of a case.

Consequently, the court vacated the district court’s judgment. To "vacate" means to set aside the lower court's ruling as if it had not happened. The court then remanded the case with instructions to dismiss it for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This demonstrates a standard procedural outcome: when a plaintiff fails to meet the constitutional requirements for standing, the case must be dismissed, regardless of the underlying merits of the legal arguments.
