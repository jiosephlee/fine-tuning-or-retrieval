Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does the court define 'speculative' causation when multiple independent agencies are involved?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's first standing theory because the causal chain is 'too speculative and attenuated.' The chain involves OSC investigating, the MSPB potentially sanctioning employees, and then the DOJ potentially producing records. The court says we can't rely on the 'unfettered choices' of the MSPB and DOJ. Does this mean that if a causal chain involves more than one independent government agency, it is automatically too speculative to satisfy the 'redressability' requirement of standing?
Answer:
No, the court does not hold that a causal chain involving multiple independent government agencies is *automatically* too speculative to satisfy the redressability requirement of Article III standing. Instead, the court’s analysis focuses on whether the causal connection relies on the "unfettered choices" of "independent actors" who are not parties to the litigation.

Under the standing doctrine established in *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, a plaintiff must demonstrate that their injury was caused by the defendant’s conduct and is "likely to be redressed" by a favorable judicial decision. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court rejected the plaintiff’s theory because the proposed causal chain was "too speculative and attenuated" due to the following factors:

**1. Independence and Discretion of Non-Parties**
The court emphasized that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." In this case, the causal chain required the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DOJ) to make specific decisions (sanctioning employees and producing records, respectively). Because these agencies are independent and their actions are not mandated by the court's judgment against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), the court cannot assume they will act in a way that redresses the plaintiff's injury.

**2. Binding vs. Persuasive Effect**
The court clarified that redressability turns on how a judgment *binds* the parties currently before the court, rather than whether the rationale of the judgment "might persuade actors who are not before the court." Because the MSPB and DOJ were not parties to the lawsuit, a favorable judgment against the OSC does not legally compel them to act. Therefore, the plaintiff failed to show that a victory would "very likely spur the next steps" required to remedy the injury.

**3. Enforcement Discretion**
The court also noted that even regarding the defendant itself (the OSC), a court generally may not compel the exercise of "enforcement discretion." This reinforces the idea that standing is undermined when the path to redress depends on discretionary choices—whether by a third-party agency or the defendant itself—rather than a direct legal consequence of the court's order.

**Summary for the Student**
To satisfy standing in a multi-agency context, a plaintiff must show a direct causal link where the defendant's actions (or inactions) are the proximate cause of the injury. If the redress of that injury depends on a "daisy chain" of discretionary decisions by outside agencies, the court will likely find the theory too speculative. The test is not the *number* of agencies involved, but the *independence* of those agencies and the *discretionary* nature of their actions relative to the parties in the case.
