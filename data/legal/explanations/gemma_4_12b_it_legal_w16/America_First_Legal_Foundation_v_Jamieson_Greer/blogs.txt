Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights in Vacuo: The High Bar for Compelling Agency Action

In the complex machinery of federal oversight, statutes often command that agencies "shall" perform specific functions—investigate misconduct, conduct audits, or initiate disciplinary proceedings. For public interest groups and advocacy organizations, these mandatory duties provide a powerful lever: the ability to sue to compel an agency to do its job. However, the District of Columbia Circuit recently signaled that these statutory mandates do not automatically grant a private plaintiff a seat at the judicial table. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court reinforced a rigorous interpretation of Article III standing, holding that a procedural right to an investigation, divorced from a concrete personal interest, is insufficient to sustain a lawsuit.

The case arose from a dispute over the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) handling of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests. America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought records regarding judicial recusals but was rebuffed by the DOJ on the grounds that the agency does not search employee emails without consent. AFL then turned to the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), the agency tasked with investigating "prohibited personnel practices" and arbitrary FOIA withholdings. When the OSC declined to investigate the DOJ’s email policy, AFL sued to compel the investigation, arguing that the OSC was violating a mandatory duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216.

The D.C. Circuit’s analysis centers on the "case or controversy" requirement of Article III, which demands that a plaintiff suffer a "concrete and particularized" injury that is "actual or imminent," not "conjectural or hypothetical." The court identified two distinct theories of standing offered by AFL, both of which failed to clear this hurdle. First, AFL argued that the OSC's refusal to investigate prevented them from obtaining the records they sought from the DOJ. The court rejected this as a "speculative" causal chain. To win on this point, AFL would have had to prove that a court order would *very likely* compel the OSC to investigate, which would then *very likely* compel the Merit Systems Protection Board to sanction employees, which would then *very likely* cause the DOJ to produce the records. Because this chain relies on the discretionary choices of multiple independent actors—none of whom were before the court—it failed the redressability test.

The more doctrinally significant portion of the opinion, however, concerns AFL’s second theory: that they had a procedural right to an investigation and the information resulting from it. Here, the court deployed the "procedural right in vacuo" doctrine. While the court acknowledged that plaintiffs alleging violations of procedural rights can sometimes enjoy "relaxed" requirements regarding imminence and redressability, those relaxations do not excuse a lack of injury in fact. The court reasoned that while § 1216 might mandate that the OSC *conduct* an investigation, it does not grant a third party a statutory *entitlement* to receive the fruits of that investigation. Without a specific right to receive notices or records from the OSC, AFL suffered no concrete injury from the agency's inaction.

This decision reflects a tightening of the gatekeeping function that standing serves in federal litigation. By distinguishing between an agency’s internal obligation to perform an oversight function and a private citizen’s right to demand that function be performed, the court is insulating the executive branch from "oversight-only" litigation. For public interest groups, this creates a significant hurdle: it suggests that simply pointing to a statute that says an agency "shall" do something is not enough to bring a suit. A plaintiff must instead demonstrate a tangible, personal harm—such as the actual withholding of a record or a specific financial loss—that the court can directly remedy.

The downstream effects of this ruling are noteworthy for administrative law and FOIA advocacy. It effectively narrows the "private attorney general" model of litigation, where NGOs use the courts to force systemic administrative reforms. If a plaintiff cannot sue to compel an investigation because the investigation itself is a discretionary administrative process, then the only remaining path for redress is to sue the primary actor (the DOJ in this case) for the underlying harm (the withholding of records). While this maintains a clear boundary for judicial power, it may also make it more difficult for outside groups to use the judiciary to ensure that internal government watchdogs are fulfilling their statutory mandates. By requiring a "concrete" interest to anchor a procedural right, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that the judiciary is less interested in auditing the internal processes of the executive branch and more focused on redressing specific, individual grievances.



# The Speculative Chain: Causation and the Limits of 'Persuasive' Redressability

Article III standing serves as the ultimate gatekeeper of the federal judiciary, ensuring that courts only adjudicate "Cases" and "Controversies" rather than abstract grievances or hypothetical harms. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit provided a rigorous exercise in this gatekeeping function. By dismissing a challenge to the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) enforcement discretion, the court clarified a critical boundary in standing doctrine: a plaintiff cannot establish a justiciable injury by constructing a "speculative chain" of events involving the discretionary choices of multiple independent government actors.

The dispute centered on a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request made by America First Legal Foundation (AFL) to the Department of Justice (DOJ). When the DOJ refused to search employee emails without consent, AFL sought an investigation by the OSC under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The OSC declined, arguing it lacked the mandate to investigate unless specific FOIA predicates were met. AFL then sued to compel the investigation, arguing that the OSC’s inaction prevented them from receiving the records they sought.

The court’s analysis of AFL’s first standing theory provides a masterclass in the limitations of "speculative" causation. AFL attempted to link the OSC’s refusal to investigate directly to the DOJ’s failure to produce records. However, the court rightly identified that this link was not a direct causal line, but a series of "what ifs." For AFL to succeed, the court would have had to assume that: (1) the OSC would have conducted the investigation; (2) the OSC would have moved for corrective action; (3) the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have sanctioned the employees; and (4) the DOJ would have subsequently complied with the FOIA request.

The court’s rejection of this theory underscores a fundamental principle of standing: causation cannot rely on the "unfettered choices" of independent actors who are not parties to the litigation. Because the OSC, the MSPB, and the DOJ are distinct entities with their own independent authorities and discretionary powers, a judgment against the OSC has no inherent power to force the DOJ's hand. The court signaled that the judiciary is not a tool for "persuading" third-party agencies to change their behavior; rather, it is a venue for adjudicating the legal obligations of the specific defendants before it.

This distinction leads to the second pillar of the court’s holding: the difference between "binding" and "persuasive" redressability. Under *Lujan*, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision will "very likely spur" the steps necessary to redress the injury. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties in the case, not whether the judgment’s rationale "might persuade" outside actors. If the only way to solve the plaintiff's problem is to hope that a judicial opinion inspires another agency to voluntarily comply, the case fails the redressability test.

The doctrinal implications of *America First Legal Foundation* are significant for litigants seeking to challenge multi-agency government policies. It suggests that plaintiffs cannot use the "domino effect" of administrative procedures to manufacture standing. If a plaintiff’s injury is caused by Agency A, they cannot seek relief by suing Agency B on the theory that Agency B’s failure to act is the "root cause" of Agency A’s behavior—unless they can demonstrate a direct, non-speculative legal nexus.

Ultimately, the court’s reasoning protects the judiciary from being transformed into a forum for speculative policy-making. By requiring a concrete, direct causal link and a high probability of redressability, the court ensures that Article III power is reserved for instances where the court can actually provide a remedy, rather than merely offering a judicial "suggestion" that might, in a best-case scenario, move a distant bureaucrat to act.



# Mandatory Duties vs. Discretionary Procedures: Navigating OSC’s Enforcement Maze

When a federal agency fails to comply with the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as a critical watchdog, tasked with investigating "arbitrary or capricious" withholdings. However, a recent decision by the D.C. Circuit in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* highlights a significant hurdle for litigants attempting to use the judiciary to compel agency oversight: the distinction between a mandate to *act* and a right to a specific *result*.

The dispute began when the Department of Justice (DOJ) refused to search employee emails for FOIA records without the employees' consent. America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel the OSC to investigate this policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, which states that the OSC "shall" conduct an investigation into such withholdings. The OSC declined, arguing that the specific statutory triggers for a mandatory investigation (found in FOIA’s own enforcement provisions) were not met. While the district court agreed that the OSC had a mandatory duty to initiate an investigation, it also noted that the specific procedural steps—such as notifying the complainant—remained within the agency's discretion. When the OSC again refused to act, AFL sued to compel the investigation, but the D.C. Circuit ultimately dismissed the case for lack of Article III standing.

The court’s analysis provides a masterclass in the limitations of "procedural standing." AFL argued that it had a procedural right to an OSC investigation and that the denial of this right caused a concrete informational injury. The D.C. Circuit, however, rejected the notion that a "procedural right in vacuo" is sufficient to bring a case into federal court. To have standing, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury caused by the defendant that is likely to be redressed by a favorable court judgment. The court found that AFL’s claims failed on both counts, primarily because the causal chain between an OSC investigation and the desired outcome—the production of DOJ records—was too speculative to satisfy the requirements of redressability.

The court’s reasoning identifies a "causal chain" of uncertainty that serves as a warning to public interest litigants. For AFL to be made whole, the court would have to assume that: (1) the OSC would initiate an investigation; (2) the OSC would pursue corrective action; (3) the Merit Systems Protection Board would sanction the employees; and (4) the DOJ would, in response to those sanctions, finally produce the records. The D.C. Circuit noted that because this chain relies on the "unfettered choices" of multiple independent actors (the OSC, the MSPB, and the DOJ) who are not parties to the lawsuit, the court cannot find that a judgment against the OSC would "very likely spur" the production of the records.

Furthermore, the court clarified the limits of "informational standing." AFL argued that it was entitled to the "fruits" of the OSC investigation—specifically, the notices and information required during a "prohibited personnel practice" inquiry. The court countered that while § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not create a statutory entitlement to the specific procedural outputs of that investigation. Because the OSC has the discretion to decide whether to follow those specific procedural notices, AFL cannot claim a concrete injury based on the denial of those notices.

This opinion reinforces a significant doctrinal boundary: the judiciary is not a tool for micromanaging agency enforcement processes. By distinguishing between the mandatory duty to *begin* an inquiry and the discretionary power to *manage* that inquiry, the court protects the agency’s operational autonomy. For litigants, the takeaway is clear: to succeed in compelling agency action, a plaintiff must show a direct, non-speculative path from a court order to the redress of a concrete injury. In the "enforcement maze" of the OSC, the court will not bridge the gap between an agency's duty to look into a matter and the plaintiff's desire for a specific, multi-agency outcome.
