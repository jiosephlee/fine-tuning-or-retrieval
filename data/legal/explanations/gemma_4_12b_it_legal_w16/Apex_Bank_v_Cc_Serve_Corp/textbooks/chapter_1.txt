Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## CC Serve Corp. and the 'ASPIRE' mark: Registration and services offered

To understand the dispute in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, one must first identify the "prior user" of the mark in question. CC Serve Corp. is a company that provides credit card services. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the standard character mark ASPIRE. This registration was issued in 1998, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. 

The nature of CC Serve’s business is central to the court’s analysis of the marks' usage. CC Serve operates by partnering with banks. Under these arrangements, the bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers, while CC Serve and its affiliates handle the servicing of those accounts. Because CC Serve’s primary business revolves around these credit card services, its ownership of the ASPIRE mark is tied specifically to that commercial activity.

## Apex Bank's application: The 'ASPIRE BANK' marks and 'banking and financing services'

The opposing party, Apex Bank, is a retail bank based in Tennessee. Unlike CC Serve, Apex Bank is a traditional brick-and-mortar institution with 18 branch locations offering personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, mortgages, and loans. Notably, the court highlights that Apex Bank does not currently offer credit cards.

The conflict arose when Apex Bank planned to launch a new internet bank under a different brand. To do so, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) for the word and design marks ASPIRE BANK. Apex sought to register these marks for "[b]anking and financing services." This creates the core of the trademark conflict: a prior user of "ASPIRE" for credit card services versus a new applicant seeking "ASPIRE BANK" for general banking and financing.

## The USPTO Prosecution and the Letter of Protest

Trademark registration is an administrative process handled by the USPTO. During the "prosecution" of Apex Bank's application—the period during which the USPTO reviews the application—CC Serve intervened. CC Serve submitted a formal letter of protest, asserting that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own ASPIRE mark.

Despite this protest, the USPTO's examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK marks for publication. The marks were officially published on December 17, 2019. The publication of a mark is a critical procedural step; it signals that the USPTO has completed its initial review and is notifying the public of the intent to register the mark, thereby opening a window for formal opposition.

## The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) Opposition

Following the publication of the marks, CC Serve initiated a formal opposition in February 2020. This moved the dispute from the initial application review phase to a quasi-judicial proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The TTAB is the administrative body responsible for adjudicating disputes regarding trademark registrations.

The TTAB sustained CC Serve’s opposition, refusing to register Apex Bank’s marks. The Board concluded that there was a "likelihood of confusion" between the two marks. Apex Bank appealed this decision, bringing the case to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, which has jurisdiction over trademark appeals.

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: Likelihood of Confusion standard

The legal foundation for the TTAB’s decision—and the basis for the current appeal—is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). This statute prohibits the registration of a mark if "confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods."

To determine if a "likelihood of confusion" exists, courts and administrative boards typically apply the *DuPont* factors. These are a set of factors (historically thirteen) used to evaluate whether a consumer would be confused about the source of the goods or services. The analysis requires looking at the similarity of the marks themselves, the similarity of the goods or services provided under those marks, and other contextual factors like the number of similar marks already in use. In this case, the Federal Circuit’s task is to determine if the Board correctly applied these factors to the facts of the ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK marks.
