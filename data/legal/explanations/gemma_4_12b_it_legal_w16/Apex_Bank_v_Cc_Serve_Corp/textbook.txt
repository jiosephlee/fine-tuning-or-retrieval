Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural History

## CC Serve Corp. and the 'ASPIRE' mark: Registration and services offered

To understand the dispute in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, one must first identify the "prior user" of the mark in question. CC Serve Corp. is a company that provides credit card services. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the standard character mark ASPIRE. This registration was issued in 1998, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. 

The nature of CC Serve’s business is central to the court’s analysis of the marks' usage. CC Serve operates by partnering with banks. Under these arrangements, the bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers, while CC Serve and its affiliates handle the servicing of those accounts. Because CC Serve’s primary business revolves around these credit card services, its ownership of the ASPIRE mark is tied specifically to that commercial activity.

## Apex Bank's application: The 'ASPIRE BANK' marks and 'banking and financing services'

The opposing party, Apex Bank, is a retail bank based in Tennessee. Unlike CC Serve, Apex Bank is a traditional brick-and-mortar institution with 18 branch locations offering personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, mortgages, and loans. Notably, the court highlights that Apex Bank does not currently offer credit cards.

The conflict arose when Apex Bank planned to launch a new internet bank under a different brand. To do so, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) for the word and design marks ASPIRE BANK. Apex sought to register these marks for "[b]anking and financing services." This creates the core of the trademark conflict: a prior user of "ASPIRE" for credit card services versus a new applicant seeking "ASPIRE BANK" for general banking and financing.

## The USPTO Prosecution and the Letter of Protest

Trademark registration is an administrative process handled by the USPTO. During the "prosecution" of Apex Bank's application—the period during which the USPTO reviews the application—CC Serve intervened. CC Serve submitted a formal letter of protest, asserting that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own ASPIRE mark.

Despite this protest, the USPTO's examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK marks for publication. The marks were officially published on December 17, 2019. The publication of a mark is a critical procedural step; it signals that the USPTO has completed its initial review and is notifying the public of the intent to register the mark, thereby opening a window for formal opposition.

## The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) Opposition

Following the publication of the marks, CC Serve initiated a formal opposition in February 2020. This moved the dispute from the initial application review phase to a quasi-judicial proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The TTAB is the administrative body responsible for adjudicating disputes regarding trademark registrations.

The TTAB sustained CC Serve’s opposition, refusing to register Apex Bank’s marks. The Board concluded that there was a "likelihood of confusion" between the two marks. Apex Bank appealed this decision, bringing the case to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, which has jurisdiction over trademark appeals.

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: Likelihood of Confusion standard

The legal foundation for the TTAB’s decision—and the basis for the current appeal—is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). This statute prohibits the registration of a mark if "confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods."

To determine if a "likelihood of confusion" exists, courts and administrative boards typically apply the *DuPont* factors. These are a set of factors (historically thirteen) used to evaluate whether a consumer would be confused about the source of the goods or services. The analysis requires looking at the similarity of the marks themselves, the similarity of the goods or services provided under those marks, and other contextual factors like the number of similar marks already in use. In this case, the Federal Circuit’s task is to determine if the Board correctly applied these factors to the facts of the ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK marks.

# Legal Framework: The DuPont Factors and Standard of Review

To understand the court’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, a student must first understand how appellate courts review the decisions of lower administrative bodies—in this case, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The court distinguishes between two different standards of review depending on whether it is evaluating a legal conclusion or a finding of fact. 

## Standard of Review: De Novo for Legal Conclusions vs. Substantial Evidence for Facts

The Federal Circuit employs a bifurcated standard of review in this case. When the court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions, it applies a *de novo* standard. This means the court looks at the issue "anew," giving no deference to the Board’s interpretation of the law. If the court finds the Board applied the wrong legal test or reached a legally unsupported conclusion, the court can overrule it regardless of the evidence.

In contrast, when the court reviews the Board’s findings of fact, it applies the "substantial evidence" standard. Substantial evidence is defined as such relevant evidence as a "reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." Under this standard, the appellate court does not re-weigh the evidence or decide if it would have reached the same conclusion as the Board; rather, it checks to see if the Board’s decision was supported by a reasonable interpretation of the record. In *Apex*, this distinction is vital because while the court may agree with the *facts* regarding the similarity of services, it can still vacate a decision if the Board’s *legal* application of those facts to a specific DuPont factor was flawed.

## The DuPont Factors: Overview of the 13 Factors

The core of trademark opposition litigation lies in the "likelihood of confusion" analysis. Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark registration may be refused if the concurrent use of a mark by an applicant and a prior user is likely to cause consumer confusion. To determine this likelihood, courts utilize the "DuPont factors."

These are thirteen specific factors derived from the landmark case *E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* The factors generally examine various elements of the trademark landscape, including the similarity of the marks, the similarity of the goods or services, the sophistication of the consumers, and the degree of inherent distinctiveness of the marks. In *Apex*, the court specifically focuses on the first, second, and sixth factors to determine whether consumers would mistakenly believe that the "ASPIRE" mark and the "ASPIRE BANK" mark emanate from the same source.

## The Role of 'Relevant' Factors in Trademark Analysis

While there are thirteen DuPont factors, the Board is not required to address every single one in every case. The court notes that the Board only needs to consider those factors that are "relevant and of record." This allows the Board to tailor its analysis to the specific facts of the dispute.

However, the court’s role on appeal is to ensure that the Board’s selection of factors is appropriate. In *Apex*, the court highlights a procedural error regarding the scope of these factors. It notes that if the Board makes a factual finding that two sets of services are "highly similar" under one factor (such as the second factor), it must maintain that same scope of similarity when analyzing other factors (such as the sixth factor). The court’s intervention in *Apex* serves as a reminder that while the Board has discretion in which factors to use, the internal consistency of its factual findings across those factors is a legal requirement.

## The Concept of 'Commercial Strength' and 'Weakness'

A significant portion of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis involves determining the "commercial strength" of a mark. This concept is closely tied to the sixth DuPont factor, which examines the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. 

The legal theory is that if a marketplace is "crowded" with many similar marks (e.g., many different companies using the word "Aspire" in the financial sector), the mark is considered "weak." A weak mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection because consumers in a crowded field become more adept at distinguishing between competing brands. Conversely, a strong, unique mark enjoys a broader scope of protection. In *Apex*, the court emphasizes that the Board must correctly identify the "relevant public" to determine what marks are actually "similar" enough to contribute to this analysis of commercial strength. If the Board narrows the scope of the public too much, it may fail to recognize the crowded nature of the marketplace, thereby leading to an incorrect conclusion about the mark's strength.

# Analysis of Service Similarity (The Second DuPont Factor)

To determine whether there is a likelihood of confusion between two marks, courts often apply the "DuPont factors." The second DuPont factor requires an analysis of the similarity of the goods and services offered by the parties. In trademark law, the standard for this factor is not whether the products or services are exactly the same. Instead, the court asks whether the services are "related in some manner" or if the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could create a "mistaken belief" that the products emanate from the same source.

## Defining 'credit card services' vs. 'banking and financing services'

In this case, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (the Board) first had to establish what specific services the parties were actually providing. Because the entry for "credit card services" had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual before CC Serve’s registration, the Board had to interpret the scope of that service. It determined that "credit card services" includes a broad range of activities: issuing cards to finance purchases, counseling on debt, processing payments, monitoring accounts, and providing access to credit scores. 

By establishing this broad definition, the Board created a framework to compare CC Serve’s services against Apex Bank’s "banking and financing services." The goal was to see if the two categories overlapped enough to confuse a consumer who might see both types of services offered under the "ASPIRE" name.

## The 'legally identical' finding: Dictionary definitions and functional overlap

The Board reached the conclusion that the services were "legally identical, in part" by looking at the functional overlap between the two industries. It relied on dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance," noting that these terms encompass the act of extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. 

Because "credit card services" are a primary method by which "banking and financing" are executed, the Board found that the two services were essentially part of the same functional ecosystem. This finding is critical to a likelihood-of-confusion analysis: if the services are legally identical in part, a consumer is much more likely to believe that a company offering one is also offering the other, or that both are provided by the same parent entity.

## The impact of third-party registrations on determining service similarity

To support its finding of similarity, the Board looked beyond the specific descriptions provided by the two litigants and examined third-party registrations. It identified other trademarks that covered both "credit card services" and "banking and financing services." 

This evidence served as a practical indicator of how the marketplace views these services. The Board reasoned that because other companies have successfully registered marks for both types of services, it demonstrates that these services are "of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." This reinforces the idea that consumers do not necessarily view credit card servicing and general banking as mutually exclusive industries; rather, they see them as related offerings.

## Evaluating Apex Bank's argument regarding non-competitive services

Apex Bank attempted to distinguish its services from CC Serve’s by arguing that the two companies were not "directly competitive." Apex pointed out that CC Serve operates by partnering with banks to manage credit card programs rather than providing "banking services" itself. Apex argued that because the business models differed—one being a service provider and the other a retail bank—the services were not the same.

However, the court rejected this argument. Under the second DuPont factor, the inquiry is not whether the companies are direct competitors or whether their business models are identical. The test is whether the services are "related in some manner." Because the court had already established that the services were functionally and legally overlapping, the fact that the companies might not be competing for the exact same customer at the exact same moment did not negate the risk of consumer confusion.

## Why the court affirmed the Board's finding on the second factor

The court affirmed the Board’s finding because the Board’s analysis was grounded in "substantial evidence." The court noted that the Board had carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services and used a logical progression—starting with dictionary definitions, moving to functional overlaps, and concluding with evidence of third-party registrations—to reach its conclusion. 

Because the Board’s analysis successfully demonstrated that the services were highly similar, the court found no legal error. The Board's conclusion that the second DuPont factor weighed heavily in favor of a finding of likelihood of confusion was supported by the facts and the established legal standards for service similarity.

# The 'Crowded Field' and Scope of Evidence (The Sixth DuPont Factor)

## The Purpose of the Sixth DuPont Factor: Crowd Theory and Consumer Education

In trademark law, the "likelihood of confusion" analysis often relies on the DuPont factors—a set of criteria used to determine if a consumer might mistakenly believe that two different marks come from the same source. The sixth DuPont factor specifically examines the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor is rooted in the "crowded field" theory: the idea that if a marketplace is saturated with various marks that are similar to the one in question, the mark itself is likely "weak."

The legal logic behind this theory is based on consumer behavior and education. When a specific word or design appears frequently across many different products or services, consumers become conditioned to distinguish between them. They learn that the word in question does not uniquely identify a single source but is instead a common industry term or a widely used descriptive element. Consequently, a mark in a crowded field is entitled to a narrower scope of protection because the "crowd" of other marks effectively educates the public to see the mark as less distinctive.

## Determining the 'Relevant Public' and the Scope of Marks

To apply the sixth DuPont factor, the court must first identify the "relevant public" and then determine which third-party marks are relevant to the inquiry. Because the goal is to see if the marketplace is "crowded," the evidence must include marks used on goods or services that are similar to those offered by the parties in the dispute. If a mark is used on a completely unrelated product, it does not contribute to a "crowded field" for the specific industry at issue.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (the Board) attempted to define the scope of the "relevant public" as "consumers of ‘credit card services.’" By doing so, the Board intended to filter out third-party marks that were used in other areas of the financial industry, focusing only on those that would directly impact the consumer's perception of credit card branding.

## The Board's Error: Restricting the Scope to 'Credit Card' Marks Only

The Federal Circuit identified a significant legal error in how the Board narrowed this scope. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor (which looks at the similarity of the services), the Board had already concluded that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" were "highly similar" and, in part, "legally identical." The Board based this on the fact that banking and financing definitions encompass the extension of credit and the issuance of credit cards.

However, when the Board moved to the sixth DuPont factor, it abruptly narrowed the "universe" of marks it considered. It chose to look only at marks used for "credit card services," excluding the dozens of third-party marks used for "banking and financing services." By doing this, the Board ignored the very similarity it had just established in its previous analysis. The Board concluded that there were only nine "Aspire-formative" marks for credit card services—a number it deemed insufficient to show that the mark was "commercially or conceptually weak."

## The Requirement for Consistency: Matching the Scope of the Sixth Factor to the Second Factor

The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's finding because of this lack of internal consistency. The court held that the Board cannot use two different standards for "similarity" within the same opinion. If the Board makes a factual finding that the parties' services are highly similar (as it did under the second factor), it must retain that same broad scope when considering the number of similar marks (the sixth factor).

The court's reasoning is straightforward: if "banking and financing" are similar enough to create a likelihood of confusion, then marks used in those fields are similar enough to contribute to a "crowded field." The court explicitly stated that there is no reason to impose a "more stringent legal standard" for similarity under the sixth factor than was used for the second factor. By restricting the evidence to only credit card marks, the Board had artificially cleared the "field," making CC Serve’s mark appear stronger than it might actually be in the broader financial marketplace.

## The Distinction Between 'Considerable' and 'Ubiquitous' Use

A secondary issue in the Board's analysis involved the quantitative threshold required to prove a mark is weak. The Board noted that while there were nine marks for credit card services, this did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use. The court pointed to previous cases, such as *Jack Wolfskin*, to illustrate that "voluminous evidence" or "notable examples" of third-party use are what typically signal a lack of commercial strength.

By vacating the Board's analysis and remanding the case, the Federal Circuit required the Board to reconsider the "crowded field" in light of the 42 other third-party marks that use "Aspire" in the broader banking and financing sectors. The court's goal was to ensure that the Board properly evaluates whether the sheer volume of these marks—now that the scope of "similarity" has been widened—is enough to deprive the mark of the "normal scope of protection" accorded to inherently distinctive marks.

# Commercial Impression and Final Disposition

## The first DuPont factor: Appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression

The first DuPont factor requires a court to assess the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." Unlike a literal comparison of words, this factor focuses on the "overall commercial impression" a consumer receives. The core question is whether a consumer, seeing the mark in a commercial context, would likely believe the services offered under that mark emanate from the same source as the mark in question. 

In the context of trademark disputes, this analysis prevents a narrow focus on minor linguistic differences if the overall "feel" or "brand identity" of the marks is confusingly similar. Because the first factor is about how the mark is perceived by the public, it is heavily influenced by how "strong" or "weak" the mark is considered to be in the marketplace.

## Interdependency of factors: Why the sixth factor's correction necessitates a re-evaluation of the first factor

A critical takeaway from this opinion is the internal consistency required when applying the DuPont factors. The court identified a logical disconnect in the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s analysis: while the Board found the parties’ services to be "highly similar" (and even "legally identical" in part) under the second DuPont factor, it arbitrarily narrowed the scope of marks considered under the sixth DuPont factor.

The sixth factor examines the "number and nature of similar marks" to determine if a mark is "weak" and entitled to a narrow scope of protection. If a mark is found to be weak because it is surrounded by many similar marks, that finding directly impacts the first DuPont factor. This is because the "commercial impression" and "strength" of a mark are inextricably linked; a weak mark carries less distinctiveness in the mind of the consumer. 

Consequently, the court ruled that it could not simply "fix" the sixth factor in isolation. Because the Board’s finding on the sixth factor (the crowdedness of the field) would likely change the determination of the mark’s commercial strength, the Board was required to re-evaluate the first factor as well. This highlights that the DuPont factors are not a series of independent checkboxes but an interrelated web of analysis where a finding in one factor often necessitates a corresponding adjustment in others.

## The Court's final holding and the mandate for remand

The court’s disposition—"affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand"—serves as a specific procedural instruction to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board. By "affirming-in-part," the court agreed with the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor: that the banking and credit card services provided by Apex and CC Serve are highly similar. This finding remains the factual foundation for the rest of the analysis.

By "vacating-in-part," the court nullified the Board's previous conclusions regarding the first and sixth DuPont factors. The court found the Board’s analysis of the sixth factor "legally flawed" because it failed to use the same broad scope of "similarity" that it had established for the services in the second factor. The court "remanded" the case, ordering the Board to perform the analysis again. Specifically, the Board must now consider a broader universe of third-party marks (those in the wider banking and finance industry, not just credit card services) and then re-determine the marks' commercial impressions based on that broader scope.

## Practical implications for trademark applicants and registrants

For trademark practitioners, this opinion underscores the importance of "consistency of scope" during a trademark prosecution or opposition. If a party argues that two sets of services are broad and similar to establish a likelihood of confusion (the second factor), they cannot then argue for a narrow, restricted "universe of marks" when trying to prove that a mark is distinctive or lacks competition (the sixth factor).

The court’s reasoning suggests that the "relevant public" and the "scope of similarity" must remain consistent throughout the entire DuPont analysis. An applicant seeking to register a mark must be prepared for the fact that if their services are deemed "legally identical" to a competitor's, the court will likely look at a much wider array of third-party marks to determine the strength of that mark. This case warns that a "piecemeal" approach to the DuPont factors—using different standards of similarity for different factors—is legally insufficient.
