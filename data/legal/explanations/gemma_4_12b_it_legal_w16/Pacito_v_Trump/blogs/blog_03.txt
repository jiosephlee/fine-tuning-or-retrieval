Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Administrative Law and the Boundaries of Executive De-funding

In the shifting landscape of executive power, few areas offer as much latitude as immigration. The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* highlights a sophisticated tension within that latitude: the distinction between a President’s authority to determine who *enters* the country and the government’s statutory obligations to those already *inside* it. While the court reaffirmed the broad deference traditionally afforded to the Executive Branch under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it simultaneously signaled that such deference does not grant a "blank check" to bypass specific, mandatory funding and service requirements established by Congress.

The core of the dispute involves two distinct executive actions. First, the administration issued Executive Order 14163 to suspend the refugee admissions program. Second, it issued Executive Order 14169 to defund the refugee services necessary for those already admitted. The Ninth Circuit’s handling of these two actions reveals a bifurcated judicial philosophy. On the former, the court leaned heavily into the precedent of *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that the "sole prerequisite" for a suspension under § 1182(f) is a Presidential finding of "detriment." By refusing to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the President’s justifications, the court upheld the hallmark of modern immigration law: the President’s near-plenary power to gatekeep the border.

However, the court took a markedly different path regarding the defunding of "reception and placement" services. Here, the court found that the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The reasoning hinges on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which contains various "shall" provisions requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide employment training, English language training, and cash assistance "to the extent of available appropriations." Unlike the admissions power, which is a discretionary tool of national security, these services are framed as a statutory obligation for a specific class of individuals—those already admitted to the United States.

The court's analytical pivot is significant. It suggests that while the President may have the authority to stop the flow of new arrivals, that authority does not automatically dissolve the government's duty to fulfill existing statutory commitments to those currently in the country. Because the government admitted it had terminated all cooperative agreements without a viable plan to replace them—despite the existence of billions in appropriated funds—the court characterized the abrupt termination as a violation of the mandatory requirements of § 1522. By ordering the reinstatement of these contracts, the court asserted that "available appropriations" create a functional mandate that the Executive cannot summarily ignore via the "detriment" finding used for admissions.

The concurring dissent by Judge Lee captures the precise doctrinal fault line at issue: is a statute that "authorizes" a program also a "mandate" to perform it? Lee argues that the title of § 1522 and the language of its subsections denote a permissive grant of power to the Executive, not a requirement to act. If Lee’s interpretation prevails on appeal or in the final opinion, the government’s ability to "zero out" programs becomes much broader. However, the majority's current stance suggests a more constrained view of executive power, seeking to protect the "substantive" rights of individuals who have already entered the legal fold.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a cautionary note for the "Unitary Executive" theory as applied to immigration. It suggests that the "detriment" clause of § 1182(f) is not a jurisdictional trump card that allows the President to override every other provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act. Instead, the court is carving out a "statutory floor." The takeaway for future litigation is clear: the Executive may have the power to close the door, but it may not be permitted to abandon those who have already walked through it if Congress has mandated a specific standard of care.
