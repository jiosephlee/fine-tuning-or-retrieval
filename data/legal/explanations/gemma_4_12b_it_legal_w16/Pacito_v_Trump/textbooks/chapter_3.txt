Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # Statutory Mandates for Refugee Services: 8 U.S.C. § 1522

## Overview of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Reception and Placement Services

To understand the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Pacito v. Trump*, a student must distinguish between the President's broad authority to regulate immigration and the specific statutory mandates the government faces once a refugee has been admitted to the United States. While the President possesses significant discretion to determine who may enter the country, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs the "reception and placement" services that the government is required to provide to refugees once they are within U.S. borders. These services are designed to facilitate the transition of refugees into American society, ensuring they have the support necessary to become self-sufficient.

The court’s analysis centers on whether the executive branch can unilaterally cease these services. The court distinguishes the President's power under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—which "exudes deference" and allows the President to suspend refugee admissions if they are "detrimental to the interests of the United States"—from the requirements of § 1522. While the President may have the authority to stop new refugees from arriving, the court suggests that the government has a continuing legal obligation to provide for those already admitted.

## Specific Mandatory Obligations: Employment Training, English Language Training, and Cash Assistance

The core of the government's legal obligation lies in the specific language of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A). The court identifies several "mandatory" conditions that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must fulfill, provided there are appropriations available. These include the requirement to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, provide opportunities for English language training, and ensure that cash assistance is available in a manner that does not discourage economic self-sufficiency. Additionally, the statute requires that women be given the same opportunities as men to participate in these programs.

For the law student, the key linguistic trigger here is the word "shall." By using "shall," Congress signaled a mandatory duty rather than a discretionary option. The court notes that while these obligations are limited "to the extent of available appropriations," the government cannot simply ignore the requirements of the statute. The fact that Congress had appropriated billions of dollars for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, despite some rescissions, reinforced the court’s view that the government had the resources to fulfill these mandates.

## Unaccompanied Minor Refugees: Legal and Financial Responsibility under § 1522(d)(2)(B)

The statute provides additional, specific protections for a vulnerable class of refugees: unaccompanied minors. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement is required to assume legal and financial responsibility for such children "if necessary" during the period they are in the United States or in transit before being placed. Furthermore, the Director is authorized to make "necessary decisions" to provide for the child's immediate care.

While these provisions include qualifying phrases like "if necessary," the court interprets them as part of a broader framework of government responsibility. These sections ensure that the government maintains a safety net for minors who lack parental supervision, creating a non-discretionary duty to act when the circumstances of the minor's arrival necessitate it.

## Funding Mechanisms: The Role of Cooperative Agreements with Private and Public Non-profits

A critical procedural element of § 1522 is how the government delivers these services. The statute allows for these services to be provided through "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with 'public or private nonprofit agencies.'" This is significant because it explains the standing of the organizational plaintiffs in *Pacito*. These organizations were the primary vehicles through which the State Department historically delivered the mandated "reception and placement" services.

The court highlights that the government’s decision to terminate these cooperative agreements—without a clear plan to replace them—was a primary driver of the litigation. Because the statute authorizes the use of these private and public partnerships to fulfill its mandates, the government’s unilateral termination of those agreements effectively stalled the delivery of services required by law.

## The Government's Failure to Provide Services: The Court's Finding of a Non-Discretionary Duty

The Ninth Circuit’s ultimate order reflects a finding that the government has a non-discretionary duty to provide for refugees already admitted to the U.S. While the court acknowledged the President’s broad power to suspend the entry of new refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it found that the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenge to the defunding of services under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

The court’s reasoning was grounded in the government's own admissions. During oral argument, the government admitted it was "unaware whether any progress had been made" toward securing new agencies to provide the services mandated by § 1522. Because the government had essentially halted the existing service infrastructure and failed to establish a replacement, the court determined that the government was in violation of its statutory duties. Consequently, the court issued a specific directive: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the "usual and customary services" to refugees already admitted to the United States.

This distinction is further highlighted by Judge Lee’s dissent, which offers a counter-argument for students to consider: the "Authorization" vs. "Mandate" debate. Judge Lee argues that because the title of § 1522 is "Authorization for programs," the word "shall" in the subsequent subsections merely imposes conditions on the government *if* it chooses to fund the programs, rather than requiring it to do so. This highlights a fundamental tension in statutory interpretation—whether "shall" creates a mandatory duty or a mandatory condition on a discretionary act.
