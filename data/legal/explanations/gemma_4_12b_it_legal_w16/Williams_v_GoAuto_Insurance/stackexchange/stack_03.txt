Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How can 'receipt' by a computer system satisfy a requirement for 'taking physical possession'?
Question:
The court cites Black's Law Dictionary, which defines 'receipt' as 'taking physical possession.' However, the court then rules that an automated computer 'listener' receiving an email satisfies this requirement. As a student, I find this contradictory. If the definition of the word requires physical possession, how can a digital data transfer to a server be considered 'physical'? Is the court creating a new legal standard for 'electronic receipt' that overrides the dictionary definition provided?
Answer:
The court is not overriding the dictionary definition of "receipt"; rather, it is interpreting what constitutes "physical possession" in the context of a "modern statute" and contemporary technology.

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the court acknowledges the definition of "receipt" as "taking physical possession" and "receive" as "to come into possession of or get from some outside source." However, the court applies these definitions to the digital environment by expanding the scope of "possession" to include "possession, delivery, or custody" of a request.

The court’s reasoning relies on a functionalist interpretation of the law. It notes that because the statute is "modern" and "set in current times," the court must recognize that "company computers do much that individuals once did." In this context, the court views the computer's "listener" service as the functional equivalent of a human recipient. When the computer system "hears" the name of the web service and "performs the activity," it has effectively taken "possession" of the data. 

Therefore, the court is not creating a new legal standard for "electronic receipt" that exists independently of the dictionary; instead, it is determining that for the purposes of this statute, a computer system "taking possession" of data satisfies the requirement of "physical possession" because the computer serves as the functional surrogate for the human actor who would have traditionally received the physical document.
