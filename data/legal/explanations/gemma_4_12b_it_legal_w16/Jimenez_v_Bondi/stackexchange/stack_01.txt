Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the 'administrative record' limitation affect the scope of the appellate court's review?
Question:
The court states that its review of the BIA’s decision is limited to 'the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.' As a student, I'm struggling to understand how this limits the court. If a petitioner submits a new, compelling piece of evidence during the appeal process that clearly proves they were persecuted, can the court ignore it because it wasn't in the original record provided to the IJ/BIA?
Answer:
The "administrative record" limitation means that the appellate court’s review is confined strictly to the evidence that was presented to, and considered by, the lower agency (in this case, the Board of Immigration Appeals). 

### The Doctrine of the Administrative Record
In administrative law and immigration appeals, the "administrative record" is the body of evidence—including testimonies, documents, and exhibits—that the petitioner submitted to the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). When a court limits its review to this record, it is essentially saying that the appellate court is not conducting a *de novo* trial. Instead, it is reviewing the *legality* and *reasonableness* of the agency's decision based on the facts the agency was allowed to see.

The purpose of this limitation is to ensure due process. It prevents an appellate court from considering evidence that the lower agency never had the opportunity to evaluate. If a petitioner fails to include a piece of evidence in their initial application, they have deprived the agency of the chance to weigh that evidence. Consequently, the appellate court cannot "fix" that omission by considering the evidence for the first time during the appeal.

### Application to *Jimenez v. Bondi*
In *Jimenez*, the court explicitly cites this limitation under the Standard of Review:

> "Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’" (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A)).

Because of this limitation, your hypothetical scenario—submitting a "new, compelling piece of evidence" during the appeal—would likely result in the court ignoring that evidence. If the evidence was not in the original record provided to the IJ/BIA, it is outside the scope of the court's jurisdiction to consider it. 

The court's role is to determine if the BIA’s decision was supported by "substantial evidence" within that specific record. In *Jimenez*, the court found that the record did not show that the Petitioners suffered persecution or that the government was unable to protect them. Even if the Petitioners were to present a "compelling" new document during the appeal, the court would likely maintain that it cannot use that document to overturn the BIA's decision because that document was not part of the "administrative record on which the order of removal [was] based."

**Summary for your studies:** If the evidence isn't in the record, it effectively doesn't exist for the purposes of the appellate review. To have a piece of evidence considered by the court, it must first be entered into the record at the initial agency level.
