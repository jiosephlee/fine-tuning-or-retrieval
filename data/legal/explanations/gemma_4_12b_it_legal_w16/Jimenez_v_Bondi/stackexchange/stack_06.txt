Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why does failing an asylum claim automatically result in a failure of a withholding of removal claim in this context?
Question:
The conclusion states that because the petitioners failed to meet asylum standards, they 'necessarily fail' to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. I thought withholding of removal was a non-discretionary relief with a different legal standard. If the facts of the case are identical, why does the outcome have to be the same? Does this mean that in practice, if you can't prove the 'persecution' element for asylum, it's impossible to win withholding?
Answer:
In the context of *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the reason failing an asylum claim results in an automatic failure of a withholding of removal (WOR) claim is due to the **hierarchical relationship between the two legal standards.**

### The Threshold Hierarchy
While asylum and withholding of removal are different forms of relief with different consequences (asylum is discretionary, while withholding is non-discretionary), they share a common foundational requirement: a well-founded fear of persecution. However, the legal "bar" for proving that fear differs significantly between the two:

1.  **Asylum:** Requires a "well-founded fear" of persecution. This is generally considered the lower of the two thresholds.
2.  **Withholding of Removal:** Requires a "higher standard" of well-founded fear (often interpreted as a "clear probability" that persecution will occur).

Because the withholding of removal standard is a higher bar than the asylum standard, it is logically impossible to satisfy the higher requirement if the lower one has not been met. As the court notes in the conclusion, because the petitioners failed to demonstrate the fear of persecution required for asylum, they "necessarily fail" to meet the more stringent requirements for withholding.

### The Role of the "Persecution" Element
To your specific question regarding the "persecution" element: Yes, in practice, if an applicant cannot prove that the harm they suffered (or fear) rises to the legal definition of "persecution," they generally cannot win withholding of removal.

In *Jimenez*, the court looked at the collective harm suffered by the petitioners—which included threatening phone calls, a brochure with a death threat, and instances of emotional and psychological distress. The court held that while these events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," they were insufficient to rise to the level of "persecution" as defined by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). 

Since "persecution" is a prerequisite for both asylum and withholding of removal, failing to establish that the harm reaches that threshold is a "fatal" error for both claims. Even though the standards for the *probability* of that persecution differ, they both rely on the underlying fact that the harm must actually constitute "persecution." If the facts do not support a finding of persecution, the claim fails at the first hurdle, regardless of which form of relief is being sought.
