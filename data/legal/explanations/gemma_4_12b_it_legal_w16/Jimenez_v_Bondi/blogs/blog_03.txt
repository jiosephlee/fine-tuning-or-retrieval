Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The Reporting Requirement and the 'Unable or Unwilling' Analysis

The path to asylum in the United States often hinges on a difficult evidentiary hurdle: proving that the home government is "unable or unwilling" to protect the applicant from persecution. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reinforced a rigorous standard for this analysis, emphasizing that while a failure to report persecution to authorities is not an automatic bar to asylum, it creates a formidable evidentiary vacuum. By distinguishing between isolated incidents of police apathy and systemic government failure, the court underscored the difficulty applicants face when they fear that seeking state protection is both futile and dangerous.

The "unable or unwilling" prong of the asylum analysis requires an applicant to demonstrate that the government is either physically incapable of stopping the persecutor or politically unwilling to do so. As the Tenth Circuit noted, the most reliable evidence for this conclusion comes from the government’s actual response—or lack thereof—to the applicant's past mistreatment. When an applicant fails to report the persecution, the court is left without a baseline for what the government *would* have done. In *Jimenez*, the court pointed out that because the petitioners had professional ties to the Colombian justice system but chose not to utilize those channels to report the threats they received, the court lacked the necessary "notice" to evaluate the government's potential response.

The court's reasoning highlights a critical distinction between "one-off" interactions with law enforcement and "systemic" government failure. The petitioners argued that their encounter with a police officer who told them to "not seek trouble" evidenced a government unwilling to protect them. However, the Tenth Circuit rejected this, noting that a single conversation with an unhelpful officer does not constitute evidence of a broader, systemic inability to provide protection. To succeed on this prong, the court suggested that an applicant must move beyond anecdotal instances of police apathy to show a pervasive failure of the state to intervene in the face of violence.

This creates a significant challenge for applicants in environments where the state is perceived as complicit in the persecution. The law allows for a "futile or dangerous" exception, where an applicant may be excused from reporting if they can show that doing so would put them at greater risk. Yet, the *Jimenez* court set a high bar for this showing. Because the record indicated that the Colombian government was already attempting searches and seizures at the property in question, the court found that the government was not "unable" to act. The court essentially ruled that if the state is taking *any* action against the persecutor’s activities, the applicant bears a heavy burden to prove that such actions are insufficient or that the specific persecutor is somehow immune to the state's reach.

The doctrinal implications of *Jimenez* suggest a shift toward a more functionalist, evidence-heavy inquiry into state protection. By prioritizing the government's documented actions over an applicant's subjective fears, the court places the burden of proof squarely on the applicant to provide a theory of state paralysis that is more than merely hypothetical. For future litigation, this means that applicants who do not report persecution will need to provide robust, corroborative evidence—such as news reports of state-sponsored violence or evidence of systemic corruption—to overcome the "evidentiary vacuum" created by their silence. The ruling serves as a reminder that in the eyes of the court, the absence of a government response is much easier to prove when the government has been given the opportunity to respond.
