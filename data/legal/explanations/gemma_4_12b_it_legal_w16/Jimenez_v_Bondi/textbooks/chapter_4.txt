Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # The Nexus Requirement and Mixed-Motive Analysis

## The Nexus Requirement: 'At least one central reason'

In asylum law, it is not enough for an applicant to prove they suffered harm or were threatened; they must also prove that the harm was linked to a specific, statutorily protected ground. This connection is known as the "nexus." Under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), an applicant must demonstrate that the persecution was "on account of" race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the petitioners sought to establish that their persecution was on account of their political opinion.

To satisfy this requirement, the court employs a specific standard: the applicant must establish that the protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. This means that the political opinion cannot be a minor factor in the persecutor's decision to target the individual. Instead, it must be a primary motivation. If the harm is driven by something else—such as a personal vendetta, a business dispute, or a criminal investigation—and the political opinion is merely a secondary or incidental factor, the nexus requirement is not met.

## Statutorily Protected Grounds: Political opinion in this case

The petitioners in *Jimenez* argued that their shared political opinion was the driver of the violence they faced. They pointed to their long-standing membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and their active role in campaigning for a presidential candidate as the basis for the threats. They contended that because they were campaigning for a rival of the current president, they were being targeted by "Don Rafa" and his associates for their political stance.

However, the court clarified that for a political opinion to qualify as a "central reason" for persecution, it must be the core of the conflict. The legal framework requires the applicant to show that the persecution would not have occurred but for their political activities. If the record suggests that the persecution would have happened regardless of the applicant's politics—perhaps because of a professional interaction or a legal dispute—the political opinion fails to serve as the necessary nexus.

## Mixed-Motive Analysis: Distinguishing central motives from tangential reasons

One of the more complex aspects of asylum jurisprudence is the "mixed-motive" analysis. The court recognizes that a persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual. A person might be targeted because they belong to a certain social group *and* because they are a business competitor. The law does not require that the persecutor have *only* one motive; rather, it requires that at least one of those motives be a statutorily protected ground.

To determine which motive is "central," the court looks for evidence that identifies the primary catalyst for the violence. The court explicitly warns against finding a nexus if the protected ground is "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." In these cases, if the primary motive is a non-protected reason (such as a property dispute), and the political opinion is merely a "minor role" in the background of the applicant’s life, the claim for asylum will fail.

## Fact-Specific Analysis: Jimenez’s professional conflict vs. his political affiliation

In applying these standards to the facts of *Jimenez*, the court had to weigh two competing narratives. On one side was the petitioners' political involvement: they had been Liberal Party members since 2012 and were actively campaigning in 2022. On the other side was Jimenez's professional conduct as a provisional lawyer. Jimenez had investigated a property owned by "Don Rafa," a leader of a narco-trafficking group, discovering that the property was being used for criminal activities.

The court looked closely at the timeline and the specific content of the threats to distinguish between these two motives. The court noted that while the petitioners received some initial threats related to the Liberal Party, the violence significantly escalated only *after* Jimenez became involved in the property dispute. Furthermore, the specific accusations made by the men who threatened Jimenez were not about his political campaigning; instead, they accused him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office specifically regarding the searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property.

## The Court’s Finding: Why the property dispute was the predominant motive

Ultimately, the court found that the petitioners failed to establish a nexus because the property dispute was the predominant motive behind the harm. The court observed that the petitioners had remained undeterred by earlier, politically-affiliated threats, continuing their campaign despite the murder of a colleague. It was only after Jimenez’s professional intervention—investigating Don Rafa’s property—that the threats became severe and physical.

Because the evidence showed that the persecutors were reacting to Jimenez’s role as a lawyer and his involvement in a property investigation, the court concluded that their political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate" to the main reason for the harm. Since the harm was primarily motivated by a professional and personal conflict with a criminal actor, rather than the petitioners' political beliefs, the court affirmed the denial of asylum.
