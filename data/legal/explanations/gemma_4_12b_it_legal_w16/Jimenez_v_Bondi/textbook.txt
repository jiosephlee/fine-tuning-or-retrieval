Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Factual Background: Political activity and the Liberal Party

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, are natives of Bogota, Colombia. They moved to the United States in December 2022 after a series of escalating events in their home country. The family were established business owners who operated a furniture factory in Bogota. Their involvement in Colombian politics dates back to 2012, during which time they became members of the Colombian Liberal Party. 

In 2022, the family actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a candidate in the Colombian presidential election. Their activities included door-to-door campaigning in local neighborhoods. It was during this period of political engagement that the petitioners began to face adversity. Starting in May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls regarding his Liberal Party involvement. The climate of danger was underscored by the fact that a colleague of Jimenez, who had also received threats, was kidnapped and murdered in early June 2022. Despite these events, the petitioners continued their political activities.

## The Conflict: Jimenez’s professional involvement with 'Don Rafa'

The trajectory of the petitioners' situation shifted significantly due to Jimenez’s professional work as a provisional lawyer and law student. In June 2022, Jimenez took on a case for a client regarding a property dispute. While investigating the matter, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the property was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, widely known as "Don Rafa." 

The court's record establishes that Don Rafa was not merely a private citizen but a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group aligned with the current Colombian president. Jimenez’s investigation revealed that the property was being used as a base for narco-trafficking, prostitution, and as a day-rental for migrants. Furthermore, the premises were linked to reports of kidnapping and murder. When Jimenez attempted to navigate the situation, a local police officer explicitly warned him to stay away from the property and to avoid seeking further "problems."

## Escalation of Threats: From phone calls to physical harassment and witness intimidation

Following his investigation into Don Rafa's property, the threats against the petitioners intensified and became more specific. Jimenez received a brochure at his factory threatening "death to snitches from Liberal Party." Shortly thereafter, while working at the factory at night, Jimenez was held at gunpoint by two men. These men accused him of being a "snitch" for the National Prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for the law enforcement searches and seizures at the property in question.

The harassment continued as the two men returned to the factory while the petitioners were out of town. On this occasion, the men assaulted a factory employee and demanded to know the location of the petitioners, again labeling them "snitches" and stating that the order to target them came directly from Don Rafa. The violence moved closer to the family's children when Jimenez witnessed a man approaching his son at school, covering the boy's mouth and ordering him to "run to your dad." These incidents prompted the family to move to the countryside for three weeks before they decided to flee the country.

## The Flight to the United States

Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez sought assistance from the authorities. He reported the incident involving his son to a police patrol officer. However, the officer informed Jimenez that nothing could be done and suggested that police officers were contributing to the underlying problems. The officer advised the family to leave the country, which is what the petitioners ultimately did. They entered the United States without being admitted or paroled by an immigration officer.

## Procedural History: From the Notice to Appear to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA)

The legal process began three months after the petitioners' arrival when the Department of Homeland Security served them with individual Notices to Appear (NTA). These notices charged the petitioners as removable from the United States. In August 2023, the petitioners admitted the allegations in the DHS notices, and the Immigration Judge (IJ) charged them as inadmissible, directing their removal to Colombia.

During these proceedings, the petitioners applied for asylum and withholding of removal. The IJ denied these applications, finding that the petitioners failed to prove they suffered past persecution, that their harm had a nexus to a statutorily protected ground, that internal relocation was unavailable, and that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The BIA subsequently affirmed the IJ's decision and rejected the petitioners' due process arguments regarding their right to counsel.

## Standard of Review: Substantial evidence and the limitations of the administrative record

The Tenth Circuit reviews these cases under specific jurisdictional and evidentiary standards. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, the court has the authority to review final orders of removal, including consolidated reviews of asylum claims. However, this review is strictly limited to the "administrative record"—the body of evidence and documents upon which the BIA based its order.

The court applies a "substantial evidence" standard to the BIA’s findings of fact. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings are considered conclusive unless the administrative record contains enough evidence to compel a reasonable adjudicator to reach a different conclusion. While the court's primary focus is the BIA's decision, it is permitted to consult the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds for the initial denial to clarify the record.

# Legal Framework for Asylum and Withholding of Removal

To understand the Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, it is necessary to first understand the statutory framework of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The court does not decide this case in a vacuum; rather, it applies specific definitions and standards of proof mandated by federal law to determine whether the petitioners qualify for discretionary relief from removal.

## Definition of a 'Refugee' under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA)

Under the INA, asylum is a discretionary form of relief granted to noncitizens who meet the legal definition of a "refugee." To qualify as a refugee, an individual must be outside their country of nationality and be unable or unwilling to return to that country, or avail themselves of that country's protection. This inability or unwillingness must be predicated on a specific ground: the individual must be facing persecution, or have a well-founded fear of persecution, on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. 

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners sought protection based on their political opinions and their experiences in Colombia. The court’s analysis begins by evaluating whether the petitioners' experiences fit into these specific statutory categories and whether those experiences rise to the level of "persecution" as defined by the INA and subsequent judicial interpretation.

## Burden of Proof: Preponderance of the Evidence

In asylum proceedings, the burden of proof lies squarely on the applicant. To be granted relief, the petitioner must demonstrate by a "preponderance of the evidence" that they qualify as a refugee. This is a standard of proof that requires the applicant to show that it is more likely than not that their claims are true based on the administrative record. 

Because the petitioners bear this burden, the court focuses on the evidence provided in their testimonies and the subsequent findings of the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). If the evidence does not reach the preponderance threshold—meaning there is substantial evidence to support the government's conclusion that the criteria were not met—the court will deny the petition.

## The Three-Pronged Test for Past Persecution

A primary pathway to obtaining refugee status is through a showing of past persecution. If an applicant can prove they were persecuted in the past, it creates a "rebuttable presumption" of a well-founded fear of future persecution. To establish this past persecution, the court applies a three-pronged test:

1.  **The Persecution Prong:** The applicant must show an incident or series of incidents that rise to the level of persecution. The court clarifies that while physical harm is not strictly required, the harm must be "offensive" and involve more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. In *Jimenez*, the court emphasized that "threats alone are insufficient" and that psychological distress alone, without more extreme harm, typically does not meet this threshold.
2.  **The Nexus Prong:** The persecution must be "on account of" one of the statutorily protected grounds (e.g., political opinion). This means the protected ground must be "at least one central reason" for the harm. It cannot be incidental or tangential.
3.  **The Government Protection Prong:** The persecution must be committed by the government or by forces that the government is "unable or unwilling" to control.

## The 'Well-Founded Fear' Standard

Even if an applicant cannot prove past persecution, they may still qualify for asylum by demonstrating a "well-founded fear" of future persecution. This standard is closely tied to the government's ability to protect the applicant. The court looks for evidence of the government’s response to past mistreatment, specifically whether the government tried to stop the persecutors or offered the applicant protection (such as protective custody). 

Crucially, the court notes that for the government to be deemed "unable or unwilling," the applicant must usually provide evidence that they sought help from the government and were denied, or that seeking help would have been futile or dangerous. In *Jimenez*, the court found that because the petitioners did not sufficiently report their issues to the government or show a systemic failure of the Colombian authorities, they failed to meet this standard.

## The Relationship Between Asylum and Withholding of Removal

The INA provides two primary forms of relief discussed in this case: asylum and withholding of removal. While they share similar criteria regarding persecution and protected grounds, they differ in their legal standards. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief, whereas withholding of removal is a non-discretionary protection.

A key procedural rule highlighted in *Jimenez* is that withholding of removal has a higher standard than asylum. Therefore, if an applicant fails to establish the requirements for asylum—specifically the showing of a well-founded fear of persecution—they "necessarily fail" to meet the higher standard required for withholding of removal. By failing the asylum analysis on the facts of their case, the petitioners were automatically ineligible for withholding of removal.

# Analyzing the Threshold of Persecution

## Defining Persecution: Beyond Restrictions to Life and Liberty

To qualify for asylum, an applicant must establish that they have suffered "persecution" or have a well-founded fear of future persecution. Because the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not provide a formal definition of "persecution," the courts have developed a qualitative standard to determine what constitutes the harm. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that persecution requires the "infliction of suffering or harm" in a manner that is "regarded as offensive." Crucially, the court distinguishes persecution from "mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty." For a law student, this distinction is vital: not every negative experience or state-sanctioned restriction qualifies as persecution. The harm must reach a specific level of severity that transcends common hardships or minor interference with an individual’s daily life.

## Cumulative Impact: Assessing Multiple Incidents as a Whole

A significant procedural and substantive rule in asylum law is that the court does not evaluate every incident in a vacuum. Instead, the court considers the "cumulative effects of multiple incidents." This means that while a single incident might fall below the threshold of persecution, a series of repeated events may collectively rise to that level. In *Jimenez*, the court looked at the totality of the Petitioners' experiences, including weekly threatening phone calls, the receipt of a threatening brochure, two separate gunpoint confrontations at their factory, and a frightening encounter involving the Petitioners' son at school. By looking at these events together, the court determines whether the aggregate experience constitutes a "persecution" profile.

## Threats vs. Physical Harm: Why Threats Alone May Be Insufficient

The *Jimenez* opinion highlights a critical hurdle for many asylum seekers: the distinction between a threat of harm and the actual infliction of harm. The court explicitly states that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." While the court acknowledges that physical harm is not a strict requirement for an asylum claim, the harm that *is* suffered must be "offensive." 

In this case, the Petitioners admitted they suffered no "lasting physical harm." Consequently, the burden shifted to proving that the threats and the emotional toll they produced were sufficiently severe to meet the legal definition of persecution. The court rejected this, noting that while the Petitioners were subjected to alarming situations, the record did not show an offensive level of harm that would compel a finding of persecution. This teaches the student that for an asylum claim to succeed without physical injury, the non-physical harm must be demonstrably severe and not merely a series of "sporadic threats."

## Emotional and Psychological Harm: The High Bar for Asylum Based on Stress

A major point of contention in *Jimenez* involves the weight of psychological distress. The Petitioners argued that their experiences caused lasting emotional harm, such as their wife’s inability to sleep and their son’s panic attacks. The court acknowledged that these events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling." However, the court drew a firm line, stating that the court has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." 

This underscores a high evidentiary bar: "distress" is not synonymous with "persecution." For a law student, this means that when analyzing an opinion, one must look for the court's distinction between a person’s internal emotional state (which may be genuine) and the external legal standard of "offensive harm" (which requires a higher threshold of severity). The court's refusal to grant asylum based on these stressors suggests that the legal system requires a more tangible or severe manifestation of harm than mere psychological anxiety.

## Comparison to Case Precedents: Distinguishing 'Stressful' Events from 'Persecution'

To justify its decision, the court relied on established case law to provide context for what the law considers "too low" a threshold. The court cited *Chhetri v. Rosen*, where telephone threats were found insufficient to constitute persecution, and *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, where even "threats with machetes and being robbed six times" were deemed insufficient because the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand. 

By citing these cases, the court illustrates the "sliding scale" of persecution. If machete threats and multiple robberies do not constitute persecution in some contexts, then the threats and gunpoint encounters in *Jimenez*—which the court found lacked the necessary "offensive" quality—certainly do not. For the student, this demonstrates how courts use precedent to calibrate the definition of persecution, ensuring that the standard remains high and consistent across different fact patterns.

# The Nexus Requirement and Mixed-Motive Analysis

## The Nexus Requirement: 'At least one central reason'

In asylum law, it is not enough for an applicant to prove they suffered harm or were threatened; they must also prove that the harm was linked to a specific, statutorily protected ground. This connection is known as the "nexus." Under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), an applicant must demonstrate that the persecution was "on account of" race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the petitioners sought to establish that their persecution was on account of their political opinion.

To satisfy this requirement, the court employs a specific standard: the applicant must establish that the protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. This means that the political opinion cannot be a minor factor in the persecutor's decision to target the individual. Instead, it must be a primary motivation. If the harm is driven by something else—such as a personal vendetta, a business dispute, or a criminal investigation—and the political opinion is merely a secondary or incidental factor, the nexus requirement is not met.

## Statutorily Protected Grounds: Political opinion in this case

The petitioners in *Jimenez* argued that their shared political opinion was the driver of the violence they faced. They pointed to their long-standing membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and their active role in campaigning for a presidential candidate as the basis for the threats. They contended that because they were campaigning for a rival of the current president, they were being targeted by "Don Rafa" and his associates for their political stance.

However, the court clarified that for a political opinion to qualify as a "central reason" for persecution, it must be the core of the conflict. The legal framework requires the applicant to show that the persecution would not have occurred but for their political activities. If the record suggests that the persecution would have happened regardless of the applicant's politics—perhaps because of a professional interaction or a legal dispute—the political opinion fails to serve as the necessary nexus.

## Mixed-Motive Analysis: Distinguishing central motives from tangential reasons

One of the more complex aspects of asylum jurisprudence is the "mixed-motive" analysis. The court recognizes that a persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual. A person might be targeted because they belong to a certain social group *and* because they are a business competitor. The law does not require that the persecutor have *only* one motive; rather, it requires that at least one of those motives be a statutorily protected ground.

To determine which motive is "central," the court looks for evidence that identifies the primary catalyst for the violence. The court explicitly warns against finding a nexus if the protected ground is "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." In these cases, if the primary motive is a non-protected reason (such as a property dispute), and the political opinion is merely a "minor role" in the background of the applicant’s life, the claim for asylum will fail.

## Fact-Specific Analysis: Jimenez’s professional conflict vs. his political affiliation

In applying these standards to the facts of *Jimenez*, the court had to weigh two competing narratives. On one side was the petitioners' political involvement: they had been Liberal Party members since 2012 and were actively campaigning in 2022. On the other side was Jimenez's professional conduct as a provisional lawyer. Jimenez had investigated a property owned by "Don Rafa," a leader of a narco-trafficking group, discovering that the property was being used for criminal activities.

The court looked closely at the timeline and the specific content of the threats to distinguish between these two motives. The court noted that while the petitioners received some initial threats related to the Liberal Party, the violence significantly escalated only *after* Jimenez became involved in the property dispute. Furthermore, the specific accusations made by the men who threatened Jimenez were not about his political campaigning; instead, they accused him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office specifically regarding the searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property.

## The Court’s Finding: Why the property dispute was the predominant motive

Ultimately, the court found that the petitioners failed to establish a nexus because the property dispute was the predominant motive behind the harm. The court observed that the petitioners had remained undeterred by earlier, politically-affiliated threats, continuing their campaign despite the murder of a colleague. It was only after Jimenez’s professional intervention—investigating Don Rafa’s property—that the threats became severe and physical.

Because the evidence showed that the persecutors were reacting to Jimenez’s role as a lawyer and his involvement in a property investigation, the court concluded that their political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate" to the main reason for the harm. Since the harm was primarily motivated by a professional and personal conflict with a criminal actor, rather than the petitioners' political beliefs, the court affirmed the denial of asylum.

# Government Protection and Internal Relocation

To qualify for asylum, an applicant must demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them from. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit evaluates this requirement by looking at the government’s response to the applicant’s past experiences and the feasibility of the applicant moving to a different part of their home country to escape harm.

## The 'Unable or Unwilling' Analysis: Evaluating Government Response

The "unable or unwilling" analysis focuses on the government’s response to an applicant's past persecution. According to the court, two types of information weigh heavily in this determination: whether the government attempted to stop the persecutors and whether the government offered the applicant specific protections, such as placing them in protective custody. This analysis is inherently tied to whether the government had notice of the persecution. Without notice, the court cannot determine how the authorities would have reacted or if they would have offered help. While a failure to report the persecution to the authorities can undercut a claim, it is not automatically fatal if the applicant can demonstrate that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

## The Significance of Reporting: Why Failing to Report Can Undercut a Claim

In the case of the Petitioners, the court noted that Jimenez and his father both worked or currently work within the Colombian government. Despite these professional ties and their knowledge of the legal system, the Petitioners failed to report the wrongdoing or corruption they experienced. The court emphasized that without a report, there is no way to know how the police would have responded. Because the Petitioners did not seek help through Jimenez’s law enforcement contacts or outside their immediate neighborhood, the court found it difficult to establish that the government was actually unwilling or unable to protect them.

## Systemic Failure vs. Individual Apathy: The Limitations of 'One-Off' Police Interactions

The Petitioners attempted to argue that the government was unable to protect them because they had spoken to two individual police officers who told them not to "seek trouble." However, the court distinguished between individual police apathy and systemic government failure. The court clarified that a "one-off conversation" with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily prove that the government as a whole is unable or unwilling to protect its citizens. To prevail on this point, an applicant generally needs to show evidence of systemic failures rather than the actions or attitudes of a single officer.

## Evidence of Government Action: The Impact of Prior Searches and Seizures

Crucially, the record in *Jimenez* contained evidence that directly contradicted the Petitioners' claim that the government was unable to control their persecutors. The court noted that the Colombian government was already aware of the property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the criminal conduct occurring there. This history of government intervention served as evidence that the authorities were, in fact, capable of taking action against the persecutor's base of operations, thereby undermining the Petitioners’ argument that the government was powerless.

## The Internal Relocation Defense: Feasibility and Reasonability of Moving Within Colombia

Even if an applicant establishes a well-founded fear of persecution, the government may rebut the asylum claim by showing that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their country. The court assesses whether such relocation is "feasible" and "reasonable." In *Jimenez*, the court found that the Petitioners could have mitigated their risk by moving to another part of Colombia. The court pointed out that the family had already lived safely with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. Furthermore, because the threats were specific to a particular neighborhood rather than a nationwide order to leave the country, the court concluded that internal relocation was a viable option.

## Rebutting Hypothetical Hardships

When evaluating internal relocation, the court will not grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships. The Petitioners argued that relocation was unworkable because of potential administrative burdens, economic costs, or the possibility that their relatives in Colombia might also hold opposing political opinions. The court rejected these arguments as "haphazard" and speculative. Because the court is tasked with assessing the evidence in the record, it will not base a ruling on "what if" scenarios regarding the difficulties of moving, provided the record supports the fact that safe relocation is a reasonable possibility.

# Conclusion and Final Disposition

## Summary of the Court's Findings

In its final analysis, the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA) decision to deny the petitioners’ applications for asylum and withholding of removal. The court’s decision rested on the "substantial evidence" standard, which requires the court to determine whether the record provides enough evidence that any reasonable adjudicator would find the BIA’s conclusions correct. In this case, the court found that the petitioners failed to meet the three essential elements required to establish a claim for asylum based on past persecution.

First, the court held that the petitioners did not establish "past persecution." While the court acknowledged the petitioners faced threatening phone calls and physical confrontations, it emphasized that threats alone—especially those lacking a corresponding history of physical harm—do not reach the legal threshold of persecution. The court noted that while the petitioners suffered emotional and psychological distress, such harm is rarely sufficient to grant asylum in the absence of more extreme physical mistreatment. Second, the court found a lack of "nexus." Because the petitioners’ primary conflict involved a property dispute and professional interference with a criminal figure (Don Rafa), their political affiliations were deemed "tangential" rather than a "central reason" for the harm. Finally, the court found that the petitioners failed to demonstrate that the Colombian government was "unable or unwilling" to protect them, largely because the petitioners did not report the incidents to the authorities and because the government had already shown an ability to act against the persecutors' property.

## The Consequence of Failing Asylum: Automatic denial of withholding of removal

A critical procedural takeaway from *Jimenez v. Bondi* is the relationship between the legal standards for asylum and withholding of removal. The court notes that because the petitioners failed to demonstrate a "well-founded fear of persecution" under the asylum standard, they "necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." 

For the student of immigration law, this illustrates the hierarchical nature of these protections. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief that requires a "well-founded fear" of persecution. Withholding of removal is a mandatory form of relief, but it requires a much higher burden of proof: the applicant must show it is "more likely than not" (a probability of at least 50%) that they will suffer persecution if returned. Because the threshold for withholding is higher than that of asylum, an applicant who cannot meet the lower "well-founded fear" standard is automatically disqualified from receiving withholding of removal.

## Key Takeaways for Asylum Practitioners: Nexus and Relocation

The opinion provides two significant pedagogical examples of how courts evaluate the "nexus" and "internal relocation" requirements. Regarding nexus, the court highlights the "mixed-motive" problem. In many cases, a persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual—for example, a combination of political rivalry and a private business dispute. To succeed, the applicant must prove that a statutorily protected ground (like political opinion) was "at least one central reason" for the harm. In *Jimenez*, the court clarified that if the primary motive is a personal or professional dispute, the political opinion is "subordinate" and does not satisfy the nexus requirement.

Regarding internal relocation, the court provides a practical standard for what constitutes "feasible" safety. Even if the petitioners had met the other criteria for asylum, the court noted that they could have reasonably relocated to another part of Colombia. The court pointed to the fact that the family had already lived safely in the countryside for three weeks and that the threats were localized to a specific neighborhood. This underscores the principle that an applicant must show that the danger is so pervasive that no reasonable relocation within the country could provide safety. Because the petitioners could mitigate their risk by moving to a different region, their claim for relief was further weakened.
