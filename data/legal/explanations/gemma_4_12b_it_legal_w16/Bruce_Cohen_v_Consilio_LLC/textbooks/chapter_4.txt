Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 4: # Dismissal of MWTA Claims and Injunctive Relief

## The MWTA Claim: Lack of Statutory Authority for Civil Penalties

In addressing the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim, the court affirmed the district court’s summary judgment because Cohen failed to establish a legal basis for the penalties he sought. Cohen’s claim was based on the allegation that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice regarding a change in its overtime policy. However, the court noted two critical factors that undermined this claim during the summary judgment phase.

First, Cohen had already stipulated that he had received all the pay that Consilio owed him under the law. Second, and more importantly for the legal analysis, Cohen failed to identify any specific statutory authority within the MWTA that would permit the imposition of civil penalties. Because the request for penalties lacked a foundation in the underlying statute and the primary debt (the wages) had been settled by stipulation, there was no viable legal argument to support the claim.

## Waiver of Issues: Consequences of Failing to Provide Legal Authority

The court’s refusal to disturb the district court’s order on the MWTA claim illustrates the principle of waiver in appellate practice. The court observed that Cohen’s brief offered "little to no discussion" of the MWTA claim and provided no legal authority to support his request for penalties. 

When a party fails to support an assertion with specific legal arguments or citations in their brief, they effectively waive the issue. Appellate courts generally will not provide independent legal research to bolster a party's position; instead, they review the arguments as presented. Because Cohen did not provide a legal roadmap for how the MWTA allowed for his specific requested relief, the court correctly declined to address the claim.

## Injunctive Relief: Procedural Requirements for Seeking Affirmative Injunctions

The court also addressed Cohen’s request for an affirmative injunction, which would have required Consilio to rescind its overtime policy. While Cohen argued that this was a necessary remedy, the court found the request procedurally improper. 

The court noted a discrepancy between the relief Cohen originally sought in his initial complaint and the relief he requested in his summary judgment brief. In the complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief, but in his later briefing, he pivoted to requesting a different form of relief—specifically, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. Because this new request was not part of the original pleadings, the court determined that the issue was not properly before it for review.

## The 'Amended Complaint' Rule: Why New Claims Cannot Be Raised in Briefs

The court’s treatment of the injunction request highlights a fundamental procedural rule: new claims or changes in the requested relief must be raised through an amended complaint, not through motions or briefs. In federal and many state courts, the scope of a lawsuit is generally defined by the allegations and prayers for relief contained in the complaint.

By attempting to introduce a new form of relief during the summary judgment phase, Cohen bypassed the required procedural steps. To properly change the scope of the relief sought, a plaintiff must move to amend the complaint, allowing the opposing party the opportunity to respond to the new request. Because Cohen did not follow this procedure, the court correctly refused to consider the injunction as a valid part of the current litigation.
