Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### How does the 'de novo' standard for summary judgment apply when the parties have stipulated to most of the facts?
Question:
The court states it reviews the district court's summary judgment 'de novo' and must view evidence in the 'light most favorable to the nonmoving party.' However, in this case, the parties actually stipulated that Cohen was paid all wages and liquidated damages. If the facts are stipulated (agreed upon), how does the 'light most favorable' standard actually function in the court's analysis?
Answer:
When a court reviews a motion for summary judgment *de novo*, it does so without deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. Under this standard, the court must view the evidence and any inferences reasonably drawn from that evidence in the "light most favorable to the nonmoving party."

However, the "light most favorable" standard only applies to facts and inferences that are actually in dispute. A stipulation—a formal agreement between parties to admit certain facts—effectively removes those facts from the "disputed" category. When the parties stipulate to a fact, that fact is no longer a matter of evidence to be weighed or interpreted; it becomes a settled premise of the case.

In the context of *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the interaction between these standards functions as follows:

**1. Stipulated Facts are Accepted as Truth**
Because Cohen and Consilio stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed" and all liquidated damages, the court does not need to apply the "light most favorable" lens to those specific figures. There is no "other" light to view them in because there is no conflicting evidence. The court accepts these facts as true for the purpose of the analysis.

**2. The Standard Applies to the Remaining Disputed Issues**
The "light most favorable" standard is invoked only when the court reaches the issues that were *not* stipulated. In *Cohen*, while the payment of wages was settled, the parties remained in dispute over whether the statutes permitted Cohen to seek "average daily wage penalties" and whether his claim was moot based on the current status of Consilio's policy. 

For those remaining issues, the court must still view the facts and inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party (Cohen). For example, when determining whether Cohen's claim was moot, the court noted that if Consilio's policy remains in place, Cohen’s claim "may still be viable." This demonstrates the court's obligation to consider the version of the facts that supports Cohen's continued interest in the litigation.

**Summary**
In short, a stipulation "shrinks" the scope of the "light most favorable" standard. The court accepts the stipulated facts as settled, but it must still apply the *de novo* standard and the "light most favorable" lens to any remaining material facts or legal interpretations that the parties have failed to agree upon.
