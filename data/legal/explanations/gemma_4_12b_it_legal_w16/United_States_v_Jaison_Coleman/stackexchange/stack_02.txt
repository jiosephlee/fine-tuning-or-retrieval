Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### How does the third-party consent doctrine apply when the defendant is physically present in the home?
Question:
The opinion cites 'United States v. Cellitti' to establish that a third party can give consent to search a place where both she and the defendant have a legitimate expectation of privacy. In this case, Jaison Coleman was actually inside the house during the interaction with Officer Kizer. Does the presence of the defendant inside the home change the legal standard for whether Lisa's consent is sufficient to bypass the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement?
Answer:
Based on the opinion provided, the defendant's physical presence inside the home **does not change the legal standard** for whether third-party consent is sufficient to bypass the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement.

The court explicitly affirms the standard established in *United States v. Cellitti* and *United States v. Matlock*, which holds that "a third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy." This means that the shared expectation of privacy does not prevent a third party (in this case, Lisa) from waiving that privacy for the entire premises, even if the defendant (Coleman) is physically present within that space.

To determine if the consent is valid under this doctrine, the court focuses on two primary inquiries rather than the defendant's location:

1.  **Voluntariness:** The court evaluates whether the consent was "freely and voluntarily" given. In doing so, the court looks at the "totality of the circumstances," including factors such as whether the person was in police custody, the presence of physical coercion, and whether the consent was prompted by repeated requests. In *Coleman*, the court found the consent voluntary because the officer’s demeanor was "concerned, not someone coercing" and Lisa had previously demonstrated the ability to deny entry.
2.  **Scope of Consent:** Even when consent is voluntary, the subsequent search must respect the "scope of consent." The court applies an "objective reasonableness" standard, asking what a "typical reasonable person" would understand the exchange to mean. In this case, the court held that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent because their stated purpose—checking the welfare of the children—justified a scan of the home and a brief discussion with the occupants.

In summary, the defendant's presence inside the home is not a complicating factor for the *standard* of consent. The court remains focused on whether the third party had a legitimate expectation of privacy, whether their waiver of that privacy was voluntary, and whether the police stayed within the boundaries of that waiver.
