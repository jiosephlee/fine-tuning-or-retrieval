Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of Voluntariness in Domestic Violence Calls

To determine whether a search of a home is lawful under the Fourth Amendment, courts must first establish whether the permission to enter was given voluntarily. In *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit applied the "totality of the circumstances" test to evaluate whether the consent provided by Lisa Coleman was "freely and voluntarily" given. This analysis is particularly nuanced in domestic violence cases, where the urgency of police action can sometimes blur the line between a persuasive request and unlawful coercion.

## Officer Kizer's Demeanor: Concern vs. Coercion

A primary factor in determining voluntariness is the demeanor of the officer. The court looked closely at Officer Kizer’s conduct, noting that his tone was one of concern rather than coercion. In the context of a domestic violence call, police officers are often tasked with making split-second decisions to ensure the safety of residents. The court reasoned that while Officer Kizer expressed "urgency and intensity" regarding the safety of the children, this did not equate to the use of physical force or psychological pressure intended to overbear the resident’s will. Because Kizer spoke calmly and respectfully, his demeanor supported the finding that the request for entry was a legitimate police inquiry rather than a coercive demand.

## The Significance of the Initial Refusal of Entry

One of the most compelling pieces of evidence regarding voluntariness in this case was Lisa Coleman’s initial refusal to let the officers inside. When first asked, she explicitly stated, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The court viewed this as a critical indicator of her autonomy. By initially saying "no," Lisa demonstrated that she understood she had the right to deny entry and that she was not being subjected to a situation where her choice was already made for her. The fact that she chose to change her mind later, after further dialogue, suggests a conscious decision-making process rather than a submission to immediate police pressure.

## The Impact of Repeated Requests and the Word 'Need'

The defense argued that Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need”—specifically, "I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—was coercive. However, the court rejected this narrow focus. Under the "totality of the circumstances" standard, the court looks at whether the request was "prompted by repeated requests" over a long period of detention. Here, the entire exchange took place over only a few minutes, and Lisa was not in custody. The court held that expressing a "need" to ensure the safety of children in a domestic violence situation is a reasonable justification for urgency. The court concluded that while the officers were persistent, their persistence was tied to the immediate safety of the children, which the district court found did not cross the line into coercion.

## The 'Totality of the Circumstances' in the Context of Child Safety

The court’s analysis was heavily influenced by the specific context of the 911 call: a report of a man threatening to kill the mother and remaining inside the home. In such high-stakes environments, the "pressure" a resident feels to cooperate with police is often a result of the dangerous situation itself, not the actions of the officers. The court balanced the police's need to act quickly against the resident's right to privacy. Because the officers were responding to a potential life-safety emergency, their request to enter to check on the children was viewed as a balanced and sound decision-making process. The court found that the tension between police urgency and resident choice did not tip the scale toward coercion.

## Lisa’s Subsequent Actions as Evidence of Voluntary Participation

Finally, the court looked at Lisa’s behavior immediately following her "okay" as a confirmation of her voluntary intent. Rather than appearing hesitant or forced, she actively facilitated the officers' entry. She opened the door, brushed her dogs away to ensure the officers wouldn't be bitten, and led them to the specific bedroom where her children were located. These proactive steps served as "clear" evidence that she was a willing participant in the police action. Her actions demonstrated that she was not merely complying with a command, but was actively cooperating with the officers to achieve the stated goal of ensuring her children's safety.
