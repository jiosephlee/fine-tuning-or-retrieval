Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Standards of Appellate Review

To understand how an appellate court reaches its conclusion, a student must first identify the "standard of review." This is the set of rules that dictates how much deference the appellate court must give to the lower court’s decisions. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit applies different standards depending on whether it is reviewing a judge’s legal ruling, a jury’s factual finding, or a district court’s discretionary decision.

## The Distinction Between De Novo Review and Substantial Evidence Review

The court distinguishes between two primary modes of review in this opinion: *de novo* and "substantial evidence." 

*De novo* review means the appellate court looks at the issue "anew," giving no deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. In this case, the Federal Circuit applies *de novo* review to the district court’s claim constructions and its denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL). Because these are legal determinations, the Federal Circuit is free to disagree with the district court’s interpretation of the law or the patents.

In contrast, a "substantial evidence" standard is more deferential. This standard is used when reviewing a jury’s factual findings, such as whether a specific device actually infringes on a patent. Under this standard, the appellate court does not ask if it *agrees* with the jury; it only asks if a "reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial."

## Reviewing the Denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) for Noninfringement

A motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) occurs when a party argues that, even when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the opposing party, no reasonable jury could possibly find in favor of that opponent. 

The Federal Circuit reviews the district court’s denial of such a motion under the law of the regional circuit (in this case, the Fifth Circuit). The standard is demanding: the appellate court will only reverse the district court's denial if the evidence "points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." 

In *Finesse*, this standard was central to the analysis of the ’134 and ’775 patents. While the district court felt there was enough evidence for a jury to find infringement, the Federal Circuit ultimately found that the evidence—specifically the contradictory testimony of the plaintiff’s expert—was not "overwhelming" enough to support the jury's verdict.

## The 'Substantial Evidence' Standard and the 'Reasonable Juror' Test

While the JMOL standard looks for "overwhelming" evidence, the "substantial evidence" standard used to review the jury’s infringement finding is slightly different. A jury’s finding of infringement is supported by substantial evidence if a "reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party."

This creates a nuanced hierarchy of evidence:
1. If the evidence is so weak that *no* reasonable jury could have found infringement, the court should have granted JMOL.
2. If the evidence is sufficient that a *reasonable* jury could have found infringement (even if the appellate court thinks the evidence is thin), the jury’s verdict stands.

In this opinion, the court used these tests to evaluate the plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Wells. Because Dr. Wells provided contradictory testimony regarding what signals were being sampled and multiplied, the court concluded that the evidence did not meet the threshold required to sustain the jury's verdict. Because the evidence was not sufficient to support a finding of infringement, the court reversed the denial of JMOL.

## Reviewing the Denial of a New Trial on Damages (Abuse of Discretion)

Finally, the court addresses the district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial on damages. The standard of review here is "abuse of discretion." 

An "abuse of discretion" standard is the most deferential level of review. It means the appellate court will only overturn the district court’s decision if it was arbitrary, capricious, or clearly erroneous. However, there is a procedural shortcut in this specific case: because the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s JMOL ruling (meaning they found no infringement occurred), the damages award was automatically vacated. Since there was no infringement, there were no damages to be awarded, making the standard of review for the new trial motion secondary to the finding of noninfringement.
