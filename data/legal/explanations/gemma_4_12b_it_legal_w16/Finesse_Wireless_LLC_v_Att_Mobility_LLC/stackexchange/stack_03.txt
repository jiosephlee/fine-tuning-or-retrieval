Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### In patent law, when does a claim amendment during prosecution constitute a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of prior claim scope?
Question:
Regarding the ’775 patent, the Appellants argued that Finesse surrendered the right to claim products using only 'two signals' when they amended the claim to say 'three signals.' The court disagreed, saying it wasn't a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal. Can someone explain the test the court uses to determine when an amendment effectively 'gives up' a certain scope of the invention?
Answer:
### Clear and Unambiguous Disavowal in Patent Prosecution

To determine whether an amendment during prosecution constitutes a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of prior claim scope, the court applies a standard that requires more than a mere narrowing of the claim language. The patentee must have surrendered the specific scope of the previous claim in a way that is unmistakable.

**The Standard for Disavowal**
The court looks for evidence that the patentee explicitly or implicitly gave up the right to claim a certain embodiment. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court emphasized that simply amending a claim to a more specific set of limitations does not automatically result in a disavowal of the broader scope previously claimed. For a disavowal to be "clear and unambiguous," the amendment must be interpreted in a way that effectively excludes the previous scope.

**Application to the ’775 Patent**
In the case of the ’775 patent, the Appellants argued that by changing the claim language from "two or three signals" to "three signals," Finesse had surrendered the right to claim products that use only "two signals." The court rejected this argument based on the following reasoning:

1.  **Interpretation of the New Limitation:** The court found that the amended limitation—reciting "three signals S1, S2 and S3"—did not necessarily require the signals to be unique. Instead, the court held that the amendment required the signals to be "separately identifiable."
2.  **Compatibility with Prior Scope:** Because the court determined that "three signals" could be satisfied by using two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals, the amendment did not actually preclude the accused radios (which used only two unique signals). 
3.  **Insufficient Disavowal:** The court concluded that the transition from "two or three" to "three" did not rise to the level of a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal because the new claim language could still encompass the same underlying technology (two unique inputs) that the previous claim covered.

**Conclusion**
The test, therefore, is whether the amended claim language *precludes* the prior scope. If the amended limitation can be interpreted to include the same configurations as the original claim—even if the phrasing is different—the patentee has not "given up" that scope. In *Finesse*, the court held that Finesse maintained its scope over products with two unique signals because the requirement of "three signals" was not strictly interpreted as a requirement for "three unique signals."
