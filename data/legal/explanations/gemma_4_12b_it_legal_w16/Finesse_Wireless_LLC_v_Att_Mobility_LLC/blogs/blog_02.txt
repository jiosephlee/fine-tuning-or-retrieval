Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Prosecution History and the 'Unique Signal' Trap: Navigating Claim Scope in Signal Processing

In the high-stakes arena of telecommunications patent litigation, the boundary between a broad, enforceable claim and a narrow, unenforceable one often hinges on a single word of technical phrasing. The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* highlights this precarious balance, particularly regarding how prosecution history affects the scope of mathematical and signal-processing limitations. By examining the '775 patent, the court offered a nuanced look at the "separately identifiable" versus "unique" distinction, providing a roadmap for how practitioners must navigate the tension between maintaining claim breadth and avoiding prosecution history estoppel.

The dispute centered on a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. Specifically, the '775 patent claimed a method of generating interference cancellation signals by "digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3" in seven specific multiplications. At the heart of the infringement analysis was a fundamental question of quantity: Did the patentee’s requirement for "three signals" exclude a device that utilizes only two unique input signals?

The district court had construed "three signals" to mean "separately identifiable" but not necessarily "unique." This construction allowed for a scenario where a device might use two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals to satisfy the "three" requirement. The court’s analysis of the prosecution history was pivotal here. Finesse had originally sought to claim "two or three signals" but was forced by the examiner to narrow that language to "three signals" to overcome an obviousness rejection based on the *Filipovic* reference. 

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to view this amendment as a disavowal of "two signals" is a significant observation for patent practitioners. The court reasoned that because the *Filipovic* rejection was based on the *type* of intermodulation (active vs. passive) rather than the *number* of signals, the amendment to "three" did not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" abandonment of products using only two unique signals. Essentially, the court found that a patentee can sometimes narrow a claim to overcome a specific prior art hurdle without completely surrendering the underlying technology if the reason for the narrowing is distinct from the technical difference between the prior art and the claimed invention.

However, the court’s ultimate reversal of the infringement finding underscores a sobering reality for technical patents: construction is only half the battle. Even though the court accepted that "three" did not require "three unique" signals, the patentee still failed to prove that the accused radio actually performed the seven specific multiplications required by the claim. The expert testimony failed to bridge the gap between the three distinct multiplications found in the Nokia technical documents and the seven multiplications listed in the patent. This highlights a "technical literalism" that often defeats broad constructions; if the underlying mathematical operations of the accused device do not map precisely onto the claim limitations, the claim’s breadth becomes irrelevant.

The downstream implications for hardware and signal-processing patents are clear. Practitioners must be wary of the "unique signal" trap. While the *Finesse* court provided some breathing room by allowing "separately identifiable" signals (like a signal and its copy) to count toward a numerical limit, the decision reinforces that the specific *process*—the "how"—is just as important as the "how many." For litigators, the case serves as a reminder that while prosecution history might not always be a death knell for claim scope, a lack of precise evidentiary mapping during trial can be equally fatal. To succeed in these spaces, a patent must be drafted with enough specificity to be defensible against prior art, yet with enough functional flexibility to survive the rigorous technical scrutiny of a jury.
