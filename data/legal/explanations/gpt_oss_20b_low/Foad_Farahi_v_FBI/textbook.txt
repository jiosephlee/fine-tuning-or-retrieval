Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# I. Background and Factual Context

In preparing to read and reason through a court opinion, it is essential first to ground oneself in the factual matrix that prompted the dispute. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the origin of the FOIA controversy lies in Farahi’s long‑standing residence in the United States and the attendant immigration proceedings that followed a visa overstay in 2002. The case will later shift to national‑security concerns, but the facts that prompted the FBI’s file creation and the subsequent FOIA request remain the backbone of the litigation.

## Foad Farahi’s Immigration Status and Removal Proceedings

Foad Farahi is an Iranian citizen who arrived in the United States in 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) placed Farahi in removal proceedings after his visa had expired. Over the years, he repeatedly sought relief from removal by filing applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. Each attempt met with a refusal, and the immigration docket evolved into a potentially messy web of petitions and denials. Importantly, the removal proceedings were the first formal context in which the FBI became involved. We are told that during this immigration litigation, DHS introduced FBI testimonies attesting that Farahi had ties to “high‑level terrorists,” a claim that would later become more tested in the appellate opinion.

## Alleged Ties to Terrorist Organizations and Individuals

The FBI’s destructive threat isn’t the immigration status itself, but the allegations that Farahi was connected to a host of designated terrorist individuals and organizations. According to the record, the FBI had collected material suggesting that Farahi was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood and that he had contact with Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, a key Al Qaeda figure. The allegations also extended to links with other known terrorists, such as Jose Padilla, and involvement with charities that were later deemed to have financed terrorism, such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. These claims, as the opinion explains, formed the basis of the FBI’s investigative narrative and thereby the core of the records that would be withheld under FOIA Exemption 7(A).

## Previous FOIA Request and FBI’s Initial Response

Farahi submitted a FOIA request to gain access to his FBI file. The FBI identified an alleged 10,750 pages and 80 compact discs that potentially contained responsive information. In its initial response, the FBI complied partially: some portions of the file were released, but the bulk of the material remained hidden. Farahi sued after the agency missed the statutory deadline for a complete answer, and the subsequent litigation forced the FBI to review its holdings and provide statements from its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). These statements, which eventually became the central evidence for the agency’s possession of an Exemption 7(A) shield, demonstrated that the FBI had “compiled” the documents for law‑enforcement purposes and that their release could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were then pending or reasonably anticipated.” The eventual summary‑judgment motion, denied at first and then granted upon updated declarations, was the primary trigger for the appellate review. The facts, as we see them in the opinion, show why the DOJ’s and the court’s focus lies squarely on the content of those seized documents and the timing of the investigations that inspired them.

# II. Procedural Posture and FOIA Regime

The court’s opinion traces a long procedural path that began with Foad Farahi’s Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request and ended with the district court’s grant of summary judgment, which was subsequently affirmed on appeal. This chapter dissects that trajectory and situates it within the statutory architecture of FOIA, emphasizing the roles of Exemption 7(A), statutory “exemptions” versus “exclusions,” and the procedural requirements for agency obligation to segregate and disclose non‑exempt portions of records.

## Statutory Structure of § 552(a) and § 552(b)

Section 552 of the United States Code codifies the FOIA. Subsection (a) establishes the **primary procedural framework** that governs how requests must be made, responded to, and contested. It requires agencies to notify a requester “in writing” of the agency’s determination (re‑opening, dismissing, or serving the record) and sets deadlines—13 days for a full, unqualified response and 30 days for a “partial” response or a request for a “public‑interest summary.” If an agency cannot comply within those time limits, it must provide a **remainder**—i.e., a list of records it intends to release after the deadline lapses—and a plausible reason for the delay.

Subsection (b) lists 18 statutory **exemptions** that can bar disclosure of otherwise responsive records. Exemptions are *fully automatic* bars once the agency meets its burden to show that the requested material meets a specific statutory criterion (e.g., the “national security” exemption or the “executive‑confidential” exemption). Exemptions differ from the 12 “exclusions” in § 552(c), which are governed by a system of “public‑interest summaries.” Exclusions are *faceted* disclosures; the agency can publish a summary of the sensitive portions while releasing the remainder, provided it meets its summary‑disclosure duty.

Both sections have operational intersections: a request must first be processed under § 552(a). If the agency finds that a request is eligible for a summary (or could compel partial compliance), it can invoke § 552(c); otherwise, it judges whether any of the 18 § 552(b) exemptions fully exclude the material. 

The section of focus in Farahi’s case is Exemption 7(A), which shields records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” *and* that “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The court repeatedly underscored that the *law‑enforcement* nexus arises automatically for any agency whose core functions involve preventing or investigating crime—such as the FBI. Consequently, the FBI’s determination that the “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” test is satisfied receives a degree of deference (see *Campbell* and *Clem*). The *interference* test, however, obliges the agency to demonstrate that the release of specific records would *interfere* with *pending or reasonably anticipated* proceedings—a standard the court scrutinized closely.

## FOIA Exemptions and the Duty to Disclose Non‑Exempt Portions

FOIA’s core principle is the balance between open government and legitimate confidentiality. Once an agency concludes that an exemption applies, the agency is required to identify which numeric sections or categories of the file are exempt and then to **segregate** and release any non‑exempt material—this is the “reasonable steps” obligation codified in § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The statute permits the agency to issue a “public‑interest summary” (if the record is subject to § 552(c) exclusions) or to disclose the entire non‑exempt content directly. 

In practice—especially with large, hard‑copy/ digital files—the **segregability** analysis can become complex. The court noted that the FBI’s *Bender* supplement tried to persuade the court that non‑exempt information was *inextricably intertwined* with the exempt portion. Jurisprudence allows an agency to keep a file whole if “nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions” (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61). Crucial here is the agency’s burden to show, with reasonable specificity, *why* segregation is impossible. The district court found this burden met in the Bender declaration and in the earlier Hardy declarations; thus the FBI need not set out a fine‑grained matrix of individual pages or documents.

The court further pointed out that the “segregability” obligation is not a matter of “tipping the scales on a line‑by‑line basis.” Even if random excerpts could be redacted, that does not reduce the overall non‑exempt portion to a protected, semipublic record. The *Mead* precedent held that the focus remains on the *body of information*, not on piecemeal editing. Accordingly, the court rejected the argument that some isolated passages could be released.

## Summary Judgment in FOIA Action and De Novo Review

The plaintiff had brought a civil action under FOIA after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding. The secretary of the agency moved for **summary judgment** in favor of the agency—a motion that is routine in FOIA litigation because the primary question often hinges on whether an exemption applies, a question that can be proven from the agency’s evidence alone. Under *Rep. Comm. for Freedom of the Press* v. FBI, *3 U. S. 4th* at 350, summary judgment in FOIA actions is reviewed **de novo** by the court. That is, the appellate panel examines the summary‑judgment motion fresh, without deference to the district court’s earlier findings.

The standard in de novo review is that the court must **confirm** that the agency satisfied its burden to establish an exemption. If the agency’s evidence is sufficient, the court gives *equal weight* to the evidence from the agency’s affidavits and the plaintiff’s counter‑evidence. The district court’s initial denial of summary judgment, after finding that only a “seemingly” met the burden, illustrates the effect of new time allowing the agency to update its basis. A **summary‑judgment motion on a revised claim** can be granted if the agency can again meet the burden—exactly what the FBI did by presenting updated declarations including Dr. Bender’s statements.

Because the appellate court de novo reviews the admission of evidence in summary‑judgment motions, it must examine whether the agency’s evidence, as recorded in the *Hardy* and *Bender* affidavits, is competent, detailed, and consistent in showing (1) the law‑enforcement nexus, and (2) that release would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The court found that satisfactory evidence was present, even after scrutinizing the “cessation of time” issue, thus affirming the agency’s summary judgment.

## Agency Obligations Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

The clause § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) obliges an agency to “make reasonable efforts to segregate and release non‑exempt records.” The concept of “reasonable steps” does not mean a perfect segregation of a massive file but requires: (1) identification of the exempt portions, (2) an explanation of why those portions are exempt, and (3) a plan (or justification) for a partial release. For *serious* files, the agency may file an **ex parte affidavit** that explains why segregation is impossible. This ex parte evidence is accepted by the court so long as the plaintiff does not offer a contrary  higher‑quality, in‑person opposed testimony—an exclusion that could be remedied by an in‑camera hearing, but only in the extraordinary event the material is so restricted that disclosing it would jeopardize national security (as argued for *ex parte* declarations in *Perioperative Services*, *57 U. S. 4th* 1061).

The appellate court places emphasis on the *FBI’s* compliance with this requirement. The “Bender” declaration, comprised of a public and an ex parte subsection, was deemed sufficiently detailed. The court concluded that the FBI had *reasonably* marked out the portions of its 10,750‑page, 80‑CD file that fell under Exemption 7(A)—mostly interrogation transcripts, surveillance logs, and interagency memos—while arguing that the remaining pages were “inextricably intertwined” with exempt information. The appellate court also noted that the *FBI* had followed the procedural steps for responding to a FOIA request: it supplied remainders, extended the deadline, and eventually filed the required declarations. No procedural steps were omitted that would render the agency ineligible for § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) relief.

In sum, the procedural analysis establishes that the **district court’s denial of summary judgment was based on an insufficient evidentiary standard**, whereas the **subsequent renewal of the FBI’s motion, fortified by the *Bender* declaration, satisfied the depository’s burden** to show that the higher‑level Exemption 7(A) applies and that the agency was unable to segregate the exempt portion. The appellate court, reviewing the motion **de novo**, confirmed that the agency’s representations were convincing and bound by statutory precedent. Consequently, the Court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment and upheld the FBI’s withholding of the requested records.

# III. Exemption 7(A): Legal Standard and Deference

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) balances the public’s right to know against the government’s legitimate need to protect certain information. Exemption 7(A) embodies that balance for records that originate within a law‑enforcement context. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* the court clarified both the doctrinal requirements of Exemption 7(A) and the policy rationale for a preferred level of deference to law‑enforcement agencies. The following analysis unpacks these elements and situates them in the D.C. Circuit’s jurisprudence.

## Definition of “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes”

Section 552(b)(7)(A) protects records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” from FOIA disclosure when they “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” A record is *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* when it is created, gathered, or maintained to fulfill a law‑enforcement activity. The onus for establishing this element lies on the government. Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its determination that a file was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” carries *fair measure of deference* under the *deference* doctrine (see *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017)).  

In practice, the government must show a *rational nexus* between the records and its law‑enforcement duty. The record must also *concern an individual or incident* that potentially represents a security risk or federal law violation. The *per‑agency* standard is satisfied when the documents are produced as part of an ongoing investigation. In *Farahi*, the FBI’s declarations—both public and ex‑parte—attested that the held files were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” thereby establishing the required nexus and satisfying the burden. The court emphasized that the FBI is “specialized in law enforcement”; therefore, its judgment that the files were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes is entitled to a “fair measure of deference.” (This deference mirrors the *strict scrutiny of reasonableness* applied in *Mapother* and *Crews*, but is narrower because the agency’s expertise is central to the record’s creation.)

## Requirement that Disclosure “Could Reasonably Be Expected to Interfere”

Once the records are shown to be law‑enforcement compiling, the government must still prove that the disclosure could *reasonably be expected* to interfere with a proceeding. The *“could reasonably be expected”* standard requires the government to articulate how release would harm the investigation. Typical harms include:

1. **Self‑incrimination for the target** (the investigator anticipates that disclosure could reveal investigative techniques).
2. **Obstruction of investigations** (targets might warn associates or destroy evidence).
3. **Exposure of confidential relationships** or sources.

In *Farahi*, the FBI argued that releasing the information could “tip off the targets about the scope of the investigation.” The court accepted that this is an established, *widely accepted* justification for Exemption 7(A) disclosure (e.g., in *CNC, v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 929 (D.C. Cir. 2003)). The *ex‑parte* declaration from Bender supplied the court with confirmation that the ongoing investigation would be jeopardized by premature release.

The *“could reasonably be expected”* threshold is satisfied on a *balance‑of‑probabilities* basis: the government need only present *reasonable* evidence that disclosure would interfere; it does not have to prove the interference would occur inevitably.

## Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

A further textual requirement is that the anticipated interference arise **from proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated**. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly stressed the “pending” component. *Mapother* defined a pending proceeding as “an existing enforcement proceeding that is live, i.e., the federal government is actively seeking a judgment.’’ *Crews* extended the definition to include proceedings that are “reasonable” to anticipate based on available evidence—e.g., “an ongoing investigation that may lead to a future criminal case.”

In *Farahi*, the FBI identified a nexus to “pending law‑enforcement proceedings” in its Hardy declarations and later confirmed continuity of those proceedings through the Bender declaration. The district court’s “seemingly” met the burden note underscores that the evidence was persuasive, though not ironclad. The court’s refusal to deny summary judgment without prejudice was premised on an acknowledgment that the *chronology* required updating; a standard rule that the *law* references *time* when evaluating “pending” versus “reasonable anticipation.”

The key takeaway is that *pending* and *reasonably anticipated* are distinct but both require evidence. The proceeding need not be completed; it merely must be *looming* with a realistic chance of materialization.

## Deference to Agencies in the FOIA Context

Deference is a pillar of FOIA adjudication. The relevant standards differ from *deference* in other contexts:

1. **Statutory Deference**: For FOIA, the highest level is *deference in the factual determination* that a record is exempt (the *“practical”* standard). The court presumes the agency’s words are truthful unless the plaintiff shows *contradictory* evidence. (cf. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32; *Clemente*, 867 F.3d 111, 119.)

2. **Policy Deference**: When the agency asserts an institutional policy (e.g., that certain documents are gathered for national‑security investigations), the court examines whether the policy *includes an exception* for FOIA disclosure. If the policy is *unanimous and appears to be a law‑enforcement policy*, *Farahi* capitalized on that deference (“such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security”).

3. **Expertise Deference**: Exemption 7(A) is steeped in *law‑enforcement expertise*. The court therefore places a higher weight on the agency’s *professional judgement* (e.g., that an investigative file is intertwined with “national security” concerns). This tenet is precisely what the court exercised when “the FBI specialized in law enforcement,” whereby its decision on applicability “is entitled to a fair measure of deference.”

In sum, the policy deference rule, coupled with the *ex‑parte* feeding of the court on sensitive details, results in a solid shield for law‑enforcement records subject to Exemption 7(A).

## Prior Cases Shaping the Standard

1. **CREW (Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014))** – *CREW* laid out the fundamental test for Exclusion 7(A): Records must be compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and the disclosure must reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The court distinguished between *“pending”* and *“reasonably anticipated”*, emphasizing that actual knowledge of ongoing enforcement proceedings is a critical element.

2. **Juarez (Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008))** – *Juarez* refined the *“reasonable anticipation”* doctrine, noting that a government can show a pending or *reasonably anticipated* proceeding merely by attesting to its existence (not requiring a formal indictment). *Juarez* also underlined the *inextricable intertwining* principle, permitting agencies to withhold entire records when the exempt part cannot be segregated from the non‑exempt part.

3. **CNSS (Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003))** – *CNSS* expanded the scope for national‑security cases, affirming that investigation of a national‑security threat can qualify it for Exemption 7(A) even if the investigation is not linked to a criminal case. The court made a *policy‑deferential* approach explicit, saying that when national‑security concerns are implicated “the executive’s position should be respected.”

4. **Mapother (Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993))** – *Mapother* established the earlier standard for “pending” proceedings, contributing to the baseline that *Crews* later refined.

5. **Clemente (Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017))** – *Clemente* reaffirmed that the FBI’s discretion to issue the “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” determination is deserving of *fair measure of deference*.

These cases collectively set a procedural and substantive framework that *Farahi* respects: proof of law‑enforcement compilation, proof of probable interference, evidence of pending or anticipated proceedings, and a heightened deference to agencies’ expertise and discrete policy.

---

**Take‑away for students:** Exemption 7(A) is a layered shield. When tackling an FOIA claim that involves potential law‑enforcement records, first confirm the agency’s *law‑enforcement nexus*. Then ask whether the request could *reasonably* interfere with *ongoing or but‑to‑occur* proceedings. Finally, expect the court to defer to the agency’s professional judgment—a deference that is far more pronounced in the national‑security context than in other FOIA controversies. Knowing how the D.C. Circuit’s jurisprudence—especially *CREW*, *Juarez*, and *CNSS*—has interpreted each element will allow you to scaffold the government’s burden and anticipate the court’s likely conclusions.

# IV. FBI’s Certifications, Segregability Analysis, and Court Agreement

## Content and Purpose of Hardy’s Public and Ex‑Parte Declarations

The FBI’s Section Chief of Records, Information, and Dissemination (RIDS), David Hardy, submitted two separate affidavits in support of the agency’s summary‑judgment motion. The first, a public declaration, set out the key facts the FBI posited that would satisfy FOIA Exemption 7(A): (1) that the extensive collection of materials—about ten thousand pages and eighty CDs—had been compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national‑security and international‑terrorism law”; and (2) that the material concerned “ongoing law‑enforcement investigations” involving “the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” By invoking the standard pre‑disclosure, Hardy aimed to establish the basic premises of the exemption, namely that the records were law‑enforcement files and that their release would pose a foreseeable risk to law‑enforcement proceedings.

Hardy’s accompanying ex‑parte declaration, filed under seal and accepted by the district court’s ex‑parte process, sought to provide the detail that could not be published without jeopardizing the investigation. This sealed affidavit furnished evidence of the FBI’s ongoing investigative work—such as the identification of suspects, the nature of their alleged offenses, and the strategic objectives of the investigation. While its contents cannot be reproduced in the record, the court relied on them to confirm the allegations made in the public declaration. The ex‑parte declaration also underscored that the FBI had complied with FOIA’s segregation duty to the extent that no material was “reasonably segregable” from the exempt portions, a point that would later be crucial to the court’s decision.

The juxtaposition of public and ex‑parte affidavits reflects the FOIA framework: the agency must avoid disclosing material that could tip off suspects or compromise an active investigation, yet the court must still be able to assess the agency’s claims. The public affidavit establishes the broad picture, while the sequestered affidavit supplies the data that substantiates the picture without revealing it to the requester.

## Bender’s Update and Its Role in the Record

In November 2022, when the district court denied the FBI’s initial summary‑judgment.

motion without prejudice, Judge J.A. ordered the agency to provide fresh evidence that the enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated and that the files remained non‑segregable. The FBI complied by submitting a new set of affidavits from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration contained a succinct statement: “the relevant investigations remain ongoing as of March 2023.” Though brief, the statement repeated the same claim made in Hardy’s earlier affidavit and reiterated as a factual update. The ex‑parte Bender declaration elaborated on the reasons the FBI viewed the investigations as ongoing—mentioning ongoing surveillance, continuation of intelligence gathering, and the absence of a closure date for the cases at hand.

From a procedural standpoint, Bender’s affidavits did not introduce new facts but rather served as a confirmation that no substantive change had occurred in the FBI’s assessment over the intervening period. The court recognized that these documents were necessary to satisfy the FOIA “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation. Since the FBI had already demonstrated the interrupting nature of the investigations in Hardy’s affidavits, the Bender affidavits functioned as an update, not a new argument. The court therefore treated Bender’s statements as an extension of the original evidence, weighing them in light of the already‑established claim of pendency.

## Demonstration of Pending Investigations

The core of Exemption 7(A) hinges on two facts: that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings. The FBI’s affidavits fulfilled both prongs. The public Hardy declaration had sufficiently described the nature of the investigations—national‑security and international‑terrorism matters linked to a foreign‑national suspect—and activated the jurisdiction of FOIA. The ex‑parte affidavits, by discussing ongoing surveillance and unfinalized criminal proceedings, gave step‑by‑step confirmation of the investigations’ continuity.

The court applied the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard with the guidance from earlier cases such as *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice* and *Mapother v. Department of Justice*. The agency’s evidence that no end date had been set, that evidence was still being collected, and that investigative staff remained actively engaged, sufficed to satisfy the “reasonable anticipation” portion of the test. The court noted that ongoing investigations constitute “proceedings” for the purposes of Exemption 7(A) and that the FBI could demonstrate the existence of such proceedings by merely stating that the investigation exists.

In its analysis, the court admitted that the Bender affidavits were brief but contextualized them: the earlier affidavits had already established pendency; Bender’s statements were consistent with that established picture and confirmed that nothing had changed—thereby making their brevity understandable.

## Analysis of Non‑Segregability Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the accompanying regulation, a government entity must identify the portions of a record that are exempt and the portions that are not, segregating the non‑exempt material so that it may be released. Exemptions are meaningful only if the exempt portion is “reasonably segregable” from the non‑exempt portion; if exempt parts are “intertwined” with non‑exempt parts, the entire record may be withheld.

The FBI’s policy, as described in the affidavits, adopted a category‑by‑category approach. Each category was defined broadly—such as all documentation relating to “Khaled Sheikh Mohamed,” all records concerning “international terrorist financing,” and so on—rather than by narrow, searchable taglines. The court determined that these categories were so wide that the FBI could not reliably separate exempt material from the rest. The records in question were large, heavily cross‑referenced, and bound by subject matter that could not be isolated with a simple delimiter without substantial editorial effort, well beyond what FOIA permitted.

The district court relied heavily on Bender’s ex‑parte declaration to conclude that the portions were “inextricably intertwined.” The declaration described the FBI’s experience with documents in the file: that each document was a composite of victim reports, intelligence summaries, suspect interviews, and cross‑references to other files. When these entwining elements pervade the file, the court found that the FBI could not demonstrate that any non‑exempt portion could be “separated” without compromising the privileged aspect of the investigation. The court therefore affirmed the agency’s determination that the entire collection was non‑segregable.

## Court’s Reliance on Agency Affidavits

The district court gave deference to the FBI’s assessments of both pendency and non‑segregability. Because the agency operates within the specialized domain of law enforcement—where the presumption of national‑security relevance is implicit—courts routinely grant a "fair measure of deference" to a government scientist's insider knowledge of its files. The court cited precedent that deference is especially warranted when FOIA claims involve national‑security investigative work. The government’s affidavits, by demonstrating the investigator’s knowledge, satisfied the burden that the exempt portions would "reasonably be expected to interfere."

Moreover, the court highlighted that the affidavits were the only viable means to resolve the dispute on segregability. The essence of the FOIA dispute was whether the exempt material could be separated from the non‑exempt. In the absence of the claims the FBI filed (which, if public, could undermine operational secrecy), the court had no other factual basis to confirm or refute the FBI’s statements. Consequently, it accepted the affidavits in determining that no meaningful, reasonably segregable data existed.

In sum, the court’s agreement rested on the interplay of FOIA’s pragmatic privacy safeguards, the statutory framework, and the DOJ‑controlled gambit: the agency’s affidavits contained the only information that could legally confirm whether the investigation was still pending or whether the records could be presented. The court found that the evidence at hand satisfied the statutory thresholds for Exemption 7(A), that the FBI complied with segregation obligations, and that the claims of interference were well founded. The court therefore affirmed the FOIA denial, respecting the functional balance between transparency and law‑enforcement effectiveness.

# V. Holding, Dissent Evaluation, and Policy Implications

In its judgment, the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment and held that the FBI’s withholding of Foad Farahi’s file was properly grounded in FOIA Exemption 7(A). The court reaffirmed that the FBI’s reliance on the exemption was justified because its submissions satisfied the two essential components of the exemption: (1) the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes,” and (2) their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.” The court also reviewed the FBI’s segregation analysis and found that the agency had met its obligation to explain why no portion of the records was reasonably segregable from the exempt material. Consequently, the court concluded that all requested records remained exempt and no FOIA entitlement was violated.

## Final Holding and Scope of the Decision

The court’s holding is two‑fold. First, it confirmed that Exemption 7(A) applies to the vast majority of the Farahi file, thereby baring the FBI from disclosure. Second, it upheld the FBI’s categorization and segregation determinations, finding no adequate basis for the agency to release any portion of the material. The decision is narrowly scoped to the facts of the Farahi case; it does not alter the statutory framework or existing precedent. Rather, it clarifies the application of Exemption 7(A) in a context where a law‑enforcement agency has an extensive national‑security investigation that continues at the time of the FOIA request. The holding is expressly limited to the records identified as “compilations for law‑enforcement purposes” that could interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.” The court did not reach policy discussions beyond the procedural contours it already controls, such as the standard for deference to the executive and the rule that agencies must explain why exempt material is inseparable from non‑exempt portions.

## Critique of Farahi’s Arguments on Lag and Categorization

Farahi contended that the passage of time between the FBI’s original declaration in 2019 and its updated submissions in 2022 weakened the pendency of the underlying investigations, rendering Exemption 7(A) inapplicable. The court rebuffed this argument on three fronts:

1. **Continuity of the Investigation**: The FBI’s public declaration by David Hardy already established that the investigations into Farahi were ongoing and that service‑connected “unlawful violations of national security” were still underway. The subsequent Bender declaration served merely as an update confirming that the pendency claim remained accurate. The court therefore treated the Bender declaration not as a standalone assertion but as an extension of the earlier evidence.

2. **Sufficiency of the Evidence**: Under Exemption 7(A), a single credible affidavit or declaration is adequate to satisfy the pendency requirement if it states that an enforcement proceeding is “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court found that the Hardy and Bender affidavits met that standard; consequently, Farahi’s insistence that the agency’s statement was too vague could not prosper.

3. **Categorization of Documents**: Farahi also challenged the FBI’s use of “categories” to shield portions of the file, arguing that the categories were overbroad. The court noted that the FBI did not explain why any of its categories were overbroad or how the categories were distinct. In the absence of specific critique, Farahi was deemed to have forfeited that line of argument, per precedent in *Khine v. DHS*.

The court therefore found that Farahi's objections failed to disturb the affirmative findings of the agency. The technical ability of the court to address these claims, even if reflexively, underscores the stringent deference afforded to agency assessments of anonymity and national‑security concerns.

## Implications for Future FOIA Requests Involving National Security

The decision reaffirms several key doctrines that will shape the practice of FOIA litigation where national security is implicated:

1. **Deference to the Executive**: The court reiterated that, for a law‑enforcement agency such as the FBI, the determination that a record is “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” merits a high degree of deference. This standard will continue to shield agencies from intrusive judicial scrutiny in similar cases, limiting the block‑by‑block evaluation often seen in commercial or civil matters.

2. **Pendancy Testing “Pending or Reasonably Anticipated”**: The case clarifies that a relatively brief buffer—often a few weeks or months—can suffice, provided the agency's factual assertions are supported. The judiciary remains cautious about requiring exhaustive evidence, thereby preventing FOIA claims from destabilizing ongoing investigations.

3. **Segregability**: The court’s thorough discussion on segregation will influence how agencies segment large, complex files. The DOI that a piece of non‑exempt information is “interspersed line‑by‑line” and therefore inseparable from exempt material is a useful benchmark for future filings. FOIA requests that involve potentially large reserves of classified or privileged data will likely expose the same defect, obliging agencies to conduct a more methodical segregation analysis.

4. **The Role of Ex Parte Declarations**: The decision echoes the precedent that, in situations where the very material is at issue, ex‑parte affidavits may be relied upon to fill gaps left by the agency’s public statements. FOIA practitioners should be prepared to argue that such declarations are permissible when indispensable, though they must also be cautious about the potential for questions of fairness that can arise in that context.

5. **Recidivist Burden on Petitioners**: The case signals to petitioners that they must bring highly specific, fact‑based arguments to counter the agency’s deference. General or conclusory claims—such as “the time has passed”—will not overcome the agency’s assertions unless the court is convinced that the agency’s evidence is empty.

## Reinforcement of Deference and Segregation Principles

The court’s reaffirmation of deference serves to preserve a clear boundary between the agency’s investigative discretion and judicial oversight. By maintaining a high bar for denial of claims under Exemption 7(A), the court prevents judicial overreach in cases where national‑security and law‑enforcement objectives intersect. In addition, the rigor applied to the segregation analysis—demonstrating that the material was the result of a singular investigative process with no practical way to extract non‑exempt elements—sets a precedent for how courts will view closely intertwined records. The result is a consistent affirmation of what the FOIA statute intends: that the public interest in disclosure must be weighed against the legitimate need to preserve the integrity of ongoing national‑security investigations.

Thus, the *Foad Farahi* judgment consolidates existing FOIA doctrines and provides a useful guidebook for future litigants navigating cases where the breadth of law‑enforcement investigations and the requirement for national‑security secrecy intersect.
